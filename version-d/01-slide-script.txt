2|The objective is to answer **“What is the mind?” from an information-processing and computational perspective**. This means explaining what information is represented, what operations act on it, and how those operations produce behavior.||Perception, attention, memory, and reasoning can each be investigated in these terms. A computational perspective also asks what problem a system solves; an information-processing explanation specifies how information changes as the system solves it.
3|**Literature, philosophy, and astrology** are other ways of understanding the mind. For each approach, consider **what we are trying to explain, how we test whether something is true, and how practitioners arrive at consensus**.||These questions distinguish a subject from a method. Approaches can discuss the same mind while using different procedures for testing claims and resolving disagreement. Cognitive psychology connects its explanations to systematic, repeatable evidence.
4|**Cognitive psychology** tries to explain **how people process information about their environment** and the **mechanisms involved in perceiving, acting, and thinking**. The aim is to connect a proposed mechanism with evidence about what people do.||Claims are tested through **controlled laboratory experiments** and **modeling**, including **simulation and statistical models**. An experiment compares conditions; a model makes an explanation explicit enough to compare with data.||**Publications and replications** contribute to how practitioners arrive at consensus, but consensus is often incomplete. Researchers can agree on a result while continuing to disagree about its best explanation.
7|An intelligent robot would need to **perceive and interpret its environment, select relevant information, learn and remember, communicate with people, and plan, decide, and act safely**. These capabilities have to work together.||Applications such as **care, disaster response, logistics, and assistance** require more than a single correct identification. The system must interpret what is happening, use what it knows, choose relevant information, and act appropriately as conditions change.
8|**Cognition involves perception, paying attention, remembering, distinguishing items in a category, visualizing, understanding and production of language, problem solving, reasoning, and decision-making**. These are different functions within mental activity.||All include **“hidden” processes of which we may not be aware**. Distinguishing categories, for example, requires information to be organized even if the final judgment feels immediate. Ease of experience does not reveal every operation that made the response possible.
9|**Cognitive psychology is the branch of psychology concerned with the scientific study of the mind.** Everyday uses of “mind” point to several different functions.||**“He was able to call to mind what he was doing on the day of the accident”** concerns remembering. **“If you put your mind to it, I’m sure you can solve that math problem”** concerns thinking and problem solving. **“I haven’t made up my mind yet”** concerns deciding.||**“He is of sound mind and body”** concerns mental functioning, while **“He has a brilliant mind”** concerns intellectual ability. Scientific investigation turns these broad references into questions about particular mental processes and their evidence.
10|**The mind creates and controls mental processes such as perception, attention, memory, emotions, language, deciding, thinking, and reasoning.** These processes concern what the mind does with information.||**The mind creates representations of the world so that we can act within it to achieve our goals.** A representation supplies information about something; a process uses or changes information.||Some theories challenge the **distinction between processes and representations**. The distinction is a useful starting point, but it is also part of what theories of the mind debate.
11|Many philosophers have had things to say about the mind. **Socrates had a distinct epistemology**, an approach to knowledge. **Laozi discussed the unreliability of perception**. Questions about knowledge and experience therefore extend beyond experimental psychology.||The scientific approach here uses **quantifiable, repeatable measurement**. A proposed explanation needs a connection to observations that can be measured and obtained again.||More than the content of the questions, **the procedures for asking questions and establishing consensus distinguish cognitive psychology from other ways of studying the mind**.
12|**Franciscus Donders**, a **Dutch physiologist**, conducted one of the first cognitive psychology experiments in **1868**. He was interested in **measuring how long it takes a person to make a decision**.||A **reaction-time (RT) experiment measures the interval between stimulus presentation and the person’s response to the stimulus**. Start timing when the light appears and stop when the response occurs.||That interval includes detecting the stimulus and producing an action as well as any decision. A comparison between tasks is needed to estimate the time contributed by deciding.
13|In **Donders’ simple RT task**, the participant **pushes a button quickly after a light appears**. The response is prepared because there is only one required action.||In the **choice RT task**, the participant **pushes one button if the light is on the right side and another if the light is on the left side**. The illustrated keys are **J for the left light and K for the right light**; the simple task uses J.||The choice task adds deciding which response is appropriate. Comparing its reaction time with simple RT provides an estimate of the additional decision time.
14|The **late-nineteenth-century reaction-time apparatus** measures the interval from presenting a stimulus to registering a response. The equipment makes that interval observable and measurable.||The interval includes **perceiving the light, any decision about the response, and pressing the button**. The apparatus records the total rather than displaying a thought directly.||Comparing conditions is therefore essential: a single reaction time cannot identify how much time belongs to each mental operation.
15|In the simple task, **the light flashes**, the person **perceives the light**, and then **presses the button**. Stimulus presentation and button pressing can be recorded; the intervening mental response is inferred.||In the choice task, a **left light flashes**, the person **perceives the left light and decides which button to push**, and then **presses the left button**.||The comparison separates the observable **behavioral response** from the inferred **mental response**. Subtraction is informative when the tasks share the relevant operations apart from the added decision.
16|**Decision time = choice RT − simple RT.** Choice RT was about **0.10 seconds longer than simple RT**, so the estimated decision took about **0.10 seconds**, or **100 milliseconds**.||Subtracting removes the time attributed to shared operations, leaving an estimate of the additional decision requirement. This assumes those shared operations are sufficiently comparable across tasks.||The result is an inference from reaction times. It does not mean Donders directly observed the decision or measured a thought independently of behavior.
17|**Donders (1868)** showed that **mental responses cannot be measured directly but can be inferred from participants’ behavior**. He measured reaction times and inferred how long the additional mental response took.||This approach extends across cognitive psychology: researchers measure an observable response and ask what it implies about underlying cognitive activity. The strength of the inference depends on the comparison.||The **“subtraction” approach** also remains important in **neuroimaging**, where conditions are compared to estimate a contribution from added processing.
18|**Wilhelm Wundt (1879) founded the first experimental psychology laboratory in Leipzig**. He used **controlled self-observation to study conscious experience**. Specifying conditions was part of bringing experience into laboratory investigation.||**Titchener and structuralism** emphasized trained observers describing the **basic elements of experience**. Introspection aimed to analyze experience into those elements.||**Introspection proved difficult to quantify and replicate.** Variable reports made it difficult for different researchers to verify the same result and settle disagreements reliably.
19|**Ebbinghaus (1885/1913), at the University of Berlin, Germany, investigated the time course of forgetting**: how rapidly information that is learned is lost over time. He used a **quantitative method for measuring memory**.||He **read a list of nonsense syllables aloud many times**, recording the repetitions necessary to repeat it **without errors**. After some time, he **relearned the list**. Nonsense syllables reduced support from familiar meanings.||**Short intervals meant fewer repetitions to relearn.** He learned many different lists at many different **retention intervals**, making it possible to measure how retained learning changed with delay.
20|**Savings = original time to learn the list − time to relearn the list after a delay.** A **savings curve shows savings as a function of retention interval**. More savings means a greater benefit from the earlier learning.||With **1,000 seconds** of original learning, relearning after **19 minutes** takes **400 seconds**, giving **600 seconds saved**. After **one day**, **650 seconds** gives **350 seconds saved**; after **six days**, **730 seconds** gives **270 seconds saved**.||The lower graph uses **percent savings**: divide saved time by original learning time and multiply by 100. Its delays include **19 minutes, one hour, 8.75 hours, one day, two days, six days, and 31 days**. Savings drops rapidly at first and then more slowly, showing retained learning even when immediate recall is incomplete.
21|**William James’ Principles of Psychology** considered **thinking, consciousness, attention, memory, perception, imagination, and reasoning**. James was an early American psychologist who **taught the first psychology course at Harvard University**.||His observations were based on **the functions of his own mind, not experiments**. This differs from controlled comparisons of reaction time or relearning.||His brother **Henry**, shown on the left, wrote psychological novels, mainly about heiresses. After finishing his major psychology book, William focused on **parapsychology**, trying to apply scientific methods to **séances**.
22|Many psychology departments worked in the laboratory tradition associated with **Wundt and Titchener**. **John Watson (1878–1958)** identified **extremely variable results from person to person** and **results that were difficult to verify** because explanations referred to invisible inner mental processes.||Watson proposed **behaviorism**: **eliminate the mind as a topic of study and instead study directly observable behavior**.||That proposal excluded unobservable explanatory processes such as **reasoning, thinking, and emotions**. The aim was a more objective account based on observable conditions and responses.
23|**Watson’s behaviorist manifesto (1913)** states: **“Psychology as the behaviorist views it is a purely objective experimental branch of natural science.”**||The goal is to **predict and control behavior without appealing to unobservable consciousness**. Observable stimuli and responses provide the basis for explanation.||This gains a publicly measurable target while restricting explanations in terms of internal mental processes. The disagreement concerns whether those observable relationships alone adequately explain behavior.
24|Watson’s proposal is to **replace the mind as psychology’s object of study with directly observable behavior**. The question changes from **“What does behavior tell us about the mind?”** to **“What is the relationship between environmental stimuli and behavior?”**||The first question uses behavior to infer mental operations. The second makes the stimulus–response relationship the explanatory target.||Both approaches can record the same action, but they differ in what they consider an explanation of it. Behaviorism focuses on observable relationships rather than an internal representation.
25|**Watson and Rayner (1920)** investigated **classical conditioning of fear** in the **“Little Albert” experiment**. A **nine-month-old** became frightened by a rat after a **loud noise was paired with presentations of the rat**.||The experiment examined **how pairing one stimulus with another affected behavior**. The rat, initially neutral with respect to this fear response, came to elicit fear after pairing with the noise.||Watson used this to support analyzing behavior without reference to the mind. It is classical conditioning because the key relationship is between stimuli, rather than an action and its consequences.
26|Watson drew on **Pavlov’s conditioning research**: **pair a neutral stimulus with one that naturally produces a response**. After repeated pairings, **the formerly neutral stimulus can elicit the response**.||Pavlov studied **digestion**, and conditioning emerged from observations of **anticipatory salivation**. A response could occur to a predictive event before the food itself arrived.||The methodological lesson is that **behavior changes as stimulus relationships change**. The learned relation concerns which stimulus predicts another event.
27|**B. F. Skinner (1940s–1960s)** studied relationships among **situations, actions, and consequences**. In **operant conditioning, consequences shape future behavior**.||**Reinforcement increases the likelihood of a behavior; punishment decreases the likelihood of a behavior.** The definition depends on how subsequent behavior changes.||The central relationship is an action and what follows it. This differs from classical conditioning, where a relation between stimuli changes the response elicited by a stimulus.
28|The historical sequence includes **Donders’ reaction-time work in 1868**, **Wundt’s scientific psychology laboratory in 1879**, **Ebbinghaus’ forgetting curve in 1885**, and **William James’ Principles of Psychology in 1890**.||**Watson’s behaviorism appears in 1913**, followed by a **Skinner operant-conditioning milestone in 1938**. These entries locate the rise of behaviorism among earlier approaches to studying minds.||The emphasis shifted toward observable stimulus–response relationships. Earlier experimental methods remained useful even as the preferred kind of explanation changed.
29|Although **behaviorism dominated American psychology for decades**, some researchers were not strictly behaviorists. **Tolman (1938) trained rats to find food in a four-armed maze**.||Initially the rat **explored the maze, running up and down the alleys**. It was then **placed in A with food in B**, and quickly learned to **turn right** to get food.||When the rat was **placed in C**, it **turned left** to get the same food. Changing the starting point separates repeating a turn from navigating to a particular location.
30|The maze sequence is **explore the maze → turn right for food → turn left for food**. From **A**, a right turn reaches food at **B**. From **C**, reaching B requires a left turn.||The food location stays the same while the required turn changes. A learned **“turn right”** response predicts a different action from a representation of **where the food is**.||The observed left turn is therefore informative: the rat changes its response in a way that preserves the goal location.
31|Two interpretations compete. A simple behaviorist interpretation predicts that the rats learned **“turn right to find food.”** The observed change in turning direction did not support that fixed-response interpretation.||**Tolman believed the rats had created a cognitive map of the maze and were navigating to a specific arm.** A cognitive map represents spatial relationships.||Even though turning right had previously been rewarded, the map indicated a **left turn** from the new starting point. The same represented destination can therefore guide a different action.
32|In **Verbal Behavior (1957), Skinner argued that children learn language through operant conditioning**. Children **imitate speech they hear**, and **correct speech is rewarded**.||The proposed explanation links speech production with its consequences: rewarded forms should become more likely to occur again.||The controversy concerns whether that process is sufficient to explain language acquisition, including sentences children have never heard and forms that were not rewarded.
33|**Chomsky (1959)** emphasized that **children produce sentences they have never heard** and **make novel errors that were not rewarded**. These observations challenge imitation and reinforcement as a complete explanation.||**Imitation and reinforcement alone cannot explain language acquisition.** Repeating a heard form does not by itself explain producing a new grammatical combination or a systematic new error.||The conclusion is that **language reflects internal cognitive structure, not only learned stimulus–response associations**.
34|The **1950s are recognized as the beginning of the cognitive revolution**. Psychology shifted from behaviorists’ **stimulus–response relationships** toward understanding **the operations of the mind**.||The aim became to **explain behavior in terms of the mind**. Observable responses remained the evidence, while processes and representations became explanatory targets.||Researchers could ask what happens between stimulus and response and test the proposed operations through controlled comparisons.
35|**Early digital computers in the 1950s processed information through a sequence of stages.** The **information-processing approach** used insights from computing to formulate **testable models of the mind**.||The example sequence is **input → input processor → memory unit → arithmetic unit → output**. Each stage performs a distinct operation on information.||Two questions follow: **“How much information can the mind absorb?”** and **“How does attention select some information and ignore the rest?”** A model specifies operations that make those questions testable.
36|The **information-processing approach** analyzes **mental operations as a sequence of stages**. In **Cherry’s dichotic-listening task (1953)**, different messages were presented to the **left and right ears**.||The participant **shadowed one message aloud**, repeating it as it arrived. This checked that the designated stream was being followed.||People **reported little from the unattended message**. The resulting question is **“Where does selection occur?”** The behavioral limit motivates a model of the stages through which the messages pass.
37|**Broadbent (1958)** used a **flow diagram** to represent what happens as a person directs attention to one stimulus. Sounds from **both attended and unattended messages** initially enter the system.||The **filter lets through the attended message and filters the unattended message**. Unattended information **does not pass through the filter**. The selected information continues to the **detector** and then to the recording or memory stage.||This analyzes the mind as **a sequence of processing stages**. Because the model specifies what information should get through, other researchers can test its predictions against listening-task results.
38|**John McCarthy and colleagues asked whether computers could model intelligent behavior.** The **1956 Dartmouth summer project** helped establish **artificial intelligence** as a field.||A working definition is to **make a machine behave in ways we would call intelligent in a person**. This turns intelligent behavior into something that can be implemented and examined.||Computers became both **tools and metaphors for studying cognition**: tools for running explicit models and metaphors for thinking about operations on information.
39|**Newell and Simon’s Logic Theorist applied heuristic search to proving mathematical theorems** and was demonstrated at the **1956 Dartmouth summer project**. Heuristics guide search toward promising alternatives.||It **modeled problem solving as a sequence of operations** that a machine could execute. The theory was therefore more explicit than a general description of reasoning.||It made **machine reasoning a concrete, testable research program**: operations and results could be inspected, run, and compared.
40|The cognitive-revolution timeline includes **Tolman’s cognitive map in 1948**, **Cherry’s attention experiment in 1953**, and a **commercially available digital computer in 1954**.||The **Dartmouth and MIT conferences appear in 1956**, followed by **Skinner’s Verbal Behavior in 1957**, **Broadbent’s flow diagram in 1958**, **Chomsky’s review in 1959**, and **Neisser’s cognitive psychology book in 1967**.||These developments brought internal representations, attention, computing, and explicit models into a shared research direction. The **1948 cognitive-map entry** is a different historical milestone from the **1938 maze-experiment date**.
41|**Models are selective representations used to explain, visualize, and predict.** They preserve information relevant to a question while simplifying other details.||**Structural models represent components and their organization. Process models represent operations and how information changes over time.**||The distinction concerns what is being represented: the arrangement of parts versus the operations performed. Both can be useful, but they answer different explanatory questions.
42|**Structural models are representations of a physical structure.** Some **mimic the form or appearance of a given object**, such as a brain model illustrating the locations of different brain structures.||They can also be **diagrams that do not resemble the structure but show connections**. Organization can be represented without realistic physical appearance.||The model preserves the locations of components or the connections among them, making that structural organization easier to examine.
43|**Models simplify complex systems. They are not identical replicas of the real thing.** They retain the **structural details relevant to a question**.||Simplification makes **targeted structures easier to study** by drawing attention to the organization that matters for that question.||Whether a model is useful depends on what it explains or predicts. More detail is helpful only when it preserves something needed for the investigation.
44|**Process models represent operations within cognitive mechanisms. Boxes represent processes, and arrows show information flow.** Simplifying the system makes proposed relationships and predictions explicit.||The example begins with **input → sensory memory → short-term memory**. Short-term memory exchanges information with **long-term memory**, supports an **output**, and has a **rehearsal loop labeled a control process**.||Following those routes explains what the model proposes happens to information. The arrangement can then generate **testable predictions** about processing and behavior.
45|To understand complex cognitive behaviors, **measure observable behavior, make inferences about underlying cognitive activity, and consider what that behavior says about how the mind works**.||Reaction times, accuracy, and errors provide measurable outcomes. A proposed cognitive mechanism explains why those outcomes change across conditions.||The strongest comparisons make competing explanations predict different results. The mental activity remains inferred, but the predictions allow evidence to support or challenge the account.
