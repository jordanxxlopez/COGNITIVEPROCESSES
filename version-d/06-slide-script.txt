2|**Psycholinguistics** aims to **discover the psychological processes by which humans acquire and process language**. The questions cover comprehension, production, representation, and acquisition.||**Comprehension:** how people understand **spoken and written language**. **Speech production:** how people **produce language**.||**Representation:** how language is represented **in the mind and brain**. **Acquisition:** how people **learn language**. The four terms distinguish understanding language, producing it, representing it, and learning it.
3|The two smallest units introduced are **phonemes** and **morphemes**. **Phonemes are the shortest segments of speech that, if changed, change the meaning of the word**.||The example **bit** separates the **b, vowel, and t sounds**. Replacing one of those sounds can distinguish it from another word; the phoneme itself need not have a meaning.||**Morphemes are the smallest units with meaning or grammatical function.** Sound contrasts and meaningful units therefore use different criteria, rather than being two names for printed letters.
4|**Morphemes are the smallest units of language that have meaning or grammatical function.** The examples are **table: one morpheme; bedroom: two morphemes; bedrooms: three morphemes**.||**Bed** and **room** contribute meanings. The plural ending **-s** contributes grammatical information even though it is not a standalone word.||A **phoneme** is defined by a speech-sound contrast, while a **morpheme** is defined by meaning or function. Counting letters or syllables is not the same as counting morphemes.
5|One striking feature of words is **how many we know and how rapidly we acquire them**. **Well-read adults can understand more than 50,000 different words**.||The **lexicon is our knowledge of the forms of words, which we use to make contact with meaning**. The **mental lexicon** proposes that forms are stored for reuse, an idea reaching back to the **nineteenth century**.||**Lichtheim observed neuropsychological dissociations between written and spoken word recognition.** The **spoken-word processing model** links **auditory word representations, concepts, and motor word representations**, with spoken input entering the auditory side and motor representations supporting output.
6|In the **word superiority experiment**, a **letter string flashes briefly and is then masked**. The sequence is **fixation → letter string, about 50 ms → mask → D/K choice**.||The same target letter **K** occurs in three conditions: **WORK, a word; K, a letter alone; and ORWK, a nonword**. The participant identifies which letter was present.||**K is identified most accurately when it appears in the word.** The result concerns recognizing a letter, rather than simply answering whether the entire string is a real word.
7|In **bottom-up word recognition**, **letters or phonemes are identified from features**, and **words are identified from letters or other constituents**. Activation proceeds from incoming evidence toward a word candidate.||In **top-down processing**, **activation from words feeds back to constituents**. A word-level candidate can support identification of its letters or phonemes.||These contributions interact: feature evidence supports a word, and the word can help resolve an uncertain component. The word superiority effect is consistent with this feedback contribution.
8|The **phonemic restoration effect** occurs when **phonemes are perceived in speech even though their sound is covered up**. Listeners **fill in missing phonemes based on sentence context and the portion of the word presented**.||With a masking **cough**, listeners may be unable to tell **when the cough takes place** relative to the missing segment. The experienced speech can seem complete despite the covered sound.||This is **top-down processing**. **The meaning of words that follow the missing phoneme also influences which phoneme is perceived**, so restoration uses linguistic context rather than only the immediately available sound.
9|In the **word superiority effect**, a stimulus that is **a word, letter, or nonword is flashed briefly**, then **followed by a mask**. **Two letters are presented**, and the task is to **pick the letter that was in the flashed stimulus**.||**Letters are easier to recognize when contained in a word than when alone or in a nonword.** Word context improves identification of the constituent.||Choosing alternatives that would both make real words prevents word status alone from supplying the answer. This keeps the response a test of letter identification.
10|The **interactive activation model** connects **feature, letter, and word levels**. Features activate compatible letter candidates, which in turn support compatible words.||The diagram includes word candidates such as **WEAR, WORK, WORD, and WEAK**. They compete according to the support provided by the visible features and letters.||**Word activation feeds back to constituents**, helping resolve uncertain letters. Compatible candidates support one another, while competing candidates can inhibit one another. This connects bottom-up evidence with top-down word knowledge.
11|Word-recognition research collects data from public sources to create **a large representative sample of utterances or written text, called a corpus**. A corpus indicates the frequency of **words, different meanings, and grammatical constructions**.||This is useful because much of language comprehension concerns **prediction**, and the ability to perceive written words depends partly on **how frequently they appear in the language**.||The **Speer (2022), wordfreq** estimates list occurrences per million: **the 53,703; to 26,915; and 25,704; of 25,119; a 22,909; in 18,621; I 12,303; is 11,749; for 10,233; that 10,233**. The data are pooled across several corpora.
12|**What does it mean for a word to be frequent “in the language,” and what would you count to find out?** Counts of superlatives over the years depend on which words, sources, and periods are included.||The **Google Books Ngram Viewer** samples printed books over time. The frequency describes how often the word occurs in those books.||A useful count specifies the sample and the denominator. Changes can reflect both changing usage and the material being sampled; the corpus should represent the experience relevant to the question.
13|The **word frequency effect** means **responding more rapidly to high-frequency words**. The example contrasts a faster response to **home** with a slower response to **hike**.||The estimates are **home: 646 occurrences per million; hike: 9**, using **Speer (2022), wordfreq**. In a **lexical-decision task**, participants read words and nonwords silently and respond **yes when the string is a word**. **HOME** is accepted quickly, **HIKE** more slowly, and **HIKM** receives **no**.||During reading, **low-frequency words are looked at longer**. Reaction time and fixation duration therefore provide related evidence about frequency and processing.
14|**Words learned early in life are recognized and named faster, even when frequency is matched.** This is the **age-of-acquisition, or AoA, effect**.||The early/later pairs are **doll/thesis**, **puppy/clause**, and **cookie/audit**. Their **Zipf frequencies** are approximately **4.06/4.05**, **4.00/3.99**, and **4.07/4.04**, respectively.||The Zipf scale expresses **log10 frequency per billion words**, using **Speer (2022), wordfreq**. Matching frequency helps separate age of learning from the possibility that early-learned words are simply more frequent.
15|**Lexical ambiguity means words have more than one meaning.** **“I saw her duck”** can mean seeing her bird or seeing her lower her head or body.||**Some meanings are more likely.** As **Traxler (2012)** puts it, **“Many words have multiple meanings, but these meanings are not all created equal.”**||Context helps clear up ambiguity after meanings become available. Their relative frequency also matters, so later comparisons distinguish balanced meanings from a strongly dominant one.
16|**Words can have more than one meaning**, as in **“I saw her duck.”** A context involving birds supports the animal reading; a context involving avoiding something supports the action reading.||**“These meanings are not all created equal” (Traxler, 2012):** more frequent meanings can be more accessible.||Context helps resolve ambiguity among activated alternatives. The same word form remains present while the intended interpretation changes, and the timing of selection depends on both **meaning frequency and context**.
17|**Meaning dominance is the fact that some meanings of words are used more frequently than others.** It affects how meanings are accessed.||**Biased dominance** occurs when meanings have **different dominance**: **TIN** favors **metal** over **food container**. **Balanced dominance** occurs when meanings have **about the same dominance**: **CAST** can mean **a member of a play** or **a plaster cast**.||The bars represent how often meanings are used, with **illustrative proportions**. The bars contrast a strongly favored meaning with alternatives used at more similar rates.
18|The **dependent variable is fixation duration**. In **“the cast worked…”**, **both meanings of cast are activated**, and readers **look longer at cast than at the control word cook**.||In **“the tin was…”**, the **dominant meaning of tin** is favored. Readers can read this **biased ambiguous word as fast as a control word**.||The comparison links meaning competition with processing time. More competition between meanings is associated with longer viewing in the balanced example.
19|**Meaning frequency is not the only factor determining accessibility; context also plays a major role.** In **“The miners went to the store and saw that they had beans in a tin,”** the context indicates the **less frequent container meaning**.||**Both meanings become activated**, and processing takes longer than the control **cup**. Context supports the subordinate meaning while the dominant metal meaning can remain available.||When context supports only the dominant meaning, competition is reduced. Accessibility therefore depends on the combination of **dominance and context**.
20|**Components of language are not processed in isolation.** To explain sentence meaning, distinguish **semantics: meanings of words and sentences**, from **syntax: rules for combining words into sentences**.||**“The cats won’t eat”** is acceptable. Changing it to **“The cats won’t bake”** creates a **semantic anomaly** in the intended situation, while changing it to **“The cats won’t eating”** creates a **syntax error** in verb form.||The first comparison changes plausibility of meaning while preserving grammatical structure. The second disrupts the grammatical combination itself.
21|Patient evidence distinguishes functions associated with **Broca’s area in the frontal lobe** and **Wernicke’s area in the temporal lobe**.||**Broca’s aphasia** includes **slow, labored, ungrammatical speech** and problems understanding some sentence structures. **“The apple was eaten by the girl”** can be understood with plausible roles; **“The boy was pushed by the girl”** requires syntax to establish who acted on whom.||**Wernicke’s aphasia** includes **meaningless speech** and difficulty **understanding speech and writing**, including both example sentences. The contrast relates different patterns of impairment to syntax and semantics.
22|**ERP studies show that syntax and semantics are associated with different mechanisms.** An **event-related potential** is a **rapid response on the timescale of a fraction of a second**, with waves at different delays after a stimulus.||Two components are **N400 and P600**. The schematic comparison shows an **acceptable sentence**, a **semantic violation associated with N400**, and a **syntactic violation associated with P600**.||The horizontal axis is **time after the critical word in milliseconds**, and the vertical axis is **microvolts, negative plotted upward**. N400 is around **400 ms** and P600 around **600 ms**. The curves are labeled **schematic, not real data**.
23|The **P600 response is associated with structures in the frontal lobe**, and frontal damage can reduce it in the comparison described. Its example contrasts **“The spoiled child throws the toy on the floor”** with **“The spoiled child throw the toy on the floor”**: the second creates a grammatical agreement problem.||The **N400 response is associated with structures in the temporal lobe**, and temporal damage can reduce it. Its example contrasts **“Whipped cream tastes sweet”** with **“Whipped cream tastes anxious”**: the second creates a semantic anomaly.||The graphs show **microvolt amplitude over milliseconds**. The different wave timings connect semantic and syntactic processing with distinguishable electrical responses.
24|**Parsing is the mental grouping of words in a sentence into phrases**, a central process for determining sentence meaning.||**Garden-path sentences begin by appearing to mean one thing, then end up requiring another interpretation.** After **“After the musician played the piano…”**, **“she left the stage”** or **“she bowed to the audience”** fits the first parse.||**“…was wheeled off the stage”** instead requires **“After the musician played, the piano was wheeled off the stage.”** The piano becomes the subject of the later clause.||This is **temporary ambiguity**: the initial words are ambiguous, but **the meaning is made clear by the end of the sentence**. The later input requires reparsing.
25|**“The horse raced past the barn fell.”** Initially, raced can be interpreted as the sentence’s main action, making fell difficult to attach.||The complete reading is **“The horse [that was raced past the barn] fell.”** The reduced relative clause describes the horse; **fell** is the main verb.||The sentence is a **garden path** because the ending requires revision of the initial main-verb interpretation.
26|**“The horse raced past the barn fell”** and **“The burglar discovered behind the bushes fled”** both create garden paths through an initially plausible main-verb reading.||For the burglar sentence, the complete interpretation is **“The burglar [who was discovered behind the bushes] fled.”** Discovered belongs to the reduced relative description, while **fled** supplies the main action.||Later words make the initial grouping fail and identify the structure needed for the complete sentence.
27|**Sentences with temporary ambiguity help reveal the mechanisms operating during parsing.** In the **syntax-first approach**, words are initially grouped into phrases by **rules based on syntax**.||If readers realize something is wrong with the parse, they **take other information into account to reinterpret the sentence**. **Late closure** means the parser assumes the **new word is part of the current phrase**, adding words there for as long as possible.||The sequence is **read the next word → attach it by syntax rules → check whether the parse works**. If it works, continue; if not, **reanalyze using other information**.
28|The piano sentence initially permits **“[After the musician played the piano]…”**. Piano is attached to played as its object.||Continuing with **was** and **wheeled** makes that grouping fail: **“[After the musician played the piano] [was]”** cannot support the intended complete interpretation.||The repair changes the boundary to **“[After the musician played] [the piano was wheeled off the stage].”** The same words are regrouped so piano becomes the subject of the main clause.
