2|**“You have never once been in contact with the world—only with your brain’s reconstruction of it.”** The perceptual experience is the result of the brain’s processing of sensory information.||**“So why is the reconstruction almost always right?”** The explanation connects that processing with regularities in the world. Sensory evidence and knowledge of those regularities help the brain select a useful interpretation even when the input is ambiguous.
3|**“What do you see?”** The black-and-white pattern by **R. C. James** contains a **Dalmatian dog**. At first, its patches may not appear to form a recognizable animal.||Once the dog is recognized, the patches are organized as parts of the dog and its surroundings. The retinal pattern remains the same, but knowing what to look for changes the experienced organization.
4|**Perception is the experience every other cognitive process is built on top of.** It provides information that attention, memory, language, and reasoning can use.||What is attended, remembered, described, or reasoned about therefore depends partly on how sensory information has been perceived. Perception provides an interpreted experience for those other processes to use.
5|**Perception is experience resulting from stimulation of the senses.** It is **the gateway to every other cognition**: attention, memory, language, and reasoning all take perception as input.||Four properties matter. **Perceptions change when information is added. Perception involves a process much like reasoning or problem solving. Perceptions occur in conjunction with actions. Prior knowledge shapes how we resolve ambiguous stimuli—usually correctly, sometimes not.**||The same retinal image can be experienced differently before and after knowing what to look for. **Perception depends jointly on the incoming sensory signal and on the knowledge the perceiver brings to it.**
6|**Perception is fast and effortless, so it feels automatic**, but the processing supporting that experience is largely invisible to us. Roughly **half the primate cortex is devoted to vision alone**, and some human perceptual abilities may have no equivalent elsewhere.||**Machine perception has repeatedly fallen short.** Each generation of systems solves a previous failure and inherits a new one. Formal reasoning—**chess, proofs, and algebra**—became manageable for machines decades before reliable object recognition.||The **DARPA Urban Challenge (2007)** demonstrates the difficulty of robot vehicles interpreting a changing environment. If perception were simple, it would have been the easy part of artificial intelligence; instead it has been a persistent challenge.
7|In **2007**, machine vision relied on **hand-written rules and sensor fusion**. Vehicles finished a **60-mile urban course**, slowly and on a **closed course**.||**Now**, deep networks trained on **millions of labeled images** match or exceed human accuracy on benchmark object recognition. **Still**, four problems remain: **novel viewpoints, occlusion, unusual lighting, and scenes whose context is odd**.||The gap is in **interpretation under ambiguity**, not just sensing: cameras can out-resolve the eye. A system can label a thousand stop signs and still miss one with stickers on it. Humans often adjust to a strange scene, while a trained network can fail confidently.
8|**Why is it so hard to build a perceiving machine?** The visual system solves problems involving **inverse projection, hidden or blurred objects, and different viewpoints** before we notice there was a problem.||The sensory image does not uniquely identify the object that produced it. A successful recognition system has to handle uncertainty about the source, missing information, and changes in the image while preserving object identity.
9|The **inverse projection problem** is **the task of determining which object produced a particular image on the retina**. We start with the retinal image and work outward to its source.||At a given viewing arrangement, an object produces a projection, but **one image could have come from infinitely many objects**. **A small object nearby and a huge object far away can produce identical retinal images**; different sources along a projection line can fit the same evidence.||Perception is therefore **an inference about what most likely caused the image**, rather than a unique source being read directly from the image.
10|**Most objects in a normal scene are partly occluded by other objects**, yet people identify them from fragments. We also recognize objects that are **blurred, poorly lit, or seen at the edge of vision**.||The visual system treats **a partial view as a partial view**, rather than as a new shape. It must determine which edges belong to the same object even though that ownership is not explicitly labeled in the image.||**Perceptual completion** means the visual system **extends contours behind an occluder**. A wheelbarrow behind a wall is still experienced as **one wheelbarrow**, not as unrelated visible fragments.
11|**Viewpoint invariance is the ability to recognize an object as the same object across changes in viewing angle.** Every rotation produces a physically different retinal image.||We have never seen most objects from most angles, yet recognize them anyway. This is difficult for machine vision but occurs readily in young children. **One object can have many projections while remaining “the same.”**||Like inverse projection and occlusion, the problem is ambiguity. Perception uses constraints beyond an exact match to the current image to maintain a stable identity.
12|There are **two directions of processing**: information flows **up from the receptors** and **down from what you already know**. These are bottom-up and top-down contributions.||The incoming signal supplies sensory evidence, while knowledge, expectations, and attention influence how that evidence is interpreted. Both can contribute to the same perception, rather than defining two completely separate kinds of experience.
13|**Bottom-up processing starts at the “bottom” of the system, where environmental energy stimulates the receptors.** It is also called **data-driven processing**.||The sequence is **light reflected from Mary → image on the retina → signals in the optic nerve → signals in the brain**. More generally, **stimulus → receptors → neural signal → representation**.||This chain begins with incoming evidence and does not require already knowing Mary. Externally stimulated sensory experience begins with that evidence; an imagined experience need not begin with a present external object.
14|**Top-down processing originates in the brain, at the “top” of the perceptual system.** It is also called **knowledge-driven processing**.||The perceiver brings **knowledge—what you already know about Mary; expectation—what you predict you are about to see; and attention—which features you sample**. These influence the interpretation of incoming evidence.||**Bottom-up and top-down are two contributions to perception.** The useful questions are **how much each is contributing and when**, rather than assigning the whole experience exclusively to one direction.
15|**Direct perception theories** emphasize that **perception comes from the stimuli available in the environment**. Parts can be identified and assembled before recognition; the information in light can be rich enough to specify the world. **Gibson** emphasizes an **optic array** available to a moving observer that specifies layout.||**Constructive perception theories** emphasize that people **actively construct perceptions using expectations**. Knowledge, experience, and context do part of the work: **the percept is built, not received**. Illusions show that the percept can depart from the stimulus.||The combined position is that **perception is constructive, but constrained by information the environment reliably provides**.
16|**Irving Biederman’s recognition-by-components theory (1987)** proposes that we perceive objects by first perceiving **elementary volumetric features**. These building blocks, called **geons**, combine into recognizable objects.||The stages move from identifying features and components to recognizing their arrangement as an object. The same kinds of parts can form different objects when arranged differently.||Approximately **36 geons** are proposed, and **three geons in the right arrangement identify most common objects**. Recognizing components can therefore support a **partial or novel view** without requiring an exact match to one full image.
17|An **image region lifted out of a photograph and shown on its own** may be difficult to identify. The isolated patch supplies only its local shape, shading, and texture.||Several objects can fit that limited information. Restoring the surrounding scene supplies context that narrows the candidates without replacing the local pattern itself.
18|In **Torralba and Oliva’s** demonstration, **the circled blobs are pixel-for-pixel identical**, yet they are perceived as **a shoe, a car, and a pedestrian**.||**Orientation and context did the identifying, not the shape.** The same region receives a different identity according to the scene around it.||**Knowledge affects perception** because scene information makes different objects plausible in different positions and settings. Recognition cannot be explained by the isolated blob’s features alone.
19|**Speech segmentation is the ability to tell where one word ends and the next begins.** Speech has almost no physical gaps that consistently mark word boundaries. The apparent spaces between words depend on interpreting the signal.||In **“I hope it’s in my desk,”** the physical sound does not insert a clean gap at each printed word boundary. An unfamiliar language can therefore sound like **an unbroken stream**.||**Understanding which word you heard from context is top-down processing.** Language knowledge helps organize continuous acoustic input into words.
20|In **spectral rotation**, the same sentence is played **once rotated in frequency and once intact**. In the **rotated version on the left**, only the longest pauses stand out; in the **intact version on the right**, words feel temporally separate.||**The temporal envelope of the sound is the same in both**. The frequency structure changes, but new pauses have not been inserted between words.||The comparison shows a contribution from **linguistic knowledge** to segmentation. When the listener cannot apply that knowledge effectively, the temporal pattern alone does not produce the same experience of separate words.
21|The **direct pathway model** is an early account in which **nociceptors in the skin send pain messages straight to the brain**. A purely bottom-up account predicts that **pain intensity should track tissue damage**.||**Expectation, attention, and distraction all change how much pain is experienced.** Identical stimulation can produce different pain depending on what the person believes is happening.||In **Atlas et al. (2012, Journal of Neuroscience)**, when participants could see pain medication being delivered, **activity increased in frontal regions and decreased in areas associated with nociception**. The experience therefore includes cognitive influences as well as incoming stimulation.
22|**Perception as inference** addresses ambiguity: **if the image is ambiguous, the percept has to be a bet** about what caused it. More than one source can fit the available evidence.||**Helmholtz’s unconscious inference** and **Bayesian inference** describe how that selection can use experience and current input. The system favors an interpretation rather than deriving a uniquely specified object from the retinal image alone.
23|**Helmholtz’s theory of unconscious inference, around 1860**, is a top-down account: **some perceptions result from unconscious assumptions we make about the environment**. We use what we know to inform what we see.||The **likelihood principle** says **we perceive the world in the way that is most likely, given our past experience**. This addresses the inverse projection problem, where many objects can produce the same image.||The inference is **unconscious and involuntary**: we cannot inspect it or decline it. **“Unconscious” means inaccessible, not Freudian**; knowing about an illusion does not necessarily switch off its appearance.
24|For retinal image **(a)**, source **(b)** is **two overlapping rectangles**. Source **(c)** is **a rectangle and an oddly notched shape, aligned exactly right**. Both are geometrically consistent with the image.||Almost everyone chooses **(b)**. The notched alternative requires a precise coincidence, and **coincidences are rare in our experience**.||The judgment favors a likely cause among possible causes. Compatibility with the image alone cannot decide between the two arrangements.
25|**Bayesian inference makes the likelihood principle quantitative.** A **prior probability** is what you believed before the stimulus arrived, built from exposure to how the world usually is—for example, **light usually comes from above**.||The **likelihood** is how well each candidate interpretation would explain the stimulus actually received. **Both a bump and a dent can fit the shading**.||The **posterior** combines prior and likelihood to give an updated interpretation. Formally, the posterior is **proportional to prior × likelihood**, with normalization. A strong prior can favor one reading of an ambiguous image, and different environmental experience can change those priors.
26|**The stimulus** is a disc **light at the top and dark at the bottom**. **The likelihood** is ambiguous: **a bump lit from above and a dent lit from below produce the identical image**. Neither explanation is uniquely selected by that evidence.||**The prior** favors light coming from above, a common pattern in the environment. **The posterior** therefore favors seeing **a bump**, immediately and without a conscious choice.||**Turn the picture upside down**: bumps can become dents and dents bumps. Rotation changes which interpretation fits the **light-from-above prior**, even though the depicted object’s physical shape has not changed.
27|**Perceptual organization** is the process by which the visual field is **carved into objects before they are identified**. Regions and contours must be grouped into candidate objects.||The image does not explicitly label which features belong together. **Gestalt laws of perceptual organization** describe systematic ways the mind groups the field into coherent units.
28|In **Wundt’s structuralism**, **overall experience is built by combining basic elements called sensations**. Perception involves **adding up sensations**.||Movement would be constructed by **summing snapshots of an object in different locations over time**, and a face would be the sum of the dots composing it. **Muybridge’s sequence of stills** makes the motion-as-snapshots idea concrete.||The claim is that perception can be analyzed into parts and the whole is nothing more than those parts added together. Gestalt psychologists challenged whether that addition explains the organized experience.
29|The **Gestalt psychologists, around 1912**, rejected the idea that **perceptions are formed by adding up sensations**. **The mind groups patterns according to laws of perceptual organization**.||In **apparent movement**, movement is perceived even though no object physically moves through the experienced path. In the ring demonstration, the changing gap appears to move around the ring.||**The whole is different from the sum of its parts—not merely greater, different.** The experienced organization includes a property that cannot be explained as literal motion of an object between positions.
30|**Good continuation** means that **lines tend to be seen as following the smoothest path**. Points connecting into **straight or smoothly curving lines** are seen as belonging together.||A contour disappearing behind an object is experienced as **continuing behind it**. In the rope display, perception follows a continuous route through crossings rather than switching arbitrarily between segments.||Following **one cable in a tangle** or **one line on a subway map** uses the same organizing principle.
31|**Prägnanz**, also called **simplicity or good figure**, means that a stimulus pattern is organized so the resulting structure is **as simple as possible**.||The **Olympic symbol** is seen as **five overlapping circles**, rather than **nine irregular closed shapes fitted together**, even though that alternative is also consistent with the lines.||Both Gestalt and Helmholtz select among interpretations that fit: **Gestalt picks the simplest; Helmholtz picks the most likely**. In ordinary scenes those choices often agree.
32|**Similarity** means **similar things appear grouped together**. In panel **(a)**, all the dots are red; in **(b)**, varying the color makes the display reorganize into columns. The geometry stays the same while the coloring differs.||Grouping can depend on **color, shape, size, orientation, and lightness**. A feature relationship can change which elements are perceived as a unit without changing their positions.||**Grouping happens before recognition: organization first, identification second.** The field is already organized into candidate objects by the time those objects are identified.
33|In the beach scene, **good continuation** makes **the horizon and waterline appear as continuous lines behind the figure**. **Similarity** groups **sand with sand, water with water, and sky with sky**.||**Prägnanz** favors **three simple horizontal bands**, rather than a mosaic of colored patches. **Figure–ground** separates **one person against a background**.||**Common region and proximity** are also present. Several grouping principles can operate together in the same scene; there are more than four Gestalt laws.
34|The **Gestalt position** treats the laws as **intrinsic—built into the visual system**. The role of experience is minor, so the laws should be **universal across cultures and present very early in life**.||An experience-based challenge is that **smooth contours and similar-looking surfaces really do tend to belong to the same object**. The principles may reflect **statistical regularities the system has absorbed**.||Experience can matter without overriding every principle. **Gestalt laws are accurate because the environment is regular**, connecting perceptual organization with knowledge of the world.
35|**The world is not random. Perception exploits that.** Recurring arrangements and properties provide regularities that make some interpretations more probable than others.||These include **physical regularities**, such as common orientations and lighting, and **semantic regularities**, such as objects typical of a kitchen. Perception can use them without a conscious decision on each occasion.
36|Perception is shaped by **knowledge of regularities in the environment**, connecting modern accounts with Helmholtz’s unconscious inference.||**Physical regularities are regularly occurring physical properties**: **verticals and horizontals are more common than obliques; light comes from above; surfaces are mostly uniformly colored; objects tend to be opaque and rigid**.||**Semantic regularities are characteristics associated with the functions carried out in different types of scenes**. **Kitchens contain stoves, kettles, and sinks; offices contain screens and chairs; streets contain cars, signs, and pedestrians**.||Physical regularities concern **how the world is put together**. Semantic regularities concern **what the world is used for** and which objects are probable in that setting.
37|The **oblique effect** means **people perceive verticals and horizontals more easily than other orientations**: better acuity, faster detection, and more accurate orientation judgments.||The environment is full of verticals and horizontals, including **forests and rock formations as well as buildings**. This is not merely an artifact of modern cities.||**More cortical neurons are tuned to vertical and horizontal than to oblique orientations.** An environmental regularity is associated with both a behavioral advantage and a neural-tuning bias.
38|The **light-from-above assumption** is that **light comes from above**, as it usually does in the environment. **Shading is therefore read as depth**.||**Light on top, shadow below → a bump protruding toward you. Shadow on top, light below → an indentation.** These readings depend on the assumed illumination.||Panels **(a) and (b)** are **the same image, one rotated 180 degrees**. Rotation changes which depth reading the prior favors, producing opposite percepts from the same photographed pattern.
39|The sand sculpture permits two readings: **shapes raised out of the sand or carved into it**. The light and shadow support an interpretation of the surface’s depth.||Under the **light-from-above assumption**, one arrangement of raised and recessed shapes becomes favored. The rotated photograph tests whether changing the shading’s orientation changes that experience.
40|**One image, one rotation, two incompatible percepts.** The sand did not change; **the photograph was turned and nothing else**.||What changed is **which interpretation is consistent with light arriving from above**. Raised-looking shapes can become carved-looking shapes, and the reverse can occur too.||The reading is not freely chosen simply because both are understood. An ordinarily unnoticed regularity influences the experienced depth even when the observer knows why the reversal occurs.
41|A **scene schema** is **knowledge of what a given kind of scene typically contains and where those things typically are**. It is built through **repeated exposure to scenes of that type**.||Imagining a kitchen commonly brings up **a stove, a sink, and cabinets**, even when those objects were not specified. The schema supplies expectations in imagined and actual kitchens.||We approach a scene expecting **a short list of likely objects in likely places**. An unfamiliar room’s **door handle** is sought in plausible positions rather than on the ceiling.||**Physical regularities constrain what shapes and surfaces are possible; semantic regularities constrain what objects are probable, given where you are.**
42|**Palmer (1975): Participants saw a scene, then a briefly flashed object.** Objects **congruent with the scene—a loaf of bread in a kitchen—were identified far more accurately than incongruent ones, such as a mailbox in the same kitchen**. Scene context changes recognition accuracy.||**Potter (1976): Pictures were presented in rapid succession, around an eighth of a second each.** Observers could still say whether a described target scene had appeared. **The gist of a scene arrives faster than its details.**||**Torralba and Oliva: Identical image regions read as a shoe, a car, and a pedestrian according to the scene around them.** The same local patch receives different interpretations with different surrounding context.||**The scene supplied a short list of probable objects. The blob was matched against that list**, rather than every object the observer knows. Context helps constrain which identity fits the available region.
43|**“Perceptions occur in conjunction with actions.”** Perception guides what we do, and what we do changes the information available to perception.||Moving around an object, reaching for it, or changing the viewing angle supplies evidence unavailable in one stationary image. **We perceive in order to act, and we act in order to perceive better.**
44|**Perception is not a still photograph delivered to a stationary observer.** We **move around objects, pick them up, turn them over, and change our vantage point**.||Movement helps resolve ambiguity: near and far objects move differently across the visual field; moving around an occluder reveals a hidden part; and **a sequence of viewpoints specifies shape that no single viewpoint does**.||In **motion parallax**, **near objects sweep past while distant ones drift slowly**, as when looking from a moving car. The relative speeds provide depth information.||**Perceiving and acting are continuous with each other.** This makes movement-generated information important to direct-perception accounts as well as to ordinary recognition.
45|The **ventral “what” pathway** runs **occipital lobe → temporal lobe** and supports **identifying objects**. Damage can impair recognition while leaving visually guided reaching relatively intact. It is also called the **perception pathway**.||The **dorsal “where/how” pathway** runs **occipital lobe → parietal lobe** and supports **locating objects and guiding action toward them**. Damage can impair reaching and grasping while recognition survives. It is also called the **action pathway**.||**Ungerleider and Mishkin (1982)** established the division through **lesion work in monkeys**. **Milner and Goodale** argued that **“how” fits better than “where.”** The streams interact: the division is one of emphasis, not complete independence.
46|**Patient D.F. had ventral damage following carbon monoxide poisoning.** Asked to **report a slot’s orientation or match it with a held card**, she could not do it accurately. Asked to **post the card through the same slot**, she rotated her hand accurately and posted it.||**Mirror neurons are premotor neurons that fire both when an action is performed and when the same action is observed.** Perceiving an action and producing it can therefore draw on shared machinery.||D.F. demonstrates that **knowing what something is and being able to act on it are separable abilities**. Orientation information supported her action even when she could not use it for an explicit judgment.
47|**The image underdetermines the world.** Inverse projection, occlusion, and viewpoint mean that a retinal image can fit many possible scenes. **Perception resolves the ambiguity** using knowledge, expectation, attention, and context.||**Both directions contribute:** bottom-up and top-down processing are contributions to perception. **Perception is inference:** Helmholtz’s likelihood principle and Bayesian inference combine prior beliefs with current evidence.||**Organization precedes recognition:** Gestalt grouping organizes the field into candidate objects. **The priors come from the world:** physical and semantic regularities shape assumptions that are usually useful because the environment itself is regular.
48|Helmholtz’s theory of unconscious inference and the Gestalt laws of organization are **not fundamentally incompatible**. Both concern **how one interpretation gets selected from many that fit the image**.||**Helmholtz selects the most likely; the Gestaltists select the simplest.** In the great majority of ordinary scenes, the simplest interpretation is also the most likely, so the accounts can agree.||They differ in emphasis—**learned probabilities versus intrinsic organizing principles**—rather than necessarily solving different problems or predicting opposite percepts.
49|**“You have never once been in contact with the world—only with your brain’s reconstruction of it. So why is the reconstruction almost always right?”**||**The reconstruction is right because the world is regular, and the assumptions were built from it.** Perception uses sensory evidence together with expectations suited to recurring patterns in the environment.||Those assumptions usually support an accurate interpretation. When a display violates them, the same ordinarily useful processes can produce a systematic illusion.
