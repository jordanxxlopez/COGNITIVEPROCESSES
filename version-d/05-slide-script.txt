2|Cognitive psychology **“in the wild”** studies behavior with **no experimenter present**. The naturalistic driving work collected data in **privately owned vehicles** using **unobtrusive instrumentation**.||Equipment included **hidden radar**, a **face camera behind smoked glass**, and other cameras approximately **one inch by one inch**.||The design is **quasi-experimental**: observe naturally occurring crashes and examine **what was happening before the crash**. The recordings make the period before the event available for analysis.
3|The **100-car study** collected data in a **naturalistic setting** to obtain **crash, pre-crash, near-crash, and conflict data**, together with **distributions of driver performance**.||**One hundred drivers** used their **own or leased cars with specialized instrumentation on public roads**, as close to unobserved as possible. They drove for an extended period, **up to 13 months**, without an experimenter present.||Drivers were **not coached or instructed to perform specific actions other than driving normally**. Instrumentation was **unobtrusive and inconspicuous to other drivers, but not invisible**. Normal-driving periods provide a comparison with hazardous events.
4|The naturalistic **100-car database** contained **42,300 hours of driving data**, approximately **two million miles**, **82 crashes and collisions**, **761 near crashes**, **8,295 incidents**, and **20,000 matched periods of normal baseline driving**.||A **crash or collision** means contact with another **vehicle, fixed object, pedestrian, pedalcyclist, or animal**. A **near crash** is a conflict requiring a **rapid, severe evasive maneuver to avoid a crash**.||An **incident** requires an evasive maneuver of **lesser magnitude than a near crash**. These categories separate event severity rather than treating every conflict as the same outcome.
5|In the **100-car results**, **inattention to the forward roadway** was identified as a primary contributing factor in many crashes and collisions. **80% of crashes and 65% of near crashes involved at least one form of inattention within three seconds before conflict onset**.||**93% of lead-vehicle conflict crashes and minor collisions involved looking away**. In **86% of those events**, headway at onset was **greater than 2.0 seconds**.||The lead-vehicle results include drivers who were looking away even when headway at onset was greater than two seconds. Headway is the time gap between vehicles.
6|**Secondary tasks that are moderately to very complex, and driver drowsiness, carry the highest associated crash risk** in the distraction analysis.||The graphs compare **crash, near-crash, incident, and baseline** periods using **time eyes off the forward roadway in seconds**, **number of glances away**, and **mean length of the longest glance away**.||Crash periods show greater off-road viewing than baseline. Total time, glance count, and longest glance are distinct measures: one prolonged glance and several short glances do not describe the same behavior.
7|The naturalistic-driving material describes **video recorders placed in cars** and reports that **accident risk is about four times higher during cell-phone use**. This is an association from driving evidence.||**Strayer and Johnston (2001)** used a **simulated driving task**. Participants on a cell phone **missed twice as many red lights and took longer to apply the brakes**. The **same pattern occurred with a hands-free cell phone**.||The result concerns **divided attention while perceiving and interacting with the environment**. Removing manual phone holding does not remove the cognitive demands of conversation.
8|**Cartwright-Finch and Lavie (2007)** presented a rapidly flashed **cross stimulus five times**. Participants indicated **which arm was longer**, with one arm slightly longer than the other.||**On the sixth trial, a square was added. Only 10% of participants saw it.** Attention remained engaged with the cross judgment.||**Inattentional blindness** is failure to perceive an unattended stimulus even when a person might be looking directly toward it. Attention remains focused on the cross judgment while the square is missed.
9|**Change blindness** occurs when **differences between two versions of a picture are not immediately apparent**. The **task of identifying differences requires concentrated attention and search**.||The observer must register relevant information and compare it across views. Simply receiving both pictures is not sufficient.||This differs from inattentional blindness: change blindness concerns **a difference across versions**, while inattentional blindness concerns **an unexpected stimulus missed while attention is elsewhere**.
10|In **Levin and Simons (1997)**, **change detection** is tested during a visual event. Viewers can fail to notice a change while continuing to understand the overall event.||Detecting the change requires attention to the relevant feature and comparison with its earlier state. A viewer can follow the event’s overall meaning while missing a change in a particular person or object.||The failure is in noticing a difference across time, even when the changed item becomes easy to see once identified.
11|**Binding is the process by which features such as color, form, motion, and location are combined to create perception of a coherent object**.||The visual system must establish which properties belong together. Detecting redness, roundness, and movement separately does not yet identify one red moving ball.||This connects with **Gestalt and direct-perception views**: an explanation has to account for how the field becomes organized and how the correct features are assigned to a particular object.
12|**How an object’s individual features become bound together—the binding problem—is addressed by Treisman’s feature integration theory, or FIT.**||The sequence is **object → preattentive stage → focused-attention stage → perception**. The first stage **analyzes the object into features**; the next **combines those features**.||The distinction predicts that features can be detected without being paired correctly. Limited attention can therefore produce a specific kind of error rather than simply erase all information about the object.
13|During FIT’s **preattentive stage**, the **object is analyzed into features**. The operation is described as **automatic**, involving **no deliberate effort or attention**, with the observer **unaware of the process**.||Separate feature registration precedes their combination. A color and a shape can both be available without the correct color–shape pairing being secure.||The focused-attention stage explains how those components are bound into a coherent object representation.
14|During the **focused-attention stage**, **attention plays a key role and features are combined**. This follows the preattentive analysis of an object into components.||Accurate perception requires assigning those properties to the correct object. Knowing which colors and shapes appeared is not the same as knowing which color belonged to each shape.||The distinction explains how reduced attention can leave feature information available while making the conjunction less reliable.
15|In the **preattentive stage**, objects are analyzed into **separate features**. A **rolling red ball** includes **red color, round shape, and movement to the right**. Different mechanisms process these features, which are initially represented separately.||In **Treisman and Schmidt (1982)**, participants reported **combinations of features from different stimuli**. These are **illusory conjunctions**.||The description **“free-floating” features** means that features are available without secure assignment to their objects. An error can recombine displayed properties rather than invent a feature that never occurred.
16|The display contains a **yellow vertical rectangle, a gray horizontal rectangle, and a blue oval**. Each has separable **color, form, and orientation** properties.||Correct binding attaches yellow to the vertical rectangle, gray to the horizontal rectangle, and blue to the oval. Focused attention combines the features in those object-specific pairings.||An incorrect combination using properties from different objects is a **binding error**, even though the component features were genuinely present.
17|The stimulus contains black digits **1 and 8**, together with **a small red triangle, a large outlined blue triangle, a yellow circle, and a green outlined circle**.||Reporting the **black digits first** directs attention away from the colored objects. Features can remain available without receiving enough focused attention for correct pairing.||The design places digit reporting in competition with binding the shapes’ **color, size, and form**. A conjunction error can therefore reflect divided processing rather than absence of the features.
18|The rectangle-and-oval pattern separates **feature registration** from **object identity**. The features include **yellow, gray, blue, vertical, horizontal, and oval**, but accurate perception requires their original combinations.||The correct pairings are **yellow vertical rectangle, gray horizontal rectangle, and blue oval**. Combining existing properties differently would produce an illusory conjunction.||FIT therefore distinguishes detecting the individual features from binding them to their correct object and location.
19|In **Treisman and Schmidt (1982)**, **the image was flashed for one fifth of a second**, or **200 milliseconds**, followed by a **random-dot mask**.||Participants were asked to **report the black numbers first**, then **report what they saw at each of the four locations where the shapes had been**.||Brief exposure, masking, and the competing digit task limit focused processing of the objects. Their later reports test whether available features were bound correctly.
20|In **18% of trials**, participants reported **objects made up of a combination of features from two different stimuli**, such as **a small red circle or a small green triangle**.||Those colors and shapes were present, but not in the reported pairing. This is an **illusory conjunction**, rather than necessarily an invented color or form.||The result supports a distinction between registering features and correctly binding them when focused attention is limited.
21|FIT predicts **illusory conjunctions**. In a brief display where participants **reported digits first and then letters and their colors**, they **mixed up letters and colors on 39% of trials**, while reporting **a letter or color absent from the display on 15%**.||The first is a **binding error**; the second is a **feature-report error**. This is a different display from the 18% colored-shape example.||FIT also predicts **object-based attention**. Illusory conjunctions can occur even when stimuli **differ greatly in shape and size**, because features have not yet been bound together through focused attention.
22|In the **focused-attention stage**, **“free-floating” features are combined**. Attention plays a key role in making their correct object assignment available.||Attending to an object supports combining its color, form, and location rather than merely knowing that those features occurred somewhere.||Insufficient binding can combine features from different objects into the reported conjunction.
23|**R.M., a patient with Balint’s syndrome**, had an **inability to focus attention on individual objects** and reported a **high number of illusory conjunctions**.||According to **FIT**, lack of focused attention makes it difficult to **combine features correctly**. The problem is consistent with impaired assignment of available features to objects.||The patient evidence connects an attentional impairment with the type of binding error predicted by the theory.
24|FIT emphasizes **mostly bottom-up processing**, but **top-down processing contributes when participants are told what they will see**, such as **“You are being shown a carrot, a lake, and a tire.”**||**Top-down processing combines with feature analysis to help perception become accurate.** Knowledge constrains likely combinations of properties.||In **Treisman and Schmidt (1982)**, when participants **ignored the black numbers and focused on the objects**, they could **correctly pair shapes and colors**. Changing attention also improves binding without changing the objects themselves.
25|**Attention enhances neural responding**, and **attentional processing is distributed across a large number of brain areas**.||A **topographic map is a spatial map of visual stimuli on the visual cortex**. Each stimulus point produces activity at a specific cortical location; **neighboring stimulus points cause activity at neighboring cortical points**.||The map describes spatial organization. Attention can enhance activity within that organization, so the location represented and the strength of its response are distinct aspects of the evidence.
26|A **topographic map** preserves orderly relationships between positions in the visual field and positions in visual cortex. **Nearby visual positions remain nearby within the cortical representation**.||The **left and right visual fields project mainly to opposite hemispheres**, while positions within the mapped areas retain systematic spatial relationships.||This organization allows researchers to relate an attended location to changes in the corresponding neural representation.
27|During a **search task**, **fMRI detects cortical activity**. **Attention to an expected direction of motion causes activity to increase in a number of brain areas**.||The effect is **distributed across the cortex**, rather than confined to one general-purpose attention point. Multiple operations can contribute to selecting and processing the expected feature.||The activity pattern is interpreted in relation to what the participant is attending to and the task being performed.
28|Visual patterns at **A, B, and C on the disk** produce spatially organized activity. **When this activity is transmitted to higher visual areas, the observer perceives the patterns at locations A, B, and C**.||The correspondence between visual position and neural representation provides a basis for testing location-specific attention.||If the observer changes which location is selected, researchers can ask whether activity increases in the representation corresponding to that position.
29|**Datta and DeYoe (2009)** used **fMRI** to ask what happens when an observer **pays attention to different locations on a disk**.||They found **increased activity in the areas corresponding to the specific locations where participants were paying attention**. The selected position changes while the display can remain similar.||The comparison links attention with the strength of spatially specific neural responding, rather than merely the presence of a different stimulus.
30|**Datta and DeYoe created attention maps** and **trained classifiers to predict where someone was paying attention** from measured activity patterns.||**After training classifiers, they predicted where someone was paying attention with 100% accuracy in the task.**||An attention map links the selected visual location with a pattern of measured brain activity. The classifier uses that learned pattern to predict the attended location.
31|In **O’Craven et al. (1999)**, **paying attention to houses increased activity in the parahippocampal gyrus**, while **focusing on faces increased activity in the FFA, the fusiform face area**.||The stimuli combine an **overlapping face and house**. Both can be present while the attended object changes.||Attention therefore changes category-selective responding beyond the mere physical presence of a face or house in the display.
32|**William James (1890)** describes attention as **“the taking possession by the mind, in clear and vivid form, of one out of what seem several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought.”**||**“Focalization, concentration of consciousness are of its essence.”** Attention can prioritize either an external object or a train of thought.||**“It implies withdrawal from some things in order to deal effectively with others.”** Selection helps explain effective processing of the attended information together with limits on competing information.
