2|The **Gestalt laws of perceptual organization** include the **law of good continuation**, the **law of Prägnanz—simplicity or “good figure”**, and the **law of similarity**. They describe how features are grouped into organized percepts.||Gestalt psychologists claimed that these laws are **intrinsic or innate: built in, not learned**. The claim concerns the origin of grouping principles, rather than simply whether grouping occurs.
3|Perception is influenced by **knowledge of regularities in the environment**, connecting with Helmholtz’s **unconscious inference**. **Physical regularities** are regularly occurring physical properties; **semantic regularities** are characteristics associated with the functions carried out in different types of scenes.||**Encoding regularities is adaptive because it helps us infer what is in the environment when information is limited.** Repeated patterns narrow plausible interpretations.||**Ontogeny** concerns an individual’s development, while **phylogeny** concerns evolutionary history. The question is whether a useful perceptual regularity reflects learning during development, evolutionary history, or an interaction between them.
4|The **oblique effect** means **people perceive verticals and horizontals more easily than other orientations**. The advantage concerns the detection or discrimination of orientation.||Vertical and horizontal edges occur often in both natural and built environments. Perceptual sensitivity can therefore favor common orientations rather than treating every direction equally.
5|The **light-from-above assumption** is that **light comes from above**, as is usually the case in the environment.||**We perceive shadows as specific information about depth and distance.** A pattern of shading can look raised or indented depending on which illumination direction is assumed.||The assumption helps resolve ambiguity: the image supplies shading, while the prior helps select a depth interpretation.
6|The sand pattern is a **light-from-above example**. Bright and dark regions can be interpreted as shapes raised out of the surface or carved into it.||Assuming overhead light favors one arrangement. Turning the photograph changes which arrangement is compatible with that assumed illumination, even though the pictured sand has not been rebuilt.
7|**The shadows help you see the deer as the foreground.** They give information about which contours appear raised and how the figure relates to its background.||The light-from-above assumption supports a depth reading of the shading. When orientation changes, the same photographed pattern can support a different organization of foreground and background.
8|**The meaning of a scene is related to what is happening within that scene.** **Semantic regularities** are characteristics associated with the **functions carried out in different types of scenes**.||A **scene schema is knowledge of what a given scene ordinarily contains**. Thinking of a **cognitive neuroscience classroom** produces expectations about its objects and activities.||Those expectations come from past experience with the setting. They help narrow what an ambiguous object might be and where relevant information is likely to occur.
9|**Palmer (1975)** asked whether **context affects perception**. **Scenes were shown for two seconds to provide a context; participants were then shown an object to identify**.||**Participants identified objects most accurately after an appropriate context. Accuracy was worst after an inappropriate context.** A kitchen provides an appropriate setting for a loaf of bread and an inappropriate one for a mailbox.||The outcome is identification accuracy. The comparison tests whether scene information contributes to recognizing the object rather than recognition depending only on its isolated appearance.
10|The **inferential approach** combines two ideas: **Helmholtz’s proposal that we resolve ambiguities by the likelihood principle**, and **regularities in the environment providing information for how those ambiguities can be resolved**.||Given an **image as input** and **knowledge about regularities of the physical world**, perception selects **the most probable interpretation of the image**.||The image constrains what fits, while prior experience helps rank the compatible alternatives. Both sources contribute to the selected percept.
11|**Thomas Bayes (1701–1761)** is associated with a formal account of probability updating. An estimate of an outcome is influenced by a **prior probability** and a **likelihood**.||The **prior probability** is **our initial belief about the probability of an outcome**. The **likelihood** concerns **the extent to which evidence is consistent with the possible outcomes**.||The equation combines the starting probability with the support supplied by the current evidence. The updated estimate is called the posterior probability.
12|For **Bayesian inference in hypothesis testing**, **P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H) / P(E)**. **H** is a perceptual hypothesis and **E** is the image received.||**P(H)** is the prior probability of percept H. **P(E|H)** is the probability of seeing image E assuming H is the correct explanation. **P(E)** is the probability of seeing E regardless of a particular perceptual hypothesis.||**What the brain does: pick the most probable percept H given the input image. People start with a prior, then use additional evidence to update the prior and reach a conclusion (Kording & Wolpert, 2006).** Updating means changing the starting estimate using the information in the image.
13|**Prior belief × likelihood of the evidence → conclusion.** Mary’s cough is most likely a **cold** because **colds are common—a high prior—and reliably cause coughing—a high likelihood**.||The illustrated alternatives are **cold, lung disease, and heartburn**. Lung disease has a lower prior but can explain coughing; heartburn has a relatively higher prior in the illustration but a lower likelihood of causing this evidence.||Combining the two factors favors the cold. The prior favors a common cause, and the likelihood favors a cause that readily produces coughing. Cold receives support from both factors.
14|Four approaches are compared: **Helmholtz’s unconscious inference**, **the Gestalt laws of organization**, **regularities in the environment**, and **Bayesian inference**.||In **Helmholtz, regularities, and Bayesian inference**, we use **data about the environment gathered through past experiences in perceiving to determine what is out there**. **Top-down processing is important**.||The classical **Gestalt** position emphasizes **built-in principles of organization** that can override experience. Modern accounts also propose that **laws of organization could have been created or shaped by experience**.
15|The **Müller-Lyer illusion** is a perceptual illusion involving central lines with different end configurations. **Equal-length shafts can appear unequal** because of those surrounding fins.||The **outward-winged line appears longer than the inward-winged line**. The relevant judgment is central shaft length, rather than total fin-to-fin extent.||The question is whether experience changes the size of that bias. Cross-cultural comparisons test whether the same physical display produces the same apparent length in different populations.
16|In **Segall et al. (1966)**, line **a had to be about one fifth longer than line b before they were perceived as equal**. The physical adjustment was about **20%** in the illustrated comparison.||**San foragers in the Kalahari were unaffected by the illusion** in the reported finding. The strength of the Müller-Lyer effect therefore differed across the compared populations.||The groups differ in how much the physical lines must be adjusted to look equal. This connects experience and environment with differences in perceived length.
17|**Is cognitive psychology universal?** One view is that **tasks involving “low-level” cognitive processes, such as vision, should not vary much across the human spectrum**, associated here with **Fodor (1983)**.||The Müller-Lyer comparison suggests that **even visual perception can show substantial variation across populations**, discussed by **Henrich et al. (2010)**. **Henrich (2008)** connects the illusion with culturally developed **carpentered corners**.||In Bayesian terms, **our priors are determined by experience with a particular kind of built environment**. Different experience can make an interpretation common in one population without making it universal.
18|**WEIRD** means **Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic**. Much psychological research is conducted in these countries, raising questions about how well those samples represent humanity.||**Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan (2010)** challenge generalizing too readily from heavily studied populations. Sampling matters even when a task concerns processes assumed to be basic.||The related **MYOPICS** critique expands to **Materialist, Young, Obsessed with self, Pleasure-seeking, Isolated, Consumerist, and Sedentary**. The sampling question concerns how research participants are selected and whether findings differ across populations.
19|**Is people’s psychological repertoire universal?** **Fodor (1983)** represents the expectation that low-level cognition such as vision should vary relatively little across people.||The Müller-Lyer example and **Henrich et al. (2010)** suggest that **even visual perception can vary substantially across populations**. **Henrich (2008)** connects the effect with culturally developed environments.||The developmental question is **how the environment can shape perceptual processes over development**. Repeated exposure to a particular environment can shape expectations about objects and geometry.
20|**Some neurons respond best to things that occur regularly in the environment. Neurons become tuned to respond best to what we commonly experience.** Horizontals and verticals are examples.||Evidence comes from **single-neuron recordings in monkey visual cortex** and **brain-scanning experiments in humans**. These methods relate environmental regularities to neural-response preferences.||**Natural selection** favors characteristics enhancing survival and reproduction across generations. **Learning can shape the response properties of neurons as well**, so evolutionary and experience-based contributions must be distinguished.
21|**Experience-dependent plasticity is the mechanism through which the structure of the brain is changed by experience.** **Blakemore and Cooper (1970)** reared kittens in a space containing **vertical black-and-white stripes**.||The kittens **batted at a moving vertical stick but ignored horizontal sticks**. Their visual cortex contained neurons that **responded mainly to vertical orientations**.||The restricted environment was associated with changed neural tuning and behavior. Experience therefore contributes to what the visual system processes effectively during development.
22|The **fusiform face area (FFA)** is a face-responsive region in the **ventral visual system**. Faces produce stronger activity there than comparison categories in the illustrated tests.||The face comparison identifies a characteristic response preference. Recognition expertise becomes relevant when examining how experience can change FFA activity.
23|The **parahippocampal place area (PPA)** responds strongly to **places and scenes**. Its preference differs from the FFA’s stronger response to faces.||Comparing the responses to different stimulus categories provides evidence for specialization within visual processing. Faces and scenes are associated with different response preferences.
24|**Faces and places in the ventral stream** engage distinguishable response patterns. **FFA** emphasizes faces; **PPA** emphasizes places and scenes.||These category-selective regions are parts of the ventral recognition pathway. Comparing their activity connects the kind of object being perceived with the location of stronger responding.
25|**Gauthier et al. (1999)** studied whether **experience-dependent plasticity plays a role in activation of the fusiform face area (FFA)**. Participants completed **four days of “Greeble recognition” training**.||**After training, the FFA responded as much to Greebles as to faces** in the reported comparison.||Learning to distinguish a novel object class can therefore change activity in a face-associated area. **Expertise** is a possible contributor to category-selective responses.
26|In **Gauthier et al. (1999)**, participants completed **four-day Greeble-recognition training**, after which **FFA activation for Greebles increased to the level reported for faces**.||This raises the question: **“Can a lifetime of experience perceiving faces be what makes the FFA respond so strongly to faces?”** The experiment shows that nonface expertise can modify activity in that region.||The change links new recognition experience with increased FFA activity, providing an example of experience-dependent plasticity.
27|**Greeble novices versus experts, Gauthier et al. (1999):** compare responses to Greebles before and after recognition training. Greater expertise is associated with stronger activity in face-associated ventral regions.||The comparison connects **experience-dependent plasticity** with category selectivity. Learning changes both discrimination of the objects and the brain’s response to them.||The training gives participants repeated experience distinguishing Greebles, accompanied by stronger responses to the trained objects.
28|**Movement helps us perceive things in the environment more accurately than static, still images.** A **horse standing still in the distance** can be harder to discern than the same horse **walking across a field**.||**Walking around the horse to see it from different angles** also facilitates accurate perception. The same principle applies to the **kangaroo** example.||Object movement adds dynamic information, while observer movement supplies additional viewpoints. Both change the evidence available beyond one stationary view.
29|**Actions within or upon the environment involve a constant stream of updating perceptions and recognition of very subtle changes.** Perception guides the next part of an action while action produces new input.||The cup sequence is **perceive the cup → reach for the cup → grasp the cup**. Location and shape information are used as the hand approaches and contacts the object.||This continual updating makes perception and action an interaction rather than two unrelated events.
30|**Visual development may be related in important ways to manual exploration. Some aspects of visual development may be accelerated by enhancing infants’ ability to manually explore objects.**||**Sticky mittens** increase opportunities to contact and act on objects before ordinary grasping is fully effective. That changes the visual and motor experience available to the infant.||Manual exploration gives infants opportunities to see objects while contacting and moving them, connecting action with visual development.
31|In **Garner interference**, participants **classify stimuli on one dimension while a second, irrelevant dimension either stays fixed or varies from trial to trial**. The response-time cost of irrelevant variation is the key comparison.||For **integral dimensions**, such as **brightness and saturation**, irrelevant variation **slows responses**: that cost is **Garner interference**. For **separable dimensions**, such as **shape and color**, there is **little or no cost**, so attention can select one dimension more independently.||**What counts as an “object” for attention is set by how perception bundles features. Perception constrains what attention can select.**
32|**Two people can experience different percepts in response to the same stimulus** because the perceivers can bring different knowledge and experience to ambiguous input.||Perception depends on **knowledge about characteristics of the environment**. Regularities guide expectations, and **experience-dependent plasticity** can help tune the brain to things likely to appear.||Questions about how **perception and memory are represented in the brain** connect current sensory input, stored knowledge, and neural organization. Behavioral and neural evidence constrain the proposed explanation.
