2|**Good continuation** favors smooth connected contours; **Prägnanz** favors a simple coherent organization; **similarity** groups elements with shared visual properties. They describe how a field becomes organized, rather than merely naming its individual features.||The classical Gestalt claim is that these tendencies are **intrinsic or innate**, not assembled entirely through learning. Whether experience helps produce or tune such principles becomes the next issue.
3|**Physical regularities** concern recurring physical properties; **semantic regularities** concern the contents and functions associated with different scene types. Both help resolve limited or ambiguous sensory evidence, connecting the account to Helmholtz.||The question about **adaptiveness** distinguishes **ontogeny**, change during an individual's development, from **phylogeny**, change across evolutionary history. A useful bias might reflect learning, evolution, or an interaction between inherited capacities and experience; usefulness alone does not identify which produced it.
4|The **oblique effect** is an advantage for **vertical and horizontal orientations** over tilted ones. It concerns how readily those orientations are detected or discriminated.||The regularity account connects this perceptual advantage with the prevalence of upright and horizontal structure in the environment. An orientation bias is therefore relevant to how the system allocates sensitivity, not only to which objects a person recognizes.
5|The system commonly interprets shading under the assumption that **light comes from above**. A dark region can then supply information about a surface turning away from the light, a recess, or an object's shadow.||The assumption is useful because overhead lighting is common, but shading is not an unambiguous depth measurement by itself. Change the assumed light direction and the same shading can support a different depth interpretation.

7|The **deer** is separated from its background partly through shadows that make its contours and surface relief interpretable. The shadows support **depth and figure–ground organization**, not simply recognition of a familiar animal name.||Explain what the illumination suggests about raised and recessed regions, then how that interpretation makes the deer appear as a coherent foreground figure.
8|A scene's meaning concerns **what happens there**. A **scene schema** includes the objects and arrangements expected in that setting because of its typical activities.||For a **cognitive neuroscience classroom**, you might anticipate students, an instructor, seating, and presentation equipment. 9|In **Palmer's 1975 experiment**, the scene appeared for **two seconds** before the object to be identified. That initial scene established an **appropriate or inappropriate context** for the subsequent target.||Identification was best after appropriate context and worst after inappropriate context. The explanatory point is that prior scene information changes object identification, rather than accuracy depending only on the target's isolated visual features.
10|The inferential account combines **the current image** with **knowledge of regularities** to select a probable interpretation. Helmholtz supplies the likelihood principle, while regularities explain why some candidate causes are favored over others.||Perception is therefore constrained guessing about the source of input. The preferred interpretation must fit available evidence as well as what is plausible from experience.
11|**Thomas Bayes (1701–1761)** supplies the probability framework. A **prior** represents the starting plausibility of a hypothesis, and the **likelihood** asks how compatible the current evidence is with that hypothesis.||These answer different questions. A rare event can strongly predict a sign when it occurs, while still being an unlikely explanation of that sign because the event is rare. Combining the two avoids confusing a good symptom match with a high overall probability.
12|Read the equation as **P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H) / P(E)**. Here **H** is a candidate perceptual hypothesis and **E** is the observed image: the posterior asks how probable H is after E is known.||**P(E|H)** measures how expected the image would be if the hypothesis were true; **P(H)** is the prior; **P(E)** normalizes across possible explanations of the image. Updating in this way, as discussed by **Kording and Wolpert (2006)**, favors the hypothesis with the strongest combined support.
13|For **Mary's cough**, the evidence is coughing and a candidate explanation is a cold. Colds have a relatively high **prior probability**, and coughing is compatible with a cold, giving the explanation a supportive **likelihood**.||The conclusion uses both how common the condition is and how well it explains the symptom. **P(cough|cold)** is not the same probability as **P(cold|cough)**; the latter requires taking alternative causes and their probabilities into account.||The three candidate causes in the figure are **cold, lung disease, and heartburn**. In the hypothetical prior panel, cold and heartburn are relatively common and lung disease relatively rare. Lung disease has a good symptom match but a weaker prior, while heartburn has a stronger prior but a poor symptom match in this example. Read the heights as **illustrative probability relations**, not clinical estimates.
14|Group the four accounts carefully: **Helmholtz, environmental regularities, and Bayesian inference** explicitly emphasize prior experience in deciding what is present. Classical **Gestalt theory** emphasizes built-in organization that can operate regardless of a person's deliberate interpretation.||The distinction is not absolute. Modern accounts allow experience to help shape organizing principles too. These theories can address the same percept while differing in the source and description of the constraints selecting it.

20|Evidence for orientation tuning includes **single-neuron recordings in monkeys** and **brain-scanning studies in humans**.
