2|The opening question asks why the brain's **reconstruction** is usually accurate even though we experience the result of processing rather than an unmediated copy of the world.||The chapter's answer will connect ambiguous sensory signals with **knowledge of environmental regularities**. Those regularities let the system favor interpretations that usually correspond to what actually caused the signal.
3|The **R. C. James Dalmatian image** contains the information needed to see a dog, but its patches may initially appear unrelated. Once the dog is recognized, the same patches become parts of a coherent object and scene.||The physical pattern has not been replaced. What changes is the organization and interpretation available to the perceiver. This prepares the distinction between incoming evidence and knowledge that helps interpret it.
4|Calling perception the experience that other cognition is built on emphasizes its role as an **input to attention, memory, language, and reasoning**. What gets remembered or described depends partly on what was perceived in the first place.||Perception is therefore more than a final label attached to a raw image. It creates an organized experience on which subsequent cognitive operations can act.
5|Keep all four properties together. **Added information can change a percept**; resolving a stimulus can resemble reasoning or problem solving; perception operates alongside **action**; and **prior knowledge** helps resolve ambiguity.||The before-and-after retinal image illustrates the last point: learning what to look for can change the experienced organization without changing the stimulus. Knowledge normally supports accurate perception, but the same dependence can sometimes support an error.
6|Perception's speed makes its processing **invisible to awareness**, not simple. Roughly half the primate cortex is devoted to vision, while rapid human recognition contrasts with persistent difficulties in machine perception.||Formal tasks such as **chess, theorem proving, and algebra** became tractable for computers before reliable object recognition. The **2007 DARPA Urban Challenge** illustrates how demanding real-world visual interpretation and action can be. Success on one kind of task does not establish that a system has solved perception generally.
7|Compare **2007 rule-based systems and sensor fusion** with present deep networks trained on millions of labeled images. Benchmark recognition has improved substantially, but changes in **viewpoint, occlusion, lighting, and unusual scene context** remain important challenges.||A high-resolution camera solves sensing, not interpretation. The stop sign with stickers illustrates how a familiar object can produce an unfamiliar input. Humans' flexibility contrasts with networks that may produce confident errors when the input departs from training conditions.
8|The next three problems are **inverse projection, incomplete or degraded views, and viewpoint changes**. Each creates a gap between the image available to the observer and the object that must be identified.||The common issue is ambiguity: the visual system must settle on an object or scene even though the current image does not uniquely specify it.
9|At a fixed viewing arrangement, an object produces a retinal projection. Reversing that mapping is difficult because **many different objects, sizes, and distances can produce the same projection**. A small nearby object and a large distant object can occupy the same retinal angle.||The projection line in the diagram represents compatible possible sources, not a unique recovered object. **Inverse projection** means working backward from the retinal image to what probably caused it; the image alone cannot supply a unique answer.
10|**Occlusion** means another object hides part of a target. Blur, low light, and peripheral viewing also reduce available detail, but we often recognize the target from the surviving fragments.||In **perceptual completion**, contours are experienced as continuing behind the occluder. The wheelbarrow behind a wall is interpreted as one partly hidden object, not several unrelated fragments. Determining which edges belong together is part of perception's work, not a label already supplied by the image.
11|**Viewpoint invariance** is recognizing an object despite a different viewing angle. The identity stays constant while rotation changes the actual retinal pattern.||An observer cannot have memorized every possible view of every object. Recognition therefore needs to tolerate changes in projection, just as it tolerates partial visibility. Across all three problems, the system uses structure or knowledge beyond a literal match to the current image.
12|The “up” direction starts with **sensory receptors** and carries evidence into the system. The “down” direction reflects what the perceiver already knows, expects, or prioritizes.||These are contributions that can interact within an experience, not labels for two mutually exclusive categories of perception. The useful question is how each contribution affects the interpretation.
13|Trace Mary's example: **reflected light → retinal image → optic-nerve signals → brain signals**. Environmental energy is converted into neural activity before an object can be represented.||This is **data-driven, bottom-up processing**. The chain describes where incoming information starts; it does not require that the observer already know Mary. Subsequent interpretation can still be influenced by knowledge. Externally stimulated sensory experience begins with incoming evidence; **mental imagery** need not begin with a present external stimulus.
14|Separate the three top-down influences: **knowledge** supplies information about Mary, **expectation** favors what you predict you will encounter, and **attention** affects which features you sample.||All originate from the perceiver's current state or previous experience rather than from the stimulus alone. Instead of asking whether the experience is exclusively bottom-up or top-down, identify what each source contributes and when it contributes it.
15|The **direct-perception** side emphasizes information available in the environment; in Gibson's account, a moving observer receives an **optic array** that specifies environmental layout. Parts-based descriptions emphasize extracting and assembling stimulus information.||The **constructive** side emphasizes expectations, experience, and context: the percept is built through interpretation, and illusions demonstrate departures from the physical stimulus. Perceptual construction is **constrained by reliable environmental information**, rather than unconstrained guessing or a purely passive readout.
16|**Irving Biederman's recognition-by-components theory (1987)** proposes elementary volumetric features called **geons**. Recognition depends on both identifying these components and representing how they are arranged.||There are approximately **36 geons**, with around **three appropriately arranged geons** sufficient to identify many common objects. A partly hidden or unfamiliar view can still work if enough diagnostic components and their arrangement remain visible; simply detecting unrelated parts would not identify the object.
17|An **isolated region from a photograph** can be difficult to name because it lacks the scene information that normally constrains its identity. Start by separating what the patch itself reveals from what you would need to know about its setting.||The following comparison restores that setting. Its logic is to hold the local pattern constant while changing the information available for interpreting it.
18|In **Torralba and Oliva's blob demonstration**, pixel-for-pixel identical local patches are interpreted as a **shoe, a car, or a pedestrian**. The patch's orientation and surrounding scene change the plausible identity.||A local shape-only account cannot explain why identical regions receive these different interpretations. The scene contributes expectations about which objects belong there and their likely scale and position. This is a concrete example of context supporting top-down recognition.
19|In **“I hope it's in my desk,”** the acoustic stream does not contain printed-style spaces at every word boundary. **Speech segmentation** is the listener's ability to locate those boundaries anyway.||An unfamiliar language can sound like a continuous stream because the listener cannot apply the same linguistic knowledge. The incoming sound is bottom-up evidence; recognizing likely words and boundaries from language and context supplies top-down support.
20|**Spectral rotation** alters the frequency structure of speech. In the comparison, rotated speech mainly exposes long pauses, while intact speech is experienced as separate words.||The **temporal envelope** is preserved, but the entire acoustic signal is not identical: its frequencies were transformed. The important control is that obvious differences in perceived word segmentation are not explained by newly inserted temporal gaps. Linguistic knowledge can organize the intelligible signal into words.
21|The **direct-pathway model** proposes nociceptors sending signals from the skin to the brain, with pain intensity tracking tissue damage. It is a bottom-up account of the relationship between stimulation and experience.||**Expectation, attention, and distraction** show why that relationship is incomplete. In **Atlas et al. (2012, Journal of Neuroscience)**, visible delivery of pain medication was associated with increased frontal activity and reduced activity in areas linked to nociception. The same stimulation can be experienced differently depending on what the person believes is happening.
22|Calling perception an **inference** means that a percept is an interpretation of the source of sensory evidence. When evidence fits several sources, an additional basis is needed for favoring one.||The next accounts connect **Helmholtz's likelihood principle** with the more quantitative language of **Bayesian inference**. Both address how the system resolves ambiguity, rather than assuming the image has only one possible meaning.
23|Around **1860**, Helmholtz proposed **unconscious inference**: experience supplies assumptions about the environment, and the system favors the interpretation most likely to have produced the current image. This addresses the inverse projection problem.||Here “unconscious” means **inaccessible and involuntary**, not a Freudian hidden motive. You cannot inspect the inference directly, and knowing that an illusion is misleading does not necessarily switch the percept off.
24|The retinal pattern **(a)** is compatible with both **(b), two overlapping rectangles**, and **(c), a rectangle aligned with an unusually notched shape**. Geometric compatibility alone therefore cannot choose between them.||The unusual-shape explanation requires a precise coincidence. Ordinary overlap is more consistent with prior experience, so it is favored. The example distinguishes **what could produce an image** from **what probably produced it**.
25|Keep the three quantities separate: a **prior** expresses how plausible an interpretation was before this input; a **likelihood** expresses how well the input fits that interpretation; a **posterior** updates the interpretation using both.||The slide's multiplication is a proportional relationship: **posterior ∝ prior × likelihood**, with normalization needed for a probability. A strong light-from-above prior can favor one reading of ambiguous shading. Different environmental experience can change priors and therefore change which interpretation is favored.
26|The disc is **light at the top and dark at the bottom**. Either a **bump lit from above** or a **dent lit from below** can produce that arrangement, so shading alone is ambiguous.||The **light-from-above prior** favors the bump interpretation. Rotate the picture and the shading orientation reverses relative to the assumed light source, so the favored reading can become a dent. The object depicted has not physically changed; the image's orientation relative to the prior has.
27|Before naming an object, the system must determine which regions and contours belong together. **Perceptual organization** is the grouping problem that makes candidate objects available for recognition.||This is why a visual field cannot be understood simply as a list of isolated colors and points. Organization determines which of those elements will be treated as one thing.
28|The structuralist account associated here with **Wundt** treats overall experience as a combination of elementary **sensations**. A face would be understood through its elements; motion through successive views at different locations.||The **Muybridge sequence of stills** makes the snapshots idea concrete. The Gestalt challenge is whether simply adding elementary experiences can explain the organized percept, especially when the experienced property is absent from each individual display.
29|The **Gestalt psychologists, around 1912**, rejected the claim that organized perception is just added sensations. Their claim is that grouping changes the experience: **the whole is different from the sum of its parts**.||In **apparent movement**, discrete changes in a display produce experienced movement even though no object physically travels along the perceived path. Fixating the ring demonstration helps separate the changing physical display from the moving pattern the observer experiences.
30|**Good continuation** favors straight or smoothly curved paths through a display. Points along such a path are treated as belonging together, including when an object temporarily hides part of the contour.||In the rope display, the traced route shows the continuous path perception favors over arbitrary switches between segments. Following a cable in a tangle or a line on a subway map uses the same grouping tendency.
31|**Prägnanz**, also called **good figure** or **simplicity**, favors a simple organization consistent with the pattern. The Olympic symbol is experienced as **five overlapping circles**, rather than **nine irregular closed shapes** fitted together.||Both organizations could fit the visible lines. Gestalt emphasizes the simpler organization, while Helmholtz emphasizes the more likely cause. These can agree in ordinary scenes even though the theories explain that agreement differently.
32|**Similarity** groups elements according to shared **color, shape, size, orientation, or lightness**. In the dot display, changing color can reorganize the perceived columns without changing the dots' positions.||This isolates a grouping influence from geometry. The point is not just that colors are recognized, but that similarity helps partition the field into organized groups **before object identification**.
33|Apply each principle to the beach scene. **Good continuation** connects the horizon and waterline behind the person; **similarity** groups sand, water, and sky; **Prägnanz** supports three simple horizontal bands rather than a patchwork.||**Figure–ground segregation** separates the person from the background. **Common region and proximity** provide additional grouping cues. Real scenes can engage several principles at once, so one example need not have a single exclusive explanation.
34|The classical Gestalt emphasis is on **intrinsic organizing principles**, with a relatively small role for experience. This suggests early appearance and broad cross-cultural similarity.||The alternative emphasis is that smooth contours and similar surfaces often belong to the same real object, so grouping tendencies may reflect **learned environmental statistics**. Experience can matter without overriding all grouping principles. The common bridge is that environmental regularity makes those tendencies useful.
35|A regularity is a pattern that recurs often enough to support a useful expectation. The visual system can exploit such patterns without consciously calculating them.||The next distinction is between **physical regularities**, concerning properties such as orientation and lighting, and **semantic regularities**, concerning which objects belong in familiar kinds of scenes.
36|Physical examples include frequent **verticals and horizontals**, light from above, relatively uniform surface color, and objects that tend to be **opaque and rigid**. These constrain how shape, shading, and overlap are interpreted.||Semantic examples connect function and content: **kitchens** contain stoves, kettles, and sinks; **offices** contain screens and chairs; **streets** contain cars, signs, and pedestrians. Physical expectations concern how a world is arranged; semantic expectations concern what is probable in a particular kind of place.
37|The **oblique effect** means better perception of vertical and horizontal orientations than of tilted ones: **finer acuity, faster detection, and more accurate orientation judgments**. The effect concerns performance, not simply a preference for looking at upright objects.||Frequent orientations in both **natural environments and buildings** are associated with a greater representation of those orientations in cortical tuning. Environmental statistics, behavioral advantage, and neural selectivity are three distinct levels of the same proposed relationship.
38|With a **light-from-above assumption**, light on top and shadow below favor a protruding bump; shadow on top and light below favor an indentation. The shading is interpreted in relation to the assumed illumination.||The two panels are the **same picture rotated 180 degrees**. Rotation changes its orientation, not the depicted surface's actual shape. The perceptual reversal illustrates a prior affecting depth interpretation rather than a change in the physical object.
39|First decide whether the sand forms **raised shapes or carved depressions**. The photograph supplies shading, but that shading must be interpreted using an assumption about where the light originates.||The point of committing to one reading is to compare it with the rotated photograph. The next display tests whether a simple change in orientation changes your experienced depth.
40|The sand was not rebuilt, and a new scene was not photographed. **The same photograph was rotated**, changing which depth interpretation fits an overhead light source.||Raised and carved readings can therefore reverse. Their involuntary quality matters: the system applies the assumption even when you understand the demonstration and know both interpretations are possible.
41|A **scene schema** includes both **what belongs in a scene and where it usually belongs**. Imagining a kitchen brings up a stove, sink, and cabinets even when those objects were never explicitly requested.||The unfamiliar door handle makes the spatial component concrete: you know roughly what to seek and do not normally search the ceiling. Physical regularities constrain possible surfaces and shapes; semantic schemas narrow probable objects and locations in the current setting.
42|**Palmer (1975)** showed a scene before a briefly flashed object: a congruent **loaf of bread in a kitchen** was identified more accurately than an incongruent **mailbox**. The scene changes the plausibility of candidate identities.||**Potter (1976)** presented pictures at approximately **one eighth of a second each**; observers could still detect a described target scene, indicating rapid access to gist. **Torralba and Oliva's identical blobs** connect this scene information to local object interpretation: the scene narrows the candidate list before the patch is identified.
43|“Perceptions occur in conjunction with actions” means that the observer can **change the available information** by moving, looking elsewhere, reaching, or manipulating an object.||The observer is not restricted to one retinal snapshot. Perception guides an action, and the resulting action changes what can be perceived next.
44|Movement addresses all three earlier problems. Moving sideways reveals different relative motion for near and far objects; moving around an occluder reveals hidden parts; changing viewpoint supplies additional information about three-dimensional shape.||In **motion parallax**, nearby objects sweep quickly across the visual field while distant ones drift more slowly. This is an especially clear example of useful information in a changing **optic array**, consistent with the direct-perception emphasis on an actively moving observer.
45|Trace the pathways anatomically: **ventral: occipital → temporal**, emphasizing object identification; **dorsal: occipital → parietal**, emphasizing spatial location and visually guided action. Different damage can impair recognition or reaching while sparing the other ability.||**Ungerleider and Mishkin (1982)** used monkey lesion evidence for “what” and “where.” **Milner and Goodale** emphasized “perception” and “action,” favoring **“how”** for dorsal processing. The streams interact; these names indicate functional emphasis, not completely isolated systems.
46|After **carbon monoxide poisoning and ventral damage**, **D.F.** struggled to report a slot's orientation or match it with a held card, yet rotated her hand appropriately when **posting the card through the slot**. The same orientation information supported action more effectively than conscious judgment.||**Mirror neurons** are a separate observation: some premotor neurons respond during both performing an action and observing that action. D.F. supports a distinction between visual functions; mirror-neuron responses support overlap between some action-observation and action-production mechanisms. Neither observation implies that all perception and action are identical.
47|Link the six conclusions: **inverse projection, occlusion, and viewpoint** leave ambiguity; knowledge, expectation, attention, and context help resolve it; bottom-up and top-down contributions interact. **Inference** connects priors with evidence, while **grouping** organizes candidate objects.||The final claim supplies the explanation for typical accuracy: the physical and semantic regularities shaping those expectations are actual patterns in the world. Useful assumptions can still fail when a particular scene violates them.
48|The statement that Helmholtz and Gestalt are **fundamentally incompatible is false** in the sense developed here. Both explain selection among multiple interpretations consistent with an image.||Helmholtz emphasizes the **most likely** interpretation based on experience; Gestalt emphasizes the **simplest organized figure**, classically treating grouping principles as intrinsic. When simplicity and real-world probability coincide, both predict the same percept despite different emphases.
49|A reconstruction can be accurate because it is **constrained by sensory evidence and regularities of the world**, rather than invented independently of either. The system's assumptions generally work in the environments from which they developed.||An illusion is useful evidence precisely because it reveals an ordinarily helpful assumption in a case where that assumption produces a misleading interpretation. Accuracy and occasional systematic error can come from the same mechanism.
