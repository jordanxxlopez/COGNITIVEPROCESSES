2|**Psycholinguistics** asks about the psychological mechanisms of acquiring and processing language. Its four areas are **comprehension**, understanding spoken and written input; **production**, creating language; **representation**, how language is organized in mind and brain; and **acquisition**, learning it.||These questions can be connected but are not interchangeable. Understanding a word, producing it, retaining its form, and learning it provide different kinds of evidence about a language system.
3|A **phoneme** is a smallest contrastive speech-sound unit: replacing it can change a word's identity or meaning. The slide breaks **“bit”** into three sound segments, conventionally **/b/, /ɪ/, /t/**.||A phoneme need not carry a meaning by itself. That distinguishes sound-level organization from **morphemes**, units that contribute meaning or grammatical function. Letters are a writing system; the phoneme definition concerns speech sounds.
4|A **morpheme** is the smallest unit with meaning or grammatical function. **“Table” has one**; **“bedroom” has two**, bed and room; **“bedrooms” has three**, bed, room, and plural **-s**.||The plural ending contributes a grammatical distinction even though it is not a standalone word. Compare phonemes and morphemes using the criterion that defines each, rather than counting written letters or syllables.
5|Well-read adults can understand **more than 50,000 words**. The **mental lexicon** is reusable knowledge of word forms that gives access to meanings, rather than a physical printed dictionary in the brain.||The idea of stored forms reaches back to the **nineteenth century**. **Lichtheim's written-versus-spoken recognition dissociations** suggest distinguishable routes to word recognition. A difference between recognizing written and spoken words helps constrain how lexical knowledge is accessed.||**Lichtheim's 1885 model**, reproduced through **Shallice (1988)**, includes **auditory word representations, motor word representations, and a conceptual center**. Spoken input enters the auditory side, while motor word representations support spoken output; the conceptual center connects word forms with meaning. A direct auditory-to-motor connection is drawn separately from the route involving concepts.
6|Follow the full trial: **fixation cross → brief letter string, about 50 ms → mask → choice between D and K**. The target letter K appears as part of **WORK**, alone as **K**, or in **ORWK**, a nonword.||The task is **letter identification**, not deciding whether the whole string is a word. Better K identification in WORK is the **word superiority effect**, showing that whole-word context can help identify a constituent letter despite the extra surrounding letters.
7|Bottom-up recognition starts with **visual or acoustic features**, identifies **letters or phonemes**, and uses those to activate **words**. Top-down processing feeds activation from candidate **words back to their constituents**.||The interaction explains why a familiar word can support identification of a weak letter. It is not a claim that a word is recognized with no sensory evidence; the candidate word is supported by the incoming pattern and can then support its parts.
8|In **phonemic restoration**, a phoneme covered by a sound such as a **cough** can still be experienced as present. Listeners use the surviving word and sentence context to supply a plausible completion.||Participants may not locate the cough accurately relative to the restored sound. Importantly, **words occurring later** can also affect which missing phoneme is experienced. The phenomenon therefore reflects interpretation of linguistic context, not merely detection of an acoustic segment that was physically intact.
9|Compare the same identification requirement across a **word, isolated letter, and nonword**, with brief exposure followed by masking. The two-letter choice makes the required response comparable across conditions.||If the target is easier to identify in the word, its lexical context has helped perception of the letter. The comparison is distinct from lexical decision, where the requested judgment is whether the entire string is a real word.
10|**McClelland and Rumelhart's interactive activation model (1981)** connects **feature, letter, and word levels**. Consistent input activates candidates upward, and activated word candidates can feed support back toward consistent letters.||The diagram includes competing candidates such as **WEAR, WORK, WORD, and WEAK**, supported to different degrees by the surviving visual features. Competition helps prevent every candidate from remaining equally active. The word-superiority result follows when a coherent word-level candidate supports a letter that would be harder to identify alone or in a string without the same lexical support.
11|A **corpus** is a large sample of utterances or written texts. It can estimate frequencies of **words, different meanings, and grammatical constructions**, providing evidence about the experience that could shape predictions during comprehension.||The slide's **Speer (2022), wordfreq** estimates list occurrences per million: **the 53,703; to 26,915; and 25,704; of 25,119; a 22,909; in 18,621; I 12,303; is 11,749; for 10,233; that 10,233**. These are estimates pooled across corpora, not a census of every speaker's experience.
12|A claim that a word is frequent needs a specified **sample, time period, and counting unit**. Counting uses in books, conversation, or another source can yield different frequency estimates because the distributions differ.||The historical superlative counts illustrate why “frequent in the language” is underspecified. Distinguish the number of occurrences from the rate relative to a corpus's size, and distinguish an aggregate language estimate from an individual's personal exposure.||The displayed **Google Books Ngram Viewer** samples printed books over time. A change in a plotted superlative's frequency can depend on which books and years were sampled, as well as changes in actual usage. This is why a book-based count should not automatically be treated as a complete measure of spoken language.
13|The **word frequency effect** is faster responses to more frequently encountered words. The example contrasts **HOME, about 646 occurrences per million**, with **HIKE, about 9**, using **Speer's (2022) wordfreq estimates**.||In **lexical decision**, answer yes to real words and no to nonwords such as **HIKM**; the higher-frequency word is typically accepted faster. During normal reading, **lower-frequency words also receive longer fixations**, offering a second behavioral measure of processing difficulty.
14|Using the **Speer (2022) wordfreq estimates**, **age of acquisition (AoA)** concerns when a word was learned. The comparison matches current frequency while contrasting early and later learning: **doll/thesis (Zipf 4.06/4.05), puppy/clause (4.00/3.99), cookie/audit (4.07/4.04)**.||The **Zipf scale** is a base-ten logarithmic frequency measure expressed relative to a billion words. Earlier-learned words are recognized and named faster even with approximately matched frequencies, so AoA contributes information beyond how often a word occurs now.
15|**Lexical ambiguity** means that one form permits more than one meaning. **“I saw her duck”** can describe seeing her bird or seeing her lower her head or body. The form alone does not settle the interpretation.||As **Traxler (2012)** emphasizes, meanings are not equally common. Context can help select a meaning, but the frequency and timing of candidate activation matter as well; the following examples refine a simple claim that every meaning is always activated equally.
16|Building on **Traxler's (2012)** distinction among meanings, keep the **word form** constant while changing the context that favors one meaning. A sentence about a bird and one about avoiding something can make the two readings of **duck** differently plausible.||Comprehension involves resolving competition among candidate interpretations. Context need not wait until the listener has consciously noticed ambiguity, and its influence can depend on how strongly the meanings are represented.
17|**Meaning dominance** is relative frequency of uses, not whether one meaning is more correct. **TIN** is presented as **biased**, with metal more frequent than container; **CAST** as relatively **balanced**, between theatrical membership and a plaster cast.||The bars are labeled **illustrative proportions**, so do not treat their heights as exact measured frequencies. Dominance predicts how readily different meanings become available and how much competition they create during reading.
18|The **dependent variable is fixation duration**. In **“the cast worked…”**, the balanced ambiguous word activates competing meanings and is looked at longer than the control **“cook.”**||In **“the tin was…”**, the dominant meaning is favored and the biased word is read about as quickly as its control. The important comparison is whether ambiguity produces measurable extra processing under that particular dominance and context arrangement.
19|In **“The miners went to the store and saw that they had beans in a tin,”** context supports the **less frequent container meaning**. That support can coexist with activation of the dominant metal meaning, producing competition and longer reading than **“cup.”**||When context favors the dominant meaning, less competition can occur. The comparison shows that **meaning frequency and sentence context work together**, rather than either being a complete explanation on its own.
20|**Semantics** concerns word and sentence meanings; **syntax** concerns rules for combining words. **“The cats won't eat”** is the baseline. **“The cats won't bake”** preserves the structure but is semantically anomalous in the ordinary intended situation.||**“The cats won't eating”** creates a grammatical problem because the verb form is inappropriate after “won't.” The point is to distinguish odd meaning from faulty structure, not to claim that an imaginative context could never make a sentence about baking cats meaningful.
21|**Broca's area is in the frontal lobe**, whereas **Wernicke's area is in the temporal lobe**. Broca's aphasia includes **slow, labored, ungrammatical speech** and difficulty with some sentence structures; Wernicke's aphasia includes seriously impaired meaningful speech and comprehension.||Compare **“The apple was eaten by the girl”** with **“The boy was pushed by the girl.”** World knowledge strongly constrains the first, while the second needs syntax to determine who did what. The classical distinction links syntax and semantics to different patterns of impairment, while actual language functions depend on broader networks.
22|An **event-related potential (ERP)** measures a stimulus-linked electrical response over fractions of a second. It contains multiple waves at different delays, allowing researchers to compare **the timing of language-related processing**.||The components introduced here are **N400 and P600**. Their names refer to a negative-going response around 400 ms and a positive-going response around 600 ms. Differences in responses to language manipulations provide evidence about mechanisms, not direct readouts of a person's full understanding.||The figure is labeled **schematic, not real data**. A gray line represents an acceptable sentence, a blue dashed line a **semantic violation with an N400**, and an orange dotted line a **syntactic violation with a P600**.||The horizontal axis is **time after the critical word in milliseconds**, and the vertical axis is **microvolts with negative plotted upward**. The early common waves are followed by the differentiated later responses. The drawing illustrates the time course; its curves are not a dataset from which exact experimental effect sizes should be calculated.
23|**N400** is associated with semantic processing and temporal structures, and **P600** with syntactic processing or reanalysis and frontal structures. Damage to the respective broad regions is associated with reductions in these responses.||Treat these as the course's simplified associations. An ERP component is not the activity of one isolated brain area, and a component's appearance alone does not prove that only one linguistic process occurred.||In **panel A**, compare **“Whipped cream tastes sweet”** with **“Whipped cream tastes anxious.”** The anomalous meaning produces a larger **N400**, shown around **400 ms**. In **panel B**, compare **“The spoiled child throws the toy on the floor”** with **“The spoiled child throw the toy on the floor.”** The agreement violation is associated with a later **P600**.||The horizontal axes show **milliseconds after the stimulus**, and the vertical axes show electrical amplitude in **microvolts (µV)**. The negative-going N400 and positive-going P600 occur at different times; inspect the plotted polarity rather than assuming that an upward line means a positive voltage.
24|**Parsing** groups words into phrases to establish their relationships. After **“After the musician played the piano…”**, **“she left the stage”** and **“she bowed to the audience”** fit an interpretation in which the piano is what the musician played.||**“…was wheeled off the stage”** instead requires **“the piano” to begin the main clause**: “After the musician played, the piano was wheeled off the stage.” This is **temporary ambiguity** and a garden path because later input forces restructuring of an initially plausible parse.
25|In **“The horse raced past the barn fell,”** the initial tendency is to take **raced** as the sentence's main verb. **Fell** then appears to have no grammatical place.||The repaired reading is **“The horse [that was raced past the barn] fell.”** The first verb belongs to a reduced relative clause describing the horse; **fell** supplies the main-clause action. The difficulty comes from incremental commitment to the earlier reading.
26|In **“The burglar discovered behind the bushes fled,”** the natural first reading can treat **discovered** as the burglar's main action. The ending **fled** then requires revision.||The intended reading is **“The burglar [who was discovered behind the bushes] fled.”** Like the horse example, this involves a reduced relative clause followed by the actual main verb. Later information identifies the correct structure, despite the plausible initial grouping.
27|The **syntax-first approach** proposes an initial grouping guided by grammatical rules, with other information used during revision if that grouping fails. **Late closure** favors attaching new material to the current phrase for as long as possible.||Follow the flowchart: read a word, attach it according to the initial syntax rule, check whether the parse still works, and either continue or **reanalyze using additional information**. This is a proposal about processing order, not a claim that meaning is never used.
28|The initial grouping **“After the musician played the piano”** treats piano as the object of played. Continuing with **“was wheeled”** makes that grouping fail because the noun must instead support the new predicate.||The revised grouping is **“[After the musician played] [the piano was wheeled off the stage].”** The changing brackets make the reanalysis explicit: move the phrase boundary so that the same words take different grammatical roles.
