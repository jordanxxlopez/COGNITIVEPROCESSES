2|**Selective attention** prioritizes one thing over others; **divided attention** distributes processing across more than one task or input. **Distraction** occurs when one stimulus interferes with another's processing.||**Attentional capture** is a rapid shift prompted by a salient event such as a loud sound, bright light, or sudden movement. These terms describe different aspects of attention: what is selected, how tasks share processing, and how selection can be disrupted.
3|Selection involves both **promoting relevant information** for further processing and limiting the processing of competing information. Much of the environment receives little attention at a given moment.||The demonstration asks you to maintain one task while other events occur. Not noticing an event does not mean the eyes received no information about it; the issue is what attention made available for the task and later report.
4|**Colin Cherry (1953)** presented different messages to the two ears. **Shadowing** means repeating the designated message aloud as it arrives, which makes continued attention to that stream observable.||The unattended message provides a test of selection: ask which of its properties are noticed or remembered while attention is engaged elsewhere. The experiment separates **message delivery** from **attention to that message**.
5|Participants generally could not report the unattended **message's content**, yet knew a message was present and could notice **speaker-gender changes or replacement by a tone**. Physical or acoustic information therefore survived even when meaningful content was poorly reported.||This motivates the cocktail-party selection problem: following one conversation does not imply that every other signal disappears at the sensory level. The evidence concerns different depths of processing, not a simple all-or-none claim that an unattended ear receives nothing.
6|The three model families differ in **where selection limits processing**. **Broadbent (1958)** proposes an early filter before meaning; **Treisman (1964)** proposes attenuation with both early separation and later effects; **MacKay (1973)** provides evidence relevant to late selection after meaning.||To compare them, ask what happens to the unattended message at each stage. Naming a model is less informative than predicting whether unattended physical features, words, or meanings can influence behavior.
7|The initial **sensory store** briefly holds incoming information from both channels for a **fraction of a second**. The filter has not yet chosen which stream will receive further analysis.||This store gives the system an initial representation of available input. In Broadbent's early-selection account, arriving in this store does not mean a message has already been interpreted for meaning.
8|Broadbent's **filter** selects using **physical characteristics**, such as ear or voice properties. Only the selected message is passed onward to the detector.||The critical prediction is that unattended content should not receive the same later semantic analysis. Selection is based on the channel's identifiable properties, rather than first understanding every message and choosing the most relevant meaning.
9|The **detector** is the later stage that analyzes higher-level characteristics, including meaning. “All information” at this stage means **the information that passed through the filter**, not both original ear streams.||Following the arrows matters: the early filter has already restricted the detector's input. A strict version therefore predicts little meaningful influence from an entirely rejected message.
10|The output of the detector enters the next memory stage, described on the slide as holding information for about **10–15 seconds**, with possible transfer to **long-term memory**.||Within the diagram, this stage follows selection and higher-level analysis. The important attention-model claim is the order: both channels arrive initially, one passes the filter, the detector analyzes it, and selected information becomes available for later retention and response.
11|A **bottleneck** restricts which information continues through a processing route. Here it is not described as merely slowing every incoming message equally; it **excludes unselected information from the later route**.||Broadbent's bottleneck is **early** because it operates before meaning, and its selection criterion is physical input properties. Distinguish restricting access from reducing the speed of processing all streams.
12|In **Moray (1959)**, roughly **one third** of participants noticed their own name in the unattended ear. The important observation is occasional meaningful breakthrough, not that everyone always notices a name.||In **Gray and Wedderburn (1960)**, the ears received **“Dear–4–Jane”** and **“8–Aunt–2,”** yet participants could report **“Dear Aunt Jane.”** Following meaning across ears challenges a rigid rule that selection remains entirely within one physical channel.
13|Treisman's **attenuator** analyzes input in terms of **physical characteristics, language, and meaning**. Early separation can occur, but it does not require complete elimination of everything unattended.||This makes the theory an **intermediate-selection** account. The explanatory change from Broadbent is reduction of unattended signal strength, allowing some later effects instead of an absolute early barrier.
14|The attended message passes the attenuator at **full strength**. Its stronger representation makes its words more likely to activate later units and support the ongoing shadowing task.||Selection therefore creates a processing advantage, rather than requiring that the unattended stream have literally no representation. The relative strength of the streams matters.
15|The unattended message continues at **reduced strength**. Weakening is not the same as deletion: a sufficiently easy-to-activate item may still influence later processing.||This provides a way to explain why unattended meaning sometimes breaks through while most unattended content is poorly reported. The result depends on both signal strength and what activation threshold must be crossed.
16|The **dictionary unit** contains word representations with different **activation thresholds**. **Common or personally important words** have relatively low thresholds, while uncommon words have higher ones.||A weak unattended signal may cross a low threshold, such as one for the listener's name, without activating many other words. This connects attenuation to the own-name finding without assuming equal awareness of the entire unattended message.
17|The proposed hierarchy proceeds from **physical cues → syllabic pattern → specific words → grammatical structure → meaning**. These are progressively more organized descriptions of the input.||The unattended stream is **attenuated, not shut off**. Explain the hierarchy together with attenuation: reduced strength limits how reliably unattended input supports later analyses, while sufficiently supported or low-threshold information can still matter.
18|In **MacKay (1973)**, the attended sentence **“They were throwing stones at the bank”** allows a riverbank or a financial-bank interpretation. The unattended ear receives **“river”** or **“money.”**||The test is whether the unattended word biases the meaning assigned to the attended sentence. Physical sound detection alone would not explain a directionally appropriate semantic bias.
19|In **MacKay's 1973 test**,  participants chose between a **river-side** paraphrase and a **savings-and-loan** paraphrase. Their choice shifted toward the interpretation related to the unattended biasing word, even though they did not report awareness of that word.||MacKay's inference is that the word's **meaning** influenced processing. This challenges a strict filter that prevents any semantic analysis of unattended input; the behavioral effect and conscious awareness need not coincide.
20|A **late-selection model** places the main selection limit **after meaning has been analyzed**. Task relevance controls which analyzed inputs enter the route to short-term retention, awareness, or a response.||Distinguish the model's claim from its evidence. A semantic effect of an unattended word supports meaningful unattended processing under those conditions; it does not require assuming that every real-world input always receives unlimited processing.
21|Compare all three on the same dimensions. **Broadbent (1958):** early, physical-channel selection, attended stream only. **Treisman:** reduced unattended strength, with low-threshold items able to break through. **Deutsch and Deutsch (1963), and late-selection interpretations of MacKay:** semantic analysis precedes selection for response or awareness.||Match the evidence accordingly: ordinary shadowing losses fit early selection; own-name and cross-ear meaning challenge rigid exclusion; unattended semantic bias supports meaningful processing beyond the attended stream.
22|The early–late debate has **no single answer for all conditions**. Researchers can demonstrate relatively early exclusion in some tasks and later influences in others.||The next explanatory step is to identify **what controls selection**, such as task difficulty and available processing capacity. A conditional account explains why different experiments need not all support the same fixed filtering point.
23|An **automatic process** requires little attentional control relative to an effortful process. The **visual-search task** makes this concrete when a distinctive feature is detected readily despite other items.||Automaticity is a claim about control and resource demands under particular conditions. It should not be assumed merely because a response is familiar or quick on one trial.
24|The **Stroop task** illustrates why an automatic process can be unhelpful. Reading the word can conflict with the more effortful goal of naming its ink color when the word names another color.||Thus automatic processing can support performance in one task and create interference in another. Connect the observation to the current goal: the irrelevant process is problematic because it competes with the required response.
25|**Processing capacity** is the amount of information the person can handle at a moment. **Perceptual load** concerns the demands of the current perceptual task: high-load tasks use more of that capacity, while low-load tasks leave more available.||The key comparison asks how much capacity remains for **irrelevant input**, not whether a harder task is generally easier to perform. Less distractor interference can occur precisely because the main task consumes more processing.
26|In **Forster and Lavie (2008)**, a **task-irrelevant stimulus** increased response time. That increase was greater when the main task was easier than when it was harder.||Interpret the difference relative to the corresponding no-distractor condition. Under low load, spare capacity can process the distractor; under high load, less capacity remains. A smaller distraction cost does not imply that the high-load task has shorter overall response times.
27|**Overt attention** involves moving the eyes toward information; **covert attention** shifts processing priority while gaze stays fixed. An **eye tracker** records **saccades**, rapid gaze shifts, and **fixations**, pauses at selected locations.||**Central vision** falls around the current point of gaze and includes the high-resolution **fovea**. **Peripheral vision** lies away from it and has lower spatial resolution. Where the eyes point and where attention is allocated are related but not identical measures.
28|**Stimulus salience** concerns what stands out because of physical properties such as **light–dark differences, contrast, color, and motion**. These image-based differences can guide bottom-up selection.||**Attentional capture** is an involuntary shift caused by such salience. The cause being emphasized is a stimulus property, rather than the observer's knowledge about where an object should be or the task they intend to perform.
29|Top-down scanning reflects **meaning, knowledge, and goals**. A **scene schema** predicts likely objects and locations and can guide successive fixations through a scene.||Different viewers or different tasks can therefore produce different gaze patterns on the same image. The sensory display is shared, but the information being sought and the expectations brought to it can differ.
30|**Võ and Henderson (2009)** tested how **scene consistency** relates to viewing behavior. An object that fits a setting can be processed differently from one whose meaning violates the scene.||The relevant measure includes **fixation duration**, an observable aspect of gaze. The explanation concerns semantic expectations, so do not equate an unusual object with one that is simply brighter, more colorful, or physically more conspicuous.
31|In the meal-preparation example, gaze goes to objects required by the **current action**. Sampling information about the next needed object can be more useful than inspecting an unrelated salient region.||The sequence of fixations is guided by the task's changing demands. This is **top-down control** because the goal helps determine what visual information should be obtained next.
32|**Precueing**, in **Posner et al. (1978)**, directs attention to an expected target location before the target appears. Participants maintain central fixation, so eye position is controlled.||Faster responses at the expected location demonstrate a **covert-attention benefit**: priority can change without an eye movement bringing the target onto the fovea. Reaction time is the behavioral evidence for that otherwise unobserved shift.
33|In **Posner et al. (1978)**, a **valid cue** points to the location where the target actually appears. An **invalid cue** points elsewhere, so target detection can require redirecting attention.||Compare response times while fixation is maintained. Valid-cue advantage and invalid-cue cost indicate that spatial expectation affects processing, rather than merely reflecting the time needed to move the eyes to the target.
34|In **Egly et al. (1994)**, directing attention to one part of an object benefits other locations **within that object**. This is the **same-object advantage**.||The critical comparison uses locations matched for distance from the cue but differing in **object membership**. If the same-object location is favored, spatial distance alone cannot explain the difference.||The cue marks **A**. A target at the cued position is **valid**; the other positions can be **invalid same-object** or **invalid different-object** targets. Compare the two invalid locations that are equally distant from the cue to isolate the object-membership effect.
35|The labels **A, B, and C** mark positions, not fixed levels of attention. Whether **C has an advantage over B** depends on how the display groups those positions into objects.||When the object arrangement changes, the advantage can disappear or return. **A and C belonging to the same object again** restores the relevant grouping relation. Attention follows perceived boundaries and organization rather than a privileged coordinate called C.
36|**Schneider and Shiffrin (1977)** combined retaining a **memory set of one to four target characters** with monitoring rapidly presented **test frames**. Frames could contain a target, distractors, or random-dot patterns.||The participant must keep the target set available and decide whether a frame matches it. The design lets researchers study how practice changes performance when these demands are combined.
37|Follow the trial sequence: establish the target set, inspect the stream of frames, and determine whether a target appears among distractors. A detection response depends on comparing current input with the maintained set.||Both the number of possible targets and the competing frame content matter to task demands. The example is a test of how monitoring and retention can be coordinated, not simply a measure of reading one familiar character.||The illustrated procedure presents **20 test frames** in rapid succession after the target set. The participant then indicates whether a target from that set occurred in any frame; speeded visual monitoring must be coordinated with the stored target information.
38|The lecture reports that after about **600 trials**, performance in the practiced task had become relatively **automatic**. Repetition reduced the control needed to perform the trained combination of operations.||The conclusion applies to the practiced conditions. The important transition is from effortful coordination toward more efficient processing, not a claim that practice removes all resource use or makes any task automatic after exactly 600 trials.
39|Schneider and Shiffrin interpreted practice as enabling more effective **division of attention**. Automatic processing can occur without a fresh intention to carry out each small step and uses fewer control resources than effortful performance.||The performer example raises a related possibility: consciously monitoring highly practiced components can sometimes disrupt fluent execution. This is a context-dependent claim, not a recommendation to stop attending to every practiced activity.
40|**Automaticity is task dependent.** The lecture emphasizes that a sufficiently difficult task may continue to require attention despite practice.||Distinguish improvement from full automaticity: faster or more accurate performance can still consume substantial resources. A practiced skill's demands must be assessed in its actual task conditions before assuming that another task can be added safely or effectively.
