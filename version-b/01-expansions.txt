2|The question **“What is the mind?”** is being approached through what the system does with information. We can ask what enters the system, how that information is represented, which operations transform it, and how those operations lead to an action or answer.||A **computational perspective** connects those steps to a problem being solved. Explaining a response therefore means identifying an information-processing account that could produce it, rather than only giving the response a name.
3|Use the same three questions for **literature, philosophy, and astrology**: What is being explained? What would count as testing whether an answer is true? How would people who disagree reach consensus?||These questions separate the subject being discussed from the method used to justify a claim. Two approaches can both discuss experience while using very different standards of evidence.
4|The target is both **information processing about the environment** and the mechanisms behind **perceiving, acting, and thinking**. An explanation should connect a proposed mechanism to something that can be measured.||A laboratory experiment controls relevant conditions; a simulation implements a proposed process; a statistical model specifies relationships in data. **Publication and replication** make claims available for evaluation, but do not guarantee consensus. Competing models can remain plausible when evidence does not yet distinguish them.
7|Walk through the robot's job in order: interpret what is around it, **select relevant information**, learn and remember useful information, communicate, and then **plan, decide, and act safely**. A failure at any stage can undermine an otherwise successful system.||The applications—**care, disaster response, logistics, and assistance**—show why cognition cannot be reduced to just recognizing an object or calculating an answer. Intelligent behavior requires coordination among multiple abilities in a changing environment.
8|Distinguishing members of a **category** means treating some items as belonging together while separating them from others. **Visualizing** involves mental imagery, while language includes both understanding other people and producing messages yourself.||Add **problem solving, reasoning, and decision-making** to perception, attention, and remembering. The important connection is that an apparently simple behavior can involve several hidden operations, including operations you cannot describe from conscious experience alone.
9|“Call to mind” highlights remembering; “put your mind to it” highlights directed thinking; “make up my mind” highlights deciding. “Sound mind and body” refers to mental functioning, while “a brilliant mind” describes intellectual ability.||These everyday expressions do not supply one precise mechanism. **Scientific study** turns broad references to the mind into specific questions about mental functions and observable evidence.
10|The first definition emphasizes **processes**: perception, attention, memory, emotion, language, deciding, thinking, and reasoning. The second emphasizes **representations** that guide goal-directed action.||For example, information about where something is can support a decision about how to reach it. The information and the operation using it play different explanatory roles. The final point leaves that distinction open to debate: some theories challenge a strict separation between processes and representations.
11|**Socrates** is included for epistemology—questions about knowledge—while **Laozi** is included for concerns about the reliability of perception. Cognitive psychology shares some of these questions about minds and knowledge.||The course restricts its method to **quantifiable, repeatable measurement**. The distinction lies especially in procedures for asking questions, testing answers, and establishing agreement, rather than in claiming exclusive ownership of questions about the mind.
12|**Franciscus Donders**, a Dutch physiologist, investigated decision time in **1868**. Start the reaction-time clock at **stimulus presentation** and stop it when the participant produces the required response.||That interval contains more than the decision itself: the participant must detect the stimulus and produce an action too. The experiment therefore needs a comparison condition to estimate the contribution of deciding.
13|In **Donders' 1868 simple RT task**, a light appears and the participant presses the same button as quickly as possible. Detecting the signal is enough to specify the response.||In **choice RT**, a right-side light requires one button and a left-side light requires another. The participant must identify which light appeared and select the corresponding response. Comparing these conditions is the basis of Donders' subtraction logic.||The illustrated response keys are **J after the light in the simple task**, and **J for the left light versus K for the right light in the choice task**. The important added requirement is selecting the correct key from the light's position.
14|The **late-nineteenth-century reaction-time apparatus** represents the practical measurement problem: present a stimulus, register the response, and measure the elapsed interval consistently.||An instrument makes behavior measurable; it does not display a decision occurring inside the mind. The mental interpretation comes from comparing task conditions and explaining why their timings differ.
15|Follow the task stages separately. A simple response requires detecting the light and making the prepared movement. A choice response additionally requires distinguishing the alternatives and selecting which movement to make.||The extra operations are proposed explanations for the extra time. They are **inferred processing stages**, not mental events that the experimenter directly watches.
16|Write the subtraction in this direction: **decision time = choice RT − simple RT**. Choice RT was approximately **0.10 seconds longer**, so the estimated additional decision time was about **100 milliseconds**.||The reasoning assumes the tasks share relevant operations and that the added choice accounts for the difference. Subtracting two response times is useful only when the comparison supports the proposed interpretation.
17|**Donders (1868)** measured **behavioral timing**, then inferred an otherwise unobservable mental operation. Saying that he directly measured a decision would skip this inferential step.||The same comparison logic remains influential in **neuroimaging**: researchers compare conditions to ask which additional processing is associated with a difference in activity. The subtraction is evidence about a mechanism, interpreted in light of what the conditions actually differ in.
18|**Wundt founded the experimental psychology laboratory in Leipzig in 1879**. Controlled self-observation attempted to study conscious experience under specified conditions.||**Titchener's structuralism** focused on the basic elements of experience described by trained observers. The methodological difficulty was making those descriptions sufficiently **quantifiable and replicable** for different researchers to verify the same result.
19|**Ebbinghaus**, at the University of Berlin, asked how rapidly learned information is lost. He repeatedly read **nonsense syllables aloud** until he could reproduce a list without errors, then attempted to relearn it after a delay.||He studied **many lists and many retention intervals**, rather than relying on one recollection of forgetting. Shorter intervals generally required fewer repetitions for relearning. This made retained learning measurable even when a list could not initially be recalled perfectly.
20|In **Ebbinghaus' work (1885/1913)**, keep the two learning times distinct: **original learning time** is the first learning effort; **relearning time** is the effort required after the retention interval. Their difference is the savings measure shown here.||On a **savings curve**, the retention interval is the delay being varied and savings is the outcome. Greater savings means more benefit from the earlier learning; declining savings with delay indicates increasing forgetting, even if some previous learning still helps.||The upper diagrams distinguish **learning time in seconds** from the amount saved: **1,000 seconds initially**, then **400 seconds to relearn after 19 minutes**, giving **600 seconds saved**. After **one day**, relearning takes **650 seconds**, giving **350 seconds saved**; after **six days**, **730 seconds** gives **270 seconds saved**.||The lower graph expresses this as **percent savings**: saved time divided by original learning time, multiplied by 100. Its labeled delays include **19 minutes, one hour, 8.75 hours, one day, two days, six days, and 31 days**. Savings declines steeply at first, then more slowly; remaining savings indicates a benefit from earlier learning even at a long delay.
21|**William James** taught the first psychology course at Harvard and wrote **Principles of Psychology**. His approach drew heavily on observations of the functions of his own mind rather than controlled experiments.||His topics included **thinking, consciousness, attention, memory, perception, imagination, and reasoning**. The photograph also includes his novelist brother **Henry James**. After his major psychology book, William investigated parapsychology, including séances, while attempting to apply scientific methods.
22|Watson identified two problems with introspective laboratory research: results varied markedly **between people**, and other researchers had difficulty **verifying** explanations framed in terms of invisible mental processes.||His proposed solution was **behaviorism**: remove the mind as the object of study and investigate directly observable behavior. This excluded unobservable processes such as reasoning, thinking, and emotions from the explanatory focus, rather than merely suggesting better introspection.
23|The **1913 behaviorist manifesto** describes psychology as an **objective experimental branch of natural science**. The aim is to predict and control behavior without appealing to unobservable consciousness.||The gain is a shared observable target for measurement. The limitation is that restricting the explanation to observable relationships can leave questions about internal information processing unanswered. This tension motivates the later cognitive approach.
24|Compare the two questions explicitly. **“What does behavior tell us about the mind?”** treats behavior as evidence for internal operations. **“What is the relationship between environmental stimuli and behavior?”** makes the observable relationship the target itself.||Both can involve experiments and measured responses. The difference is what the researcher accepts as the explanation of those responses.
25|In **Watson and Rayner's 1920 Little Albert study**, a **nine-month-old** becomes afraid of a rat after rat presentations were paired with a **loud noise**. The critical manipulation is the relationship between the two stimuli.||The rat initially need not produce the fear response that the loud noise produces. After pairing, the rat can elicit fear. Watson used such changes to argue that behavior could be analyzed through stimulus relationships without an explanation in terms of inner mental experience.
26|Pavlov's original research concerned **digestion**. Anticipatory salivation showed that responding could come to occur before the food itself, in relation to predictive events.||In **classical conditioning**, pair a previously neutral stimulus with a stimulus that naturally elicits a response. After repeated pairings, test whether the formerly neutral stimulus now elicits responding. The important change is the learned relationship between stimuli, not a consequence delivered after a voluntary action.
27|For **B. F. Skinner**, especially in the **1940s–1960s**, the unit to track is the relationship among **situation, action, and consequence**. A consequence changes the future probability of the action.||**Reinforcement increases** that probability; **punishment decreases** it. This is operant conditioning. Describe which behavior changes and what follows it, rather than using “reinforcement” simply to mean any pleasant event.
28|Place behaviorism between earlier introspective approaches and the later cognitive revolution. Its emphasis on **observable stimulus–response relationships** made learning and experimental control central concerns.||The later return to mental explanation did not make behavioral measurement unnecessary. Cognitive psychology retained measured behavior while using it to test accounts of internal operations and representations.
29|In **Tolman's 1938 four-armed maze**, rats first explored the alleys. During training, a rat starting at **A** reached food at **B** by turning **right**.||The critical test changed the start to **C**, while keeping food at B. The rat then turned **left**. This separates learning a fixed turning response from learning where the goal lies within the environment.
30|Keep the food location constant while changing the starting point. From the original start, turning right and going toward the food are compatible descriptions of the same action.||From the opposite start, those predictions separate: repeating the old turn would lead elsewhere, whereas reaching the same goal requires a different turn. This is why changing the start is more informative than simply repeating training.
31|The simple response account predicts **“turn right to find food.”** Tolman's **cognitive-map account** predicts navigation to a particular place, even when reaching it requires a left turn.||The observed change in turning direction supports the map interpretation over that fixed-turn account. The proposed representation is spatial information about the maze that can guide a new route to the same goal.
32|In **Verbal Behavior (1957)**, Skinner extended operant conditioning to language acquisition. Children hear speech, **imitate** it, and receive reinforcement for correct speech.||The explanatory sequence is hearing a model, producing a response, and having consequences shape future speech. The disagreement concerns whether this mechanism is sufficient to explain the full range of language children produce.
33|**Chomsky's 1959 critique** stresses two observations: children produce sentences they have **never heard**, and they make **novel errors that were not rewarded**.||Imitation explains reproducing a heard sentence more easily than creating a new one. Reinforcement alone also struggles to explain systematic new errors. Chomsky used these points to argue for internal cognitive structure, without showing that experience or learning is irrelevant.
34|The **1950s cognitive revolution** shifts the explanatory target from stimulus–response relationships alone to the **operations of the mind**. Behavior remains the observable evidence.||The new question is what processes and representations could produce that behavior. The change is therefore a change in explanation, not an abandonment of experiments or measurement.
35|Early digital computers processed information through **sequences of stages**. This offered a way to express mental explanations as ordered operations with identifiable inputs and outputs.||Two questions follow directly: **How much information can the mind absorb?** And **how does attention select some information while ignoring the rest?** An information-processing model should make these questions precise enough to test.||The computer diagram shows **input → input processor → memory unit → arithmetic unit → output**. Each stage performs a different operation on information. The sequence illustrates how a mechanism can be described through intermediate transformations instead of explaining only the final answer.
36|In **Cherry's 1953 dichotic-listening task**, the two ears receive different messages. The participant **shadows** one by repeating it aloud, which checks that attention is directed to that stream.||People report little about the unattended message. That does not by itself identify exactly where it was lost: selection might limit which information reaches later stages. Locating that selection is the problem the next model addresses.
37|Trace the **Broadbent (1958)** diagram from incoming sounds through selection to the record of selected information. Both attended and unattended messages arrive initially, but the **filter passes the attended message** and blocks the unattended one from the later route.||Each box has an explanatory role and the arrows specify order. A researcher can test the model by asking what information from an unattended message should or should not be available if this proposed sequence is correct.
38|**John McCarthy and colleagues** helped establish artificial intelligence through the **1956 Dartmouth summer project**. The working goal was to make machines behave in ways that would count as intelligent in a person.||Computers had two roles: they were **tools** for implementing and testing proposed operations, and **metaphors** for thinking about how minds process information. A useful simulation makes an account concrete; resemblance in output alone does not establish that humans use the same mechanism.
39|**Newell and Simon's Logic Theorist** used **heuristic search** to prove mathematical theorems and was demonstrated at the **1956 Dartmouth project**. Heuristics guide search toward promising possibilities instead of exhaustively trying everything.||Its importance was methodological as well as technological: problem solving could be written as a sequence of operations that another researcher could examine, run, and compare with behavior.
40|Connect the developments rather than treating them as isolated names: computers made staged processing concrete; listening experiments exposed limits on selection; process diagrams made proposed mechanisms explicit; AI implemented reasoning operations.||Together these developments supported the cognitive revolution's return to the mind as an explanatory target. The shared emphasis is an internal account that produces **testable predictions**.
41|A **model is selective**: it represents what matters for an explanation and helps researchers visualize a system or predict an outcome. It need not reproduce every feature of the real system.||A **structural model** emphasizes components and organization. A **process model** emphasizes operations and changes over time. Identify what a model represents before deciding what evidence would test it.
42|Some structural models **resemble a physical object**, such as a brain model that shows where anatomical structures are located. Physical resemblance helps explain spatial organization.||Other structural diagrams do not look like the real structure but show **connections among parts**. Both are structural because they describe organization; neither automatically explains the sequence of processing that produces a behavior.
43|Simplification is intentional. A model keeps structural details that matter for the current question and leaves out others so the relevant organization can be studied.||The question is whether the retained features are sufficient for the model's purpose. A more detailed-looking model is not automatically a better explanation, and an intentionally simplified model is not an identical replica.
44|Read a process diagram as a proposed mechanism: **boxes identify processes**, and **arrows indicate information flow**. The order and connections matter, because they determine what information is available at a later stage.||The model's value lies in the predictions produced by that arrangement. If behavior contradicts those predictions, researchers can reconsider a process, its order, or the route information takes.||In the illustrated model, input enters **sensory memory**, then **short-term memory**, with exchanges between short-term and **long-term memory** and an output route from short-term memory. **Rehearsal** appears as a loop labeled a control process. The diagram's purpose here is to show how stages, routes, and a repeated operation make a process model explicit.
45|Move through the inferential chain: **measure observable behavior**, propose the underlying cognitive activity, and ask what that account implies about how the mind works.||An observation and its explanation are different parts of the argument. Stronger evidence comes from a design in which competing cognitive accounts predict different observable outcomes.
