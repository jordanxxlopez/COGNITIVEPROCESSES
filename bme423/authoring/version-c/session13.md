## 1 | Describe how large an association is
A categorical test asks whether the observed pattern is compatible with no association. Relative risk and odds ratios instead describe the size and direction of the association.

The study design determines which quantities can be estimated. Following treatment or exposure groups forward provides event risks, while selecting cases and controls leads naturally to an odds comparison.

NNT and NNH express absolute outcome differences as numbers of patients. They connect a rate change with how many people must receive treatment for an additional benefit or harm.

## 2 | From the nominal test branch to association measures
The chooser places relative risk and odds ratios in the association column for nominal variables. Their inputs are category counts, like the contingency tests already studied.

The purpose has changed from obtaining a rejection decision to quantifying a group relationship. A large sample can make a small association statistically significant, so the association's size deserves its own description.

Read the event and comparison direction before calculating. A treatment-to-control ratio and its reciprocal describe opposite reference choices even when they use the same table.

## 3 | Follow-up studies versus case-control sampling
A randomized clinical trial assigns treatment and then observes outcomes. A prospective cohort study also follows people forward, but exposure is not randomly assigned.

A retrospective case-control study begins by selecting people with and without the outcome. Researchers then investigate their earlier exposure histories.

These designs build their samples in different directions. Forward follow-up can estimate outcome probabilities within exposure groups; sampling by outcome does not directly reproduce those population event risks.

## 4 | Association means the quantities vary together
Two measured quantities are associated when knowing one changes expectations about the other. Their patterns are statistically dependent.

An association by itself does not establish a causal direction. A relationship may reflect other factors as well as a direct effect.

For nominal variables, the question becomes how category membership changes the observed outcome pattern. Relative risk and odds ratios put numbers on that relationship using explicitly defined groups and events.

## 5 | Relative risk compares two event probabilities
Calculate the event probability within the treatment group and within the control group. Relative risk divides the treatment probability by the control probability.

A ratio below one means the event is less common in the treatment group; a ratio above one means it is more common. One corresponds to equal event risks.

The measure requires meaningful event-risk denominators, as supplied by a clinical trial or prospective cohort. Keep the event definition fixed in both groups before forming the ratio.

## 6 | Aspirin: relative and absolute reductions
The event is thrombus formation. Its observed risk is six out of nineteen with aspirin and eighteen out of twenty-five with placebo.

Dividing the aspirin risk by the placebo risk gives a relative risk near 0.44. Subtracting the aspirin risk from the placebo risk gives an absolute reduction near 0.40.

These describe the same data on different scales. The ratio compares how large one risk is relative to the other; the difference describes how many fewer events occur per patient treated.

## 7 | Interpret the aspirin effect in words
A relative risk around 0.44 means the aspirin group's thrombus risk is about forty-four hundredths of the placebo group's risk. The placebo group is the denominator reference.

The absolute reduction is about forty percentage points. It describes a difference between probabilities, rather than saying that every patient's risk falls by the same number of percentage points.

Name the event when stating either result. A smaller risk is favorable here because the event is thrombus formation; a beneficial event would require a different interpretation of direction.

## 8 | NNT converts an absolute benefit into patients
Number needed to treat describes how many patients receive the treatment for one additional favorable outcome compared with the reference condition. It is based on the absolute outcome difference.

A larger absolute benefit means fewer people need treatment for one additional benefit. A small absolute benefit produces a larger NNT.

Use probabilities as decimals when taking the reciprocal. A percentage-point difference must first be converted to a proportion, and a request for a whole number of patients calls for rounding upward.

## 9 | NNT for preventing a thrombus
The aspirin-placebo absolute risk reduction is a little above 0.40. Its reciprocal is approximately 2.5 patients per additional avoided thrombus.

That is an average comparative benefit, rather than a promise about a particular small group of patients. It comes from the difference in event rates between the conditions.

If a whole-patient answer is requested, round upward to three. Keep the original unrounded risk difference through the reciprocal calculation before performing that final rounding.

## 10 | NNH converts an absolute harm into patients
Number needed to harm uses the absolute increase in an adverse outcome associated with treatment or exposure. Its reciprocal expresses one additional harmful outcome per number exposed.

A smaller NNH means harm occurs more frequently in the comparison. A larger NNH means more people must be exposed for one additional harm on average.

Define the adverse event and reference group explicitly. The arithmetic resembles NNT, but the direction and clinical meaning depend on whether the outcome difference represents benefit or harm.

## 11 | A large relative reduction can have a small absolute benefit
The hypothetical disease causes ten deaths per thousand patients without the drug and eight per thousand with it. The relative reduction is twenty percent, but the absolute reduction is only two per thousand.

That small absolute benefit gives an NNT of five hundred. The baseline risk matters because the same relative reduction can correspond to very different numbers of prevented events.

If serious harm occurs once per twenty treated patients, treating five hundred leads to many harms per additional prevented death. Compare benefit and harm on the same patient scale and over the stated outcome period.

## 12 | Why case-control data lead to odds
A retrospective study selects people who already have the disease and a comparison group without it. Exposure histories are then assessed within those outcome groups.

The numbers of cases and controls are chosen by the study, so their combined table does not directly reproduce the source population's disease rate. Direct disease risk by exposure cannot be read from those sampled totals.

The odds of exposure within cases and within controls can still be compared. Matching controls and choosing them independently of exposure help make that comparison meaningful.

## 13 | Constructing an odds ratio from a two-by-two table
Within cases, divide exposed people by unexposed people to get exposure odds. Do the same within controls, then divide the two odds.

The resulting cross-product ratio compares exposure odds between outcome groups. An odds ratio of one indicates equal odds; values above or below one indicate the direction of association.

For prospective data, the same cross product can also compare disease odds between exposure groups. Keep odds separate from risk: odds use events relative to nonevents, while risk uses events relative to everyone at risk.

## 14 | Secondhand smoke and breast-cancer odds

## 16 | Interpret the oral-contraceptive odds ratio
The cross product gives an odds ratio near 3.72 for users relative to nonusers. It can be expressed as disease odds by exposure or exposure odds by disease for this table.

The two readings use the same algebraic cross product. Their words must still match the numerator and denominator groups being described.

This is an odds comparison, even though the event is rare and an odds ratio may resemble a risk ratio numerically. Preserve the measure's name and the three-year outcome period in the interpretation.

## 19 | R functions for categorical comparisons
The chi-squared function analyzes a supplied contingency table. Table-building functions first organize individual records into the appropriate category counts.

The goodness-of-fit function instead compares one observed category vector with specified expected probabilities. Equal probabilities are one possible null, but the course examples also use unequal benchmark probabilities.

Read the function's inputs to identify the question: association across rows and columns, or fit of one category distribution to a target. The statistical interpretation comes from that setup rather than the command name alone.
