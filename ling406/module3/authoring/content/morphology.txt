2|Generative morphology|**Morphology is generative because speakers can form words they have not memorized individually.** Derivational morphology creates a new lexical form, while inflection marks grammatical information such as plural or past tense.|The central question is how a speaker extends a familiar pattern to a new item. English plural and past-tense formation provide tractable examples.~Generalization can also produce playful or incorrect forms. This shows productive use of patterns, rather than a vocabulary containing only previously heard responses.|**Productive word formation goes beyond retrieving a stored list.** The theoretical issue is whether that productivity requires symbolic rules or can emerge from learning.|The playful “crew” and “crode” examples extend irregular-sounding patterns to CRY. They demonstrate creative generalization, not correct standard English past-tense forms.
3|The wug test|**Berko Gleason's wug test measures morphological generalization to a novel word.** A child learns a new label and then supplies an inflected form that has not been provided.|The pictured task introduces one WUG and then asks about two. Producing WUGS applies plural knowledge to a new noun.~The same logic can test past tense using novel verbs. Success shows productive knowledge, but does not by itself distinguish symbolic rules from statistical generalization.|**A novel item prevents success from being explained as retrieval of that item's memorized inflection.** The child must extend a learned pattern.|The drawing depicts plural formation, even though the accompanying discussion also mentions past tense. Keep the displayed plural task separate from the broader application of the method.
4|Regular and irregular past tense|**Regular past tense follows the productive -ed pattern.** SHOW becomes SHOWED, BAKE becomes BAKED, and a novel verb such as BLICK can become BLICKED.|Irregular pairs such as GO–WENT, TAKE–TOOK and EAT–ATE do not follow that default form. Some irregulars share smaller patterns with other verbs.~A theory must explain both familiar exceptions and productive extension to novel verbs.|**Past-tense knowledge includes generalization and item-specific variation.** The contrast creates a test for different theories of morphology.|Compare regular real verbs with regularized nonwords, then with learned irregular pairs. The nonwords test extension, while the irregulars test successful handling of exceptions.
5|Words and Rules|**The Words and Rules account gives regular and irregular inflection different mechanisms.** A symbolic rule generates a regular past tense, while irregular forms are stored in an associative lexical system.|The regular process can apply to a new verb because it does not require an entry for that particular past tense.~The irregular system stores word-specific information and can be influenced by frequency and similarity. A successful explanation also needs to prevent an available irregular form from being replaced by a regularization.|**In this account, productive rule application and remembered exceptions are separate kinds of knowledge.** That is stronger than simply observing that regular and irregular forms look different.|“Add -ed” summarizes the symbolic operation. The associative system is not proposed as another copy of the same rule.
6|A dual-process past-tense architecture|**The lexicon and grammar offer different ways to produce a past tense.** Lexical retrieval supplies an irregular form such as HELD, while grammatical combination can produce WALK plus -ed.|Pinker and Ullman's diagram associates lexical lookup with declarative memory and grammatical combination with procedural processing.~Like dual-route reading, the account separates word-specific knowledge from a productive operation. Unlike reading aloud, its task is to transform a stem according to a grammatical feature, rather than convert spelling into sound.|**Similar-looking architectures can solve different computational problems.** Keep morphological inflection separate from grapheme–phoneme conversion.|The input includes a stem and a past-tense feature. The diagram distinguishes lexical association from combination, and its blocking connection prevents a retrieved irregular from competing unchecked with a regular form.
7|Why separate rules from exceptions?|**The Words and Rules proposal was motivated partly by a theory of grammar.** A categorical symbolic grammar describes productive patterns, but language also contains exceptions.|Reading dual-route theories were strongly motivated by neuropsychological dissociations. The morphology proposal also reflects a commitment to keeping the grammatical rule system distinct from exceptional lexical information.|**The motivation for an architecture matters when evaluating its evidence.** Similar diagrams do not mean that the theories were derived from the same observations.|The contrast is between a dissociation-driven reading account and a grammatical argument for separating productive rules from exceptions.
8|Properties of the exception system|**An associative lexicon handles information that does not fit a categorical rule.** Its representations are word-specific and sensitive to frequency and phonological similarity.|KEEP–KEPT, WEEP–WEPT and LEAP–LEAPT illustrate similarity among irregular forms. An exception can belong to a small family rather than being wholly unrelated to other words.~Assigning these properties to lexical memory lets the rule system remain categorical and general.|**Separating exceptions protects a simple rule, but creates a second mechanism that must also be explained.** The associative system carries graded and item-specific influences.|The shared sound pattern in the listed verbs motivates associative similarity. It is not evidence that every irregular verb uses exactly the same transformation.
9|An associative alternative|**Rumelhart and McClelland modeled past tense as a learned association between present and past sound patterns.** A single trainable network learns across verbs instead of applying a separately specified -ed rule.|The model encodes a root's phonology, transforms that representation through modifiable connections, and decodes an output pattern.~Regularity is a pattern learned from many examples. Irregular forms are also learned within the same system.|**One learning mechanism is proposed for both regularities and exceptions.** The challenge is to explain accurate generalization as well as familiar forms.|The fixed encoding and decoding stages surround a modifiable pattern associator. Wickelfeatures represent sound patterns, while the trainable connections store the learned mapping.
10|U-shaped learning|**U-shaped learning describes an initial success, a decline, and later recovery.** A child may correctly say RAN, later produce RUNNED, and eventually return to RAN.|The middle error is a **regularization**, because the child applies the common past-tense pattern to an irregular verb.~The decline does not necessarily mean that the child has stopped learning. A changing system can generalize more broadly while temporarily disrupting an earlier correct response.|**Development can involve temporary errors as knowledge changes.** A theory must explain the entire trajectory, not just the final adult response.|The vertical dimension is successful performance and the horizontal dimension is development or training. A U shape concerns accuracy over time, not the shape of a word.
11|Words and Rules interpretation of development|**The Words and Rules account explains U-shaped learning through changes in the use of rules and lexical exceptions.** Early correct forms can be imitated before a productive rule is available.|When the rule is learned, it can be applied too broadly, yielding RUNNED. Later, learned irregulars and the blocking process prevent that regularization.~This account treats the developmental stages as involving distinguishable processes rather than only gradual changes in one mapping.|**Overregularization is interpreted as productive rule use that is not yet sufficiently constrained by exceptions.** Recovery depends on the irregular form winning over the rule output.|Blocking means that retrieval of the appropriate irregular prevents the otherwise available regular form. It is a proposed mechanism, not merely another name for correct performance.
12|U-shaped learning in an associative model|**Changing the training vocabulary can produce U-shaped learning in one network.** Early training emphasizes frequent irregular verbs, allowing those particular mappings to be learned.|As more regular verbs enter training, their common pattern changes the network's responses. That generalization can interfere with previously learned irregulars.~With further learning, the network can recover the irregular responses while retaining broad regularity. No new symbolic rule component is inserted at the point of decline.|**A shift in the distribution of experience can temporarily disrupt familiar responses.** The learning account explains the developmental pattern through changing weights and input.|The lower plot contrasts correct irregular responses with regularized alternatives. Their changing strengths illustrate competition during training, rather than the sudden appearance of a separate rule box.
13|Testing model generalization|**Testing untrained verbs asks whether the network learned transferable structure.** A real English verb can be novel to a model if it was excluded from that model's training set.|The test therefore uses the logic of a wug task even when the items are real words to humans.~The model can generate many appropriate past-tense features, but feature-level success must be distinguished from producing an entirely correct word.|**Generalization is defined relative to the learner's experience.** A familiar human word is still a valid novel-model test when it was withheld from training.|The summary reports proportions of Wickelfeatures generated correctly. A high proportion of correct features does not imply that the same proportion of whole words was pronounced perfectly.
14|Unusual model outputs|**Some model outputs contain changes that are unlike ordinary human regularizations.** Examples include MADE(D)-like changes to MATE and the unusual outputs MEMBLED for MAIL and TOUREDER for TOUR.|The model authors acknowledge that people are unlikely to make some of these errors. That proposed mechanism is an explanation to be tested, not a repair already demonstrated by the quoted result.
15|The blending criticism|**Prince and Pinker argued that feature representations can blend distinct alternatives.** A model may activate features belonging to several possible past-tense forms without keeping those candidates separate.|For SING, partial support for different sound patterns can overlap. Decoding the combined activation can produce a hybrid rather than selecting a coherent word.~The criticism targets the representation and its treatment of competing outputs, not merely the presence of learning.|**Combining evidence for alternatives is different from blending the alternatives into a new malformed output.** A model must preserve the distinctions needed to produce a coherent response.|The highlighted MEMBLED example makes the criticism concrete. It is presented as an unwanted hybrid, not as a normal past tense that children commonly produce.
16|Phonology and semantics in inflection|**Joanisse and Seidenberg propose that phonology supports rule-like generalization while semantics especially supports irregular forms.** The same system links speech input, meaning and speech output.|Reliable sound representations support extension from familiar present–past pairs to new forms. Irregular mappings receive additional support from the particular word's semantic representation.~The hypothesis concerns different degrees of dependence on shared systems, rather than a separate symbolic rule route for every regular response.|**The regular–irregular contrast can arise from how phonological and semantic information contribute.** A graded system can produce systematic differences between item types.|The diagram connects speech input and semantics through learned mappings to speech output. Its components represent domains of information, not a list of regular and irregular words in separate boxes.
17|Targeting a neuropsychological dissociation|**The model aims to explain the dissociation between irregular and nonword past-tense performance.** These tasks differ in how much they depend on learned word-specific information and phonological generalization.|A nonword has no familiar item-specific semantic history to support its inflection. Drawing phonology and semantics in a diagram is not enough unless damaging them produces the relevant outcomes.

23|Generalization and item-specific support|**Phonological representations support learning the common mapping from present to past.** A degraded representation makes that generalization less reliable, especially for new words.|Semantic information supports forms that depart from the dominant -ed pattern. 24|Specific language impairment|**Specific language impairment, or SLI in the course terminology, involves impaired language development despite relatively typical nonverbal performance.** Difficulties can include morphology and syntax.|The profile can also involve phonology, reading, working memory and speech perception. 28|Why phonology affects all forms|**Phonology contributes to every spoken past-tense response.** Weakening it can therefore reduce performance on regulars, irregulars and nonwords.|Nonwords are particularly dependent on generalizing a sound pattern to a new form.
