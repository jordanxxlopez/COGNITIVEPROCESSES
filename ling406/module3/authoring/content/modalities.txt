2|Signed languages and Braille|**A signed language is a natural language, while Braille is a writing system.** Signed languages have their own linguistic structure. Braille allows a language to be read through touch.|The distinction matters because changing the modality of a language is not the same as changing how a written language is accessed.~Both cases reveal how sensory experience shapes the organization of systems involved in language and reading.|**Do not treat Braille as a signed language or signing as a tactile alphabet.** They provide different tests of language and neural plasticity.|The shared theme is modality, but the linguistic status of the two cases differs. A comparison should preserve that distinction before discussing their brain responses.
3|How signed languages differ from speech|**Signed languages use a visual modality and manual and bodily articulators.** The hands, face and upper body contribute to a coordinated linguistic signal.|Space can carry linguistic relations. Some signs are iconic, meaning that aspects of their form resemble aspects of their meaning.~Iconicity is easy to overestimate when a familiar translation is supplied. A visible resemblance does not eliminate the need to learn a language's forms and structure.|**Changing the articulators does not remove linguistic organization.** Signed languages remain structured systems rather than collections of transparent gestures.|The pictured signs include actions, objects and less directly depictable concepts. A gloss names the translation but does not make every feature of a sign self-explanatory.
4|Coordinated signing|**Fluent signing coordinates hands, facial expression, gaze and upper-body movement.** The linguistic signal is distributed across these visible articulators rather than produced by one hand movement at a time.|Like speech, a fluent stream is continuous enough that word boundaries are not always obvious to a learner.~Eye contact and movement timing are therefore relevant to perceiving the interaction, alongside the form of individual signs.|**The unit of analysis is coordinated language production, not an isolated hand pose.** Fluency depends on how the articulators work together.|The linked demonstration invites attention to coordination and segmentation. The original video link is retained separately from the static slide.
5|Contrastive features in sign|**Signed-language phonology includes contrastive hand configuration, location and movement.** Changing one of these features can distinguish signs, just as changing a speech feature can distinguish spoken words.|The PLEASE–SORRY and ONION–APPLE examples demonstrate that closely related visible forms can have different linguistic identities.~Phonology therefore concerns the organization of contrastive form, not only acoustic sound.|**A feature is linguistically contrastive when changing it changes the sign identity.** Visual similarity alone does not determine whether two forms belong to the same category.|The paired images hold some properties similar while contrasting others. Handshape, where the gesture is made and how it moves are separate dimensions to inspect.
6|Testing categorical perception in sign|**Categorical perception can be tested by varying a sign feature continuously.** The hand-configuration continuum moves between forms while the observer's identification and discrimination responses are measured.|One continuum crosses a phonemic distinction between PLEASE and SORRY. The allophonic comparison varies hand configuration without the same change in sign identity.~A sharp identification boundary accompanied by better discrimination near that boundary would indicate category-sensitive perception.|**The test distinguishes a physical change from a linguistically meaningful category change.** Both continua vary visually, but only one crosses the relevant contrast.|The stimulus sequences progress through intermediate hand configurations. Comparing equal-sized changes within and across a linguistic boundary is more informative than comparing only the two endpoints.
7|Identification and discrimination results|**Deaf signers show a sharper categorical boundary for phonemic than allophonic hand-configuration changes.** In the phonemic condition, identification shifts steeply around stimuli 4–6.|Discrimination is also strongest near the phonemic boundary. Comparable physical steps are easier to distinguish when they cross that category boundary.~The allophonic identification function is shallower and lacks the same localized discrimination peak. This links the perceptual pattern to linguistic contrast rather than any visible hand change.|**The combination of a sharp identification shift and a boundary-related discrimination peak supports categorical perception.** Identification alone would provide a less complete test.|The top plots show categorization and the lower plots discrimination. Compare the position of the steep change with the discrimination peak in the phonemic column.
8|Spatial information in grammar|**Signed languages can use positions in signing space to represent relationships.** A classifier sign made at a particular location can indicate the relative position of an object.|Understanding a sentence can require retaining where entities were introduced and how subsequent signs relate to those locations.~Spatial processing therefore contributes to linguistic comprehension, rather than functioning only as background vision.|**Where a sign is made can be part of the sentence's meaning.** The listener must track linguistic relations across space as well as over time.|The classifier example uses relative placement to express a relation between objects. The signing location is instructional information, not a decorative feature of the display.
9|Shared and modality-related brain activity|**Signed-language comprehension recruits classical language regions and shows left lateralization, as spoken-language comprehension does.** Inferior frontal and posterior superior temporal regions are part of this overlap.|Signing also engages visual and superior parietal regions relevant to its perceptual and spatial demands.~The combination suggests both common language organization and modality-related contributions. Similarity does not require identical activation everywhere.|**Different sensory inputs can engage overlapping language networks.** The additional visual and spatial activity reflects the demands of processing a signed signal.|The figure compares deaf signers, hearing signers and hearing audiovisual-speech participants. Hearing signers are particularly useful because signing experience is not restricted to people who are deaf.
10|Deafness, signing and peripheral attention|**Bavelier and colleagues compared hearing controls, deaf signers and hearing signers on visual attention.** Participants monitored dots for brief changes in luminance or velocity, with attention directed centrally or peripherally.|Deaf participants showed an advantage when attention was directed to the periphery.~Including hearing signers helps separate experience with signing from lack of auditory input. If all signers had the same advantage, signing alone would be a stronger explanation.|**The comparison groups are essential to the causal question.** A deaf-versus-hearing contrast alone would bundle deafness and signing experience together.|The display distinguishes center and peripheral monitoring. Luminance concerns brightness and velocity concerns motion, so the task measures attention to brief visual changes rather than sign comprehension itself.
11|Two different effects of experience|**Signing experience and deafness show different associations with visual processing.** Both deaf and hearing signers show a leftward shift in motion-processing lateralization relative to hearing nonsigners.|Enhanced peripheral attention is found in deaf individuals, not hearing signers. Signing experience alone therefore does not explain that enhancement.~The proposed interpretations involve links between motion and language systems for signing, and reduced reliance on auditory peripheral cues in deafness.|**Do not combine lateralization and peripheral attention into one undifferentiated advantage.** They track different aspects of experience.|The upper comparison concerns hemispheric bias in motion-related areas. The lower comparison concerns center-versus-periphery attention. The sign and size of a bar must be read using its own axis.
12|Sign language versus tic-tac gestures|**Comparing sign comprehension with another organized gesture system helps control visible movement demands.** Tic-tac uses sophisticated hand gestures to communicate horse-racing odds.|Both tasks involve meaningful, sequenced hand movements. A difference between them is more informative about linguistic processing than a comparison between signing and no movement.~The control is not identical to a natural language, which is precisely why the remaining difference is of interest.|**A useful control shares some demands while differing in the process being tested.** Tic-tac helps separate language comprehension from hand-motion processing.|The original demonstration link concerns the control gesture system. The comparison does not assume that every meaningful gesture is a natural language.
13|Neural difference between sign and gesture|**Sign language produces stronger activity around posterior superior temporal language regions than tic-tac gestures.** This supports a distinction between processing organized hand movements and processing a natural language.|The comparison reduces a simple explanation based only on the presence of hands moving through space.~The frontal activation shown is superior to classic Broca's area, so it should not be casually relabeled as a precise Broca's effect.|**Shared visual movement does not explain the entire neural response to signed language.** The temporal-region difference is the central language-related result.|The highlighted posterior temporal activation is near Wernicke's region. The figure supports a regional comparison, not the claim that one highlighted spot is solely responsible for all signing.
14|Evidence about signed language|**Signed languages have linguistic form, grammar and language-related neural organization.** Contrastive sign features can produce categorical perception, and comprehension recruits posterior superior temporal language regions.|Signing experience is associated with changes in motion lateralization. Deafness is associated with enhanced peripheral attention in the reported comparison.~These findings jointly show that linguistic experience and sensory history contribute in different ways.|**A coherent account must separate shared language organization from sensory adaptations.** Neither “sign is just gesture” nor “modality makes no difference” captures the evidence.|The summary combines behavioral category evidence, group comparisons and neural contrasts. Each addresses a different question, so one result should not be used as if it established all the others.
15|Perspective in signed spatial descriptions|**Comprehending a signed spatial description can require a perspective transformation.** A room can be described from the speaker's perspective or the addressee's perspective.|The figure's caption reports better comprehension for descriptions from the speaker's perspective despite the mental transformation that this requires.~This illustrates that the most linguistically natural representation is not necessarily the one that eliminates every spatial transformation.|**Spatial comprehension depends on conventions as well as geometric convenience.** The observer may transform a speaker-centered description rather than prefer an already reversed one.|The two signing sequences describe the same table location in the room diagram. The difference is whose perspective organizes the description, not a change in the table's actual location.
16|Braille as tactile reading|**Braille represents written language through tactilely distinguishable symbols.** It is a way to read a language, rather than a separate natural language with its own grammar.|The symbols are optimized for touch. Fingers acquire letters sequentially, creating perceptual demands that differ from viewing printed text.~The comparison asks how reading networks operate when the input arrives through touch instead of vision.|**The linguistic task can remain reading while the sensory input changes.** This makes Braille useful for studying cross-modal organization.|Sequential tactile sampling is a processing constraint. It does not imply that Braille readers must consciously name every individual dot before understanding a word.
17|Blindness onset and visual-cortex recruitment|**Braille reading activates visual cortex in blind individuals, with more extensive recruitment after earlier sight loss.** Early- and late-blind groups therefore provide a comparison of developmental sensory history.|Early-blind participants show stronger involvement of regions associated with visual word processing than late-blind participants.~This indicates that a region's usual sensory label does not completely determine the information it can help process.|**The timing of sensory experience relates to later functional organization.** Visual cortex can participate in tactile reading even when the stimulus is not visual.|The early-blind examples are on the left and late-blind examples on the right. Compare the extent of activation rather than assuming that every displayed colored voxel has the same function.
18|Five days of blindfolding|**Merabet and colleagues tested rapid plasticity by blindfolding sighted adults for five days while training Braille.** A comparison group received training without the same continuous removal of visual input.|The blindfolded group learned more quickly. This suggests that eliminating competing visual input can affect tactile learning over a short timescale.~Because the participants were sighted adults, the experiment tests a different question from lifelong or early blindness.|**Cross-modal changes do not have to be limited to early development.** A short-term manipulation can reveal latent contributions to tactile processing.|The procedure includes baseline testing, training, repeated imaging and blindfold removal. The group comparison distinguishes training effects from the additional effect of continuous blindfolding.
19|Rapid and reversible recruitment|**Visual-cortex responses to Braille developed during blindfolding and reversed after the blindfold was removed on Day 6.** The time course shows that the change was rapid and reversible.|Removing visual input can allow tactile-related activity to become more evident. Restoring vision changes that balance again.~This pattern is consistent with unmasking existing inputs rather than requiring the creation of an entirely new pathway over a few days.|**Reversibility helps constrain the mechanism.** A rapid response that disappears after vision returns suggests a change in functional contribution or competition.|The images and signal plot compare Days 1, 3, 5 and 6. Day 6 is specifically the post-blindfold condition, not simply another day under unchanged deprivation.
20|TMS tests functional contribution|**Disrupting visual cortex with TMS impaired Braille discrimination in the blindfolded group.** This goes beyond showing that the region becomes active during touch.|On Day 5, the blindfolded group's error rate rises under 1 Hz TMS relative to its baseline and sham conditions. The nonblindfolded group does not show the same selective disruption.~The effect is no longer present in the same way after blindfold removal. The result supports a temporary functional contribution of visual cortex to tactile performance.|**Activation and necessity are different kinds of evidence.** Interfering with a region and changing behavior provides stronger evidence that its activity contributes to the task.|The solid symbols identify blindfolded participants. The vertical axis is error rate, so the upward TMS change means worse performance, not improved reading.
21|Cross-modal plasticity and language|**Language-related organization is resilient across sensory modalities while remaining shaped by experience.** Signed language recruits classical language regions, and Braille engages networks associated with text processing.|Competition among available inputs offers a way to understand reorganization. When an input is absent or reduced, other information can make a larger contribution.~The evidence does not imply that all regions are interchangeable. It shows both stable functional relationships and experience-sensitive recruitment.|**A brain region's contribution reflects development and competition as well as its usual sensory input.** Common language functions can coexist with modality-specific adaptations.|Distinguish the language-network overlap from the visual-attention and tactile-recruitment findings. They support a related account of plasticity through different experimental comparisons.
