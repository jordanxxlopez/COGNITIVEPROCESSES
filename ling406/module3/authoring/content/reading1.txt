2|Phonemes and letters|**Phonemes are speech-sound categories, not letters.** A writing system represents language, but its symbols do not have to correspond one-to-one with those categories.|A letter can contribute to different pronunciations, and a phoneme can be represented by different spellings.|**Keep spoken categories separate from written symbols.** Identifying a letter is not the same task as identifying a phoneme.|The question concerns the relation between sound and writing, rather than how many letters a word contains.
3|Orthographic depth|**Orthographic depth** describes how consistently spelling corresponds to pronunciation. English is relatively deep because many spellings do not transparently specify their sounds.|COLONEL and PARADIGM make the problem concrete. Applying common letter–sound patterns can produce a plausible-looking pronunciation that is wrong for the actual word.~In shallower writing systems, such as Finnish and Korean, spelling–sound correspondences are more predictable. The comparison concerns the writing system, not the intelligence or ability of its readers.|**An unfamiliar spelling cannot always be decoded correctly from its letters alone.** Deep orthographies create more exceptions that readers must learn through experience.|The English examples contrast ordinary expectations from the letters with the word's learned pronunciation. A deep script is less predictable, not completely random.
4|Transparent spelling in Finnish|**Finnish has a shallow orthography.** Letters correspond relatively unambiguously to phonemes, so learning a spelling–sound correspondence helps with many different words.|Doubled letters represent a meaningful length distinction. A doubled consonant is geminated, and a doubled vowel is long.~This regularity helps Finnish children learn decoding quickly. Decoding means generating a pronunciation from a written form, whether or not its meaning is familiar.|**Consistent correspondences make decoding easier to generalize.** Finnish readers can apply what they learn about letters to unfamiliar written forms.|The linked Finnish lesson is a source demonstration. Its sound is not embedded in the PDF, so the written claims establish the correspondence and length distinctions without supplying a recovered recording.
5|Learning to decode across languages|**Pseudoword reading tests decoding.** Because a pseudoword is not a familiar stored word, the child must generalize from learned spelling–sound patterns.|The comparison involves Grade 1 children learning different alphabetic writing systems. Children learning many other languages produce plausible pseudoword pronunciations more successfully than English learners.~English's less predictable correspondences help explain why decoding develops more slowly. This task does not measure the child's entire language ability.|**A made-up word separates decoding from remembering a familiar word.** The lower English performance is consistent with greater orthographic depth.|Compare accuracy across languages for the same type of task. The important outcome is the relative difficulty of English pseudoword decoding, not a claim that English children cannot decode.
6|Korean letter forms and sounds|**Korean writing represents sound structure systematically.** Related phonemes can have related written shapes, so the visual forms themselves reflect aspects of phonological organization.|The letters are arranged into syllable blocks with initial, medial and sometimes final positions. Those positions are not three unrelated pictures of whole words.~The velar consonant forms share the basic angular ㄱ shape. Additional strokes or doubling distinguish related consonants.|**The organization of Hangul supports more than consistent pronunciation.** Similar sound categories can also be visibly related in their written symbols.|The diagrams label initial, medial and final positions. The g, kk and k examples share a shape family, illustrating how a place-of-articulation relation can be reflected in the script.
7|Pseudoword decoding|**GLORP, SPLING, JONK, FEESH and QUISP can be pronounced without being familiar words.** Their forms follow enough English spelling patterns for a reader to construct a plausible pronunciation.|Success cannot depend on retrieving a stored pronunciation for each particular item. The reader is using knowledge that generalizes across words.~The demonstration establishes generalization. By itself, it does not decide whether that knowledge consists of categorical rules or learned statistical patterns.|**Reading a novel letter sequence reveals knowledge beyond memorized words.** Familiar spelling patterns support pronunciation of new combinations.|The items are instructional stimuli. Their role is to test decoding, not vocabulary knowledge or knowledge of a definition.
8|Speed of pseudoword naming|**Pseudoword decoding is fast and largely automatic.** In the Lupker, Brown and Colombo example, naming pseudowords is only about 5–12% slower than naming low-frequency real words.|Participants read the items aloud, and naming time measures how quickly a pronunciation becomes available. The small difference shows that unfamiliarity does not make pronunciation an entirely slow, deliberate process.~Both rules and statistical generalization might produce efficient decoding. The speed result constrains a theory without selecting one architecture on its own.|**Fast nonword naming is evidence that decoding is a normal part of skilled reading.** It does not by itself prove that a separate rule system performs the task.|Compare pseudowords with low-frequency words rather than treating every real word as an equally familiar baseline. A percentage slowdown describes relative naming time, not an error rate.
9|Ambiguous pseudowords|**An unfamiliar spelling can support more than one plausible pronunciation.** VONTH, PHANGE, STREAD and SOUGH contain patterns whose pronunciations are not all uniquely determined in English.|A response reflects the spelling–sound patterns available from previously learned words. Different familiar patterns can favor different responses to the same novel form.|**Generalization can be graded rather than all-or-none.** Ambiguous pseudowords reveal competing pronunciation possibilities.|These items probe how readers resolve uncertainty in spelling-to-sound mappings. There is no requirement that every unfamiliar form have one universally agreed pronunciation.
10|Generalization and competing patterns|**STREAD can plausibly be pronounced with either /ɛ/ or /i/.** The same written sequence can resemble more than one learned spelling–sound pattern.|Testing novel items reveals what a reader has generalized, because the particular word cannot simply have been memorized.~The challenge for a categorical rule account is to explain why multiple outputs seem acceptable. A statistical account can represent differing support for alternative pronunciations.|**Accepting both /strɛd/ and /strid/ is information about the knowledge being generalized.** A model must explain the alternatives, not merely choose one output and ignore the other.|The contrast changes the vowel while keeping the rest of STREAD constant. This isolates ambiguity in the spelling–sound relation.
11|Regularity and consistency|**The regularity effect is the difficulty of reading words whose pronunciation conflicts with common spelling–sound patterns.** PINT, ACHE and CHOIR are examples of words for which straightforward decoding can mislead.|A strict regular–irregular division is less clear when a spelling neighborhood contains several pronunciations. ROLL, POLL and TOLL support one pattern, while DOLL differs.~COOK, TOOK and BOOK likewise contrast with SPOOK. These neighborhoods motivate asking how much support a pronunciation has from similar words.|**A word's difficulty depends partly on how its spelling neighbors are pronounced.** The issue is not simply whether the reader knows the letters.|A neighborhood is a set of words sharing relevant spelling patterns. The comparison concerns their pronunciations, not similarities in their meanings.
12|Sublexical and lexical information|**Sublexical information concerns units smaller than a whole word.** Rapid nonword reading and the advantage for typical spelling–sound patterns show that readers use this information.|**Lexical information** concerns knowledge of particular words. A complete account must explain how experience with a specific word contributes alongside general spelling patterns.|**General decoding explains part of reading, but familiar-word knowledge also needs an account.** The two sources of information are conceptually distinguishable even before deciding how they are implemented.|Nonwords test generalization, while differences among familiar words can reveal word-specific knowledge. These are evidence types, not proof that two independent modules must exist.
13|Interactive activation and ambiguous letters|**Word knowledge can help identify an ambiguous letter.** In an interactive activation network, visual features activate letters and letters activate candidate words.|Activated word candidates send feedback to their constituent letters. A letter supported by a plausible word receives additional activation even when its local visual evidence is unclear.~Competing candidates do not remain equally active. Their activation changes as the network settles on an interpretation.|**Letter recognition can depend on the word surrounding the letter.** Feedback supplies a mechanism by which whole-word knowledge influences perception.|The network links features, letters and words. The letter-output graph shows one candidate increasing while competing candidates remain low, illustrating a developing preference rather than instantaneous recognition.
14|Feedback in the Interactive Activation Model|**Feedback carries word-level activation back to letters.** A partially activated known word increases activation of the letters that belong to it.|This makes recognition interactive. Information does not flow only from isolated features upward to a finished word.~The mechanism helps explain why an ambiguous letter can become easier to recognize when it appears in a familiar word context.|**Recognizing a word and recognizing its letters can support one another.** Top-down feedback is the word-to-letter part of that interaction.|The direction of a connection matters. Letter-to-word activation provides evidence for a candidate, while word-to-letter feedback supports the candidate's components.
15|Lexicality and frequency|**Lexicality and word frequency reveal effects of word-specific experience.** Real words are generally named faster than pseudowords, and frequently encountered words are named faster than less frequent words.|Lexicality compares whether the item is a word. Frequency compares how often a particular word has been encountered.~The English Lexicon Project data show that the frequency effect occurs across many observations, rather than depending on a few unusually easy examples.|**Familiarity with a particular word contributes to reading speed.** Knowing general spelling patterns does not remove the benefit of repeated encounters with that word.|A frequency effect is a relation between prior exposure and naming performance. It is not the claim that all high-frequency words have the same length or pronunciation pattern.
16|Controlling confounds in frequency effects|**Multiple regression tests whether frequency predicts reading time after accounting for other word properties.** Length and sound-related characteristics could otherwise be mistaken for effects of frequency.|The analysis uses about 3,000 words read by young and older adults. Frequency still contributes when other predictors are considered.~This makes a simple length-confound explanation less plausible. It does not turn an observational predictor into an experimentally isolated cause.|**The frequency effect is not adequately explained by saying that frequent words are merely shorter.** Its contribution survives statistical control of other properties.|The highlighted objective- and subjective-frequency coefficients are negative. Higher frequency is associated with shorter reaction times, with separate columns for lexical decision and naming in the two age groups.
17|Frequency by regularity interaction|**Frequency and regularity interact.** Low-frequency exception words are slowed much more than low-frequency regular words, whereas high-frequency words show a much smaller regularity difference.|Naming latency is the time needed to begin saying the word. Higher values mean slower responses.~An interaction means the effect of one factor depends on the other. The regularity cost is not a fixed amount added equally to high- and low-frequency words.|**Experience matters especially when pronunciation conflicts with the usual spelling pattern.** Frequent exposure reduces the disadvantage of exception words.|The lines are close at high frequency and separate at low frequency. Exception words rise to roughly 580 ms, while regular words rise only to roughly 555 ms in this figure.
18|Dual-route interpretation of the interaction|**A dual-route interpretation separates lexical retrieval from sublexical decoding.** The lexical process supplies the learned pronunciation of a particular word, while decoding computes pronunciation from spelling units.|For a high-frequency word, lexical information becomes available quickly enough to reduce the influence of the decoding output.~For a low-frequency exception, the lexical response is less efficient and the regularized decoding output can interfere. Nonwords depend on decoding because they lack a stored whole-word entry.|**Two routes can explain why regularity matters most for unfamiliar words.** Fast lexical processing protects familiar exceptions from competing sublexical output.|This is an interpretation of the interaction, not the interaction itself. Other architectures must be evaluated against the same observed pattern.
19|Acquired phonological dyslexia|**Acquired phonological dyslexia involves disproportionate difficulty reading pseudowords after brain injury.** Familiar words can remain relatively well read even when novel forms are difficult.|Cacciopo van Vliet and colleagues compare real-word reading with several nonword tasks. The patients can often recognize and pronounce known words while struggling to generate a plausible pronunciation for an unfamiliar spelling.~The selective difficulty suggests that familiar-word success and novel-form decoding do not rely on every process to the same degree.|**Preserved word reading does not guarantee preserved decoding.** A patient can draw on learned word information while performing poorly on novel letter sequences.|The real-word table contains many high accuracy values, while several nonword lists have much lower scores. The deficit is relative, not a claim that every nonword response is wrong.
20|Acquired surface dyslexia|**Acquired surface dyslexia involves disproportionate difficulty reading irregular words after brain injury.** Errors often regularize the word by applying a more common spelling–sound pattern.|DEAF pronounced like “deef” and SHOE pronounced like “show” illustrate regularization. The response is related to the spelling, but it is not the target word's learned pronunciation.~Relatively good regular-word and nonword reading shows that generating pronunciation from spelling can remain useful despite difficulty with exceptions.|**Surface dyslexia exposes the cost of relying on typical correspondences when a word is exceptional.** The errors are systematic rather than arbitrary failures to see the letters.|HFR and LFR refer to high- and low-frequency regular words. HFE and LFE refer to exceptions. The highlighted exception columns are lower, especially for low-frequency exceptions.
21|The reading double dissociation|**A double dissociation compares opposite relative impairments.** Phonological dyslexia particularly affects pseudowords, while surface dyslexia particularly affects exception words.|The first pattern shows that unfamiliar decoding can be difficult despite relatively preserved familiar-word reading. The second shows that exception reading can be difficult despite relatively preserved pseudoword reading.~Together, the patterns are harder to explain by saying that one task is simply harder for everyone.|**Opposite patient patterns support separable contributions to reading.** They are stronger evidence than a single group doing worse on one difficult task.|“Relatively spared” does not mean perfect. The inference concerns the difference between task profiles, not an absence of every error in the better-performing condition.
22|Why double dissociations matter|**A double dissociation challenges a single fixed difficulty ranking.** If task A were merely harder than task B, damage might consistently hurt A more, but it would not readily explain another patient showing the reverse pattern.|The reversal suggests that the tasks depend differently on underlying processes. For reading, pseudowords and exception words place different demands on the system.~The evidence still requires a theory of what those underlying processes are. It does not uniquely identify a particular set of boxes in a model.|**The strength of a double dissociation comes from the reversal across cases.** The interpretation must explain both directions of impairment.|Compare the relative task costs within each patient or group, then compare those profiles. Two low scores without an opposite pattern are not a double dissociation.
23|Locating deficits in a dual-route model|**A dual-route model attributes phonological dyslexia to damage in grapheme–phoneme conversion and surface dyslexia to damage in lexical processing.** These proposed locations connect the task dissociation to specific mechanisms.|Damage to conversion particularly affects nonwords because there is no familiar whole-word pronunciation to retrieve.~Damage to input or output word representations, called logogens in this account, or their connections makes exceptions vulnerable because general decoding supplies the wrong pronunciation.|**The model turns an observed dissociation into a claim about damaged processing components.** Each proposed lesion must produce the corresponding pattern of spared and impaired performance.|The lexical and conversion paths are theoretical components. Their labels should not be confused with a direct anatomical map of two single brain regions.
24|Complications for a strict two-route account|**Patterson's discussion shows why reading errors can complicate a clean lexical–nonlexical division.** Errors attributed to a purely nonlexical route can still show lexical influences.|The example “incense” read as “increase” is a real-word response. A simple letter–sound mistake could instead produce a nonword, so the actual response raises a question about whole-word influence.~Possible responses are to allow a more complicated combination of routes or to revise the supposedly nonlexical process itself. The observation motivates refinement rather than settling the issue alone.|**A patient's error type matters as well as overall accuracy.** Lexical-looking errors challenge the idea that the remaining reading process is wholly independent of word knowledge.|The highlighted passage presents two alternatives for interpreting lexical influence in surface dyslexia. It does not simply declare that the patient evidence is irrelevant.
