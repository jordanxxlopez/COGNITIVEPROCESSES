2|Linguistic relativity|**Linguistic relativity concerns whether differences in language influence how people organize or use concepts.** The Sapir and Whorf quotations suggest that linguistic categories can shape how experience is interpreted.|The strongest reading would make language determine what a person can think. A weaker claim is that habitual language use biases attention, memory or preferred ways of representing a situation.~These claims require different evidence. A preference associated with language does not establish that alternative thought is impossible.|**Influencing thought is different from completely determining thought.** Keep that distinction explicit when interpreting a language-group difference.|The quotations express broad theoretical ideas. They are not experimental demonstrations that two speakers are unable to understand the same physical event.
3|Language, culture and the strength of the claim|**Language is embedded in cultural practices and experience.** A difference between language communities may therefore covary with factors other than linguistic structure.|Sapir and Whorf should not automatically be assigned the strongest deterministic interpretation of selected quotations.~A precise study must identify which cognitive process is affected and what alternative explanations the comparison controls.|**Testing linguistic relativity requires a narrower claim than saying that every language creates a completely separate world.** Space and time provide specific domains in which to test representational preferences.|Historical framing explains why language and culture were often discussed together. It also highlights the need to distinguish their contributions in an experiment.
4|Locating the ball|**A location description needs a frame of reference.** Saying where the ball is requires choosing what counts as left, front or a stable direction.|The ball can be located with respect to the viewer, the chair's own orientation or a direction independent of either.~Different descriptions can identify the same physical position while organizing that position differently.|**The object does not have to move for its verbal description to change.** Changing the reference frame changes the relation being described.|The chair and ball are the meaningful stimuli. The question is which reference determines the ball's location, not a judgment about the objects' appearance.
5|Intrinsic, relative and absolute frames|**An intrinsic frame uses an object's own orientation, a relative frame uses an observer's viewpoint, and an absolute frame uses a stable direction.** These are different ways to specify the same location.|“At the chair's front” uses the chair's intrinsic front. “To the left of the chair from my viewpoint” depends on the observer.~“North of the chair” uses an absolute direction that does not reverse when the observer turns around.|**Ask what fixes the direction in each description.** The object's orientation, the observer's body and a stable external direction are not interchangeable.|The ball–chair example holds the scene constant while changing the reference. Rotating the observer changes relative left and right but does not change north.
6|Egocentric and absolute spatial language|**Egocentric spatial descriptions use the speaker's position and orientation.** Left, right, in front and behind are familiar examples for nearby objects.|Tzeltal commonly uses absolute directions in circumstances where English speakers might use these relative terms.~The contrast motivates asking whether habitual language use relates to nonverbal spatial coding, rather than merely comparing two ways of giving spoken directions.|**A direction can stay fixed when a person turns.** Absolute coding differs from coding an object as being on the viewer's left.|The language comparison concerns preferred spatial descriptions. It does not imply that speakers of either language are incapable of learning another frame.
7|Tzeltal uphill and downhill|**Tzeltal's uphill and downhill terms function as an absolute directional system.** Their historical connection to a slope does not mean that each use simply points to the nearest visible hill.|A direction is retained as a stable spatial reference rather than being recalculated from the speaker's current left and right.~This allows positions to be described consistently as the speaker changes orientation.|**A landmark origin and an absolute use are different properties.** A historically landscape-based term can become a stable directional reference.|The contrast is with a description that depends entirely on the current viewpoint. The relevant feature is how the reference behaves across changes in location and orientation.
8|Landmark-based directions|**A landmark-based direction is tied to a particular environment.** Uptown and downtown organize movement within Manhattan but do not provide a universally usable compass system.|A city's conventional map orientation can also differ from magnetic north. Familiar spatial language therefore reflects local practices as well as geometric directions.|**A stable local convention is not automatically an absolute frame that transfers everywhere.** Its usefulness depends on the landmark system being available.|The Manhattan example distinguishes local orientation from cardinal direction. It is a contrast about reference systems rather than an experiment demonstrating linguistic causation.
9|The rotated spatial-memory task|**The chips task separates relative from absolute spatial memory by rotating the participant 180 degrees.** A participant sees a configuration, waits, and then chooses its match at a second table.|A relative response preserves the arrangement with respect to the person's viewpoint. An absolute response preserves its orientation in the environment, so the two responses diverge after rotation.~Dutch speakers predominantly gave relative responses, while Tzeltal speakers gave more absolute responses. The association is consistent with their preferred linguistic frames but does not isolate language from all other experience.|**The rotation makes two otherwise similar descriptions predict different choices.** The nonverbal response reveals a preferred spatial coding strategy.|The north arrow stays fixed while the person changes orientation. Red bars represent relative choices and blue bars absolute choices, showing different response distributions across the two language groups.
10|Spatial inference across displays|**Spatial inference can combine relations learned in different displays.** Participants learn the relation between two objects, rotate to another display, and learn a second relation involving a shared object.|They then place an object relative to another using the combined information. Relative and absolute coding lead to different placements because the person's orientation has changed.~Dutch and Tzeltal response patterns again differ in the direction associated with their preferred spatial frames.|**The task goes beyond reproducing one remembered arrangement.** It asks whether a frame is used when combining spatial relations to make an inference.|The cube appears in both learning displays, linking the cone and cylinder relations. The final placement distinguishes a viewer-relative inference from one preserving environmental direction.
11|A testing-environment alternative|**Testing conditions could contribute to the apparent language-group difference.** Li and Gleitman noted that Tzeltal participants had been tested with visible, familiar outdoor landmarks, unlike many laboratory participants.|An environment with useful landmarks can encourage landmark-based coding. A windowless laboratory can make viewer-relative descriptions more convenient.~Manipulating landmark availability within English speakers tests whether the behavior can change without changing native language.|**A language comparison can be confounded with a setting comparison.** The environment must be considered before assigning the entire difference to linguistic structure.|The critical contrast is access to spatial cues, not simply the label “laboratory.” Windows, outdoor views and familiar surroundings can alter which references are useful.
12|Blinds down, blinds up and outdoors|**English speakers' preference for relative coding weakened when external landmarks were available.** They strongly preferred relative responses with the blinds down, but the preference was less pronounced with a street view or outdoors.|The manipulation changes the spatial information available while keeping the language group the same.~This demonstrates that task context can influence the response. It challenges an explanation in which language alone fixes the chosen frame.|**Spatial strategy is sensitive to the testing environment.** A within-language change helps reveal a potential confound in a between-language comparison.|A reduced relative preference is not necessarily a complete switch to absolute coding. Describe the observed change in preference without exaggerating it into an all-or-none reversal.

14|What the environmental confound means|**If language groups are tested under different conditions, their response difference may partly reflect those conditions.** Visible landmarks and familiarity can covary with native language in a cross-community study.|A controlled comparison must separate the effect of the environment from any contribution of habitual linguistic coding.~Showing an environmental effect does not prove that language never matters. It limits what can be inferred from an uncontrolled comparison.|**The relevant question is what the study actually isolates.** A language-group effect can be real while its causal interpretation remains unsettled.|A confound is a factor that changes with the variable of interest and offers another explanation for the result. It is not merely any feature present in the experiment.
15|Levinson's methodological response|**Levinson and colleagues argued that the simplified task could make the experimenter's purpose too obvious.** Low memory demands and short time or distance between displays may encourage a deliberate strategy about rotation.|Participants who infer what is being tested can alter their response for reasons different from habitual spatial coding.~The response therefore focuses on memory load and task transparency, rather than denying that landmarks can ever matter.|**Changing a task can change the process it measures.** A landmark effect in an easy, transparent task may not settle how people habitually remember spatial relations.|The proposed alternative is a demand-characteristic explanation. Participants' interpretation of the experiment may influence the response independently of their normal coding preference.
16|Increasing memory load and reducing transparency|**Levinson and colleagues retested Dutch students with four animals, a longer delay and greater distance between tables.** The extra item and delay increased memory demands, while the distance made the rotation manipulation less conspicuous.|Under these conditions, the preference for relative coding remained.~The result suggests that the environmental effect depends on how the task is arranged, including how much memory and strategic inference it encourages.|**A relative preference can persist despite changes in the environment when the memory task is more demanding.** This narrows the conditions under which the landmark alternative explains behavior.|Each procedural change has a purpose. More animals and a longer delay tax memory, while separation of the tables reduces the salience of the rotation.
17|Memory as part of the explanation|**A robust spatial-frame preference under memory demands does not automatically identify a purely spatial representation.** Remembering the task can recruit verbal strategies as well as spatial ones.|The relative preference is reported when memory demands are substantial and the experimenter's interest is less obvious.~That pattern makes it important to ask how the sequence is stored during the delay, not only which direction is chosen at the end.|**The involvement of memory creates another mechanism to examine.** A task can be nonverbal in its response while still allowing verbal recoding internally.|The two conditions concern memory load and task transparency. They specify when the effect is robust, rather than proving that all other explanations have been eliminated.
18|Verbal recoding of a spatial sequence|**Participants might remember the animals as a verbal list such as “lion, tiger, bear.”** Reconstructing that sequence from left to right can produce a relative-looking response.|This strategy would connect language to the task through working memory and ordering habits, rather than through an inability to represent absolute space.~The interpretation changes because the same choice can arise from different internal codes.|**A nonverbal task does not guarantee nonverbal processing.** Verbal recoding is a concrete alternative mechanism for the spatial-memory result.|The proposed list preserves order but does not explicitly preserve compass direction. Translating that order into a left-to-right display can mimic a spatial-frame preference.
19|Metaphor and abstract concepts|**Metaphorical language expresses one kind of experience using terms from another.** Saying that an argument is not “cutting” describes its effectiveness rather than a literal action with a blade.|Physical and spatial terms can therefore help express abstract relations. Time is especially useful for studying whether such linguistic habits are associated with nonlinguistic representations.~The question is empirical. Using a spatial phrase does not by itself prove that every thought about time has the same spatial format.|**An expression can be meaningful without being literal.** The study of time asks whether familiar spatial expressions also relate to cognitive processing.|The lyric is an example of metaphorical expression. It is not experimental evidence for a particular direction of causation between language and thought.
20|Spatial language for time|**English and Mandarin both use horizontal spatial language for time, but Mandarin also frequently uses vertical terms.** Earlier events can be described with an “up” term and later events with a “down” term.|The linguistic contrast motivates testing whether speakers differ in the spatial organization they use during temporal tasks.~The existence of the expressions is the starting observation. A behavioral task is needed to investigate whether the contrast extends beyond words.|**Habitual ways of talking about time can suggest testable predictions about processing.** Mandarin's vertical terms make a vertical temporal organization a relevant possibility.|The examples contrast spatial and temporal uses of the same terms. “Up” in an expression for an earlier month is a temporal use, not a claim that the month occupies a physical location.
21|Spatial priming and temporal judgments|**Boroditsky's priming task tests whether spatial processing influences subsequent time judgments.** Participants first process horizontal or vertical relations, then respond to temporal earlier–later questions.|English speakers respond faster after horizontal than vertical primes, while Mandarin speakers show the opposite pattern in the displayed comparison.~Training English speakers on vertical metaphors changes the pattern toward a vertical-prime advantage. This suggests sensitivity to experience, rather than an unchangeable division between language groups.|**The prime changes what representation is active before the temporal judgment.** Response-time differences reveal compatibility between the prime and the later task.|The vertical axis is reaction time, so a lower bar means faster processing. Compare horizontal and vertical bars within each group rather than interpreting the tallest bar as the best performance.
22|Reading direction as an alternative|**Reading and writing habits can also organize sequences spatially.** Differences attributed to temporal metaphors may covary with whether text is commonly read left-to-right, right-to-left or vertically.|Casasanto and Bottini use differences in text orientation and arrangement preferences to motivate this alternative.~The point is to identify another source of spatial experience, not to assume that a writing-system label perfectly predicts every individual's habits.|**Language-group differences can bundle linguistic and literacy experiences together.** Reading direction therefore needs to be measured or controlled.|The diagrams distinguish starting side and line direction. The table shows several arrangement patterns within the sampled groups, making individual experience relevant.
23|Controlling reported vertical reading|**Boroditsky and colleagues excluded Chinese readers who reported top-to-bottom reading.** This tests whether the vertical temporal effect can be explained entirely by that reported reading experience.|English speakers show an advantage for a left-is-earlier horizontal mapping. Mandarin speakers also show an advantage for a top-is-earlier vertical mapping in the displayed comparison.~Retaining a vertical effect under this restriction weakens that particular reading-direction explanation, without proving that every possible experiential confound is removed.|**A targeted control tests a specific alternative explanation.** Its conclusion should be limited to the experience that was actually controlled.|White bars are canonical mappings and black bars noncanonical mappings. For vertical trials, canonical means top-is-earlier. Lower reaction times indicate a mapping advantage.
24|Past–future button mappings|**Participants classify events as past or future using colored buttons.** The color-to-category mapping is reversed across blocks, so the past response is sometimes on the left and sometimes on the right.|With standard text reading, responses are faster when past maps left and future maps right.~Because the response concerns time rather than an explicit judgment of spatial direction, the compatibility effect reveals an implicit spatial association in this task.|**Changing which side corresponds to past allows a direct test of spatial–temporal compatibility.** Color alone cannot explain the pattern because the mappings are counterbalanced.|The lower black bars represent the left-to-right temporal mapping. The effect concerns response time across mappings, not a change in which events count as past or future.
25|Mirror reading changes the mapping effect|**Mirror reading tests whether short-term reading experience can alter spatial associations with time.** Participants read reversed text and perform the past–future button task.|By the second mirror-reading block, the compatibility effect reverses. Responses are faster when earlier maps right and later maps left.~The result supports a contribution of reading-direction experience and shows that the association can change over a short period.|**A flexible temporal mapping is difficult to explain as a fixed consequence of native-language grammar alone.** Recent perceptual and reading experience contributes to performance.|The first block retains the usual advantage, while the second favors the opposite mapping. Compare the two colors within each block before comparing overall reaction times between blocks.
26|Evaluating vocabulary anecdotes|**Claims about a language's vocabulary are not automatically evidence for cognitive limits.** Pullum's discussion criticizes how an account of different word roots became an exaggerated story about language and thought.|Languages can express distinctions through separate roots, derived words or phrases. Counting one of these forms without considering the others can misrepresent expressive capacity.~A reliable claim requires accurate linguistic description and an appropriate behavioral test, rather than repetition of a striking anecdote.|**Different lexical packaging does not show that one group cannot understand another group's distinctions.** The linguistic facts and the cognitive inference both need scrutiny.|The excerpt distinguishes roots from derivationally related forms and phrases. That distinction is central to why simple vocabulary counts can be misleading.
