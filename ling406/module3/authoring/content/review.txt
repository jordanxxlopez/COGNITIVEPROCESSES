28|The classical aphasia contrast|**The classical contrast associates Wernicke's aphasia with fluent but often poorly interpretable speech and Broca's aphasia with effortful, telegraphic speech.** The former can include neologisms, while the latter can omit grammatical particles and show word-order difficulty.|This makes a lexical-versus-grammatical interpretation attractive. However, a descriptive syndrome is not itself a demonstration of an isolated processing module.~Content words may be relatively preserved in telegraphic speech, while fluent speech can retain sentence-like structure without conveying a clear message.|**Fluency, grammatical organization and meaningful word selection are distinguishable dimensions.** The two example profiles emphasize different disruptions.|The quoted utterances contrast fluent but difficult-to-interpret output with short, effortful content-word sequences. The diagnostic terms summarize patterns rather than guaranteeing identical symptoms in every patient.
29|Historical Broca cases and modern classification|**Broca's original patients are not necessarily straightforward examples of what is now called Broca's aphasia.** Profound inability to articulate speech can reflect motor-planning impairment rather than a selective grammatical deficit.|**Apraxia of speech** concerns planning or coordinating speech movements. That differs from explaining an omission of grammatical structure in otherwise producible speech.~The historical label therefore should not substitute for a precise description of the impaired behavior.|**A familiar syndrome name does not settle which level of processing is affected.** Articulation and grammatical formulation must be distinguished.|The case description emphasizes extremely restricted spoken output despite some preserved understanding. It motivates examining speech production at multiple levels rather than locating grammar from the name alone.
30|Grammar, lexicon and aphasia|**A grammar–lexicon distinction makes the classical aphasia contrast theoretically appealing.** A grammatical system combines forms, while lexical knowledge concerns words and their properties.|Chomsky's example of a grammatical but semantically unusual sentence shows that syntactic well-formedness cannot be reduced to whether a sentence describes a sensible event.~That conceptual distinction does not establish a simple one-to-one mapping from each syndrome to one isolated component.|**Syntax and meaning can come apart in a judgment without proving a perfectly modular neural organization.** The behavioral and theoretical claims should be kept separate.|“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously” contrasts with an order that violates English syntax. The example isolates grammatical organization from familiarity or ordinary plausibility.
31|Levels of speech production|**The functional level selects word meanings and semantic roles, while the positional level organizes word forms into a grammatical sequence.** Phonological encoding and articulation concern later aspects of producing the utterance.|A **lemma** is a representation involved in lexical selection, distinct from simply executing its sound pattern. Positional processing contributes ordering and morphological structure.~Combinations such as HOUSEBOAT show that complex-word meaning can be partly compositional. Errors such as “easy enoughly” provide evidence about the organization of morphological operations.|**Word selection, ordering, sound encoding and articulation are different explanatory levels.** An apparent lexical–grammatical contrast should be related to those operations carefully.|The diagram runs from message-level conceptual preparation through functional and positional levels to phonological encoding and speech gestures. Its order represents a processing proposal, not a direct picture of separate brain locations.
43|Exercise and maturational-state hypotheses|**The exercise hypothesis proposes that early language use preserves language-learning capacity, while the maturational-state hypothesis allows capacity to decline despite early use.** Both assume an early advantage, but they differ in what early successful experience protects.|Later second-language difficulty in people who already acquired a first language is more directly compatible with maturation than with complete preservation through exercise.~This does not make early first-language experience unimportant. Early access and age-related change address related but distinct questions.|**A late L2 learner has already exercised language capacity.** That history is what makes late-L2 outcomes informative for distinguishing the two proposals.|The two text panels differ in the condition attached to decline. The exercise account includes preservation through use, while the maturational-state account predicts decline with maturation itself.
44|Early language access and later learning|**Mayberry, Lock and Kazmi compared later language learning after different early-language histories.** Deaf individuals without accessible early language performed worse than those who had acquired an early spoken or signed language.|Children first exposed to ASL at ages 9–15 had particular difficulty when it was also their first language. Those who had first learned a spoken language did much better, and the differences persisted.~The comparison distinguishes late first-language learning from late acquisition of an additional language.|**Age of starting ASL is not the same as age of first language access.** Earlier linguistic experience can support learning a new language later.|The bars separate no-early-language groups from early spoken or signed language groups. The contrast concerns access to language, not a simple superiority of speech over signing.
45|Controlling visual similarity in blocking|**Semantic blocking effects must be separated from visual-recognition difficulty.** Objects from one semantic category could resemble one another visually, creating a potential confound in naming.|The materials were normed so that same-category items were not more visually similar on average. A separate visual-similarity manipulation also produced a different time course.~Visual blocking affected the first block, whereas the semantic pattern of interest developed differently. This weakens a purely visual explanation.|**A control is informative when it tests the alternative mechanism directly.** Matching appearance and manipulating visual similarity provide complementary evidence.|The comparison concerns when interference appears across repetitions. A difference in time course helps distinguish visual recognition from the proposed lexical contribution.
46|Simultaneous displays and serial naming|**Belke and colleagues presented four objects simultaneously but required their names in sequence.** This tests whether the absence of an effect in another simultaneous-display study depended on presentation format.|Displaying all objects at once reduces dependence on a succession of isolated pictures. Serial naming still requires selecting and producing one label after another.~Eye tracking provides additional timing information about the processing of each object.|**Separating display timing from naming order helps locate the source of interference.** Simultaneous visibility does not remove the need for sequential lexical selection.|The four pictured animals are visible together. The instruction concerns producing their names in order, rather than choosing only one target from the display.
47|Gaze duration and eye–speech lag|**Eye movements and speech onsets provide different timing measures during serial naming.** Gaze duration records how long the eyes remain on an object, while eye–speech lag concerns the interval between leaving it and beginning its spoken name.|A participant can move the eyes toward the next object before articulating the current one. The measures therefore separate overlapping parts of preparation.~Different blocking effects on these measures can help constrain the processing stage affected.|**One total naming time can conceal several processes.** Coordinating eye and speech measures reveals where a condition begins to change performance.|The GD boxes represent gaze durations and the lag boxes extend toward articulatory onset. The timeline shows why looking at an object and speaking its name are not the same event.
48|Where the blocking effect appears|**Semantic blocking increases gaze duration after the first presentation cycle, but not overall speech duration or eye–speech lag.** The measures therefore do not show a uniform slowdown.|Speech duration decreases with repetition without a blocking difference. Gaze duration shows the condition difference after the first cycle.~The nonsignificant lag difference should not be reported as evidence for a reversed effect. The reported pattern is interpreted as supporting a contribution at lemma selection rather than articulation or phonological encoding.|**The location of the effect across measures matters more than a general statement that naming is slower.** Its time course and selectivity constrain the proposed mechanism.|HOM and HET compare homogeneous and heterogeneous contexts. The three panels correspond to speech duration, gaze duration and lag, so their vertical scales describe different measurements.
49|Thresholded or cascaded speech processing?|**McMurray and colleagues tested whether fine phonetic information affects lexical competition.** Participants heard a /b/–/p/ continuum while viewing pictures including BEAR and PEAR, with eye movements recorded.|**Voice onset time**, or VOT, varies the timing between release and voicing. It can change how strongly the sound supports /b/ or /p/.~A thresholded account would pass a completed categorical decision onward. A cascaded account allows graded information to influence word candidates before uncertainty is fully resolved.|**The critical test is whether within-category differences influence lexical responses.** A binary final choice alone may hide that information.|The visual-world display includes a target, a phonetic competitor and unrelated fillers. Looking at the competitor is informative because its name overlaps with the heard word near the manipulated contrast.
50|Categorical mouse choices|**Participants selected the picture that best matched the spoken item.** Their BEAR–PEAR choices produced a typical step-like identification function across VOT.|Shorter VOTs favored the /b/ response and longer VOTs the /p/ response. Near the boundary, the preferred category changed rapidly.~A categorical-looking final response does not establish that all earlier lexical activation was categorical.|**A forced choice can end in one category even when processing used graded evidence.** Eye movements provide a separate measure of that processing.|The horizontal axis is VOT in milliseconds and the vertical axis is proportion of /p/ responses. The steep transition is around 17.5 ms in the displayed function.
51|Graded lexical competition|**Competitor looks vary with phonetic ambiguity even within a chosen category.** Tokens closer to the /b/–/p/ boundary produce more competition than clear tokens farther from it.|A participant can ultimately select BEAR while spending more time looking at PEAR when the sound is close to the boundary.~This supports the claim that fine phonetic information remains relevant to lexical activation, rather than disappearing completely after a categorical phoneme decision.|**Final identification and moment-by-moment competition reveal different aspects of processing.** A sharp choice boundary can coexist with graded lexical uncertainty.|The gaze curves group trials by VOT and final response. The effect concerns looks to the unchosen competitor, not merely how often participants click the wrong picture.
52|Distance from the boundary and time|**Competitor fixation increases as a token approaches the category boundary from either side.** For BEAR responses, more /p/-like VOT increases looks to PEAR. For PEAR responses, more /b/-like VOT increases looks to BEAR.|The graph separates early and late observation windows. The gradient remains visible in the later competition measure, supporting continued sensitivity to within-category phonetic detail.~The result favors cascaded use of graded evidence over a model that transmits only a completed phoneme label with all within-category detail discarded.|**The relevant predictor is distance from the boundary, not only which side of it the token occupies.** Similar final choices can still have different lexical-competition profiles.|The left and right halves plot different final-response categories and their corresponding competitors. Early is 300–1100 ms and late is 1100–1900 ms, so the line types distinguish time windows rather than two participant groups.
