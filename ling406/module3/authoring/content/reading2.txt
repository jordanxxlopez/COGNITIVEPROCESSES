2|Why propose two reading routes?|**Dual-route models distinguish a lexical route from a sublexical route.** Lexical processing uses knowledge of particular words, while grapheme–phoneme conversion generates pronunciation from spelling units.|A model must explain fast pseudoword reading, faster familiar-word reading, frequency effects, regularity effects and their interaction.~It must also explain why pseudoword and exception-word reading can be differently impaired after brain injury. These observations motivate the architecture but do not uniquely prove it.|**The two-route proposal connects several reading findings to a common processing account.** Its success depends on explaining the full pattern, not only nonword reading.|A grapheme is a written unit associated with a sound. The route labels specify kinds of information used during reading.
3|The Dual Route Cascaded model|**The Dual Route Cascaded model, or DRC, combines interactive lexical processing with a separate grapheme–phoneme rule system.** The two routes can contribute activation toward a spoken response.|The lexical portion includes orthographic and phonological lexicons, representing written and spoken word forms. These representations are distinguished from meanings.~The implementation described here does not implement semantics. The GPC rule system also has processing assumptions different from those of the interactive lexical network.|**The DRC does not treat every connection as the same kind of computation.** Word-form activation and rule-based conversion are separate mechanisms.|The circled part of the diagram corresponds to interactive activation. The separate GPC path joins the output system rather than becoming another interactive lexical layer.
4|How GPC processing works|**The DRC's grapheme–phoneme conversion rules operate serially, letter by letter.** Their operation is not adjusted by word frequency and receives no lexical feedback.|The GPC and lexical routes do not interact throughout their internal computations. They converge in the phoneme response buffer.~If the routes favor different phonemes, they can compete at that shared output. An exception word can therefore receive a correct lexical pronunciation and an incompatible regularized pronunciation.|**Encapsulation does not mean that the routes never affect the same response.** They remain separate internally but meet at the phoneme output.|Follow the route to the response buffer. Shared output units explain how competing pronunciations can influence naming even without feedback into the GPC rules.
5|Rules and rules for applying rules|**GPC rules map written units onto phonemes.** Some rules concern individual letters, while others concern several letters or a particular letter context.|A model also needs a way to decide which applicable rule takes priority. A larger-unit rule can supersede rules for its individual letters.~Context-sensitive rules restrict a pronunciation to specified surrounding letters or positions. This makes the procedure more than a simple lookup of one sound per letter.|**A rule list is incomplete without a procedure for resolving overlapping rules.** Both the mapping and its conditions of application matter.|The table distinguishes multiletter rules from context-sensitive rules. Position labels specify whether a mapping applies at the beginning, middle, end or all positions.
6|Categorical rules and statistical support|**A categorical rule does not express the strength of the evidence behind it.** The DRC can choose a mapping because it is frequent, yet treat that mapping as a fixed rule during processing.|The possible pronunciations of OUGH have more divided statistical support than those of EIGH. A rule can still operate categorically for both spellings.~A probabilistic account instead allows alternatives to receive different strengths of support. The distinction concerns processing, not whether the model designer ever consulted frequency information.|**Using statistics to select a rule differs from representing graded statistics while reading.** The two approaches can make different predictions for ambiguous spellings.|The table distributes pronunciation frequencies across alternatives. A spread across many rows indicates greater uncertainty than a distribution concentrated on one pronunciation.
7|Why imperfect rules were defended|**The DRC's simple rules are intended to explain systematic human errors as well as correct reading.** More finely tuned rules might pronounce more exceptions correctly but remove behavior the model is meant to explain.|A regularizing route helps account for errors in surface dyslexia and for slow naming of low-frequency exception words.~The theory therefore claims that people's sublexical computation is constrained, rather than assuming that the best possible spelling algorithm is the correct cognitive model.|**A cognitively useful model should reproduce the relevant failures.** Maximizing pronunciation accuracy is not the only goal.|The crucial test is whether the same processing assumptions produce the observed pattern of success, interference and regularization, rather than an isolated incorrect response.
8|Informational encapsulation|**Informational encapsulation means that a process does not freely use every source of relevant knowledge.** In the dual-route account, GPC conversion proceeds without consulting whole-word meaning or lexical feedback.|This resembles a modular parsing proposal in which an early syntactic process follows structural principles without immediately using world knowledge.~Pseudoword naming exposes the conversion procedure because a familiar lexical entry is unavailable. Regularization errors expose it when conversion favors the wrong pronunciation.|**A modular account predicts restricted access to information.** The GPC route may produce an inappropriate response even when another part of the system has word-specific knowledge.|The comparison with parsing concerns processing restrictions. It does not mean that syntactic parsing and letter–sound conversion use the same representations.
9|Breadth of DRC simulations|**The DRC is evaluated against many reading phenomena.** These include frequency, lexicality, regularity, interactions, lexical decision and patterns in acquired dyslexia.|Accounting for several effects with one architecture is stronger than fitting a single result. It also requires examining which assumptions stay fixed and which are changed between tasks.~The model's research strategy places strong weight on human behavioral evidence as a constraint on architecture.|**A broad list of successful simulations is informative, but the conditions of each success also matter.** Model evaluation includes both coverage and the assumptions used to obtain it.|The listed effects span reading aloud, lexical decision and patient performance. These are related but distinct tasks, so a task-specific adjustment must be identified as such.
10|Modeling lexical decision|**Lexical decision requires deciding whether a written item is a word.** The DRC simulation adds decision criteria to the activation produced by its recognition system.|A “yes” response can depend on a strongly activated single word or on total activity across the orthographic lexicon. A “no” response occurs if the criteria are not met by a deadline.~The implementation also changes inhibition and uses time-dependent thresholds. These choices help obtain a fit, but they add assumptions beyond the basic two-route diagram.|**Fitting lexical decision requires an account of the decision itself.** Recognition activity does not automatically specify when to say yes or no.|The thresholds and cycle deadlines implement response rules. Their conceptual role is more important here than memorizing each numerical cutoff.
11|Modularity in the DRC|**The DRC routes are modular in several related senses.** They use different processing assumptions, restrict information exchange and accept particular kinds of input.|The GPC system takes letters as input and sends phoneme activation toward the response system. This is **domain specificity** in the account.~The absence of lexical feedback into the GPC system is **informational encapsulation**. Having two pathways alone would not establish those stronger restrictions.|**Modularity is a claim about how components operate and communicate.** It is more specific than drawing two arrows in a model.|Distinguish the restrictions on inputs, the rules of computation and the restrictions on communication. These are separate properties of the proposed architecture.
12|An emergentist reading model|**PDP models treat reading as learned mappings among orthography, phonology and semantics.** Orthography is spelling, phonology is sound structure, and semantics is meaning.|Seidenberg and McClelland trained an associative network to map spelling onto pronunciation. The modeled spelling-to-sound pathway used one learned mapping for different kinds of words.~Reading skill emerges from learning rather than being specified entirely as a hand-built list of rules and lexical entries.|**An emergentist account asks how experience creates useful reading behavior.** Regularity can arise from learning across words rather than from a separate rule mechanism.|The triangle's labels identify representational domains. A connection between domains represents a learned mapping, not an additional stored dictionary for every item.
13|Distributed knowledge and general learning|**In a PDP account, rules are generalizations learned across words.** Whole-word knowledge is distributed across learned mappings instead of being stored only in a distinct lexical route.|The same learning mechanism can capture both patterns shared by many words and information specific to a familiar word.~The research strategy asks whether domain-general learning can explain reading. This contrasts with choosing separate adult processing components primarily from neuropsychological dissociations.|**Word-specific knowledge and generalization can coexist in the same learned system.** They do not have to be implemented as a dictionary and a separate symbolic rule list.|“Distributed” refers to how knowledge is represented across the network. It does not mean that the model has no memory of individual words.
14|Learning the frequency–regularity interaction|**A learned network can produce a frequency-by-regularity interaction without separate frequency and rule mechanisms.** Regular words receive support from patterns shared across many words.|An exception conflicts with those patterns, so learning its particular mapping is especially important. Frequent exposure strengthens that word-specific mapping.~A low-frequency exception has both weak item-specific learning and poor support from the dominant pattern. This combination creates a larger disadvantage than either factor alone.|**Consistency supplies support from other words, while frequency strengthens experience with the particular word.** Their joint influence explains why infrequent exceptions are especially difficult.|The interaction is shared evidence that both architectures must explain. Producing it does not by itself establish whether the implementation contains symbolic rules.
15|Consistency rather than a single rule boundary|**Consistency describes how many similar spellings support a pronunciation.** “Friends” have the relevant spelling pattern and a compatible pronunciation, while “enemies” support a competing pronunciation.|LATE and DUST illustrate consistent patterns. GAVE and MINT have many friends but also enemies such as HAVE and PINT.~DOWN versus KNOWN and POUR versus SOUR illustrate ambiguity. An exception such as PINT has relatively little support compared with its competing neighborhood.|**Spelling–sound support varies in degree.** A statistical description captures differences that a simple regular-versus-irregular label can hide.|Friend and enemy are labels for pronunciation relations among similar spellings. They do not refer to semantic relationships between the words.
16|Using learned weights for nonwords|**A PDP model can pronounce nonwords using the weights learned from real words.** A new spelling activates patterns that overlap with familiar spellings, allowing the network to generalize.|No stored entry for the particular nonword is required. The same learned correspondences that support consistent words can support a new combination.~This is a proposed mechanism for decoding without a separate GPC rule module.|**Novel pronunciation can result from applying learned statistical structure.** Nonword reading therefore does not automatically establish a separate symbolic route.|The relevant comparison is whether one learned mapping can handle both familiar words and new items. The claim must still be checked against the model's actual performance.
17|Theory versus implementation|**Poor nonword performance can challenge either a theoretical proposal or a particular implementation.** The early model's nonword accuracy was weaker than desired.|One possibility is that a single associative mechanism cannot explain the task. Another is that the chosen representations fail to capture the similarities needed for generalization.~Improving the implementation while retaining the theoretical mechanism helps distinguish these possibilities.|**A failed simulation does not identify its own cause.** The failure must be traced to the architecture, learning assumptions, representations or other implementation choices.|The important question is what changes when the model is repaired. A representational improvement is a different theoretical move from adding a separate nonword route.
18|Phonological representation matters|**Generalization depends on how sound similarity is represented.** The early Seidenberg–McClelland model used Wickelfeatures, whose representation of phonology was problematic for nonword reading.|Plaut and colleagues improved the phonological representations and obtained better nonword performance. This supports a representational explanation for at least part of the earlier failure.~Behavioral relations between phonological processing and decoding also make phonology a relevant target for explanation.|**Changing the representation can improve decoding without adding a symbolic rule system.** That distinction matters when evaluating the original theoretical claim.|The comparison concerns models with different representations. Better performance after that change is evidence about implementation, not proof that every associative model will succeed.
19|Why degraded phonology hurts generalization|**A network generalizes through similarity.** If its phonological representation poorly captures similarities among words, the learned mapping cannot transfer as effectively to new forms.|A familiar word can retain support from its own training history. A nonword lacks that item-specific experience and depends more heavily on generalization.~This produces a reason for unfamiliar forms to be especially vulnerable to poor representations without requiring a dedicated nonword mechanism.|**The same representational weakness can have unequal effects on words and nonwords.** Familiarity can partly compensate where new-item generalization cannot.|Distinguish a weaker shared representation from damage to a separate route. Both can create a task difference, but they locate the explanation differently.
20|Harm and Seidenberg's noise manipulation|**Harm and Seidenberg manipulated noise in phonological representations.** Increasing noise makes the sound representations less reliable while keeping the model's general learning framework.|The manipulation produced poorer nonword decoding and relative sparing of word recognition. It also linked the severity of the phonological deficit to the degree of decoding difficulty.~The model therefore connects a reading pattern to the quality of a more general phonological system.|**A selective-looking reading deficit can emerge from degraded shared representations.** Nonwords are especially dependent on reliable generalization.|Noise is the manipulated model property. Word and nonword performance are outcomes, so the simulation tests a directional mechanistic proposal rather than merely observing a correlation.
21|Semantics and surface dyslexia|**Damaging semantic support in a triangle model can impair exception-word reading especially strongly.** Plaut and colleagues added a simplified semantic contribution and examined what happened when it was lesioned.|An exception has weak support from typical spelling–sound regularities. Meaning-related support can help produce its learned pronunciation.~Reducing that support creates a surface-dyslexia-like pattern without defining a separate exception-word list as the sole explanation.|**The model links exception reading to semantic processing.** A reading dissociation can reflect unequal dependence on broader language systems.|“Lesion” here means a controlled change to the computational model. It is distinct from directly observing where a particular patient's brain injury occurred.
22|Why the triangle is not just DRC renamed|**The triangle model's pathways share graded learning assumptions and interact during learning.** Semantic representations are directly involved rather than replaced by a lexicon defined as independent of meaning.|Spelling-to-sound learning develops in the presence of meaning-related support. The contribution of one pathway therefore affects what another pathway learns.~This allows graded impairments. Phonological dyslexics can make some exception errors, and surface dyslexics can make some nonword errors.|**Multiple pathways do not necessarily imply modular routes.** Their learning rules, information types and interactions distinguish the accounts.|O denotes orthography, P phonology and S semantics. O-to-P and S-to-P mappings use the same general probabilistic assumptions, unlike DRC's distinct GPC and lexical procedures.
23|Semantics and exception reading in patients|**Woollams and colleagues tested the predicted association between semantic ability and exception-word reading.** Lower semantic scores were associated with poorer low-frequency exception reading in patients with semantic dementia.|The model and patient panels show comparable overall relationships, although individual patients vary. This supports an association between meaning and exceptional pronunciation.~The finding is informative because it links a reading weakness to a broader semantic measure rather than describing only a reading-task dissociation.|**The triangle account predicts that exception reading should vary with semantic ability.** The patient data broadly follow that relationship.|The semantic score decreases from left to right on the patient plot. The downward slope therefore means that poorer semantics goes with poorer reading, not that better semantics harms reading.
24|Interpreting apparent exceptions|**Some patients have relatively good exception reading despite low semantic scores.** These cases challenge a perfect one-to-one relationship, but an account of graded association does not predict zero variability.|Woollams and colleagues interpret such observations in relation to the full distribution rather than treating each outlier as an automatic refutation.~The scientific issue is whether the unusual cases show a stable separate pattern or arise within a noisy overall association.|**An outlier and a clean dissociation are not automatically the same thing.** The strength and reliability of the apparent exception matter.|Points above the horizontal reference line and left of the specified vertical boundary are singled out as unusual. Evaluate their location relative to the broader patient cloud and repeated measurements.
25|Following semantic dementia over time|**Longitudinal observations track whether semantic and reading abilities change together within a patient.** Because semantic dementia progresses, repeated testing can reveal a trajectory rather than a single unusual score.|In most cases, declining semantic ability accompanies declining exception-word reading. Some initially unusual observations move closer to the broader relation on retesting.~This strengthens the association while leaving room for variability between individuals.|**Repeated observations help test whether an apparent dissociation persists.** A one-time exceptional score is less compelling if the patient's later trajectory follows the general relation.|Lines connect observations from the same person. Movement toward lower semantic scores and lower reading accuracy shows coupled decline rather than a comparison of unrelated people only.
26|Fitting the same patients with DRC|**Coltheart and colleagues generated 40,000 model patients with different lexical-route and GPC impairment levels.** For each real patient, they selected the model run that best matched the observed data.|This produced an excellent fit to the patient scores. The selection procedure is part of how that fit was achieved.~The fit should be distinguished from predicting each patient's pattern before choosing among many possible parameter combinations.|**A flexible model can closely reproduce observed data.** Evaluating the explanation also requires asking how the matching simulation was selected.|The plotted patient and model lines lie close together. That visual agreement describes goodness of fit, while the large search over simulated patients describes flexibility.
27|Fit and explanatory constraint|**A better fit can be less informative if it relies on much greater flexibility.** Selecting the best match from many parameter combinations can reproduce a dataset without strongly constraining the underlying explanation.|The question is not whether close agreement is bad in itself. It is whether the account predicts the relation with principled assumptions or accommodates each case after seeing the outcome.~Comparing theories therefore requires attention to both fit and how much freedom produced it.|**Goodness of fit is only one part of model evaluation.** A model should explain why the pattern occurs, not only contain a setting that resembles it.|The contrast is between constrained prediction and flexible post hoc matching. It does not justify rejecting a model merely because its numerical fit is strong.
28|Shared targets for competing models|**Dual-route and triangle models address many of the same findings.** Both must explain rapid nonword decoding, exception difficulty, the frequency–regularity interaction and acquired reading dissociations.|Agreement on the phenomena does not mean agreement on mechanisms. One account emphasizes specialized routes, while the other emphasizes learning across interacting representational systems.|**The models compete over explanation, not over whether these basic reading patterns exist.** Shared behavioral success makes their differing assumptions especially important.|A useful comparison holds the phenomenon constant and asks what each model changes or computes to produce it.
29|Comparing explanations across models|**DRC attributes nonword decoding and regularity effects to GPC rules, while the triangle account attributes them to statistical learning.** Word-specific exposure strengthens learned mappings in the triangle account and affects lexical processing in DRC.|For phonological dyslexia, DRC emphasizes GPC damage and the triangle account emphasizes broader phonological impairment.~For surface dyslexia, DRC emphasizes lexical-route damage and the triangle account emphasizes semantic impairment.|**The same task pattern receives a different mechanistic explanation in each model.** Keep the observed phenomenon separate from the proposed locus of impairment.|Read each table row across the two model columns. The row identifies the finding, while the columns specify competing explanations rather than two names for identical components.
30|Dissociations and associations|**A major contrast is whether the explanation begins with separate reading routes or broader language associations.** Dual-route models use different task impairments to motivate independent processes.|The statistical-learning account links phonology to spelling–sound generalization and semantics to words whose pronunciation is poorly supported by that generalization.~Both dissociations and associations matter. A theory should explain selective weaknesses and the related abilities that vary with them.|**Phonological and semantic measures can help identify what lies behind a reading dissociation.** They extend the evidence beyond the reading scores alone.|The table's dyslexia rows contrast GPC versus phonological damage and lexical-route versus semantic damage. These are the key theoretical alternatives, not just different diagnostic labels.
