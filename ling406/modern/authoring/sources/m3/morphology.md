## Page 8 · Properties of the exception system
Slide image: ling406/module3/assets/morphology/008.webp

### Original slide text
Similar architecture, different origins
Rather than make the grammar inelegant by adding machinery to handle the exceptions, linguists would often just leave the exceptions out of the grammar.

So dual-process model proposes a way to “handle” the exceptions while keeping the grammar elegant, symbolic, categorical, etc.

This second process had all the properties the rule route did not. It was:

## Page 10 · U-shaped learning
Slide image: ling406/module3/assets/morphology/010.webp

### Original slide text
Target phenomena of Rumelhart & McClelland
Because this was one of the first learning models to address a language process, the focus was on capturing developmental phenomena.

Specifically: “U-shaped” learning:

	- some frequent irregular verbs are learned quite early, e.g., run-ran

	- then, spontaneously, children begin producing “regularizations:” runned.

	- eventually, they start producing correct forms again.

Learning is “U-shaped” because children often get worse before they get better, so performance goes down before it goes back up.

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **U-shaped learning describes an initial success, a decline, and later recovery.** A child may correctly say RAN, later produce RUNNED, and eventually return to RAN. The middle error is a **regularization**, because the child applies the common past-tense pattern to an irregular verb.
  - The decline does not necessarily mean that the child has stopped learning. A changing system can generalize more broadly while temporarily disrupting an earlier correct response.
- **Development can involve temporary errors as knowledge changes.** A theory must explain the entire trajectory, not just the final adult response.
  - The vertical dimension is successful performance and the horizontal dimension is development or training. A U shape concerns accuracy over time, not the shape of a word.

## Page 11 · Words and Rules interpretation of development
Slide image: ling406/module3/assets/morphology/011.webp

### Original slide text
Why is U-shaped learning important to the “words and rules” approach? 
It’s understood in terms of distinct stages/processes:

	- early correct forms are just imitations, don’t depend on the same mechanisms as adult processing.

	- when the rule is initially learned, it is applied to all cases. We can think of the rule as the “best hypothesis” for all items generally.

	- over time, exceptions are learned associatively, one-by-one and incorporated into lexical knowledge. Further, the “blocking process” develops.

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **The Words and Rules account explains U-shaped learning through changes in the use of rules and lexical exceptions.** Early correct forms can be imitated before a productive rule is available. When the rule is learned, it can be applied too broadly, yielding RUNNED. Later, learned irregulars and the blocking process prevent that regularization.
  - This account treats the developmental stages as involving distinguishable processes rather than only gradual changes in one mapping.
- **Overregularization is interpreted as productive rule use that is not yet sufficiently constrained by exceptions.** Recovery depends on the irregular form winning over the rule output.
  - Blocking means that retrieval of the appropriate irregular prevents the otherwise available regular form. It is a proposed mechanism, not merely another name for correct performance.
