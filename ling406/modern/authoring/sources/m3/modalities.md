Slide image: ling406/module3/assets/modalities/002.webp

Note
Sign language and Braille are really different cases.
Sign languages, as we’ll see, are natural languages that have their own linguistic 
structure.
Braille is a system that allows blind individuals to read a spoken language they share with 
sighted individuals.
I’ve lumped them together because they reveal interesting things about the plasticity of

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **A signed language is a natural language, while Braille is a writing system.** Signed languages have their own linguistic structure. Braille allows a language to be read through touch. The distinction matters because changing the modality of a language is not the same as changing how a written language is accessed.
  - Both cases reveal how sensory experience shapes the organization of systems involved in language and reading.
- **Do not treat Braille as a signed language or signing as a tactile alphabet.** They provide different tests of language and neural plasticity.
  - The shared theme is modality, but the linguistic status of the two cases differs. A comparison should preserve that distinction before discussing their brain responses.

### Original slide text
Cross-modal plasticity and language
An important point is the resilience of language circuitry despite differences in modality. 
- Classical language areas are activated by sign language, although the input is 
dramatically different in its surface form.
- Braille reading seems to engage networks that are important for processing text.
We can think of cross-modal plasticity as demonstrating that brain organization is shaped 
over the course of development by competition.
For example, when auditory input is unavailable, putative auditory areas are used to 
process sign, and visual attention is enhanced at the periphery.
Recall that the competing cognitive/perceptual processes shaping language processing 
were also important when we considered the Competition Model as relevant to second 
language learning.

  - The evidence does not imply that all regions are interchangeable. It shows both stable functional relationships and experience-sensitive recruitment.
