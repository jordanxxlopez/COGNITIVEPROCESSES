## Page 2 · Linguistic relativity
Slide image: ling406/module3/assets/thought/002.webp

### Original slide text
“No two languages are ever sufficiently similar to be considered as representing the same 
social reality. The worlds in which different societies live are distinct worlds, not merely 
the same world with different labels attached.”
Sapir (1929)
Linguistic Relativity, a.k.a. The “Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis”
“We dissect nature along lines laid down by our native language. The categories and 
types that we isolate from the world of phenomena we do not find there because they 
stare every observer in the face; on the contrary, the world is presented in a 
kaleidoscope flux of impressions which has to be organized by our minds—and this 
means largely by the linguistic systems of our minds. We cut nature up, organize it into 
concepts, and ascribe significances as we do, largely because we are parties to an 
agreement to organize it in this way—an agreement that holds throughout our speech 
community and is codified in the patterns of our language [...] all observers are not led 
by the same physical evidence to the same picture of the universe, unless their linguistic 
backgrounds are similar, or can in some way be calibrated.”
Whorf (1956)
(The Wikipedia page on this is actually pretty useful, BTW)

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Linguistic relativity concerns whether differences in language influence how people organize or use concepts.** The Sapir and Whorf quotations suggest that linguistic categories can shape how experience is interpreted. The strongest reading would make language determine what a person can think. A weaker claim is that habitual language use biases attention, memory or preferred ways of representing a situation.
  - These claims require different evidence. A preference associated with language does not establish that alternative thought is impossible.
- **Influencing thought is different from completely determining thought.** Keep that distinction explicit when interpreting a language-group difference.
  - The quotations express broad theoretical ideas. They are not experimental demonstrations that two speakers are unable to understand the same physical event.

Slide image: ling406/module3/assets/thought/020.webp

### Original slide text
Spatial metaphors for time in different languages
(Boroditsky et al., 2010)

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **English and Mandarin both use horizontal spatial language for time, but Mandarin also frequently uses vertical terms.** Earlier events can be described with an “up” term and later events with a “down” term. The linguistic contrast motivates testing whether speakers differ in the spatial organization they use during temporal tasks.
  - The existence of the expressions is the starting observation. A behavioral task is needed to investigate whether the contrast extends beyond words.
- **Habitual ways of talking about time can suggest testable predictions about processing.** Mandarin's vertical terms make a vertical temporal organization a relevant possibility.
  - The examples contrast spatial and temporal uses of the same terms. “Up” in an expression for an earlier month is a temporal use, not a claim that the month occupies a physical location.

## Page 21 · Spatial priming and temporal judgments
Slide image: ling406/module3/assets/thought/021.webp

### Original slide text
Boroditsky, 2001)
A priming experiment
Priming with a spatial task (horizontal or vertical) has different effects on temporal 
processing in the two languages.
Short-term training on vertical metaphors for time (“Cars were invented above fax 
machines”) also has an impact.

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Boroditsky's priming task tests whether spatial processing influences subsequent time judgments.** Participants first process horizontal or vertical relations, then respond to temporal earlier–later questions. English speakers respond faster after horizontal than vertical primes, while Mandarin speakers show the opposite pattern in the displayed comparison.
  - Training English speakers on vertical metaphors changes the pattern toward a vertical-prime advantage. This suggests sensitivity to experience, rather than an unchangeable division between language groups.
- **The prime changes what representation is active before the temporal judgment.** Response-time differences reveal compatibility between the prime and the later task.
  - The vertical axis is reaction time, so a lower bar means faster processing. Compare horizontal and vertical bars within each group rather than interpreting the tallest bar as the best performance.

Slide image: ling406/module3/assets/thought/022.webp

### Original slide text
Casasanto & Bottini (2005, Cognitive Science Proceedings)
An alternative explanation: direction of text…

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Reading and writing habits can also organize sequences spatially.** Differences attributed to temporal metaphors may covary with whether text is commonly read left-to-right, right-to-left or vertically. Casasanto and Bottini use differences in text orientation and arrangement preferences to motivate this alternative.
  - The point is to identify another source of spatial experience, not to assume that a writing-system label perfectly predicts every individual's habits.
- **Language-group differences can bundle linguistic and literacy experiences together.** Reading direction therefore needs to be measured or controlled.
  - The diagrams distinguish starting side and line direction. The table shows several arrangement patterns within the sampled groups, making individual experience relevant.

## Page 23 · Controlling reported vertical reading
Slide image: ling406/module3/assets/thought/023.webp

### Original slide text
Boroditsky et al. (2010, Cognition)
Excluding Chinese readers who reported 
reading top-to-bottom. 
…so Boroditsky et al. controlled for that…

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Boroditsky and colleagues excluded Chinese readers who reported top-to-bottom reading.** This tests whether the vertical temporal effect can be explained entirely by that reported reading experience. English speakers show an advantage for a left-is-earlier horizontal mapping. Mandarin speakers also show an advantage for a top-is-earlier vertical mapping in the displayed comparison.
  - Retaining a vertical effect under this restriction weakens that particular reading-direction explanation, without proving that every possible experiential confound is removed.
- **A targeted control tests a specific alternative explanation.** Its conclusion should be limited to the experience that was actually controlled.
  - White bars are canonical mappings and black bars noncanonical mappings. For vertical trials, canonical means top-is-earlier. Lower reaction times indicate a mapping advantage.

## Page 24 · Past–future button mappings
Slide image: ling406/module3/assets/thought/024.webp

### Original slide text
Casasanto & Bottini (2010, Spatial Cognition)
Task: Push a blue button for events in the past, a red button for events in the future 
(or the reverse, in different blocks). 
Thus, half the time, the “past” is on the left, and the other half it’s on the right.

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Participants classify events as past or future using colored buttons.** The color-to-category mapping is reversed across blocks, so the past response is sometimes on the left and sometimes on the right. With standard text reading, responses are faster when past maps left and future maps right.
  - Because the response concerns time rather than an explicit judgment of spatial direction, the compatibility effect reveals an implicit spatial association in this task.
- **Changing which side corresponds to past allows a direct test of spatial–temporal compatibility.** Color alone cannot explain the pattern because the mappings are counterbalanced.
  - The lower black bars represent the left-to-right temporal mapping. The effect concerns response time across mappings, not a change in which events count as past or future.

## Page 25 · Mirror reading changes the mapping effect
Slide image: ling406/module3/assets/thought/025.webp

### Original slide text
Casasanto & Bottini (2010, Spatial Cognition)
Task: Push a blue button for events in the past, a red button for events in the future 
(or the reverse, in different blocks). 
Thus, half the time, the “past” is on the left, and the other half it’s on the right. 
Now ask people to do a few minutes of 
“mirror reading:”

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Mirror reading tests whether short-term reading experience can alter spatial associations with time.** Participants read reversed text and perform the past–future button task. By the second mirror-reading block, the compatibility effect reverses. Responses are faster when earlier maps right and later maps left.
  - The result supports a contribution of reading-direction experience and shows that the association can change over a short period.
- **A flexible temporal mapping is difficult to explain as a fixed consequence of native-language grammar alone.** Recent perceptual and reading experience contributes to performance.
  - The first block retains the usual advantage, while the second favors the opposite mapping. Compare the two colors within each block before comparing overall reaction times between blocks.

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Claims about a language's vocabulary are not automatically evidence for cognitive limits.** Pullum's discussion criticizes how an account of different word roots became an exaggerated story about language and thought. Languages can express distinctions through separate roots, derived words or phrases. Counting one of these forms without considering the others can misrepresent expressive capacity.
  - A reliable claim requires accurate linguistic description and an appropriate behavioral test, rather than repetition of a striking anecdote.
- **Different lexical packaging does not show that one group cannot understand another group's distinctions.** The linguistic facts and the cognitive inference both need scrutiny.
  - The excerpt distinguishes roots from derivationally related forms and phrases. That distinction is central to why simple vocabulary counts can be misleading.
