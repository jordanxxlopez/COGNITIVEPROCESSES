## Page 2 · The reasoning behind phrenology
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture05/002.webp

### Original slide text
A simplified phrenological chart.
An early attempt at relating structure to function: Phrenology
The premise: 
1. different parts of the brain are responsible for 
different processes. 
2. aptitude is related to differences in brain region size. 
3. brain region size influences skull shape. 
Therefore:
 
Careful measurements of people's skulls will 
reveal relationships between skull shape and 
various brain functions. 
No one believes in phrenology anymore. 
But which of these premises are actually completely and obviously wrong?

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Phrenology attempted to infer mental functions from the shape of the skull.** Its reasoning linked three claims. Different brain regions were assigned different functions, differences in aptitude were attributed to differences in the sizes of those regions, and those size differences were assumed to affect the skull. Measuring the skull was then supposed to reveal a person’s psychological characteristics. The conclusion depends on the full chain of assumptions. The idea that different parts of the brain can contribute differently to behavior is distinct from the claim that skull measurements reveal individual capacities. Separating the premises makes the historical argument easier to evaluate.
- A claim about specialization is not yet a valid measurement method. Phrenology required a dependable mapping from mental ability to regional brain size and from brain size to skull shape before a skull measurement could support its psychological conclusions.
  - The labeled skull assigns regions to language, memory, thinking, love and other categories. The map encodes the proposed relation between visible head anatomy and mental characteristics.

- **Phrenology combined an unsuitable anatomical measure with poorly constrained definitions of psychological functions.** Skull shape is not a sensitive measure of brain shape. Experimental lesions and a critique of Phrenology

His experiments (mainly with pigeons and rabbits) 
established distinct functions of cerebral cortex, 
cerebellum, and mid-brain structures.
BUT, he failed to find any correspondence between

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Flourens investigated brain function by making experimental lesions, mainly in pigeons and rabbits.** Removing or damaging tissue allowed him to compare behavior after changes to the brain. His work distinguished broad functions of the cerebral cortex, cerebellum and midbrain, but he did not find the proposed correspondence between particular cortical sites and particular senses. He emphasized the size of the lesion. Extensive cortical removal could disrupt several senses together, leading him to argue that their loss depended on the amount of tissue destroyed rather than a precise map of sensory centers. This placed lesion evidence within the debate between specialized and more general cortical function.
- A lesion experiment changes anatomy and measures the behavioral consequence. Flourens used that intervention to challenge phrenological localization, while the pattern of widespread losses guided his emphasis on lesion extent.

## Page 16 · Subtraction logic isolates differences between tasks
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture05/016.webp

### Original slide text
Applying the logic of cognitive psychology to 
metabolic imaging data...
You can't just say “x part of the brain is active 
during y task,” because there's activity all over 
the brain all of the time.
So, there has to be some kind of control 
condition...
How did Posner et al. apply subtraction logic to 
isolate identification of abstract word and letter 
forms from semantic processing of word 
meaning?

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **An imaging comparison needs a control condition because the brain is active even outside the target task.** Subtracting a control condition from a task condition identifies the additional activity associated with what differs between them. The cognitive interpretation depends on designing the conditions so that the relevant operation is the meaningful difference. A motor-task image minus a resting-condition image illustrates the logic. In language experiments, tasks can share visual input and response demands while differing in whether word meaning must be accessed. The comparison connects an imaging difference with a particular processing demand rather than with every activity occurring during the task.
- Subtraction is an argument about operations as well as an arithmetic operation on images. The researcher specifies which processes the conditions share and which additional process the target condition requires, then interprets the remaining signal in light of that contrast.
  - The MOTOR minus REST pairs leave a smaller region of difference than the extensive activity visible in either original image. The reaction-time diagram similarly distinguishes physical, name and rule-based comparisons to motivate different processing demands.

## Page 17 · Semantic task contrasts and left inferior frontal activity
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture05/017.webp

### Original slide text
Interestingly, the region they identify as involved in semantic 
processing of words corresponds roughly to Broca's area.

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Posner and colleagues found left inferior frontal activation shared across several semantic-processing contrasts.** The tasks required monitoring visually presented words for dangerous animals or generating uses for visually or auditorily presented words. The identified region corresponded roughly to the region associated with Broca’s area. The comparisons were specific. Dangerous-animal monitoring was compared with passive viewing of words. Generating uses was compared with repetition, separately for visual and auditory input. Shared activation across these contrasts links the region with demands of semantic processing even when the input modality differs.
- The evidence concerns the activity remaining after a defined comparison. Generating a use requires retrieving meaning-related information beyond repeating the word, so the generating-uses minus repetition contrast helps identify the additional processing demand.
  - The arrows mark left inferior frontal areas active across the three semantic conditions. The color scale indicates relative activation strength, with purple lowest and white highest within a condition. The anatomical comparison connects this activity with the historical Broca-region question.

## Page 18 · Evaluating a modern localization claim
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture05/018.webp

### Original slide text
A rhetorical question (but one worth exploring):
Is this better than phrenology?

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **A modern localization claim depends on valid measurements and a clear definition of the operation being tested.** An attractive image of an active region does not supply those definitions by itself. Comparing such claims with phrenology directs attention to how the psychological category was established and how the anatomical evidence was obtained. Controlled task contrasts give imaging studies a different evidential structure from assigning broad traits to skull regions. The scientific issue is whether the contrast isolates the proposed operation and whether the measurement supports relating that operation to the observed region.
- The useful comparison with phrenology concerns the reasoning. A study must connect an operationally defined cognitive demand with a measured biological change, instead of treating a label on a brain map as an explanation.

## Page 19 · Localizing component operations rather than entire tasks
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture05/019.webp

### Original slide text
A rhetorical question (but one worth exploring):
Is this better than phrenology?

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Posner and colleagues argued for localizing component operations on visual, phonological and semantic codes.** Their conclusion distinguished these operations from whole cognitive tasks. Reading a word, forming a visual image or shifting attention involves multiple computations that must be coordinated, so the complete task is not assigned to one brain area. The PET evidence complemented lesion evidence by relating cognitive distinctions to activity during performance. Averaged blood-flow patterns also suggested some consistency across the studied participants, who were right-handed and had good reading skills. The claim was about the component operations identified by the analysis.
- A cognitive task can recruit several operations, and different operations can involve different regions. Localization at the component level is therefore compatible with the distributed coordination required to carry out a complete task.

## Page 20 · Why language is useful for studying brain–behavior relations
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture05/020.webp

### Original slide text
Tying it all together...
Language has turned up at a number of key moments in 
debates about localization of function:
Broca's presentation of Tan helped establish the 
neuropsychological approach of lesion-behavior analysis.
Some of the very first PET and FMRI studies used 
activation of “Broca's Area” in language tasks as evidence 
that cognitive functions could be localized.
What about language makes it so well suited to these 
investigations?

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Language provides distinguishable abilities that can be compared in behavior and in the brain.** Restricted speech production, apparent comprehension, repetition and semantic judgments place different demands on processing. Contrasts among those abilities make language useful for investigating whether components of cognition can be separated and related to brain systems. Broca’s case helped establish lesion–behavior analysis, while early PET and fMRI work used language-task activation to investigate localization during ongoing processing. These methods contribute different observations to the same general problem of relating function and structure.
- Language tasks can be decomposed into questions about forms, meanings and responses. That decomposition gives researchers a basis for selecting control conditions and for interpreting why an injury affects one ability more than another.
