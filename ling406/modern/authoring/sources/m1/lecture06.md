## Page 2 · Language as a formal symbolic system
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture06/002.webp

### Original slide text
Language as a formal/symbolic system
There is a very old tradition of describing natural languages in abstract, formal terms 
similar to those used in Artificial Intelligence.
Experimental psychology in the 50’s and 60’s was actively hostile to talking about this 
kind of “internal state.”
Skinner’s Verbal Behavior tried to describe how language could be learned by operant 
conditioning.
Chomsky’s (1959) review of Verbal Behavior is better remembered than the book itself.
This was an important moment in “The Cognitive Revolution”

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **The Cognitive Revolution brought internal mental representations back into explanations of language.** A formal account describes language through abstract symbols and rules. This contrasts with the behaviorist goal of explaining learning through relations among observable stimuli, responses, and consequences. Skinner’s Verbal Behavior attempted to explain language through operant conditioning, while Chomsky’s 1959 critique challenged whether that approach could explain linguistic knowledge. Experimental psychology in the 1950s and 1960s often resisted explanations that appealed to an internal state. The debate therefore concerned what an explanation was allowed to contain, as well as which particular learning process produced speech.
  - A grammar is an internal system that can characterize sentences a person has never encountered. Taking that system seriously made language a central case for a psychology of mental structure.
- The central change was to treat an unobservable representation as something that could be investigated through its consequences. A theory could ask what knowledge a speaker must have to produce and understand language, rather than describing only the environmental conditions associated with a response.

## Page 8 · Chomsky’s critique of a conditioning account
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture06/008.webp

### Original slide text
Some highlights of Chomsky’s critique
The construction of a grammar which enumerates sentences in such a way that a meaningful structural 
description can be determined for each sentence … characterizes abstractly the ability of one who has 
mastered the language to distinguish sentences from nonsentences, to understand new sentences (in part), 
to note certain ambiguities, etc. These are very remarkable abilities.
It is easy to show that the new events that we accept and understand as sentences are not related to

identity of grammatical frame. Talk of generalization in this case is entirely pointless and empty.
It appears that we recognize a new item as a sentence not because it matches some familiar item in any

remarkable rapidity suggests that human beings are somehow specially designed to do this, with data-
handling or "hypothesis-formulating" ability of unknown character and complexity.

## Page 15 · Marr’s three levels of analysis
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture06/015.webp

### Original slide text
Chomsky is trying to describe language at the “computational level 
of analysis.”
This term comes from David Marr, a visual neuroscientist who talked 
about cognitive systems as having three “levels of analysis:”
1.Computational
2.Algorithmic
3.Implementational
An example will help here…

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Marr distinguished computational, algorithmic, and implementational descriptions of a cognitive system.** The computational level characterizes the problem the system solves. The algorithmic level describes the representations and procedures used to solve it. The implementational level concerns how those procedures are physically realized. These levels distinguish different questions about the same ability. An account of what counts as a successful result is different from an account of the steps used to obtain it or the physical machinery carrying out those steps.
  - Chomsky’s abstract characterization of linguistic knowledge is interpreted here as a computational description. It characterizes the structural system that language processing must realize.
