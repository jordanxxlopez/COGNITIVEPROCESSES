### Original slide text
A classic cautionary tale:  
The frequency effect, and its confound with length
Oldfield and Wingfield (1965) asked

between frequency and response time. 
Oldfield, R. C. & Wingfield, A. (1964) The time it takes to

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- Oldfield and Wingfield asked people to name pictures of objects and related their response times to the frequency of the object names. More frequent names were produced faster. The relation was approximately linear when frequency was expressed on a logarithmic scale. This makes frequency a useful predictor of naming time, but it also raises a design problem because **frequent words tend to be shorter**. If the high-frequency names are also short, the experiment changes frequency and length together. Either property could contribute to a difference in naming time. A confound is a variable that covaries with the intended manipulation and supplies another explanation for the observed result.
- The dependent variable is how long naming takes. Frequency is the property of interest, while length is a competing explanation that must be addressed in the materials. Finding a clear relationship on a graph is only the beginning of identifying which property accounts for it.
  - The horizontal axis gives log frequency of occurrence, and the vertical axis gives response latency in milliseconds. The downward trend means that higher-frequency names have shorter naming latencies. The object drawings illustrate the picture-naming task rather than being an additional manipulation.

Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture09/008.webp

### Original slide text

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- To separate frequency from length, the low-frequency comparison should be **selected deliberately rather than at random**. Researchers can choose relatively long high-frequency words and relatively short low-frequency words so that the two conditions have comparable lengths while retaining their frequency difference. Matching changes the relationship between conditions in the experimental sample. It does not claim that frequency and length are unrelated in the language. The purpose is to compare naming performance under a design where length is held similar, allowing the frequency difference to be evaluated more directly.
- The matching problem is solved by selecting from the overlapping parts of the distributions. Suitable words are less typical of their frequency groups, but they make the desired contrast possible. The important check is whether the final groups actually differ on frequency while agreeing on length.
  - The same HF and LF boxplots reveal where overlap can be found. Choosing toward the longer end of HF and the shorter end of LF moves the two experimental samples toward a common length range.

## Page 9 · Frequency effects after length matching
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture09/009.webp

### Original slide text
Oldfield and Wingfield eventually addressed this by matching their 
high- and low-frequency words for length.
They found a robust frequency effect, so they concluded that 
frequency effects are not merely length effects in disguise.
Really rather a lot of psycholinguistics can be characterized as an

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- Oldfield and Wingfield **matched high- and low-frequency words for length** and still found a robust frequency effect. This result distinguishes an effect associated with frequency from an explanation based entirely on the typical length difference between frequent and infrequent words. The logic depends on the controlled comparison. If length were the whole explanation, making length comparable should remove the naming difference attributed to frequency. Its persistence supports a contribution beyond length. The design turns a correlation among word properties into a more informative comparison between conditions.
- A confound is addressed by changing the materials or procedure so that its prediction differs from the prediction of the proposed explanation. Here, frequency remains different and length becomes similar. That separation is what gives the persistent frequency effect its evidential value.
  - The critical comparison is between length-matched frequency groups. The result is a surviving naming difference, rather than the original observation that common words happen to be shorter.

## Page 14 · Belke, Meyer and Damian’s semantic-blocking study
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture09/014.webp

### Original slide text
(visual only)

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- Belke, Meyer and Damian’s 2005 study used **cyclic semantic blocking** to examine picture naming. Participants repeatedly named sets of objects whose names were either semantically related or unrelated. Naming was slower in related sets once the objects had already been encountered. The experiments used changes in presentation, eye tracking and new items to investigate where this cost arises. Experiment 1 established that the blocking effect appeared after the objects had been named once. Experiment 2 presented objects simultaneously and found blocking in naming latency and gaze duration, but not a significant blocking effect in the gaze-to-speech lag. Experiment 3 showed that the cost generalized to new names from the same categories.
- The three experiments connect a behavioral effect to a proposed processing locus. The important evidence is the pattern across conditions and measures. Related context increases a cost of selecting names even though repetition produces its own facilitation.
  - The paper title identifies the task as picture naming and the manipulation as semantic blocking. Its abstract defines homogeneous sets as semantically related and heterogeneous sets as unrelated, and distinguishes gaze duration from the lag between gaze offset and articulation onset.

  - The generalization result is especially important because it changes the actual names while preserving category history. The eye-tracking result is important for a different reason, because it divides the naming sequence into measured intervals. These designs contribute complementary evidence for locating the effect around lemma selection.
- Each major alternative is paired with its test. Visual recognition has matching and a control experiment. Phonological encoding has sound-form and timing comparisons. Specific list repetition has transfer to new items. This mapping is the logic of the experimental argument.
