### Original slide text
The Positional Level
Let’s take the simple-seeming sentence we used as evidence that lemmas are a useful concept:
Where’d I bring it when I put it in?
By the time we’re done at the functional level, we know: 
1. that we have two Verbs PUT and BRING
2. that I/ME is the Agent of both verbs
3. that IT is the Undergoer of both verbs
What else? 
What is the temporal relationship between the verbs?
Expressing all of those things syntactically is what goes on at the Positional Level.

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- In “Where’d I bring it when I put it in?”, the **functional level** supplies two verb lemmas, BRING and PUT, together with their participants. The speaker, expressed as I or ME, is the agent of both actions. IT is the undergoer of both. This information identifies the events and who participates in them, but a grammatical sentence also has to express how the events are related. The positional level organizes that information into clauses and grammatical positions. The relationship between the actions, the question structure and the forms required by tense all contribute to the eventual sentence. Knowing which participant performs an action is therefore different from knowing where each word belongs in the spoken sequence.
- Think of the functional representation as identifying two actions with the same agent and undergoer. The positional representation has the additional job of arranging those actions in an English sentence. The distinction helps explain how a speaker can preserve the intended participants while putting a verb into the wrong grammatical location.
  - BRING and PUT name the selected verb lemmas. I/ME and IT identify participant roles across both events. These role assignments do not themselves specify the final order or the verb forms demanded by the question and the embedded clause.

### Original slide text
If we skip the part where we organize the sentence into a question, and work out the 
appropriate tenses for the verbs, etc., we get something like:

Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture08/009.webp

### Original slide text
This is evidence that derivational morphology is, like, a thing.
Specifically affixes seem to be able to float around at the Positional Level.
An alternative view might be that derived forms are just other words that are related to 
their root forms in some rule-like way.
So, you might have a lexicon that looks like this:
needy <> need <> needless 
unlearn <> learn <> learner

2. Affixes have a “slot” in the Positional Level

### Original slide text

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- The collected examples involve different kinds of change. Buyed contrasts with the irregular past form bought, making it a **morphological regularization**. Arising becoming arriving changes a consonant within a closely similar word form. Pronouncing close as [kloʊz] in “pretty close to here” changes the final consonant from the voiceless form expected for the adjective. ## Page 21 · Perception shapes reported speech errors
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture08/021.webp

### Original slide text
Some issues with observational studies
In the next few weeks we’ll look more closely at speech perception and you’ll see 
that:
1. People aren’t very good at hearing things that are “halfway” between one 
phoneme and another, especially for consonants.
2. People are biased against perceiving phonotactically illegal strings.
3. People are biased in favor of perceiving ambiguous stimuli as words (e.g.
something partway between “giss” and “kiss” will sound more like “kiss.”)
This is what philosophers of science talk about as theory-dependent observation. 
We might miss a lot of errors, or whole classes of errors, because of the way our 
perceptual systems work.
Experimental methods and careful measurement techniques can remedy some of 
these issues (but not all, not by a long shot!)..

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Speech perception** can shape the errors that observers record. Listeners are not especially good at hearing sounds halfway between phoneme categories, particularly consonants. They also tend to perceive phonotactically legal sequences and familiar words. An ambiguous sound between giss and kiss can therefore be heard more like kiss. These tendencies can make some production errors less noticeable than others. A listener may report a clean phoneme substitution even when the articulation was intermediate, or perceive an unfamiliar sequence as a familiar word. Theory-dependent observation also includes the influence of the observer’s expectations on the description of the event.
- Experimental methods add control over what is presented and how responses are measured. This helps separate the produced signal from the perceptual and reporting processes that shape an informal error record. The methodological issue follows from particular properties of speech perception, not from a general claim that observation is useless.
  - The giss/kiss contrast makes lexical familiarity relevant to an ambiguous signal. The other two examples concern category boundaries and legal sound sequences. Together they explain why the distribution of reported errors can differ from the distribution of produced events.
