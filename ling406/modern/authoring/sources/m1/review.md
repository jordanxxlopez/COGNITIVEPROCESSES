Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/review/004.webp

### Original slide text
Note this chart is organized by features:
 
PLACE (x-axis: front to back of vocal tract)

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Consonants can be compared by place of articulation, manner of articulation, and voicing.** Place identifies where the vocal tract is constricted. Manner identifies how the airflow is constrained, such as by a complete closure for a plosive or a narrower passage for a fricative. Voicing concerns the timing of vocal-fold vibration relative to the articulation. The IPA chart places progressively farther-back articulations across its columns. Its rows separate different ways of controlling airflow. Within a paired position, the right-hand symbol represents the voiced member. A sound can therefore share two of these properties with another sound while differing on the third.
- Classifying a consonant means locating the constriction, describing what that constriction does to airflow, and identifying the voicing pattern. These are separate dimensions. Two consonants can be produced at the lips in the same manner and still differ in when vocal-fold vibration occurs.
  - Bilabial sounds appear toward the left of the chart because their constriction occurs at the lips. Velar and more posterior articulations appear farther right. The paired p and b symbols occupy the plosive row in the bilabial column.

## Page 9 · Hockett’s overlapping-eggs analogy
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/review/009.webp

### Original slide text
“The best analogy I have been able to think of for this is a very homely one.  Imagine 
a row of Easter eggs carried along a moving belt; the eggs are of various sizes, and 
variously colored, but not boiled.  At a certain point, the belt carries the row of eggs 
between the two rollers of a wringer, which quite effectively smash them and rub 
them more or less into each other. The flow of eggs before the wringer represents 
the series of impulses from the phoneme source; the mess that emerges from the 
wringer represents the output of the speech transmitter.  At a subsequent point, we 
have an inspector whose task it is to examine the passing mess and decide, on the 
basis of the broken and unbroken yolks, the variously spread-out albumen, and the 
variously colored bits of shell, the nature of the flow of eggs which previously 
arrived at the wringer. Note that he does not have to try to put the eggs together 
again—a manifest physical impossibility—but only to identify.     
The inspector represents the hearer.”
Hockett (1955)

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Hockett’s 1955 analogy explains why recognizing speech differs from recovering a sequence of separate physical pieces.** Eggs enter a wringer as distinguishable objects, but their contents mix as they pass through. The listener is compared to an inspector who uses the mixed remains to identify which eggs were originally present. The analogy maps the distinguishable eggs onto an intended sequence and the mixed output onto the speech signal after production. Information about neighboring elements spreads together. The inspector’s job is identification from the resulting pattern, not physically rebuilding each original egg.
- Overlapping acoustic information can still support recognition. Hockett’s listener identifies the original elements from a mixture whose parts are no longer cleanly separated. The example highlights the difference between a discrete linguistic description and the combined physical consequences of speaking.
  - The egg colors, yolks, whites, and shell fragments stand for different traces of the original elements. Their mixture explains why perceptual identification does not require perfectly separating and reconstructing every original physical unit.

### Original slide text

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- Belke, Meyer and Damian’s 2005 study used **cyclic semantic blocking** to examine picture naming. Participants repeatedly named sets of objects whose names were either semantically related or unrelated. Naming was slower in related sets once the objects had already been encountered. The experiments used changes in presentation, eye tracking and new items to investigate where this cost arises. Experiment 1 established that the blocking effect appeared after the objects had been named once. Experiment 2 presented objects simultaneously and found blocking in naming latency and gaze duration, but not a significant blocking effect in the gaze-to-speech lag. Experiment 3 showed that the cost generalized to new names from the same categories.
- The three experiments connect a behavioral effect to a proposed processing locus. The important evidence is the pattern across conditions and measures. Related context increases a cost of selecting names even though repetition produces its own facilitation.
  - The paper title identifies the task as picture naming and the manipulation as semantic blocking. Its abstract defines homogeneous sets as semantically related and heterogeneous sets as unrelated, and distinguishes gaze duration from the lag between gaze offset and articulation onset.

## Page 51 · Activation and lemma selection are different processes
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/review/051.webp

### Original slide text
Belke et al.
In speech production, we have to distinguish carefully between activation and selection.
Spreading activation from, e.g., duck to fish means that activating one can help activate 
the other. But this can cause problems when our task is to select the correct lemma from a 
bunch of active ones…
The WEAVER++ model assumes that selection time is determined by a ratio of the activity 
of the most active lemma and the overall activation of all lemmas.
Thus, when there’s feedback from the category level, this slows down lemma selection 
because of all the collateral activation for items from the same category.

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- WEAVER++ distinguishes **activation of candidate lemmas from selection** of the lemma to be spoken. Selection time depends on the target’s activation relative to the total activation across lemmas. Increasing activation of several semantically related competitors can therefore slow selection even when the target itself is active. Activating duck can spread activation to related animal concepts and their lemmas. Category feedback adds collateral activation to other members of the category. If several candidates remain active, the target is less dominant relative to the whole set. The speaker then takes longer to select the correct name.
- The predicted cost follows from relative activation. A target considered alone might appear easier to retrieve after semantic activation, but the model evaluates it in relation to competitors. This explains how semantic relatedness can produce interference in naming rather than only facilitation.
  - The concept network fans out through shared properties and categories, then reaches the corresponding lemma nodes. Those connections illustrate why selecting duck can be affected by activation of fish, snake and mouse even though only one name is required.
