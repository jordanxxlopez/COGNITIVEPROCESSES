### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Speech errors reveal how an utterance is planned.** An error can separate parts of a word, move a sound into another position, or replace an intended word with a related one. The form of the error provides evidence about the units that the production system represents and the processes that organize them. Fromkin’s title “Speech errors as linguistic evidence” takes errors seriously as a source of knowledge about language. If linguistic competence is studied separately from the disruptions of performance, an error might be set aside. Examining its structure instead makes performance informative about the organization of the system.
  - The useful question is why this particular error occurred. A theory should explain which elements were active, what was selected, and how the produced form differs from the intended one.
- An occasional error can expose a distinction that fluent speech conceals. When the intended and produced forms are compared, the part that changed and the parts that remained stable help identify what the system was doing.

## Page 3 · Spreading activation and competing candidates
Slide image: ling406/module1/assets/lecture07/003.webp

### Original slide text

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Spreading activation explains why an unintended item can become available during speech planning.** Words and other representations activate related items. Similarity can concern meaning, grammatical properties, or sound. The intended item therefore competes with alternatives that may receive activation from several sources at once. Frequency affects how readily an item becomes active. Common words are generally easier to activate than uncommon words, so the amount of activation required to produce a particular candidate differs across items.
  - Recently spoken material remains active, and material planned for an upcoming part of the utterance is already becoming active. Other thoughts can contribute activation as well. “Noise” includes fluctuations and interference within this system, rather than referring only to sounds in the environment.
- A production error becomes understandable when the activation of a competitor is traced. It may resemble the intended item, have been spoken a moment ago, or be waiting to be spoken. These influences can combine, allowing an unintended candidate to enter the utterance.

### Existing reviewed notes (PSYC301 v20-style edition), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Slips of the tongue differ in the direction that a sound moves.** In anticipation, a sound planned for later appears too soon. In perseveration, an earlier sound carries into a later position. In metathesis or exchange, elements trade positions within the utterance. Comparing the intended and produced forms identifies the pattern. “Week long race” becoming “reek long race” is anticipation because the upcoming r replaces the beginning of “week.” “John gave the boy” becoming “John gave the goy” is perseveration because the earlier g carries into “boy.”
  - “Keep a tape” becoming “teep a cape” is an exchange. The initial k and t trade positions while the remaining parts of the two words remain in place. The classification concerns the relation between the two forms, not whether the resulting expression makes sense.
- The order of the intended sounds is essential. The same kind of sound substitution can reflect a later item arriving early or an earlier item persisting. An exchange has the further property that both displaced elements appear in each other’s positions.
  - The examples align intended and produced expressions. In “keep a tape” and “teep a cape,” the changed initial consonants reveal the exchange. In the anticipation and perseveration examples, only one position changes, and the source of that replacement is respectively later or earlier in the utterance.
