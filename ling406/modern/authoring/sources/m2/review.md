### Existing reviewed notes (Version 18), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- The **Revised Hierarchical Model of Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** accounts for interactions between a first language, **L1**, and a second language, **L2**, through **spreading activation**, where activating one representation partly activates the representations linked to it.
  - The model joins an L1 vocabulary, an L2 vocabulary and a shared store of concepts.
  - **Lexical links** connect words across the two languages, and **conceptual links** connect each vocabulary to meanings.
  - L1 words are strongly linked to concepts, and L2 words are linked more weakly.
- In the **Marian and Spivey** task in **L2**, Russian–English bilinguals hear an English instruction to pick up the **speaker**.
  - **The concept for “speaker” partially activates the Russian word for “matches,”** which is **spichki**.
  - Speaker and spichki are connected only by **sound overlap**. They begin with the same sounds, so activity spreads from one to the other, although speakers and matches share no meaning.
- **The activity of spichki feeds back to the conceptual activation for “matches.”**
  - L1 words are tightly linked to their concepts, so the partly active Russian word strengthens the concept it names.
  - **Conceptual activation for “matches” leads to eye movements toward the L1 competitor**, the matches, even though the listener still picks up the speaker.
- **When the task is done in L1**, the **conceptual links with L2 might be too weak to create this kind of interference reliably**.
  - An English competitor would need a strong link from its English name to its concept to draw the eyes during a Russian task.
  - The model gives L2 words only weak conceptual links, so interference runs from the first language into the second but not the other way.

- The **Revised Hierarchical Model of Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** explains interactions between a first language, **L1**, and a second language, **L2**, in terms of **spreading activation**. Spreading activation means that activating one representation partly activates the representations connected to it.
  - The model connects an L1 vocabulary, an L2 vocabulary and a shared store of concepts.
  - **Lexical links** join words across the two languages, and **conceptual links** join each vocabulary to meanings.
  - L1 words have strong links to concepts, while L2 words have weaker conceptual links.
- Consider what happens in the model during the **Marian and Spivey** task in **L2**, when Russian–English bilinguals hear the English instruction to pick up the **speaker**.
  - **The concept for “speaker” partially activates the Russian word for “matches,”** which is **spichki**.
  - The link between speaker and spichki is **sound overlap**. The two words begin with the same sounds, so activity for speaker spreads to the similar-sounding Russian word.
  - Speakers and matches share no meaning, so the competition comes entirely from the shared sounds.
- **The activity of spichki feeds back to the conceptual activation for “matches.”**
  - Because L1 words are strongly connected to their concepts, the partly active Russian word strengthens the concept it names.
  - **Conceptual activation for “matches” leads to eye movements toward the L1 competitor**, the picture of matches.
  - The listener can still pick up the speaker correctly. The looks to the matches reveal a competitor that was active along the way.
- **When the task is done in L1**, the **conceptual links with L2 might be too weak to create this kind of interference reliably**.
  - An English competitor would need a strong link from its English name to its concept to attract the eyes during a Russian task.
  - L2 words have only weak conceptual links in this model, so the reverse effect is unreliable.
  - The unequal strength of the links explains why the other language interferes in one direction but not the other.
