### Existing reviewed notes (Version 18), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Categorical perception** is the pattern in which listeners hear differences between speech sounds in terms of categories. **Early theories treated it as a language-specific and species-specific phenomenon**.
  - **Language-specific** means it was thought to occur only for language. **Species-specific** means it was thought to occur only in humans.
  - At the time, **only humans were known to show categorical perception, and only for speech sounds**, so it looked like a unique feature of human language.
- The classic evidence comes from a synthetic consonant continuum. The upper formant transition changes in equal steps from one stimulus to the next, while everything else stays the same.
  - In **labeling**, listeners switch sharply from **/b/** to **/d/**, and later from /d/ to **/g/**, each over just a step or two.
  - In **discrimination**, accuracy peaks near those boundaries and drops inside each category, whether the pairs are one, two or three steps apart.
- Categorical perception is **a compelling example of how similarity between stimuli is defined in linguistic, rather than physical, terms**.
  - Two sounds that differ by the same acoustic amount can sound almost identical if they belong to the same category.
  - They can sound clearly different if they fall on opposite sides of a category boundary.

### Existing reviewed notes (Version 18), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Eimas and colleagues (1971)** tested categorical perception in **4-month-old infants** using **high-amplitude sucking**. The sounds varied in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**, the delay between the release of a stop consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration.
  - In a syllable with a long VOT, voicing starts much later after the release than in a syllable with a short VOT.
- The experiment had **three conditions**. Infants heard a syllable over and over until their sucking slowed down, and then the sound either changed or stayed the same.
  - In **20D**, the two sounds differed by **20 milliseconds** of VOT, **+20 versus +40 milliseconds**, and came from **different adult phonemic categories**.
  - In **20S**, the sounds also differed by **20 milliseconds**, such as **−20 versus 0** or **+60 versus +80 milliseconds**, but came from the **same** category.
  - In **0**, the **control** condition, the same sound continued throughout.
- In every condition, sucking rose at first and then declined as the sound became familiar.
  - In **20D**, sucking jumped back up after the change.
  - In **20S**, sucking showed little recovery and kept falling.
  - In the control condition, sucking kept declining.
- Infants responded to a change that crossed an adult phoneme boundary but not to an equally large change within a category.
  - The two changed conditions were matched in acoustic size, so the difference comes from crossing the boundary.
  - The control condition establishes what happens with repetition alone.

  - At **6–8 months**, most English-learning infants discriminated both contrasts.
  - At **8–10 months**, fewer did.
  - At **10–12 months**, only a small minority did.

  - Without experience of a contrast, English-learning infants lose much of their ability to hear it by about their first birthday.
  - Infants who hear the contrast in their language keep it, so the decline depends on language experience.

  - As infants form the categories of their native language, sounds that their language groups together become harder to tell apart.
  - A nonnative distinction that falls within one native category comes to be heard as a within-category difference.

- not limited to language

### Existing reviewed notes (Version 18), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- This view faces **some problems**. Three concern **categorical perception**.
  - Categorical perception is **not limited to language**, since it also appears for nonspeech sounds.
  - Categorical perception is **not limited to humans**, since animals without language also perceive speech sounds categorically.
  - Categorical perception is **task-dependent**. Results look more or less categorical depending on the task, such as naming a sound versus judging whether two sounds differ.

  - Infants do not begin able to hear every contrast equally well.
  - Some contrasts are hard for young infants to detect, even contrasts that occur in their own language.

  - One kind limits **which stimulus properties can be contrastive**. A **contrastive** property tells words apart, such as voicing in /b/ versus /p/. **Speaker** identity and **speech rate** change the sound a lot but do not change which word is spoken, so they are **not** contrastive.
  - A learner who tracked every pattern would form categories for different voices and speeds. Learning has to focus on the properties that carry meaning.
- A second kind is **based on earlier learning**.
  - What has already been learned shapes what is easy to learn next.
  - Once native categories exist, new distinctions that cut across them are harder to notice and learn. Constraints like this are central to **sensitive periods**, when earlier experience limits later learning.
- A third kind comes from **social and interactive contexts for learning**.
  - Sounds are learned better in live, responsive interaction than from a recording.
  - Caregivers’ contingent responses shape the sounds infants produce.
- Learning depends on the patterns in the input, on what the learner already knows and on the social setting of the input. Together these constraints explain why learning is fast and why it settles on the distinctions that matter.

### Existing reviewed notes (Version 12, more detail), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Statistical learning with constraints** describes learning from patterns in the input that is guided and limited in several ways. **Constraints** can mean a lot of things.
  - First, constraints on **which stimulus properties can be contrastive**. A **contrastive** property is one that distinguishes words, such as voicing in /b/ versus /p/. Properties such as **speaker** identity and **speech rate** change the sound a great deal but do not change which word is said, so they are **not** contrastive.
  - A learner tracking every statistical pattern would create categories for different voices and speeds. Learning has to focus on the properties that matter for meaning.
- Second, constraints **based on earlier learning**.
  - What a learner has already acquired shapes what is easy to learn next.
  - Once native categories are in place, new distinctions that cut across them become harder to notice and learn. Constraints of this kind are central to **sensitive periods**, in which earlier experience limits later learning.
- Third, **social and interactive contexts for learning**.
  - The same sounds are learned more effectively in live, responsive interaction than from a recording.
  - Contingent responses from caregivers shape infants’ own vocal behavior.
  - Learning depends on the patterns in the input, on what the learner already knows, and on the social situation in which the input arrives.
- The three kinds of constraint each narrow what statistical learning has to do. Constraints on which properties are contrastive keep learners from building categories for irrelevant variation. Constraints from earlier learning shape which new distinctions are easy.
  - Social and interactive constraints make some input much more effective than other input.
  - Together, these constraints help explain why learning is fast and why it focuses on the distinctions that matter.
