### Existing reviewed notes (Version 18), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- **Speech perception** is the process of recovering a linguistic message from the sound of speech. Listeners take an acoustic signal that seems like a mess and pull out a message as clear as “I can see you.”
  - In the acoustic signal for “I can see you,” the energy of each sound runs into the next, and nothing in the signal cleanly separates letters or even words.
  - Real-time MRI of the **vocal tract**, from USC SPAN, reveals why. The tongue, lips and other articulators move without pause, and the movement for one sound overlaps the movement for the next, so their acoustic effects overlap as well.
- We also get **the same message from very different physical stimuli**. Voices and vocal tract sizes differ, so one word has different acoustic properties from each speaker, yet listeners hear the same word.
  - Across 76 speakers, each of ten vowels covers a broad range of first and second formant values instead of a single point.
  - Many of those ranges **overlap**, so one pair of formant values can be one vowel for one speaker and another vowel for a different speaker.
- Recognizing speech therefore means finding a **stable linguistic identity** inside enormous physical variation.

## Page 3 · From an analog signal to a symbolic representation
Slide image: ling406/assets/slides/speech1/03.webp

### Original slide text

### Existing reviewed notes (Version 18), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- A working premise is that we must **turn a highly variable, analog signal into something more abstract** that the language system can use. An **analog signal** varies continuously, so frequency, loudness and timing can each shift by tiny amounts.
  - A continuous signal has no built-in markers of where one speech sound stops and the next starts.
  - Many small changes leave the speech sound the same, so two physically different productions can still be the same sound.
- The abstract form is a **symbolic representation**, which identifies a language unit such as a particular consonant or vowel and sets its fine physical detail aside. The unit is the **phoneme**, a sound category that distinguishes words in a language.
  - /b/ in “bat” and /p/ in “pat” are different phonemes, because swapping them changes the word.
  - Many slightly different acoustic versions of /b/ all count as one phoneme.
- **Categorical perception** is thought to reflect this **“translation” from physical to symbolic**. Listeners grow less sensitive to differences inside a category and more sensitive to equally large differences that cross a category boundary.
  - A change stands out when it alters the identity the listener gives the sound.
  - A change of the same physical size can pass almost unnoticed when both sounds remain the same phoneme. Categorical perception is one account of the translation, and other accounts exist.

## Page 4 · Formants reflect the shape of the vocal tract
Slide image: ling406/assets/slides/speech1/04.webp

### Original slide text
Formants and formant transitions
The shape of the vocal tract is related in lawful ways to the amplification of 
particular resonant frequencies (from Ladefoged, 1996).

### Existing reviewed notes (Version 18), for facts only, do not copy the wording
- The **vocal tract** is the passage sound travels through in speech, from the larynx through the throat and mouth to the lips, and its shape changes as the tongue, jaw and lips move. Following Ladefoged (1996), the shape of the vocal tract is related in systematic ways to **which resonant frequencies get amplified**.
  - A **resonant frequency** is one that a space naturally strengthens, so a tube of a given shape boosts some frequencies and dampens others.
  - Changing the vocal tract’s shape changes which frequencies get the biggest boost.
- **Formants** are the resonant frequencies the vocal tract emphasizes, and every vowel has its own combination of them.
  - Across **heed, hid, head and had**, the tongue moves from high and front toward low. Heed has its second formant far above its first, and the two draw closer through hid, head and had.
  - Across **hod, hawed, hood and who’d**, the tongue moves back and the lips round more. The amplified frequencies gather at the low end, with the first and second formants close together.
  - A vowel is defined by the **relationship among its resonances**, which stays similar across speakers even when the exact frequencies differ.
- A **formant transition** is a change in a formant’s frequency over time. As the mouth moves from a consonant into a vowel, the resonances glide from one set of values to another.
  - The steady part of a vowel carries information about the vowel.
  - The moving part carries information about how the neighboring sound is made.

  - After a **within-category** change, sucking also stayed low.
  - After a **between-category** change, sucking jumped back up to about 18 and then declined again as the new sound became familiar.
- Infants therefore responded more strongly to a non-speech change that crossed a category boundary than to a change within a category.
  - This mirrors the pattern Eimas and colleagues found for speech.
  - Early categorical perception therefore operates on sounds in general, including sounds that are not speech.
- Using nonspeech sounds with infants removes any possible role for language knowledge. Infants cannot be drawing on knowledge of words or phonemes when they respond to plucked and bowed sounds.
  - Their categorical response must therefore come from more general auditory processing.
  - The same kind of processing could also underlie their categorical response to speech.
