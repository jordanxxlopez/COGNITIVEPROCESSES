2|**Critical periods** and **sensitive or “optimal” periods** both describe how the timing of experience affects learning, and they differ in how strict the time limits are. || A **critical period** has a **discrete, “programmed” onset and offset**, and learning, which is change in response to the environment, is **strictly limited to this period**. >> There is **no sensitivity to the environment before or after** the window, so any late improvement counts against a strict critical period. || In a **sensitive period**, **learning is possible throughout the life span** but is **optimal during particular windows**, which may have a **gradual onset and offset** that experience itself can affect. >> Late learning is harder but still possible, and early learners keep a lasting advantage. >> Even the classic critical period in vision fits the term sensitive period better, and the same question comes up for language.
3|**Amblyopia** is **poor vision in one eye despite normal peripheral function**, which means the eye itself works and the trouble lies in how the brain handles that eye's input. || Three conditions can lead to amblyopia in one eye. >> **Strabismus** is a misalignment of the eyes, so the brain receives two conflicting images, and a **cataract** is a clouding of the lens that blocks or blurs the incoming light. >> **Anisometropia** is a large difference in focusing power between the eyes, so one eye's image is much blurrier than the other's. || **Periods of deprivation from normal visual input** caused by these conditions can leave **poor vision long after the conditions have been remediated**, meaning corrected. >> Correcting the eye restores its input, but the brain may still fail to use that input well, so a temporary early problem can have a lasting effect.
4|**Amblyopia occurs because of competitive dynamics between cortical inputs**, because both eyes send input to **visual cortex** and the two inputs **compete** for control of the neurons there. >> **Abnormal vision in one eye** can lead to **“colonization”** of that eye's cortical representation by the other eye, so the stronger eye takes over neurons that would have served the weaker one. || **Monocular deprivation**, which means blocking or degrading input to one eye, reveals the takeover in mice. >> Within a few days the **dendritic spines**, the small projections where neurons receive connections, become unstable, and then spines are lost and cut-off fibers break down in **Wallerian degeneration**. >> After more than a week the open eye's axons sprout and new spines form in the space the deprived eye lost. || When normal function returns to the deprived eye, it **stays at a competitive disadvantage**, so **normal experience cannot restore normal cortical responses**.
5|**Treatments for amblyopia are based on competition**, and one of the most effective is **intermittently patching the “good” eye**. >> The stronger eye is patched because covering it blocks its input for part of the day, so the brain has to rely on the weaker eye. || Patching **restores the competitive balance between the eyes** and lets the brain's own **plasticity**, its ability to change its connections in response to experience, **restore normal function**. >> The patch does not repair the eye, because it only changes the experience the brain receives while the weaker eye wins back cortical connections. || The treatment is **most effective early in development** and **may be ineffective by late childhood**, because the visual system becomes less plastic as it matures. >> The timing of treatment matters as much as the treatment itself.
6|**Levi and Polat** found that **plasticity is not abolished entirely, even well into adulthood**, in patients with amblyopia who were as old as their fifties. || The adults did **long-term, intensive training on specific psychophysical tasks** with the affected eye, and they **improved in both task performance and general visual function**. >> A **psychophysical task** measures the relationship between a physical stimulus and what a person perceives, such as the smallest contrast a person can detect. >> Over many thousands of trials the **threshold**, the smallest stimulus a person can reliably detect, **dropped steadily**, then **leveled off** for a long stretch, and then **dropped again** to a new lower level. || A lower threshold means better vision, so the adult visual system keeps some **experience-dependent plasticity** after ordinary late treatment stops working.
7|**Li, Ngo, Nguyen, Lam and Levi** found that **playing video games enhances visual acuity and visual attention in adult amblyopia**, when adults played an action video game, a first-person shooting game set in a battle scene, with the amblyopic eye. >> **Visual acuity** is the sharpness of vision, and **visual attention** is the ability to select and focus on the relevant parts of a scene. || Action games demand fast visual judgments and constant attention, so they push the visual system to use the weak eye's input. >> The players' childhood visual development was long over, so the improvement came from the training itself. || The result adds to the evidence for **plasticity** in adult amblyopia, because an early optimal window can close without removing every possibility of improvement.
8|**Amblyopia is a classic example of a sensitive period**. A **peripheral disturbance in early experience** can lead to **life-long disruption in visual function**, and that long-term loss is the **sequela**, the lasting result, of a **temporary deficit during experience-dependent organization of the brain**. >> The brain organized itself around abnormal input, and that organization stays after the eye is corrected. >> Treatment is **most effective during a limited developmental window**. || However, new evidence suggests that **the right kind of experience can reverse deficits late into adulthood**, as intensive perceptual training and video game play did. >> A strict critical period would rule out any later improvement, so a strong early window with some later capacity for change fits a **sensitive period** better.
9|The vision evidence is related to language through two lines of work. || The first is **Lenneberg's** early attempts to synthesize **developmental neurology**, the study of how the nervous system develops, with the notion of a **“language acquisition device”**, a proposed innate capacity for learning language. >> Lenneberg asked whether changes in the developing brain could open and close a window for language, so that learning depends on the state of the brain as well as on the input. || The second is **studies of early deprivation**, with data from **neglected children** and from **Deaf children with limited input to sign**. >> These people first met an accessible language unusually late, so comparing them with typical learners tests whether the age of first exposure affects the final outcome.
10|In **Biological Foundations of Language**, **Lenneberg** was attempting to synthesize **contemporary linguistics** with what was known about **brain development**. || Studies of language development were starting to find a fairly robust, **stage-like pattern**, which held across Austrian, British and American children. >> **Single words** come first, from late in the first year into the second year, then **two-word phrases**, and then **sentences of five words or more** toward the end of the third year. >> Language milestones line up with motor milestones, so reduplicated babbling becomes frequent when infants stand holding on, and words such as **mama** or **dada** emerge when they walk with one hand held. || The idea that language has **maturational milestones** was seen as consistent with the **unfolding of a biological program**, because Lenneberg proposed that brain maturation sets the pace for both language and motor development.
11|**Data on typical brain development were sparse** in Lenneberg's time, because they came almost entirely from **post-mortem analyses of tissue**, meaning tissue studied after death. >> Measurements of **neuron size** had no data from age two to age eighteen, a gap that overlaps much of the period of language acquisition. || Nonetheless, Lenneberg observed that **abrupt changes in neuron size and packing density before age two** might be **permissive for the beginning of language acquisition**. >> **Packing density** is how closely neurons are crowded together, and **permissive** means the changes make language acquisition possible without directly causing it. || On this view, **the critical period seems to open after this initial period of rapid development**, once the early burst of brain growth settles. >> Lenneberg filled the gap in the data with a reasonable guess that later research would need to test.
12|Studies of recovery from stroke gave more extensive data, and they found that **aphasia is surprisingly rare in children with brain lesions**, which are areas of damaged brain tissue. || In extreme cases a **hemispherectomy** was performed, which removes or disconnects a whole cerebral hemisphere, typically to relieve seizures in people with epilepsy. >> **Children in whom the left hemisphere is removed nonetheless seem to develop typical language abilities**, while **adults do not recover language after this procedure**. >> In Lenneberg's summary of cases, nearly all children who lost the left hemisphere before the teens kept or improved their speech, while every adult who lost it had permanent aphasia. || These outcomes led Lenneberg to hypothesize that a key factor in **closing the critical period** is the development of adult-like **cerebral dominance**, the specialization of one hemisphere, usually the left, for language. >> In a young child language is not yet fixed in the left hemisphere, so the right hemisphere can take over.
13|Systematic studies of children with **perinatal strokes**, which happen from the prenatal period through the first four weeks after birth, make the findings on recovery more complicated. >> Outcomes are **clearly much better than for adults with similar injuries**, but the injury still leaves effects. || **Early language deficits appear in both right-hemisphere and left-hemisphere patients**, and **some deficits are worse for children with right-hemisphere damage**. >> That is the opposite of the adult pattern, because adult language problems mainly follow left-hemisphere damage. || **Language outcomes are generally in the low-normal range**, which suggests a **persistent delay rather than a catching up**. >> Early plasticity is real but limited, because a young brain can reorganize to support language while the reorganized system may not work as well as the original would have.
14|**Curtiss and colleagues** studied the **effects of early deprivation** through the case of **Genie**, who was thirteen when researchers first met her and so thin that she looked six or seven. || From the age of twenty months she was confined to a small room under increasing physical restraint, harnessed to an infant's potty chair or kept in a sleeping bag inside a covered crib. >> She was **physically punished by her father if she made any sounds**, and her father and older brother never spoke to her, though they sometimes barked at her like dogs. >> The home had no television or radio, and she had almost no contact with other people. || When she was hospitalized for malnutrition, Genie could not stand up straight or chew food, and she **did not speak, cry or produce any vocal sounds**. >> Her lack of language came with severe physical, social and emotional deprivation, so the effect of missing language input cannot be separated from her other hardships.
15|During the **four years** that her language was observed, Genie **learned quite a bit**. >> She came to **comprehend hundreds of words**, **some aspects of morphology** and **some prepositions**, and she was **particularly good with plurals**. >> **Morphology** is the system of meaningful word parts, such as the plural ending. || A test of prepositions had her move **buttons and plastic boxes** according to spoken instructions such as “Put the red box behind the blue box.” >> She gave **more correct than incorrect responses for every preposition** tested, and she was strongest on **in** and **on**. || Some language can therefore be **acquired late**, because this learning took place after puberty, long after the window Lenneberg proposed. >> The learning was uneven across different parts of language.
16|Genie's **production lagged severely behind her comprehension**. >> **Some lag is typical of development**, because all children understand more than they can say, and Genie had also been **punished for making sounds** as a child. || Her **grammatical abilities developed much more slowly than her vocabulary**, as a test of **conjunctions** with **five familiar objects in a row** made clear. >> She was asked to point in response to items such as “Show me the fork and pencil,” “Show me the crayon or the knife” and “Show me either the spoon or the crayon.” >> **And** requires pointing to every named object, while **or** requires pointing to just one, which depends on understanding a logical relationship. || At first she treated **or** like **and** and pointed to both objects, and later she told them apart but never understood **either/or**. >> She answered almost every **and** sentence correctly and got most **or** sentences wrong, so her grammar stayed weak despite a vocabulary of hundreds of words.
17|Genie's case leaves **lots of questions**, so one case cannot separate the possible causes of her difficulty. >> Genie may have been **learning disabled** from birth, and **neglect and abuse** may have left residual impacts on her social development that affected learning beyond the missing language input. >> **Funding to study her was pulled after four years**, so nobody can know what she might have learned after a longer time. || Nonetheless, the **disparity between her vocabulary development and her grammatical development** is widely interpreted as suggestive that **grammar learning in a first language is specifically impaired after a sensitive period**. >> She learned hundreds of words but made little progress with grammar, so on this interpretation vocabulary can be learned at any age while grammar depends on early exposure.
18|**Mayberry, Lock and Kazmi** studied deaf people who acquired a first language late. **Many deaf children do not get sign language input** but have an **otherwise typical upbringing**, so unlike Genie their only unusual experience is the lack of early accessible language. || Children aged nine to fifteen **have difficulty learning American Sign Language (ASL) if it is their first language**, while a **comparison group** who went deaf after initially learning a spoken language **do much better**. >> **These differences persist into adulthood**, and adults with no early language recalled complex ASL sentences about half as well as adults with an early spoken language. >> In judging whether complex English sentences were grammatical, deaf adults with no early language were close to chance, while deaf adults with ASL in infancy matched hearing adults with an early spoken language. || The advantage comes from **having any language early**, signed or spoken, because early language provides the foundation for learning languages later.
19|The evidence supports age effects in learning a first language, or **L1**, but it does not settle whether the window is a **“critical period”** or a **“sensitive period”**. || **Recovery from stroke, and certainly hemispherectomy, looks very different in childhood and adulthood**, because children can develop language after damage that would leave adults permanently aphasic. >> **Deprivation of input does not appear to extend the critical period**, because Genie and the deaf adults who learned sign late both have lasting difficulty, especially with grammar. >> The window seems to follow a maturational schedule whether or not language has been experienced. || All of these data come from **highly unusual cases with lots of other things going on**, so firm conclusions are difficult to reach. >> There is also a **lack of animal models**, and **gene knockout** experiments, which disable specific genes to test their role, cannot be done with people.
