2|**L2 learning**, meaning second-language learning, works as a **model system** for sensitive period research, which is a common and easily studied situation used to answer questions about a process that is hard to study directly. || **Lenneberg** proposed that the sensitive period had to do with the development of **cerebral dominance**, in which one hemisphere, usually the left, becomes specialized for language. >> This is also described as a change in **language laterality**, which is how much language relies on one side of the brain, so the window closes once language is fixed in the left hemisphere. || The theory rested on a rough alignment of **brain development** with **language milestones**, and on a review of **resilience after brain injury or surgery**. >> Cortical neurons grow quickly for about two years and then level off, and children who lost the left hemisphere often developed language while adults did not regain it. >> The theory is about the brain's **maturational state**, its stage of physical development, because a young brain can move language to other tissue and a mature, specialized brain has fewer ways to do so.
3|Most of what is known about **L1 sensitive periods** comes from **idiosyncratic cases**, meaning cases particular to one individual, or from relatively rare phenomena. >> Cases such as children recovering from surgery that removed a hemisphere, or Genie, also involve injury, illness or abuse, so the true cause of an outcome is hard to identify. || In contrast, **many people learn second languages** at different times in their lives, often with **somewhat limited success**, so researchers can study large groups who started at every age. || Those limits are used to reason about the **timing of the sensitive period**, because if later starters reliably end up lower, the age where the decline appears could mark when the period closes. >> The first language complicates this, because every L2 learner already knows a language that shapes how the new one is learned.
4|**Johnson and Newport** asked whether the **critical period** that **Lenneberg** proposed for first-language acquisition, running from early infancy to puberty, also extends to second-language learning. >> If it does, young children should learn a second language better than adults and reach higher final proficiency. || They compared the English of native **Korean or Chinese speakers** who had arrived in the United States at ages ranging from early childhood to adulthood, using a **grammaticality judgment task** on a wide variety of English structures. || Earlier arrivals had a **clear and strong advantage**, because performance was **linearly related to age of arrival up to puberty** and was **low, highly variable and unrelated to age of arrival** after puberty. >> The effect appeared on every structure tested and was not an accidental result of experience with English, motivation, self-consciousness or American identification. >> The authors concluded that the critical period **extends its effects to second-language acquisition**.
5|**Johnson and Newport** devised a **grammaticality judgment test (GJT)**, in which participants listened to recorded sentences and said whether each one was grammatical, with **no time limit**. >> A grammaticality judgment is a decision about whether a sentence follows the rules of the language. || Each ungrammatical sentence breaks one rule of English, so each example set tests one rule. >> “The farmer bought two pig at the market” tests **plural marking**, and “The little boy is speak to a policeman” tests the **progressive -ing** form of the verb. >> “When Sam will fix his car?” tests **wh-question** word order, and “What they sell at the corner store?” tests the need for the helping verb “do” in a question. || Three of the sets test articles and other **determiners**. >> “Tom is reading book in the bathtub” is missing a needed article, and “A boys are going to the zoo this Saturday” has a determiner that does not agree with the noun in number. >> “Larry went the home after the party” has an article in a spot where English allows none.
6|The **Johnson and Newport** data relate each participant's grammar score to **age of arrival (AOA)**, and they are split at puberty, the end of the critical period that Lenneberg proposed. >> A **correlation** measures how closely two variables are related, and a strong negative one means that one variable falls steadily as the other rises. || Among **early arrivals**, who came as children, the **correlation is strongly negative**, so scores fall steadily with each later year of arrival. >> Those who arrived youngest score near the maximum, and age of arrival predicts most of the score in this group. || Among **late arrivals**, who came after puberty, the **correlation is weak**, and scores are lower overall and widely scattered. >> Some adults score nearly as well as older child arrivals while others who arrived at the same age score far lower, so age of arrival predicts little among adults.
7|The **Johnson and Newport** data have three main features. >> **Age of arrival is strongly related to accuracy**, with earlier arrival going with higher scores. >> **Early learners**, up to about age eight, are often **near-perfect**, which means native-like. || Late arrivals have a **nearly random distribution of accuracy**, which means that among adults age of arrival and score are only weakly related. >> Late arrivals still scored well above chance, but two adults who arrived at the same age could end up far apart. || Children who arrived after about age eight reach steadily lower levels, so the **decline begins well before puberty**.
8|**Johnson and Newport** argue that the data are consistent with a **sensitive period** for L2 learning, which they call a **“critical period,”** for two reasons. || First, **ultimate attainment**, the final level a learner reaches, depends on age of arrival, which is consistent with a **loss of language-learning ability over development**. >> The decline starts around age seven or eight and not at puberty, so the timing does not entirely fit what Lenneberg proposed, but he was working with a large gap in the data between early childhood and adolescence. || Second, **attainment in late acquisition is all over the place**, so they argue that after the period closes attainment depends on **idiosyncratic learning abilities**, such as memory, motivation or analytical skill, and not on the usual language acquisition processes. >> Children share one natural acquisition process and so reach similar levels at similar ages, while adults must lean on their own abilities, which differ a great deal between people.
9|The **“critical period” hypothesis** has two versions, and both say that early in life humans have a **superior capacity for acquiring languages**. >> On the **exercise hypothesis**, the capacity disappears or declines only if it is not exercised early, so a person who learned an L1 normally should learn an L2 as well as a child does. >> On the **maturational state hypothesis**, the capacity disappears or declines with maturation whether or not it was used, so even a person with a normal L1 should learn an L2 less well as an adult. || **Johnson and Newport** see their data as more consistent with the **maturational state hypothesis**, because every participant had a normal first language and later starters still learned the L2 less well. || The L1 data cannot separate the two versions, because in cases like Genie the capacity was never exercised and both versions predict poor learning. >> Only L2 learners have already exercised the capacity before learning another language.
10|**Test design** is a methodological issue, because the **grammaticality judgment task is very different from fluent language use**, where people speak and understand quickly without stopping to think about rules. >> The worry is that people use **metalinguistic** or **explicit knowledge**, which is conscious knowledge about language, such as knowing that English plurals add “-s.” || **Ellis** contrasts **implicit knowledge** with **explicit knowledge**. >> Implicit knowledge is intuitive, **procedural**, variable but systematic, reached by **automatic processing**, used in fluent performance, **nonverbalizable**, and potentially only learnable **within the critical period**. >> Explicit knowledge is conscious, **declarative**, often anomalous and inconsistent, reached by **controlled processing**, used when a speaker is planning or having difficulty, **verbalizable**, and learnable at **any age**. || An untimed GJT lets people draw on explicit knowledge, so it may miss the implicit knowledge that the critical period is supposed to affect.
11|One suggestion is that the task demands of the **untimed GJT** make it a better measure of **explicit learning of “metalinguistic” knowledge** than of grammar acquisition. || **Ellis** compared five tests on their design features. >> **Imitation**, **oral narrative** and the **timed GJT** rely on feel and are done under time pressure, so they require no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **untimed GJT** and the **metalanguage** test rely on rules and have no time pressure, so metalinguistic knowledge can be used, and only imitation and oral narrative focus on meaning and not on form. || Only two relationships with background measures were significant. >> **Starting age** was related to the **timed GJT**, and **years of formal instruction** were related to the **untimed GJT** with ungrammatical items. >> Formal instruction teaches explicit rules, so an age effect found with an untimed GJT may partly reflect taught knowledge and not natural grammar acquisition.
12|**Transfer**, which means using first-language knowledge while learning a second language, is both a methodological and a theoretical issue. >> Languages that are **typologically more similar**, meaning similar in structure such as word order and word forms, can benefit more from transfer. >> Cognates and loan words such as French **auto**, **rivière** and **bicyclette** and Japanese **korokke** and **monoreru** give learners familiar words to build on. || **Johnson and Newport** tested people with **English as L2** and **Chinese or Korean as L1**, languages that differ greatly from English, so these learners could get little help from transfer. || If the languages were more similar, learners could reuse much of what they know, and their scores might relate to age differently. >> The age pattern would then depend on the particular languages involved as well as on age.
13|**Not all aspects of the grammar are equally difficult**. **Johnson and Newport** compared errors on twelve types of English rules across native speakers and groups who arrived at different ages. >> The rule types are determiners, plurals, **verb subcategorization** (which kinds of words or phrases may follow a verb), past tense, pronouns, particles, auxiliaries, third-person singular, wh-questions, yes/no questions, word order and progressive -ing. >> Native speakers and the youngest arrivals make very few errors of any type, while the latest arrivals struggle most with **determiners** and **plurals**. || Some of this looks like the **order in which children learn language**, because **progressive -ing** and **word order** are easiest for late learners and are among the first structures children master. || Some could be **transfer**, because **Chinese has no determiners**, so its speakers have no L1 category for “a” and “the.” >> Children learn **questions** very late, yet questions are among the easiest structures for late learners, who may bring general cognitive skills or L1 knowledge to them.
14|**Birdsong and Molis** repeated the Johnson and Newport study with **Spanish speakers learning English** and found almost the reverse pattern. >> For Chinese and Korean speakers, the correlation with age of arrival was strong among early arrivals and weak among late arrivals. >> For Spanish speakers, it was **weak among early arrivals**, who scored near the top regardless of age, and **strong among late arrivals**, who declined steadily with age. || **Maturational constraints** are limits on learning caused by the brain's development, and a fixed maturational schedule should give the same pattern for every first language. || The pattern changed with the first language instead, so the age effect depends on the **language pairing** as well as on age. >> Spanish is much closer to English than Chinese or Korean, so **transfer** may help Spanish speakers reach native-like grammar even when they arrive late in childhood.
15|These findings may not even count as “conclusions,” but overall **L2 attainment is lower for older learners**. || How much lower depends on **what constructions are tested**, **how they are tested** and the **language pairing**. >> Determiners are far harder for late learners than word order, timed and untimed tests can disagree, and Spanish speakers differ from Chinese or Korean speakers in their age patterns. || Several things are **hard to dissociate**. >> **Age of acquisition** goes together with the amount and quality of experience, and **transfer effects** are mixed with what was learned in L2 acquisition. >> **Competence** is underlying knowledge and **performance** is how well it is used on a task, so a score measures performance and may not reveal competence directly.
