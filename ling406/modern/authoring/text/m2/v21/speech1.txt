2|**Speech perception** is the process of recovering a linguistic message from the sound of speech, so listeners pull a message as clear as “I can see you” out of a signal that seems like a mess. >> The energy of each sound runs into the next, because the tongue, lips and other articulators in the **vocal tract** move without pause and their movements overlap. || Listeners also get **the same message from very different physical stimuli**, because voices and vocal tract sizes differ and yet the same word is heard. >> Across many speakers each vowel covers a broad range of formant values, and the ranges **overlap**, so one pair of values can be different vowels for different speakers. || Recognizing speech means finding a **stable linguistic identity** inside enormous physical variation.
3|The premise is that listeners must **turn a highly variable, analog signal into something more abstract** that the language system can operate on. >> An **analog signal** varies continuously, so it has no built-in markers of where one speech sound stops and the next starts. || The abstract form is a **symbolic representation**, which identifies a unit such as the **phoneme**, a sound category that distinguishes words, and sets fine physical detail aside. >> /b/ in “bat” and /p/ in “pat” are different phonemes because swapping them changes the word, while many slightly different versions of /b/ count as one phoneme. || **Categorical perception** is thought to reflect this **“translation” from physical to symbolic**, because listeners are less sensitive to differences inside a category and more sensitive to equally large differences that cross a boundary. >> It is one account of the translation, and alternative ideas exist.
4|The **vocal tract** is the passage from the larynx to the lips, and according to **Ladefoged** its shape is related in systematic ways to **which resonant frequencies get amplified**. >> A **resonant frequency** is one that a space naturally strengthens, so changing the shape of the vocal tract changes which frequencies get the biggest boost. || **Formants** are the resonant frequencies the vocal tract emphasizes, and every vowel has its own combination of them. >> From **heed** through hid, head and had the tongue lowers and the first two formants draw closer, and from **hod** through hawed, hood and who’d the tongue moves back, the lips round, and both formants sit low and close. >> A vowel is defined by the **relationship among its resonances**, which stays similar across speakers. || A **formant transition** is a change in a formant’s frequency over time, which happens as the mouth moves between a consonant and a vowel. >> The steady part of a vowel tells about the vowel, and the moving part tells about the neighboring sound.
5|A **spectrogram** maps acoustic energy over time and frequency, and the bands of strong energy in each vowel are its **formants**. >> The recordings are Rob Hagiwara’s samples of English vowels. || The words **bead, bid, bade, bed, bad, bod, baud, bode, “bood” and booed** all start with /b/ and end with /d/, so the differences between them come mainly from the vowels. >> **F1** is the lowest formant and **F2** is the next one up, and the pattern of these two carries most of what tells vowels apart. || In **bead**, F1 is very low and F2 is very high, and through bid, bade, bed and bad F1 rises while F2 falls, so the two move closer together. >> In the back rounded vowels such as **bode, “bood” and booed**, F2 drops low and sits near F1, so booed pairs a low F1 with a low F2.
6|**Source-filter synthesis** builds speech from two parts, the **source**, which is the sound energy the voice produces, and the **filter**, which is how the vocal tract shapes that energy. >> Pulling them apart in **Praat**, a free program for speech analysis, makes it possible to change one while the other stays fixed. || The recording is a speaker saying **/u/**, the vowel in “booed,” and its **spectrogram** breaks the sound into frequency components while the **waveform** records air pressure over time. || This /u/ has a **low first formant** and a **very low second formant**, which fits its back rounded quality. >> If changing only the filter changes the vowel, then the vowel’s identity must lie in the filter, which is its pattern of formants.
7|**Praat can find formants automatically** by following each resonance through the recording, and in this /u/ it identifies **five formants**. >> Several resonances run at the same time, and each one is a frequency component of the same sound. || **Only the first three formants are usually important for phonemic processing**, because F1, F2 and F3 carry most of the information that separates phonemes, while higher formants depend more on the individual voice. || The example deals with only **the first two formants**, because changing just these two is enough to turn one vowel into another. >> The higher formants stay fixed, so any change that listeners hear comes from F1 or F2 alone.
8|The **source** can be **extracted** from the recorded /u/ by building a model of the filter from the measured formants and removing that filter’s effect from the recording. >> What is left is a **more-or-less “unfiltered” version of the voice**, and it is only more or less unfiltered because the separation is an estimate. || The extracted source **sounds like a buzz**, because it keeps its energy and pitch but loses the resonance pattern that made it sound like /u/. >> Pitch survives because it comes from vocal fold vibration, and vowel quality disappears because it comes from the filter. || The separation is **a little bit like doing a multiple regression analysis**, in which the contribution of one predictor is estimated and removed so that the rest can be examined. >> Once the source is isolated, new filters can be applied to it.
9|Three **filters** can be applied to the same source, and each filter is a resonance pattern that specifies which frequencies get more emphasis and which get less. || The **first filter** is just the first four formants of the original /u/, so applying it should bring back the original vowel. >> The **second filter** raises **the first formant a little**, and the **third filter** raises **the second formant a lot**, while the other formants stay roughly in place. || The **source stays the same** for all three filters, so any change in vowel quality comes from the filter. >> No new speaker or recording is needed, and comparing the outputs isolates what a change in the lowest resonance does compared with a change in the second.
10|**Multiplying the source by the filters gives new vowels**, because the output is the source shaped by the filter and each filter boosts a different set of frequencies. >> The source provides the raw sound energy, and the filter decides how much of that energy stays strong in each frequency region. || With the **original filter** the result keeps the quality of /u/, with **F1 raised a little** the vowel changes, and with **F2 raised a lot** the back rounded /u/ moves toward a more front vowel. || All three outputs share the same buzz, pitch and timing, yet they sound like different vowels, so **vowel quality comes from the filter**. >> A researcher can change vowel quality by swapping only the filter, without asking a speaker to record each version.
11|Formant values are only **partly** set by the intended vowel, because they also depend on **anatomical features** that differ between speakers, on **rates of speech**, and on **context**. Context means the **phones**, or particular spoken sounds, before and after a vowel. >> A longer vocal tract gives lower resonances, fast speech gives the articulators less time to reach their targets, and the tongue and lips are already heading toward the next sound. >> Across many speakers the ranges for different vowels overlap, so no single pair of frequencies defines a vowel for everyone. || Formant values **nonetheless** give a pretty good description of **vowel space**, the layout of a language’s vowels by their formant frequencies, especially for **a specific talker speaking carefully**. >> All else being equal, reasonable approximations of vowels can be made by manipulating **steady-state formants**, the resonances in the stable middle of a vowel.
12|The **pattern playback synthesizer** turns a painted spectrogram pattern back into sound, so researchers paint bands on a transparent sheet wherever they want acoustic energy and the machine plays the pattern aloud. || The machine shines light through a **cylindrical lens** onto a spinning **tone wheel**, whose rings modulate the light at many frequencies at once. >> A lens and **mirror** send that light onto the painted **spectrogram**, where the painted areas pass along the matching frequencies. >> A **light collector** and **photocell** turn the light into an electrical signal, and an **amplifier** and **loudspeaker** turn that signal into sound. || A researcher can change a single formant band or transition while everything else stays the same, so any change in what listeners hear can be traced to **the one painted change**. >> Series of patterns differing in tiny steps revealed how formant transitions signal consonants.
13|**Stop consonants are distinguished by formant transitions**, which is sensible because the audible part of a /b/, /d/ or /g/ happens **while the jaw is opening**. >> During the opening the vocal tract changes shape quickly, so the formants glide from the consonant’s position toward the vowel’s position. || **Liberman and colleagues** used the **pattern playback synthesizer** to create speech-like sounds with two formants that replicated these transitions. >> The lower formant was nearly the same in every stimulus, while the upper formant started at a different frequency in each stimulus and then settled at the same vowel level. || The patterns kept the vowel the same and changed only where the upper transition began, so the responses of listeners could be tied to the transition. >> A **rising** transition was heard as /b/, a roughly **flat** one as /d/ and a **falling** one as /g/.
14|A **speech continuum** is a series of sounds that change step by step along one controlled acoustic property. >> Here **F1 is kept constant** and the **F2 onset frequency**, where the second formant begins its transition, is **varied linearly** from the first stimulus to the last. >> Because the change is linear, **adjacent stimuli differ by the same amount across the entire continuum**. || Along the series the F2 transition goes from rising, to nearly flat, to falling as the onset climbs past the vowel frequency. || Every step is physically equal, so the continuum fairly tests whether perception follows the acoustics or the categories. >> A purely acoustic listener would hear every step as the same size, while a categorical listener would hear steps across a boundary as different and steps within a category as alike.
15|An **ideal observer model** predicts how a listener would respond under a particular account, and this one assumes perception is **non-categorical**, tracking the acoustic signal gradually with no sharp boundaries. >> It is a baseline that real data can be compared against. || Its first prediction is that **categorization gets “blurry” as you move away from category prototypes**, where a **prototype** is the clearest example of a category. >> The endpoints are /b/ and /g/ with /d/ in the middle, and **stimulus ambiguity scales with the distance from the prototype**. >> The second stimulus would be heard as /b/ about three quarters of the time and as /d/ about one quarter of the time. || **Labeling** would change smoothly, with responses for one category fading out in a straight slope while the neighboring category fades in, so two labels are equally likely halfway between prototypes.
16|The ideal observer can also assume that perception is **linear**, so equal acoustic differences are perceived as equally large, and this predicts that **discrimination performance should be independent of labeling**. >> **Labeling** asks a listener to identify a sound as /b/, /d/ or /g/, while **discrimination** asks only whether two sounds are “same” or “different.” || Labeling **has to have a “peaked” function**, because there are three categories and listeners are choosing between labels, so each category’s responses rise toward its prototype and fall away. || Every discrimination pair is two steps apart with the same physical separation, so if perception of differences in F2 onset were constant over the whole continuum, **discrimination accuracy would also be constant**. >> A flat pattern would mean boundaries play no role in hearing differences, and a peaked pattern would mean boundaries make some differences easier to hear.
17|**Liberman and colleagues** had listeners label each stimulus on the continuum from /b/ through /d/ to /g/ and judge pairs that were one, two or three steps apart, and the results are **categorical perception**. || Labeling had **sharp changes**, because the first few stimuli were heard as /b/ almost every time and responses then switched almost completely to /d/, and later just as quickly to /g/. >> The narrow region where the label flips is the **category boundary**, and these switches are far more abrupt than the gradual slopes the ideal observer predicted. || **Listeners were best at discriminating sounds near the category boundaries**, while pairs inside one category were discriminated poorly, close to **chance**, the level expected from guessing. >> Larger steps raised accuracy overall, but the peaks stayed at the boundaries. >> Discrimination predicted from labeling alone captured much of the pattern, but real accuracy was often a little higher, especially within categories, so listeners keep some sensitivity beyond the labels.

19|Data from students in prior years tested categorical perception along a continuum from **beach** to **peach**, two words that differ in their first consonant, /b/ or /p/. >> Each listener did an **identification** task, labeling every stimulus, and a **discrimination** task, judging pairs two steps apart. || For the first listener, identification stays on one word for the early stimuli and **switches to the other word** around the middle, with mixed responses only near the switch. >> That listener discriminates best for the pairs that straddle the identification boundary, and pairs well inside one category are hard to tell apart even though their physical separation is the same. || The second listener **switches labels later** along the continuum and discriminates best near that later boundary. >> For each listener, the best discrimination falls close to that person’s **own identification boundary**.
20|**Not everyone has exactly the same category boundary**, so a pair that straddles one person’s boundary may stay inside one category for someone else. >> When individual boundaries differ, averaging across the group can flatten the boundary peak. || **In general, discrimination is best for stimuli perceived as being in different categories**, because the same size of difference is far easier to hear when the two sounds are heard as different words. || To make the lab doable in a short time, the discrimination experiment included **only “different” trials**, so the correct answer on every trial was “different” if the listener could discriminate the pair. >> Accuracy is the share of pairs heard as different, so low accuracy on within-category pairs means listeners often failed to hear differences that were really there.
21|More results from prior years compare a continuum from **bear** to **pear** with the one from **beach** to **peach**. >> For bear and pear, identification switches fairly clearly, but discrimination jumps up and down from one pair to the next, including a miss on a pair that crosses the boundary. >> Another listener’s data for beach and peach fluctuate even more, with identification zigzagging instead of switching once. || The one iron-clad law of experimental research is that **stuff happens**, so responses to a carefully built continuum can still bounce around because of noise in a few responses. || The experiment could have been better in two ways. >> Only “different” trials were used, so nothing measured how often listeners said “different” when the sounds were identical. >> The short session gave each listener few trials per pair, so a single response could move accuracy a great deal.
22|Categorical perception helps by giving **stable word recognition**, even though being worse at hearing differences within a category seems like throwing away useful detail. >> Every production of a phoneme differs physically from every other, because of speakers, speaking rates and neighboring sounds. >> If each small acoustic change were heard as a new identity, everyday versions of one word would sound like unrelated sounds. || Reduced sensitivity within a category lets many acoustic versions count as **one phoneme**, and that variation is tolerated because it leaves the word unchanged. || Differences **between categories** stay easy to hear, so the distinctions that change words are kept and the listener is free to focus on the differences that change meaning.

24|**Categorical perception in infants** can be tested even though infants cannot name sounds, because infants react to novelty and researchers can watch for a behavior that changes when an infant notices something new. || In the **conditioned head turn procedure**, from Janet Werker’s lab, an infant learns to turn the head when a sound changes and earns a visual reward such as a moving toy. >> After the infant learns this link, a head turn following a change means the infant detected it. || The results of interest come from **high-amplitude sucking**, in which the infant sucks on a pacifier and strong sucks make a sound play. >> As the same sound repeats the infant gets used to it, which is **habituation**, and sucking slows. >> If a new sound makes sucking **increase again**, the infant has noticed the change.
25|**Eimas and colleagues** tested categorical perception in **four-month-old infants** with **high-amplitude sucking**, using sounds that varied in **voice onset time (VOT)**, the delay between the release of a consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration. >> Adults hear a short VOT as a voiced consonant such as /b/ and a longer VOT as a voiceless consonant such as /p/. || Infants heard one syllable until sucking slowed, and then the VOT changed by the same amount in two conditions or did not change in a control condition. >> In one condition the change **crossed** the adult category boundary, and in the other both sounds came from the **same** adult category. || Sucking **recovered** sharply after the change across the boundary, but it kept falling after the change within a category, much like the control group. >> The two changes were equal in size, so the difference comes from crossing the boundary, and infants **perceive voicing categorically** before they can produce or label speech.
26|**Perceptual narrowing** is the process in which experience tunes perception to the sounds of the surrounding language while sensitivity to some distinctions from other languages declines. || **Werker and Tees** tested English-learning infants at three ages on two **non-English contrasts**, a **Hindi** contrast between two “ta”-like sounds and a **Salish** contrast between “ki” and “qi.” >> At six to eight months most infants reached the discrimination criterion on both contrasts, at eight to ten months fewer did, and at ten to twelve months very few did. >> Hindi-learning and Salish-learning infants succeeded nearly every time on the contrast from their own language. || These results are described as **perceptual reorganization during the first year of life**. >> Infants who hear a contrast in their own language keep it, so the decline depends on **language experience**.
27|A **very compelling narrative** links these findings in three claims. >> First, speech perception depends on **categorical perception abilities that are a special property of the language system**. >> Second, these abilities are **present very early in development**, as the infant sucking and head turn experiments suggest. || Third, children **start off as “citizens of the world,”** able to hear **all** contrasts, and then **rapidly tune their perceptual systems to their specific language**. >> Tuning means that contrasts of the surrounding language stay available while contrasts from other languages fade, as in the Werker and Tees results. || The narrative proposes broad, language-specific abilities at birth and treats development mostly as narrowing from that broad start.
28|The compelling narrative meets **some issues**, and three of them concern **categorical perception**. >> It is **not limited to language**, because it also appears with nonspeech sounds, and it is **not limited to humans**, because nonhuman animals also perceive speech sounds categorically. >> It is **task-dependent**, because choosing between labels makes responses more categorical than a task that measures fine acoustic differences. || **Infant speech perception** is **not actually “universal,”** because some contrasts are harder for infants to detect than others from early on. || Together these issues weaken the strong claim that every speech distinction starts out equally available in a mechanism devoted only to language.
29|**Cutting and Rosner** tested categorical perception with **non-speech sounds** that varied in **rise time**, which is how quickly a sound reaches full loudness after it begins. >> A **rapid rise time** sounds **plucked**, like a plucked string, and a **slow rise time** sounds **bowed**, like a bowed string. >> The stimuli included sawtooth waves, sine waves and speech syllables, each with short and long rise times. || For the sawtooth waves, listeners labeled short rise times as pluck and long rise times as bow, and the label switched over a narrow range in the middle of the continuum. >> Discrimination was only a little above chance for most pairs, but it **peaked** right where the labels switched. || The link between a label boundary and better discrimination appears for a sound distinction that has **nothing to do with language**.
30|**Jusczyk and colleagues** tested whether **infants** have categorical perception of **non-speech sounds**, using high-amplitude sucking to measure whether a change was detected. >> Infants heard one sound repeatedly until sucking declined, and then the sound was shifted in one of three conditions, **no change**, a change **within categories** or a change **between categories**. || After no change and after a within-category change, sucking stayed low, but after a between-category change it jumped back up and then declined again as the new sound became familiar. >> This mirrors the pattern that Eimas and colleagues found for speech. || Infants cannot be drawing on knowledge of words or phonemes when they respond to plucked and bowed sounds, so their categorical response must come from **more general auditory processing**, which could also underlie their response to speech.
31|**Ohlemiller and colleagues**, following earlier work by **Kuhl and Miller**, found categorical perception of speech sounds in **chinchillas**. >> The animals were trained with **operant conditioning**, learning in which consequences change how often a behavior occurs, that either the zero-lag stimulus or the long-lag stimulus was an **SD**, a **discriminative stimulus** signaling that a response will earn food. >> The lag is **voice onset time**, and humans hear the zero lag as /ga/ and the long lag as /ka/. || When tested on sounds in between, chinchillas labeled short VOTs as /ga/ almost every time and long VOTs almost never, and the switch had the **same steep, categorical shape** as in human listeners. >> A louder **burst**, the brief noise at the release of a stop, and louder **aspiration noise**, the breathy noise after a voiceless release, shifted the boundary toward shorter VOTs or made the switch steeper, for one chinchilla and one human. || Animals without human language can sort a speech continuum into categories, which argues against the view that categorical perception is special to human language.
32|Some contrasts are **less salient**, meaning harder to notice, than others for infants. >> The syllables **[ma]**, **[na]** and **[ŋa]** differ in **place of articulation**, which is where in the mouth the sound is made, and [ŋa] begins with the sound at the end of “sing.” || The **looking time** of infants was compared on same trials and change trials, and longer looking on change trials means the infant noticed the change. >> English-hearing infants at four to five months looked longer on change trials for the native contrast between [ma] and [na], but not for the nonnative contrast between [na] and [ŋa]. >> **Filipino-learning infants**, who hear [na] and [ŋa] as a native contrast, did not detect the change at six to eight months but did by ten to twelve months. || Experience **strengthens** sensitivity to some native contrasts, so development includes gains as well as losses.
