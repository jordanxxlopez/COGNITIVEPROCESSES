2|Speech is **messy** because **there are no physical boundaries between words**, so **word segmentation**, the job of working out where one word stops and the next starts, is hard. >> In “I hope it’s in my desk,” the sound energy of each word flows straight into the next word with no silent gap. || The **pauses** that do occur come from **closures**, moments when the vocal tract is fully blocked as for /p/, /t/, /d/ and /k/, and closures are **as likely within words as between words**. >> A listener who treated every silence as a boundary would split some words and merge others. || Adults are still **really good at finding words** in this “sea of sounds” because they can lean on words they already know. >> **Learning to do this** is the harder problem, because infants must discover the words in the stream before they can learn what the words mean.
3|Several **cues to word boundaries** exist, but none works for every word. Some words **appear in isolation**, as in “Hello” or “Stop,” but not all words do. >> **Phonotactics**, the sound sequences a language allows, helps because **some sequences of phonemes are very unlikely within words**, such as /db/ in “wordboundary” and /tʃk/ in “speechcode.” The /vm/ in “movement” is a caution, because movement is one word. || **Stress patterns** help because **most English words are stressed on the first syllable**. **Jusczyk and colleagues** familiarized infants about seven and a half months old with one word and then played them passages. >> Infants familiarized with “kingdom,” stressed on its first syllable, **preferred the kingdom passage**, while infants familiarized with “device,” stressed on its second syllable, **showed no such preference**. || **Stress may signal the beginnings of words** in connected speech, so an infant who starts a word at each stressed syllable finds kingdom but cuts device wrongly. >> This cue fails for some English words and in other languages, because languages put stress in different places.
4|**Transitional probabilities** are another cue to word boundaries, and learners pick them up through **statistical learning**, which means learning the patterns in how often things occur together. || In “pretty baby,” the syllable **/ti/** is **very strongly predicted by the syllable /prɪ/**, because pretty is a common word and “ty” very often follows “pre.” >> This is **less true for syllable pairs across words**, because many different words can follow “ty,” so “ba” is poorly predicted. || The frequency of “pretty” divided by the frequency of “pre” is **far greater** than the frequency of “tyba” divided by the frequency of “ty.” >> Syllables inside a word predict each other strongly and syllables that cross a word boundary predict each other weakly, so a learner who counts which syllable follows which can find the joints in speech.
5|Stated abstractly, **the likelihood of syllable B following syllable A is greater within words than between words**, and that likelihood is the **transitional probability** from A to B. >> Inside a word the same syllables always come in the same order, while almost any word can come next at a boundary, so a drop in transitional probability is a good guess for a boundary. || The cue is **probabilistic**, so it works most of the time but not every time. >> “What’s that in the road a/head?” can be heard as “a head” or “ahead” with identical sounds, so the meaning of the whole sentence decides the boundary. >> “I scream you scream we all scream for ice cream” works because “I scream” and “ice cream” have almost the same sounds. || Transitional probabilities work alongside stress, phonotactics and words heard alone, and listeners fall back on **context** when the cues clash or are missing.
6|**Saffran and colleagues** separated **statistical information from other cues** to find out whether **transitional probabilities** alone can be used to identify words, so the stimuli were tightly controlled. || The speech was **monotone**, with no changes in pitch, duration or amplitude, so stress could not mark word beginnings. >> Every syllable was a **simple CV syllable**, one consonant followed by one vowel, so there were **no phonotactic cues**. >> The syllables were **presented continuously** with **no pauses**, so silence could not signal a boundary. || The researchers built an **artificial language** of **three-syllable “words”** such as tokibu, gikoba and gopila, and joined them in random order into one long stream. >> The only clue to where each word began and ended was the transitional probability between syllables, so infants who find the words must be using that cue alone.
7|The stream of the artificial language has **no breaks at all**, as in “tokibugikobagopilatipolutokibu,” and this unbroken run of syllables is exactly what the infant hears. >> Four words, tokibu, gikoba, gopila and tipolu, are **hidden in the stream**, and each recurs many times in random order. || The syllables still follow **regular patterns**, because inside tokibu “to” is always followed by “ki” and “ki” is always followed by “bu.” >> After “bu” the stream may continue with “gi,” “go” or “ti,” depending on which word comes next. || Everything needed to find the words is in the stream, because a learner who **tracks which syllables follow which** discovers that some pairs are fully predictable and others are not.
8|Marking each of the four words in its own color makes **tokibu**, **gikoba**, **gopila** and **tipolu** visible to a reader, but the infant gets no such marking and hears one unbroken run of syllables. || Each word keeps the **same inner order** every time while its **neighbors keep changing**, so transitional probabilities are high inside each word and lower across word edges. >> The syllables “to,” “ki” and “bu” always come in that order, but tokibu may be followed by gikoba, gopila or tipolu. || The word edges are exactly where **transitional probability drops**, so a learner who notices where predictability drops recovers where one word ends and the next begins. >> The signal itself contains no spaces, so the boundaries come only from which syllables reliably belong together.
9|After a long stream of syllables, infants are trivially more familiar with sequences they have heard than with ones they have not, so a preference for heard sequences could reflect **familiarity** alone. The test added two details to remove that possibility. >> **Half of the words were repeated more frequently** than the other half. >> The test compared words with **“part-words”** that were **matched for frequency** with real words, where a part-word is a three-syllable sequence joining the end of one word to the start of the next. || In “tokibu gikoba gopila gikoba tokibu gopila tokibu gikoba gikoba tokibu,” the word **GOPILA** and the part-word **BU-GIKO** each appear twice. >> BU occurs four times and is followed by GI only twice, so the probability of **GI given BU** is two out of four, while both transitional probabilities inside GOPILA are two out of two. || Frequency is equal, so any difference between words and part-words must come from **how predictable the syllables are**.
10|The test produced a **novelty effect**, because **infants looked longer in the direction of the “part-word” stimuli** than toward the words, and the difference was statistically significant. || Longer looking is a **novelty preference**, because infants tend to look longer at whatever seems new. >> The words had grown familiar during listening, so they were less interesting, while the part-words seemed less familiar although infants had heard them just as often. || The result suggests that infants **had learned the statistical regularities of the speech stream** and **were not merely picking up on simple frequency of occurrence**. >> Words and part-words were matched for frequency, so only their transitional probabilities could separate them, and infants used that predictability after a short exposure.
11|**Identifying word referents** is the problem that follows segmentation, where a **referent** is whatever a word refers to. Children who can find the words still have to learn what the words mean, and they do it with further evidence from the situation in which a word is used. >> A child may hear “cat” while looking at a cat, but recognizing that sound pattern and knowing that it names the furry animal are separate achievements. || Segmentation gives a recurring chunk of sound and **says nothing about meaning**, and any scene holds many objects, parts, properties and actions. >> Learning a word therefore means **linking its form to the speaker’s intended meaning**, so the learner needs evidence about which thing the speaker has in mind.
12|**The inductive problem of early word learning** is that a single experience fits many possible meanings, where **induction** means reasoning from particular examples to a general rule. >> A new word heard while watching a cat might mean **CAT**, **ANIMAL** or **PET**, the name **FELIX**, a property such as **FURRY** or **ALIVE**, an action such as **WALKING**, or a part such as **WHISKERS** or **LEG**. || All the candidate meanings make up the **hypothesis space**, and every candidate fits what the child sees, so nothing in one scene singles out the intended meaning. || This is a version of **Quine’s “gavagai” problem**, in which a linguist hears a speaker of an unknown language say “gavagai” as a rabbit runs by. >> The word might mean rabbit, undetached rabbit parts, a stage of a rabbit or something else. >> Children still learn words fast, because joint attention, intentional cues and default strategies narrow the possibilities.
13|Children acquire word meanings partly through **joint attention**, which is **sharing an attentional focus with another person**. >> Two people look at the same object, and each knows that the other is attending to it too. || **Gaze following**, which means looking where someone else is looking, **emerges around nine months of age**, so the infant follows an adult’s eyes to an object. || Gaze following **signals an understanding of reference**, because the infant grasps that the adult’s attention is aimed at something specific. >> If the adult then says a word, the shared object becomes the most likely referent, so joint attention shrinks the **hypothesis space** by tying the word to what the speaker is attending to.
14|**Baldwin and colleagues** found that **joint attention optimizes word learning**, using two groups of infants who heard a novel word while playing with an object. >> In the **joint attention group**, an **adult interacting with the baby** labeled the object the baby was playing with. >> In the **no joint attention group**, a **voice from behind a screen** labeled the object the baby was playing with. || The groups matched in that the word came while the infant was focused on the same object, so they differed only in whether a visible person was engaged with the infant. || **Eighteen-month-olds learned the novel word in the joint attention group but not in the no joint attention group**. >> Hearing a word while seeing an object was not enough, because infants needed evidence that a speaker meant the word to refer to that object.
15|Children narrow the **hypothesis space** for new words partly through **intentional cues from the speaker**, which are signals of what the speaker means to refer to, such as **eye gaze direction**. || **Baldwin** taught new words to sixteen-to-seventeen-month-olds and eighteen-to-nineteen-month-olds under two conditions. >> In **follow-in labeling**, the adult looked at the **toy the infant was focused on** while saying the new word, so both cues pointed to one toy. >> In **discrepant labeling**, the adult looked at a **toy in a bucket**, a different toy from the infant’s, while saying the new word. || The discrepant condition separates the infant’s focus from the speaker’s focus, so it tests whether infants treat the **speaker’s gaze as evidence** of meaning. >> Infants who attach a word to whatever they are looking at would link it to their own toy, while infants who follow gaze would link it to the toy in the bucket.
16|**Gaze direction** helps a child work out which object a speaker is naming when several objects are present, because a speaker who looks at one object while saying a new word ties the word to that object. >> The child can follow the speaker’s eyes to the **intended referent**. || A speaker’s focus can be **different from the child’s own focus**, so following it means using information about someone else’s attention. >> The child can then attach a new word to an object the child was not looking at when the word was spoken. || Gaze **narrows the possible meanings** although the sound of the word gives no clue, because the child understands that a speaker’s eyes point to what the speaker means. >> The result is a far more specific mapping than linking the word to everything in view.
17|Intentional cues also include **affective and behavioral cues**, where affective cues are the speaker’s emotional expressions and behavioral cues are the speaker’s actions. >> An expression can reveal whether an object is the one the speaker was looking for, and an action such as reaching can reveal what the speaker wants. || **Akhtar and colleagues** found that **infants used adults’ facial expression to identify the intended referent of a novel word**. >> The adult’s emotional reaction to each object signaled whether it was the one meant, so infants linked the new word to the object that the expression picked out. || Several objects may be present when a word is spoken, and the speaker’s reaction can **single out one of them**. >> Satisfaction signals that the speaker found the wanted object, and disappointment signals that an object is not the one meant.
18|A speaker’s **emotional reaction** works as an **intentional cue** that can pick out the intended object from several candidates. >> Satisfaction or excitement can tie one object to what the speaker was trying to find or do, so the learner can use that tie to interpret a new label. || **Intention is conveyed through more than gaze direction**, because facial expressions and actions also reveal what a speaker means. >> The helpful information lies in how the adult responds to each object, and connecting the label to that response shrinks the **hypothesis space**, the set of meanings the word might have. || Infants who use emotional reactions are **following the speaker’s goals**, so the word attaches to the object that meets the goal and objects that disappoint the speaker are ruled out.
19|Children also bring **word learning strategies**, which are **default strategies that children bring to the word learning situation**, where a default is a starting assumption that holds until evidence says otherwise. >> These strategies **narrow the hypothesis space for new words**, so children have a place to start when other cues are weak. || The **whole object assumption** leads a child to expect a new word to name **a whole object**, not one of its parts, its color, its texture or an action. >> A child who hears a new word while looking at a cat will favor “cat” over “whiskers,” “furry” or “walking.” || The assumption **rules out many unlikely meanings at once** and fits the way adults usually name objects for children. >> It is only a starting preference, so children still learn words for parts and properties once the evidence points that way.
20|**Mutual exclusivity**, from **Markman**, is the default assumption that **objects can have only one name**, and its implication is that **new words map to nameless objects**. >> It **emerges during the second half of the second year**. || Suppose a child knows the word “dog” but has no word for a parrot, and both animals are in view. >> The child takes a new word to mean the parrot because the dog already has a name, and the child makes this choice without any gaze or emotional cue. || Mutual exclusivity works with the **whole object assumption**, which says that a new word names an object, by adding that the object has no name yet. >> It is a default, so later learning can override it when an object turns out to have more than one name.
21|Under **mutual exclusivity**, a child who hears an unfamiliar word gives it to an object that **has no known name yet**, because an object the child can already name is an unlikely target for a new word. >> The choice comes from comparing the objects that are in view. || **Prior vocabulary** decides which objects count as already named, so two children in the same scene can guess differently if they know different words. || Vocabulary growth **builds on itself** for that reason, because a child who knows many names can rule out many objects. >> The unnamed objects that remain become the likeliest referents, so every word the child knows helps narrow the meaning of the next new word.
22|When children produce only **one, two or three words at a time**, it is hard to tell how they are putting words together, because very different processes can produce the same short utterance. || **Menn** distinguishes four kinds of early utterance. >> **“Just a bunch of relevant words”** fit the situation with no grammar linking them, and **formulas** are phrases memorized as whole units without the child knowing their parts. >> **Word combinations** join separate words productively, and **stable constructions** are consistent patterns that are **not necessarily adult-like**. || A child might say “Up!” or “Pick you up!” or “Mommy up!” and the longer “Pick you up!” may be a formula copied whole while the shorter “Mommy up!” may be a true combination. >> Length cannot settle it, so **a full description of the context** of an utterance is **necessary but often not sufficient** to distinguish among these.
23|**Comprehension** runs ahead of **production**, because **even children at the one-word stage in production can use basic transitive word order in comprehension**. >> A **transitive** sentence has a verb with a subject and an object, and English puts the actor first and the one acted on after the verb. || The **structure-mapping account** of **early syntactic bootstrapping**, from **Gertner and Fisher**, describes how children use sentence structure to help learn meaning. >> From the input sentence “The boy is tickling the girl!” the child builds a **partial sentence representation** with tickle, boy as first noun and girl as second noun. >> From the observed scene the child builds **conceptual representations** of possible events, including tickling with the boy as **agent**, the doer, and the girl as **patient**, the one affected. || A **one-to-one mapping between nouns and participant roles** lines up the two nouns with the two participants in the tickling event, so noun order tells the child who did what.
24|A **generalization test** asks whether children have really learned the **transitive structure**, because children who have learned it will **generalize it to new verbs**. >> Choosing the scene that fits “The duck is gorping the bunny” requires knowing that the first noun is the doer and the second is the one acted on, because “gorp” is a made-up verb. || **Gertner and colleagues** found that **two-year-olds can understand this**. >> Children saw two novel actions side by side, one with the duck acting on the bunny and one with the bunny acting on the duck, while hearing the sentence. >> They **looked significantly longer at the duck acting on the rabbit** than at the reverse. || Children this age typically have a **mean length of utterance (MLU)**, the average number of morphemes in each utterance, of **around two**, so their own three-morpheme utterances might be like “Duck gorp bunny.” >> Their speech is short and incomplete, yet they understand the full transitive structure.
