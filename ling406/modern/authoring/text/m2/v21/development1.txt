2|**Nativist positions** grew out of **Chomsky's critique of Skinner**, the behaviorist who explained language with general learning mechanisms such as reward and punishment. >> For Chomsky the important feature of language is its **generativity**, the ability to produce and understand unlimited new sentences, so acquiring a language means **identifying the abstract grammar** that produces its sentences. || General learning mechanisms were **not up to this task**, because grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are not "similar" in any straightforward way, and because acquisition does not look like **operant conditioning**. >> Children are not reliably rewarded for grammatical sentences or punished for ungrammatical ones, so the reward and punishment contingencies do not hold. || Nativist approaches instead assert **innate knowledge of what language is like**, called **Universal Grammar**, plus **language-specific acquisition processes**, which are learning mechanisms built specifically for language. >> Chomsky argued that because all normal children acquire comparable grammars of great complexity with remarkable rapidity, human beings seem specially designed to do this.
3|**Categorical perception** is the pattern in which listeners hear differences between speech sounds in terms of categories, and early theories treated it as **language-specific** and **species-specific**. >> Only humans were known to show it, and only for speech sounds, so it looked like a unique feature of human language. || On a synthetic consonant continuum that changes in equal steps, listeners switch sharply in **labeling** from **/b/** to **/d/** and later to **/g/**, and **discrimination peaks near those boundaries** and drops inside each category. || It is a compelling example of how **similarity between stimuli is defined in linguistic, rather than physical, terms**. >> Two sounds that differ by the same acoustic amount sound almost identical inside one category and clearly different across a category boundary.
4|**Categorical perception in infants** is measured through behaviors that change when an infant notices something new, so neither method asks the infant to name or produce a sound. || In the **conditioned head turn procedure**, used in Janet Werker's infant studies lab, an infant learns to turn toward a sound when it changes, because a correct head turn earns an interesting visual reward such as a lit-up moving toy. >> Once the link is learned, a head turn after a sound change means the infant **heard the change**. || In **high-amplitude sucking**, the infant sucks on a pacifier attached to a pressure recorder, and strong sucks make a sound play. >> As the same sound repeats, the infant grows used to it, which is **habituation**, and sucking slows. >> If a new sound makes sucking **increase again**, the infant has noticed the change.
5|**Eimas and colleagues** tested categorical perception in **four-month-old infants** with **high-amplitude sucking**, using syllables that varied in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**, the delay between the release of a stop consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration. || Infants heard one syllable until sucking slowed, and then one of **three conditions** followed. >> In one, the sound changed by a fixed amount of VOT and **crossed an adult phonemic category boundary**, and in another it changed by the same amount but stayed in the **same category**. >> In the **control** condition the same sound continued throughout. || Sucking **jumped back up** only after the change that crossed the boundary, while it showed little recovery after the within-category change and kept declining in the control. >> The two changes were matched in acoustic size, so infants were responding to **crossing the category boundary**, and the control establishes what happens with repetition alone.
6|The result from **Eimas and colleagues** was a big deal because categorical perception was considered the **first step in turning speech into a symbolic format** for later linguistic processes to use. >> A symbolic format stores a sound as a category, such as /b/. || **Four-month-olds are really little**, with gross motor milestones such as manipulating objects with two hands and holding the head and chest up while prone. >> Their color vision and general visual acuity are still "in progress," and they are just starting to produce vowel-like sounds instead of creaky whines. || This is **very, very early in development** for an apparently language-specific ability to emerge, so it **seemed like evidence for innateness**. >> Speech categorization ran far ahead of other abilities, so it looked built in instead of learned through long experience.
7|**Perceptual narrowing** is the decline in sensitivity to speech contrasts that the language a child hears does not use. || **Werker and Tees** tested **English-learning infants** on a **Hindi contrast** between two "ta"-like sounds and a **Salish contrast** between "ki" and "qi", counting how many infants at each age reached a discrimination criterion. >> At around six months most infants discriminated both contrasts they had never heard, fewer did a couple of months later, and only a small minority did by the end of the first year. >> Hindi-learning and Salish-learning infants discriminated their own native contrast nearly every time, so the decline **depends on language experience**. || Narrowing is related to categorical perception because infants are forming native categories, and sounds that their language groups together become harder to tell apart. >> A nonnative distinction that falls inside one native category comes to be heard as a **within-category difference**.
8|In a **very compelling narrative**, speech perception depends on **categorical perception abilities** that are a **special property of the language system** and are present very early in development. || Children start off as **"citizens of the world,"** able to hear **all contrasts**, which means they have **innate, language-general knowledge** about speech that applies to the sounds of every language. >> They then **rapidly tune their perceptual systems** to the specific language in their environment. || The **role of learning** is then to **select** among the many innately specified phonetic contrasts and narrow the **inventory**, the full set of contrasts the child can perceive, to those meaningful in the native language. >> Learning works by elimination, because it drops the contrasts the native language does not use.
9|The narrative has **some problems**, and three concern **categorical perception** itself. >> It is **not limited to language**, because it also occurs for nonspeech sounds, and it is **not limited to humans**, because animals without language perceive speech sounds categorically. >> It is also **task-dependent**, so results look more or less categorical depending on whether listeners name a sound or judge whether two sounds differ. || **Infant speech perception** is also **not actually "universal,"** because infants do not begin able to hear every contrast equally well. >> Some contrasts are hard for young infants to detect even when their own language uses them. || A full account of development must therefore explain contrasts that **improve with experience** as well as contrasts that are lost, so learning must build categories as well as remove them.
10|**Cutting and Rosner** tested categorical perception with **non-speech sounds** that varied in **rise time**, which is how quickly a sound grows to full loudness after it starts. >> A **short rise time** sounds like a **plucked** string and a **long rise time** sounds like a **bowed** string, and the stimuli included sawtooth waves, speech syllables and sine waves. || For sawtooth waves, listeners called short rise times pluck and long rise times bow, with a fairly sharp switch in the middle of the range. >> Discrimination was only a little above chance for most pairs, and it **peaked** for the pairs right where the labels switched. || A label boundary with better discrimination at that boundary therefore appears for sounds that have **nothing to do with phonemes**, so categorical perception can arise in **general auditory processing** without a mechanism specific to language.
11|**Jusczyk and colleagues** used high-amplitude sucking to test whether **infants** show categorical perception of **non-speech sounds**. >> Infants heard one sound until sucking slowed, and then there was **no change**, a change **within categories** or a change **between categories**. || Sucking stayed low after no change and after the within-category change, and it **jumped back up** only after the between-category change, before declining again as the new sound became familiar. || Infants responded to a non-speech change that **crossed a category boundary**, which is the same pattern that **Eimas and colleagues** found for speech. >> Adults label plucked and bowed sounds categorically and infants discriminate nonspeech changes categorically, and neither needs language knowledge, so early categorical perception reflects a **general auditory ability**.
12|**Ohlemiller and colleagues**, following **Kuhl and Miller**, found categorical perception of **speech sounds** in **chinchillas**. >> The animals were trained with **operant conditioning** that either the zero-lag stimulus or the eighty-millisecond-lag stimulus was an **SD**, a **discriminative stimulus** signaling that a response will be rewarded with food. >> The lag is the **voice onset time**, and humans hear the zero lag as /ga/ and the eighty-millisecond lag as /ka/. || Tested on intermediate VOTs along the continuum from /ga/ to /ka/, chinchillas labeled short VOTs /ga/ almost every time and long VOTs almost never, with the same **steep, categorical switch** as human listeners. || **Increasing the intensity of the burst and aspiration noise** shifted the boundary to a **shorter VOT**, made the switch steeper, or both, for one chinchilla and one human listener. >> The **burst** is the brief noise at the release of a stop, and **aspiration** is the breathy noise that follows a voiceless release. >> Animals without language sort a speech continuum into categories, so categorical perception of speech is **shared with other species**.
13|**Narayan and colleagues** found that **some contrasts are less salient**, meaning less easy to notice, than others for infants. >> They tested **nasal consonants** in the **onset** of a syllable that differ in **place of articulation**, with /m/ made at the lips, /n/ just behind the teeth and /ŋ/ at the soft palate. || Infants heard a syllable that either repeated or switched, and longer looking after a switch meant they noticed it. >> English-hearing infants of four to five months looked longer for the native switch between **[ma] and [na]** but not for the nonnative switch between **[na] and [ŋa]**. || The place contrast between /na/ and /ŋa/ is **not readily perceived by four-month-olds, independent of language experience**, while /ma/ and /na/ is **relatively salient**. >> The gap comes from the sounds themselves, so not every contrast begins equally available.
14|**Narayan and colleagues** found that between six and ten months, exposure to the contrast between /na/ and /ŋa/ in a **Filipino language environment increases its salience**. >> Filipino treats [na] and [ŋa] as different syllables, so Filipino-learning infants hear the contrast regularly. >> The younger Filipino-learning infants did not yet look longer when the syllable switched, while those near the end of the first year did. || So it **cannot be that all speech contrasts are available innately**, because sensitivity to this contrast starts out weak even for infants whose language uses it. || **Exposure to contrasts in the environment is necessary for acquisition in some cases**, so learning can strengthen a contrast as well as preserve it. >> Development therefore includes building up sensitivity to hard contrasts, not only losing sensitivity to unused ones.
15|The alternative to the nativist approach is the **emergentist approach**, which assumes that **associative learning mechanisms** are sensitive to **statistical regularities in the environment**. >> **Associative learning** is learning that two things go together because they repeatedly occur together, and statistical regularities are patterns in how often things occur and which occur together. || Classical conditioning is the simplest case, described with a **bell** neuron, a **food detector** neuron and a **salivation** neuron. >> Food already produces salivation, and when the bell repeatedly occurs with food, the **synapse** from the bell neuron to the salivation neuron is **strengthened** until the bell alone produces salivation. || This can extend beyond conditioning to **pattern recognition**, which is identifying a recurring structure such as a speech sound across varied examples, because the same strengthening could link acoustic features that tend to occur together. >> Repeated co-occurrence in speech could then build categories that were never specified in advance.
16|When **Kuhl and others** talk about **learning the statistics of distributions in the environment**, they mean patterns like those of the **American vowels**, where a **distribution** describes how often each value occurs across many examples. || Each vowel occupies a region of a **vowel space** determined by the **first and second formants**, and the regions **overlap** heavily. >> A single pair of formant values can belong to more than one vowel, so listeners must learn where each vowel is centered and how far it spreads. || Maybe we learn the vowels of our language by **extracting the statistics of these distributions**, so a **"prototypical" /a/** is just the **mean**, or perhaps the **mode**, of all the /a/ sounds we hear. >> The mean is the average value and the mode is the most frequent value, and either way the prototype comes from experience.
17|**Maye and colleagues** tested whether infants really learn from distributions by having them **listen passively** to a continuum from [da] to [ta] that varied in **voice onset time**. >> In the **bimodal distribution**, sounds near each end were most frequent, which gives **evidence for two categories**. >> In the **unimodal distribution**, sounds in the middle were most frequent, which gives **evidence for a single category**. || At test, infants heard stimuli three and six in **alternating** trials, where the two switched back and forth, or **non-alternating** trials, where one repeated. || **Only infants pre-exposed to the bimodal distribution** were sensitive to the difference between the two kinds of trials. >> The unimodal infants looked about equally long on both, as if they had learned to treat stimuli three and six as **members of the same category**. >> Brief exposure to a distribution changed how infants grouped the sounds, which supports statistical learning of speech categories.
18|Experiments give evidence that **social interaction is important for learning**. || Nine-month-old American infants were exposed to **Mandarin** either live, with a speaker playing and reading in person, or through audio or television, while controls heard English. >> On a later head-turn test of a Mandarin phonetic contrast, only the **live** group beat the controls, so the same exposure is **more effective in person and interactive** than as a video. || In a second experiment, mothers of eight-month-olds responded either right after the infant vocalized, the **contingent** condition, or on a schedule copied from another infant, the **yoked-control** condition. >> Contingent feedback **increased the quantity and complexity of vocalizations**, and the infants stayed higher even after the responses stopped. >> Contingent social interaction therefore seems to work like a **reinforcing stimulus**, either because humans find social interaction **innately reinforcing** or because we are innately good at using **social cues** to learn from other people.
19|**Statistical learning with constraints** is learning from patterns in the input that is guided and limited, and "constraints" covers a lot of things. || One constraint is on **which stimulus properties can be contrastive**, meaning able to distinguish words. >> Speaker identity and speech rate change the sound a great deal without changing the word, so they are not contrastive and learning must ignore them. || Other constraints are **based on earlier learning**, because once native categories are in place, new distinctions that cut across them become harder to learn, which is central to **sensitive periods**. >> **Social and interactive contexts** are a third constraint, because sounds are learned better in live, responsive interaction than from a recording.
