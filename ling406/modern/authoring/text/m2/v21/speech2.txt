2|**Klatt** described a very old and very general model of speech perception, in which the **speech waveform** passes through a series of stages. The stages run from **spectral analysis** in the cochlea, through **acoustic property detectors** and **phonetic feature detectors**, to **segmental analysis** and **lexical search**. >> Lexical search compares the sequence of segments with stored words and produces **lexical hypotheses**, which are candidate words that might match. || **Categorical perception** fits this model, because **information is lost** somewhere between the analog signal from the cochlea and the activation of phonemes. >> This **loss is helpful**, because a phoneme sequence can make contact with higher levels, so it seems that identifying words requires **first identifying their constituent phonemes**. || Two problems challenge this simple view, which are **cascaded processing**, where partial information moves on before a decision is complete, and **feedback**, where a higher level such as words influences a lower level such as phonemes.
3|The **beach-peach continuum** is a familiar **voicing continuum**, in which the first consonant changes from /p/ to /b/ as **voice onset time (VOT)** gets shorter in equal steps. >> VOT is the delay between the release of the lips and the start of vocal fold vibration, so a long delay sounds like peach and a short delay sounds like beach. || One listener's **identification** responses are **step-like**, because the early stimuli get one word every time, the late stimuli get the other word every time, and only one stimulus near the middle gets mixed labels. >> The **category boundary** is the narrow region where the preferred label switches. || The same listener's **discrimination** follows the boundary, because pairs inside one category are almost never heard as different while pairs that **straddle the boundary** are heard as different every time. >> Equal changes in VOT are much easier to detect when they change the word than when the word stays the same.
4|A **threshold model** is one answer to how categorical perception happens, because in it **activity is only passed forward from one level to the next when an activity threshold is crossed**. >> A **threshold** is a set level of activity that must be reached, so activity below it can rise and fall with no effect on the next level. || In this kind of model, **categorical perception arises at phoneme identification**. >> Voicing evidence at the feature level swings between a **+voiced** threshold and a **-voiced** threshold while /b/ and /p/ both stay silent, and only when the evidence crosses the +voiced threshold does **/b/** switch on **all at once**. || An ambiguous sound either crosses a threshold or does not, so the word level receives a finished /b/ or a finished /p/. >> Two different sounds that both end up as /b/ become **identical** by the time they reach word recognition, because the earlier uncertainty stays behind at the feature level.
5|A **cascaded model** gives a different answer, because in it **activity is passed forward continuously over time from one level to the next**, while the decision is still forming. >> Feature activity and phoneme activity rise and fall together, so growing +voiced evidence raises **/b/** and a shift toward -voiced moves activation toward **/p/**. || In this kind of model, **categorical perception might happen later on, or might even be an artifact of the task structure**, where an **artifact** is a result created by the way something is measured. >> An identification task forces a click on beach or peach even if both words were partly active, so the answer looks categorical because the **choices are categorical**. || Mixed voicing evidence gives /b/ and /p/ activation in proportion to their evidence, so a sound near the boundary **partly activates both beach and peach**. >> Two sounds labeled /b/ can still differ in how much they supported /p/ along the way.
6|**Making furniture** is a metaphor for **thresholded activity**, because each operation turns one specific input into one specific output. >> A **lumber mill** turns **trees** into **lumber**, a **furniture factory** turns lumber into **unassembled furniture**, and an **IKEA customer** turns unassembled furniture into finished **furniture**. || Each step needs a **finished product** in a form it can use, so trees must become lumber before furniture is made, and lumber must become parts before assembly starts. || **Thresholded processing** depends on its inputs in the same way, because a higher level **waits** until the lower level delivers the required representation. >> Information moves forward only as a completed product.
7|In the furniture metaphor, each **“layer”** of the system **can only take a specific input that has been transformed completely by the previous layer**, so manufacturing waits for the shipment of lumber. >> **Many computer programs** function this way, with each stage handing on only its finished output while the higher stage sits idle. || In speech recognition, a strict threshold account makes **word processing depend on an identified phoneme**, so a partly resolved acoustic signal stays at the lower level and the word level receives only the finished segment. || This design also decides **what information is lost**, because different sounds that become the same phoneme never pass their earlier differences to word recognition. >> That helps treat varied sounds alike, but later stages never learn **how uncertain** the earlier stages were.
8|A **food web** is a metaphor for **cascaded processing**, because its **“levels” continuously interact with one another**. >> A drought that reduces the number of desert plants **immediately** affects the health of kangaroo rats, which affects the snakes that eat them, and no population waits for the level below to reach a finished state. || **Cascaded processing** has the same continuous dependence, because shifting feature evidence changes phoneme activity as it happens, and shifting phoneme activity changes support for words. || The food web also has clear **feedback loops**, because adding more hawks changes the populations of snakes, lizards and rats, and those changes in turn affect the hawks' own food supply. >> In a recognition model, **feedback** is influence from a higher level back down to a lower level, while cascading sends developing evidence forward.
9|**Phoneme identification for word recognition** could be more like **thresholded processing**, the lumber mill, or **cascaded processing**, the food web, and the eye-movement evidence favors cascaded processing. >> In a thresholded account, word recognition starts only once a phoneme decision is complete, while in a cascaded account a word can gather support while the phoneme is still uncertain. || Two sounds can both be labeled **/b/** and still differ in how much they resemble **/p/**. >> A thresholded system turns both into the same finished /b/, so **pear should not compete with bear**. >> A cascaded system passes along more /p/ support for the more ambiguous sound, so **pear should compete with bear for a while**. || Both accounts can predict the same final word, so the deciding test measures **temporary support for a competing word** during recognition.
10|**McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey** tested whether phoneme identification is thresholded or cascaded by looking for **gradient effects of within-category VOT on lexical access**. >> **Lexical access** is retrieving a word from memory, **gradient** means changing by degrees, and **within-category VOT** means VOT differences among sounds heard as the same phoneme. || Listeners identified sounds from a **/b/-/p/ continuum** in a **“visual world” display** while **eye-tracking** recorded where they looked among the pictures. >> Each display had a **target** such as pear, a **competitor** such as bear that differs only in its first consonant, and two unrelated **fillers**, a lamp and a ship. || Eye movements are fast and continuous, and listeners look at objects as they think about them, so looks to the competitor measure **how active the competitor was** before the final click.
11|In the study by **McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey**, participants used the **mouse to select** whichever of the pictures was most consistent with what they heard, such as a **“bear”** or a **“pear.”** >> The click records the word the listener finally settled on. || The clicks produced a **typical, step-like identification function**, because short VOTs were almost never heard as pear, long VOTs almost always were, and the switch happened abruptly at a **category boundary**. || Judged by the clicks alone, perception looks **fully categorical**, which on its own would seem to support the threshold model. >> A click records only the word chosen at the end, while eye movements record which other words received support before that choice. >> A sharp identification function can therefore exist alongside gradual differences in competition.
12|**Eye movements reveal that ambiguity is gradient**, which means that uncertainty varies by degree, because there are **more looks to the competitor close to the boundary**. >> The analysis holds the final response constant by comparing only trials that ended with the same click, so differences in competitor looks cannot come from listeners changing their answer. || On trials where listeners clicked **bear**, sounds closer to the boundary drew **more and longer looks to the pear** than the clearest /b/ sounds. >> On trials where listeners clicked **pear**, the pattern was the mirror image, with the most looks to the bear for sounds just past the boundary. || The effect of VOT is mainly on the **duration of activation**, meaning how long the competitor stays active. >> The recognition system keeps track of how ambiguous each sound was, which is information a **threshold model would throw away**.
13|The gradient effect is clearer when competitor looks are compared across the **whole VOT range**. >> The measure is looks to the pear on the side of the **category boundary** where listeners clicked bear, and looks to the bear on the side where they clicked pear. || In both an **early** and a **late** time window after the word begins, competitor looks **increase as VOT approaches the boundary from either side**. >> Looks to the pear grow steadily toward the boundary on the bear side, and looks to the bear fall steadily away from the boundary on the pear side. >> Early looks are higher overall, but they follow the same pattern. || The effect appears in both windows, so uncertainty about the first sound shapes looking **throughout recognition**, and rival support is stronger the closer the sound is to the boundary.
14|The eye-movement finding is consistent with the idea that **activation flows in a cascaded way from features to phonemes**. >> **Even stimuli that are always identified as /b/ can vary in how strongly and unambiguously the “voiced” feature is activated**, so a /b/ near the boundary activates voicing only weakly. || /b/ and /p/ are both **bilabial**, with the closure made by the two lips, and both **stops**, with airflow fully blocked and then released. >> A stimulus that is obviously **+bilabial** and **+stop** supports both equally, so weak voicing evidence creates **momentary ambiguity between /b/ and /p/**, which is reflected in the eye movements while the participant decides where to click. || The **TRACE** model captures this cascade with a **feature layer**, a **phoneme layer** and a **word layer**. >> The feature layer takes a **pseudo-spectral input representation**, a simplified version of the frequency content of the sound over time, and has detectors for power, vocalic, diffuseness, acuteness, gradualness of onset, voicing and burst. >> Activity passes up from features to phonemes and from phonemes to words, and units within a layer compete through **inhibition**.
15|**Andruski and colleagues** found that **semantic priming**, the speed-up in recognizing a word after hearing a word related in meaning, can happen even with **nonwords**, so **partial activation can reach all the way to the lexical level**. >> **“Beace”** primes **“war”** and **“ging”** primes **“queen,”** because they are peace and king with a voiced first consonant. >> In **word-to-word** items the altered word is another real word, as when **“bill,”** an altered form of pill, primes **“drug.”** || Participants did a **lexical decision task**, deciding as quickly as possible whether a target is a real word, after primes that were **unaltered**, had VOT reduced by **one third** or by **two thirds**, or were **unrelated**. Every related prime sped up responses compared with unrelated primes. >> Word-to-nonword priming stayed strong at every level of alteration, while word-to-word priming weakened at two thirds, because the altered prime also partly activates a competing real word such as bill. || **Lexical representations can be activated even by incomplete or imperfect input**, because beace partly activates peace and that activation spreads to meaning, which fits cascaded processing.
16|The bear and pear eye-tracking result is one piece of evidence for **cascaded processing**. A thresholded model can hardly explain why **the number of looks to the other member of the minimal pair increases gradually as you approach the category boundary**. >> A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in a single sound, such as bear and pear. || In a thresholded model, graded feature activity **never reaches the phoneme level**, so every token placed in a category sends the **same finished phoneme** to the word level. >> Word-level competition would then be identical for every /b/, which leaves nothing to produce gradually changing competitor looks. || A cascaded account explains the effect directly, because more support for the competing phoneme passes on more support for its word.
17|The **TRACE** model also includes **feedback from the lexical layer to the phoneme layer**, so activity runs up from phonemes to words and back down from words to phonemes. >> **Lexical feedback** means that an active word sends support back to its own phonemes, so a word that an uncertain sound partly activates can strengthen the reading of that sound. || Influence therefore travels **in both directions**, because acoustic evidence changes which words are active and active words shift the balance between possible phonemes. || **Feedback is different from cascading**, because cascading sends incomplete evidence forward while feedback sends influence from words back down to phonemes. >> An interactive model can have both, and experiments in which **word knowledge changes how sounds are heard**, such as the **Ganong effect**, are the reason to add feedback.
18|**Ganong** looked at how **phoneme identification is affected by word knowledge**, which is the source of **the Ganong effect**. >> The phrase “Gisses and kifts for Valentine’s day” plays on the effect by swapping the voicing of the first consonants in “kisses and gifts.” || He came up with **word/nonword pairs** such as **“corpse/gorpse”** and produced continua that crossed the relevant phoneme boundary in small steps of VOT. >> Hearing /k/ gives the real word corpse and hearing /g/ gives the nonword gorpse, while the rest of the word stays the same at every step. || The design pairs **uncertain phonetic evidence** with a **difference in lexical status**, so if word knowledge affects identification, an ambiguous first consonant should more often be heard as the **sound that makes a word**. >> The crucial test is in the **ambiguous middle** of the continuum, where word knowledge has the most room to act.
19|One prediction for the experiment of **Ganong** comes from a **late “correction” process**, in which **categorical perception functions as usual, but there is a bias to report having heard a word**. >> The listener first categorizes the sound with no help from word knowledge and then leans toward the real word at the response stage. || This account predicts a **general reporting bias** across **word-nonword** continua, where the voiced end forms a word, **nonword-word** continua, where the voiceless end forms a word, and **neutral** continua. >> A bias added after perception pushes responses toward the word by the **same amount everywhere**, so even a clearly voiceless sound would sometimes be reported as voiced when the voiced version makes a word. || The **lexical effect**, which is the difference in voiced responses between the two lexical conditions, is therefore the **same size at every step** of the continuum.
20|A second possibility is that **word knowledge shifts the categorical boundary**, so it changes where the listener switches from one phoneme category to the other. >> Near an ambiguous onset the interpretation that forms a real word gets extra support, which changes categorization itself and not only the reported answer. || This account predicts that the lexical conditions differ **only in the middle** of the continuum, because at both ends the sound is clear and every condition gives the same answer. || The **lexical effect** should form a **peak**, since it is **strongest for the items closest to the category boundary**, where lexical information can help resolve some ambiguity about the initial phoneme. >> A **flat** effect points to a reporting bias, while a **peaked** effect points to a shift in the boundary itself.
21|The results of **Ganong** are strikingly consistent with the **second model**, the **boundary-shift** account, because the **lexical effect** is small at the endpoints and largest near the boundary. >> A peak at the boundary **rules out a simple reporting bias**, because word knowledge matters most where the acoustic evidence is weakest. || Ganong raised a number of possibilities for how this could happen, focusing on different kinds of **guessing strategies**, which would let listeners use word knowledge only when unsure of the sound. || Later models would emphasize the potential role of **feedback** in these shifts. >> In **interactive models**, an active word sends support back to its own phonemes, so the whole word can shape how an ambiguous part of it is interpreted.
22|A **simulation of the Ganong effect in the TRACE model** tracks **phoneme and word activations** as the input unfolds. The input starts with a sound **ambiguous between /b/ and /p/** and continues with /l/, the vowel /ʌ/ and /g/, which together make **plug**. >> When only the first sound has arrived, /b/ and /p/ are both weakly active at about the same level, and word activation is very low. || As /l/ and the vowel arrive, several candidates gain activation together, including **plug**, **blood** and **blush**, because all of them fit the beginning heard so far. || When the final **/g/** arrives, it fits plug but not the endings of blood or blush, so **plug** becomes the most active word by a wide margin. >> At the phoneme level, **/p/ ends up more active than /b/**.
23|As the input unfolds, **activity accumulates for “plug,”** especially when the **final /g/** arrives, because /g/ is highly inconsistent with **“blood”** and **“blush,”** which had also become active. || The TRACE model explains **word effects on phoneme category boundaries** in terms of **top-down processing**, which means that higher-level knowledge influences lower-level analysis. >> **Activity from the lexical level produces feedback to the phoneme level**, which gives **greater activation to the phoneme consistent with a word**, so /p/ gains extra support from plug. || The ambiguous beginning becomes clearer because /p/ belongs to the word that the whole sequence supports. >> Information that arrives at the end of a word can reach back and shape how an earlier sound is heard.
24|The **TRACE** model is a version of the **Interactive Activation model** for **written word recognition**, from **McClelland and Rumelhart**, and many of the principles are the same. >> Feature units connect to letter units and letter units connect to word units such as **WEAR, WORK, WORD** and **WEAK**, with support between compatible units, inhibition within a level, and support from active words back down to their letters. || Suppose a reader sees W, O and R clearly, but ink blots hide part of a final letter that fits both **R** and **K**. Only **WORK** fits, so WORK sends support back to K and the letter is perceived as K. >> This is the same way plug supports /p/ in speech. || A key issue in speech perception is that **the stimuli unfold over time**, so TRACE repeats feature, phoneme and word units at every point in time.
25|**Categorical perception** still holds, because **given the same physical difference, it is more difficult to distinguish stimuli from the same category than from different categories**. || Two findings add to it at the **lexical-phonetic interface**, where words and sounds meet. >> **Ambiguity about phoneme identity does have an impact on word recognition**, because sounds with the same label differ in how strongly they activate a competing word. >> **Word-level information seems to have an impact on phoneme categorization**, because in the Ganong effect word knowledge moves the category boundary. || It therefore **cannot be the case that the word recognition system just sits there waiting for the phoneme recognition system to finish doing its job**, like a lumber mill. >> **Spoken word recognition is more interactive**, because partial information moves forward and active words send support back down while uncertainty is still being resolved.

27|**Spoken word recognition is also very fast**, as **Allopenna and colleagues** found with a visual world eye-tracking task in which participants followed spoken instructions to move objects shown in a display. >> The display had a **beaker** as the **referent**, a **beetle** as a **cohort competitor** that shares the beginning of the word, a **speaker** as a **rhyme competitor** that shares the ending, and an **unrelated** carriage. || Looks to the beaker and the beetle **rose together early**, because both fit the first sounds, and looks to the beetle dropped once the sounds that separate the two words arrived. >> Looks to the speaker rose later and stayed smaller, and looks to the carriage stayed near zero. || Looks to the target began rising **before the word had finished**, so recognition begins while the word is still unfolding, and candidates drop out as soon as they stop matching.
28|The eye-tracking data can be compared with a **TRACE simulation** of the same task, in which the word activations of the model over time were converted into predicted looks. >> The predictions **closely match** the real looks for the **referent**, for the **cohort** competitor with its early rise and decline, and for the **rhyme** competitor with its smaller and later rise. || The **granularity** of the simulation is fine, which means it predicts activity moment by moment, and **eye movements give us an idea about the changes in “activity” for different competitors over time**. >> That makes it possible to **see cognitive processing before a final response is issued**. || This parallels the **graded effects of category goodness** in the categorical perception task, where better and worse examples of a category produce different amounts of competitor activity even when the final label is the same.
