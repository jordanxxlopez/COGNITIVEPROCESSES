2|**Aphasia** is a loss or impairment of language caused by brain damage, and in the eighteen seventies German neurologists such as **Lichtheim** and **Wernicke** began observing it more systematically. >> They ascribed specific losses of function to **“disconnections” between “centers,”** where a **center** is a brain region that stores one kind of language representation and a disconnection is damage to the pathway between two centers. || In **Lichtheim's “House” model**, **A** is the center for **sound images**, **M** is the center for **movement images**, which are motor plans for saying words, and **B** is the center for **concepts**. >> A lost ability to **understand spoken words** might come from a break between **A and B**, because the sound of the word arrives but cannot reach its concept. >> A lost ability to do **verbatim repetition** might come from a break between **A and M**, because the heard word cannot pass directly to the plan for saying it. || This kind of **“box and arrow” model** is foundational in **aphasiology**, the study of aphasia, and in modeling typical language function, as in the **interactive activation model**. >> This history comes from **Eling and Whitaker (2009)**.
3|**Norman Geschwind** revived the box-and-arrow approach in the nineteen seventies, and the result is the **“Geschwind-Wernicke model”**, which ties language functions to brain regions and the pathways that join them. >> **Broca's area** sits in the left frontal lobe, **Wernicke's area** sits farther back in the temporal lobe, and the **arcuate fasciculus** is the bundle of nerve fibers that connects them. >> The **central sulcus**, or fissure of Rolando, separates the frontal lobe from the parietal lobe, and the **lateral sulcus**, or fissure of Sylvius, separates the temporal lobe from the lobes above it. || In the century between Wernicke and Geschwind, linguistics developed a model in which **syntax was independent** of other aspects of language and had **its own computational principles**, so researchers could theorize about dissociations in more sophisticated ways. >> In a **dissociation** one ability is impaired while another is spared, and in a **double dissociation** one patient loses the first ability and keeps the second while another patient has the reverse pattern. >> A double dissociation is strong evidence that the two abilities depend on separate systems.
4|**Fromkin (1997)** describes the classic profiles of the two main aphasias, which are Broca's aphasia and Wernicke's aphasia. || In **Broca's aphasia**, speech has **word-finding pauses**, **loss of grammatical morphemes** and often **disturbed word order**, while comprehension of everyday conversation seems generally good even though **controlled testing reveals considerable impairments**. >> A **grammatical morpheme** is a small word or word part that marks a grammatical relationship, such as “the,” “is” or the past-tense ending “-ed.” >> **Agrammatism**, meaning difficulty producing or understanding grammatical structure, is almost a synonym for Broca's aphasia, yet some patients with lesions in Broca's area are **not agrammatic** and some agrammatic patients would not be classified neurologically as Broca's aphasics. || In **Wernicke's aphasia**, speech is **fluent** with good intonation and pronunciation, but it has many **word substitutions**, **neologisms**, which are invented words, and **phonological errors**, which are errors in the sounds of words. >> These patients also have **comprehension difficulties**, so fluency is separate from whether the words carry a coherent message.
5|An attractive **dichotomy**, meaning a two-way division, describes **Wernicke's aphasia** as **“word salad”** or **“Jabberwocky,”** often with **neologisms**, while **syntax stays roughly intact**. >> A patient reported by **Kriendler and colleagues (1971)**, asked about his health, gave a long smooth answer that began “I felt worse because I can no longer keep in mind from the mind of the minds” and never formed a clear message. >> Another patient said “My wires don’t hire right” about his poor vision, and an aphasic physician used the neologisms **demaploze** and **tubaboys** when he was asked whether he was a doctor. || **Broca's aphasia** is described as **“telegraphic” speech**, with difficulty with **word order and grammatical particles** and relative **sparing of important content words**. >> A patient reported by **Badecker and Caramazza (1985)**, asked what brought him to the hospital, produced content words such as Dad, hospital and doctors with many pauses and without the grammatical words that would connect them. || The two patterns look like mirror images, because Wernicke's patients have fluent grammar with wrong words and Broca's patients have meaningful words with little grammar.
6|Stated in terms of what is damaged, **Wernicke's aphasia** reflects problems with **activation, or possibly inhibition, in the lexicon**, and **Broca's aphasia** reflects **problems with grammar**. >> The **lexicon** is the mental store of words, **activation** makes a candidate word available, and **inhibition** suppresses competing words, so weak inhibition could let wrong words slip into speech. || That account is **fairly distant from how Broca described his original cases**, because his patient **Leborgne**, known as **“Tan,”** could only produce the one jargon syllable “tan” while he seemed to understand what was being said. >> His other senses were intact, so the loss was specific to speech, which makes it a dissociation, and a modern examination would describe it at phonetic, phonological, lexical and syntactic levels. || So **Broca's actual patients would not necessarily be classified as Broca's aphasics** today, because they were arguably unable to produce speech at all due to **motor control issues**. >> They would be classified as having **apraxia of speech**, a disorder in planning and coordinating the movements needed to speak, which can limit output severely without any loss of grammatical knowledge.
7|The dichotomy is interesting partly because it lines up with a sharp distinction that late twentieth-century formal linguistics drew between **the grammar and the lexicon**. >> The **grammar** is the system of rules for combining words into sentences, and the **lexicon** is the store of individual words, which must be memorized. || The distinction starts with **Chomsky's formulation of the computational problem of language learning**. A new string is recognized as a sentence because it is **generated by the grammar** each person has internalized, and not because it matches some familiar item. || **Chomsky (1955)** also argued for the **independence of syntax and semantics** with “colorless green ideas sleep furiously” and “furiously sleep ideas green colorless,” because neither has occurred in an English speaker's experience and yet **only the first is grammatical**. >> Both are equally unfamiliar and equally meaningless, so the difference must come from grammar as its own system. >> The aphasia dichotomy maps onto this split, with Wernicke's aphasia as a disorder of the lexicon and Broca's aphasia as a disorder of the grammar.
8|The dichotomy also fits the **speech production model**, where the **functional level** arouses word meanings and **semantic roles**, which are the parts participants play in an event, and the **positional level** gets word forms in grammatically appropriate order. >> The conceptual **message level** comes before these two, and **phonological encoding** and the **speech gesture level** come after them. || At the functional level, **lemmas have an arbitrary relationship to their referents**, which means nothing about a concept determines its word, while at the positional level **syntactic trees and affixes are the product of abstract syntactic processes**. >> A **lemma** is a word's entry in the lexicon, linking its meaning to its grammatical properties, and an **affix** is a word part added to a stem, such as “-ly.” || Words like **HOUSEBOAT** and **DOGHOUSE** seem to be an objection, because their meanings are clearly not arbitrary, but **speech error evidence** places **derivational morphology**, which builds new words from existing parts, at the positional level. >> In the error **“easy enoughly,”** the speaker meant “easily enough,” and the “-ly” suffix moved to the wrong word as a separate piece, so a grammatical operation must have added it.
9|Popular theories of **sentence processing** hold that the **syntactic structure** of a sentence is **computed independently of meaning** as people listen or read, so an autonomous parser builds the structure first. || **Isolating these “parsing” mechanisms** requires carefully constructed stimulus materials, such as “The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite,” which has two structures built from the same words. >> The **preferred** structure attaches “with the dynamite” to the **verb phrase**, so the dynamite is the instrument, and the **unpreferred** structure attaches it to the **noun phrase** “the safe,” so the phrase tells which safe. || One possibility is that **Broca's patients generally understand what is being said even though they have a core grammatical deficit**, because in conversation word meanings, familiar events and context all point toward the same message. >> A real test of a parsing deficit needs sentences whose grammatical relationships cannot be guessed from the most likely event, such as reversible sentences where either person could plausibly do the action.
10|None of this theory was available to **Broca** in the eighteen sixties, so for him it was interesting enough to **dissociate “articulate speech” from comprehension**. >> Leborgne could produce only one syllable but appeared to understand what was said to him, so speaking and understanding came apart. || The modern goal is to find patients whose difficulty is **more generally with syntax**, and the way to do it is to **test comprehension**. >> A **syntactic impairment** concerns the organization of grammatical relationships, so it should affect both producing and understanding sentences. >> A comprehension test lets the patient respond without speaking, so if the patient still struggles with grammatical relationships, the deficit goes beyond articulation.
11|Showing that Broca's patients have **comprehension deficits** was interesting because **Broca's aphasia is almost always accompanied by motor dysfunction**, so agrammatic speech might merely reflect an attempt to communicate despite motor limitations. >> **Hughlings Jackson** made the related point that we observe what the individual can do despite the impairment, so agrammatic speech does not literally mean that **the grammar itself is injured**. >> A version of this idea from **Lenneberg (1973)** says that the strain of speaking is so great that the patient speaks asyntactically in order to economize effort. || **Chomsky's distinction between competence and performance** also applies, where **competence** is a person's underlying knowledge of language and **performance** is the actual use of that knowledge. >> When motor deficits obviously interfere with performance, **comprehension may provide a clearer window into competence**. || **Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** therefore tested comprehension, because normal understanding of grammar would mean a motor performance problem, while failure to understand grammatical relationships would mean the deficit reaches the grammar itself.
12|**“Man bites dog”** is the newspaper example of an unexpected event, and **psycholinguistic tests** are needed because ordinary conversation offers many **redundant cues**, which are separate sources of information that all point to the same meaning. || For “The dog bit the man,” a listener can combine DOG, BITE and MAN into the **most likely event**, with or without syntax, or rely on **canonical word order**, which is agent, then verb, then undergoer. >> The **agent** is the doer and the **undergoer** is the one the action happens to, and the **discourse context**, the surrounding conversation, clarifies a lot as well. || Broca's patients may depend on these **compensatory heuristics**, which are mental shortcuts that make up for a weakness elsewhere, so they should be tested with sentences like “The dog that the man is biting is angry.” >> In that sentence the **man** does the biting even though the dog comes first and dogs are more likely biters, so only grammatical analysis gives the right answer.
13|**Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** devised a list of exactly this kind of sentence, with four groups that change how much meaning helps the listener. >> **Control sentences** are simple and in canonical order, such as “The girl is kicking a green ball.” || The other three groups are **center-embedded**, which means a relative clause sits in the middle of the main clause, so the first noun is acted upon and the doer is inside the clause. >> With **semantic constraints**, as in “The apple that the boy is eating is red,” meaning alone reveals who does what, because apples cannot eat boys. >> In **reversible** sentences such as “The cow that the monkey is scaring is yellow,” either participant could do the action, and in **improbable** sentences such as “The bird that the worm is eating is blue,” common sense points to the wrong answer. || A listener who relies on meaning should succeed on the control and semantically constrained sentences and fail on the reversible and improbable ones, while a listener who parses normally should succeed on all four.
14|**Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** used a **sentence-picture matching task**, in which people heard a sentence and chose which of the pictures matched it, so accuracy reveals who the listener thinks did what to whom. >> They compared healthy controls with Broca's, conduction and Wernicke's aphasia patients on the four sentence types. || **Healthy controls** were about equally good at all sentence types, while **Wernicke's patients were poor overall**, even on simple control sentences, probably because of their general comprehension deficits. || **Broca's patients** did well when meaning supplied the answer but had particular difficulty keeping **“who did what to whom”** straight when **syntax conflicted with common sense**, as in the reversible and improbable sentences. >> This specific comprehension deficit was taken as evidence that **Broca's aphasia impacts syntax specifically**. >> Their paper calls it a dissociation of algorithmic and heuristic processes, because Broca's patients lose the **algorithmic** process of grammatical analysis and keep the **heuristic** shortcuts based on meaning.
15|A more specific theory of the **“parts of language”** opens up new ways of exploring **structure-function relationships**, which are links between a brain structure and the function it supports. || Instead of only sorting aphasics by good or poor comprehension, or by more or less fluency, researchers can **isolate problems with particular aspects of language**. >> One patient might know word meanings but assign the wrong roles in complex sentences, while another might produce a fluent grammatical frame filled with words that miss the message. || The key division is **the grammar versus the lexicon**, where grammar concerns the rules that relate words in a sentence and the lexicon concerns stored words and retrieving the right ones. >> A **grammar deficit** predicts trouble with reversible sentences but not with word meanings, and a **lexical deficit** predicts trouble with word meanings and naming but not with grammatical structure.
16|There are **problems with this dichotomy**, and they start with a task in which **Dick and colleagues** had participants listen to **four types of sentences** and indicate **which animal was doing the action**. >> A dog and a cat were on a screen, and the event was always a dog biting a cat while the sentence structure changed. || Two types put the doer first, the **active** “The dog is biting the cat” and the **subject cleft** “It’s the dog that is biting the cat,” so choosing the first animal mentioned gives the right answer. >> A **cleft** sentence splits a simple sentence with “It’s” and “that” to put focus on one part. || Two types put the receiver first, the **object cleft** “It’s the cat that the dog is biting” and the **passive** “The cat is bitten by the dog,” so the first-animal shortcut fails. >> Only a listener who parses the grammar succeeds on all four types.
17|When aphasia patients and **healthy aging controls** do this task, a pattern emerges much like the one **Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** observed. >> Elderly controls are nearly perfect on all four sentence types, and **Wernicke's** patients score low on every type, near chance. || **Broca's aphasia patients have particular difficulty with the syntactically complex stimuli**, which are the object clefts and passives, and these are **noncanonical** structures that depart from the usual doer-first order. >> Their performance is **overall not so great**, because even on simple active sentences they are far below the controls, so a general drop combines with an extra cost for complex structures. || The other patient groups also drop on complex sentences. **Anomic** patients, whose main problem is word finding, stay fairly accurate on actives and subject clefts and drop on object clefts and passives, and **conduction** aphasia patients fall sharply on object clefts.
18|The same task was given to **healthy undergraduates** under conditions that **stress the language system**, such as speeding up the speech, adding noise, or asking people to remember lists of numbers as a secondary task. >> **Compressed** speech is played faster than normal, a **low-pass filter** removes the higher frequencies so speech sounds muffled, and the digit lists use up memory resources. || Accuracy stayed high on actives and subject clefts, but **object cleft and passive sentences were particularly vulnerable to all the manipulations**, and the drop grew larger when two stressors were combined. >> With muffled speech that was also sped up, accuracy on object clefts and passives fell to about two thirds. || These students have no brain damage, so stressing the processing system is enough to produce an aphasia-like profile, which suggests that the comprehension deficits in Broca's aphasia may be a **“performance” issue after all**. >> Complex structures may demand more processing resources, so patients may keep their grammatical knowledge but lack the resources to use it efficiently.
19|**Saygin and colleagues (2004)** asked whether the comprehension deficits of Broca's aphasics are **language-specific** by having patients **match an action to an object**. >> In the **non-linguistic** version a drawing has a boy pantomiming an action such as licking, and patients choose the matching object from pictures such as an ice cream cone, a cake, broccoli and a rooster. >> In the **linguistic** version the action was given in words instead of by pantomime. || Controls were nearly perfect in both versions, while aphasic patients made more errors in both and somewhat more in the linguistic version, so **Broca's aphasics had difficulty whether the stimuli were linguistic or not**. >> Patients made the most errors when the wrong choice, called the **distractor**, was **semantic**, meaning related in meaning to the correct object, and fewer with unrelated or **affordance-based** distractors, where an **affordance** is an action an object allows. || A purely grammatical deficit predicts good performance on the nonverbal version, so the difficulty there points to a broader problem with action meanings that is shared with language.
20|Performance in the **non-verbal version** of the action-matching task from **Saygin and colleagues (2004)** was **significantly correlated with damage to Broca's area**. >> Damage linked to deficits in **non-linguistic pantomime interpretation** clustered in the left frontal region in front of the **central sulcus**, which includes Broca's area. || The task only asked patients to interpret a pantomimed action and choose an object, so they did not have to produce a sentence or work out the grammar of a spoken instruction. || Damage to Broca's area therefore affects **more than syntax**, because patients with damage there can struggle to connect an action with its meaning even when no language is involved. >> Any account of Broca's area has to explain both the grammatical comprehension findings and this nonlinguistic difficulty with actions.
21|**Saygin and colleagues** asked whether the comprehension deficits of Wernicke's aphasics are **language-specific** by having patients **match environmental sounds to pictures**. >> In the **nonverbal** version a speaker plays a sound such as a cow mooing, and patients choose between pictures such as a cow and a sheep. >> In the **verbal** version the speaker plays a spoken phrase such as “cow mooing,” and patients choose between pictures such as a cow and a violin. || Controls, patients with **right hemisphere damage** and anomic patients were all nearly perfect, Broca's patients were a little less accurate, and **Wernicke's patients were particularly bad at this task**. || **No significant difference** was observed between the verbal and nonverbal versions, so the deficit is not specific to words. >> Their difficulty concerns linking sounds of any kind to their meanings, whether the meaning arrives through language or through ordinary sounds.
22|Deficits in **both verbal and nonverbal sound-picture matching** are associated with **Wernicke's area**. >> Eight patients were impaired in the nonverbal version and ten in the verbal version, and in both groups the damage overlapped most in the **posterior temporal area of the left hemisphere**, around Wernicke's area. || The verbal and nonverbal impairments therefore come from damage to the same general region, even though a spoken phrase carries meaning through words and an animal call carries it without language. || Both tasks require connecting meaningful sounds to what they represent, so Wernicke's area appears to support **meaningful sound processing in general**, with language as one important case.
23|How language-specific the deficits in aphasia are is **still a controversy**. >> Some patients are especially vulnerable to complex grammatical structures, yet the same patients can struggle with action meanings or environmental sounds in tasks with no language, so an account of aphasia has to cover both findings. || What is clear is **how the terms of the debate have evolved**, because the field now has relatively **sophisticated tests** that tap specific aspects of language, **quantitative analyses of behavior**, and **quantitative analyses of structure-function relationships**. >> The tests include reversible, improbable and cleft sentences and nonverbal matching tasks, and the structure-function analyses relate patterns of brain damage to performance across many patients. || The field has come **a long way from the nineteenth century**, when Wernicke and Lichtheim drew boxes and arrows from a small number of individual cases.

25|**Voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping**, from **Bates, Wilson, Saygin, Dick, Sereno, Knight and Dronkers**, formalizes **structure-function relationships** in aphasia with MRI data by relating the location of brain damage to the severity of symptoms across many patients. >> A **voxel** is a small three-dimensional unit of an MRI brain image, and a voxel is linked to a symptom when patients with damage there score much worse than patients without damage there. || The symptoms are **defined very broadly**, with **fluency deficits** linked most strongly to damage toward the front of the left hemisphere and **comprehension deficits** linked to damage farther back, in the posterior temporal region. || Data from a large number of patients is therefore consistent with a **rough double dissociation**, where **posterior regions** are associated with **perception** and **anterior regions** are associated with **production**.
