2|**Babies are overrated** as language learners, so talk of **sensitive periods** for a second language, or **L2**, should not be carried too far. >> A typical toddler at a year and a half uses at least twenty words, and an adult who knew only about that many after the same stretch of language instruction would count as a very poor learner. || **Adults initially learn more quickly than children**, largely because of **transfer from L1**, the first language, and because of **better general cognitive skills** such as memory, attention and reasoning. || Where adults do worse is **ultimate attainment**, which is the final level of skill a learner reaches, because adults **rarely become native-like**. >> This may also be due in part to transfer, since the first language that gives a fast start can also hold a learner back from native-like skill.
3|Some toddler **language milestones** are really **general conceptual abilities**, such as dealing with **spatial relations between objects** or with **event representations**, which are mental representations of who does what in an event. >> Understanding “in and out” and “off and on” depends on spatial relations, and pretend play such as giving teddy a drink depends on event representations. || **Later learners** of a second language already have much of this in place, so they can focus on how those concepts are represented in the **target language**, meaning the language being learned. >> A toddler has to build the concepts and the words at the same time, which is a bigger job. || Later learners also already know another language, and that knowledge affects how the second language is learned because some of its ready-made ways of expressing concepts fit the new language and others clash with it.
4|**Transfer** is the use of native language, or **L1**, processes and representations for a second language, or **L2**, so a learner brings L1 habits for hearing sounds, building sentences and finding words into the L2. || Transfer can have both positive and negative impacts on second-language learning and performance. >> **Positive transfer** happens when an L1 pattern matches what the L2 requires, so the learner gets a head start. >> **Negative transfer** happens when an L1 pattern conflicts with the L2, so errors appear wherever the two languages differ. || A man who asks “Do you not be happy with me as the translator of the books of you?” is using English words with the structure of another language, because word-by-word translation carries over the word order of the first language, which is negative transfer in grammar.
5|Transfer affects **phonetic and phonological learning**, the learning of speech sounds, because **categorical perception** leads to **acquired similarity**, which means that sounds a language groups into one category come to sound more alike. || Hindi has **minimal pairs**, which are pairs of words that differ in only one sound. Each pair contrasts a **dental** consonant, made with the tongue against the teeth, with a **retroflex** consonant, made with the tongue curled back toward the roof of the mouth. >> Two words that both sound like “tap” to an English speaker mean hot and a kind of beat, two that sound like “dab” mean pressure and a kind of fruit, and two that sound like “ari” mean saw and crooked. || Native English speakers tend to use their **alveolar** category, made with the tongue on the ridge behind the teeth, to interpret both dental and retroflex consonants, so the two Hindi sounds seem the same. >> In this case transfer makes learning the L2 contrast **more difficult**, because the learner must separate sounds that English taught them to treat as one.
6|Transfer also affects **grammar learning**, and **classifiers** are a somewhat unusual grammatical feature across languages. A classifier is a word that must come between a number or demonstrative and a noun, and the choice depends on the kind of thing the noun names. >> In Chinese **yí gè rén**, “one person,” **gè** is the general classifier, while **bǎ** goes with objects that have a handle, **běn** with books, **zhāng** with flat objects and **zhī** with long, thin objects. || Native English speakers learning Chinese **have difficulty** with classifiers, because saying “a chair,” “that dog,” “the car” or “one book” in English never depends much on noun semantics, while in Chinese different nouns require different classifiers. >> One way to think about this is that English speakers **learned to ignore the cues for classifiers** as part of learning English grammar, so learning Chinese means attending again to cues such as shape and function.
7|**Grammatical gender** is another place where transfer causes problems, and it is different from social or cultural gender because it sorts nouns into classes, often called masculine and feminine. >> There is nothing “male” about a book or “female” about liberty, yet French **le livre** is masculine and **la liberté** is feminine. || Grammatical gender matters for **agreement**, which means that pronouns, adjectives and some forms of verbs all need to be coordinated with the gender of the noun. >> English nouns have no grammatical gender, so English speakers have no habit of tracking it. || French is famous for **synonyms with different gender**, such as **auto** and **voiture** for car, **rivière** and **fleuve** for river, and **vélo** and **bicyclette** for bicycle, so gender has to be learned word by word. >> Word endings give clues, since masculine nouns often end in -age or -isme, as in le fromage, and feminine nouns often end in -ie or -ion, as in la boulangerie and la nation.
8|**Transfer is not all bad**, because vocabulary learning in some L1 and L2 situations is aided by the **similarity of words across languages**. >> French **auto**, **rivière** and **bicyclette** resemble English auto, river and bicycle, and Japanese **コロッケ (korokke)** and **モノレール (monoreru)** mean croquette and monorail. || Sometimes the similarity is due to **shared etymology**, meaning a shared word history, and words with a common origin are called **cognates**, as with rivière and river. || Sometimes the similarity is due to more recent **borrowing**, which produces a **loan word**, a word that one language takes from another. >> Korokke and monoreru were borrowed from European languages with their sounds adapted to Japanese. >> Both kinds of word give **positive transfer**, because the learner already knows a similar form with a similar meaning and can anchor the new word to it.
9|**Not all similar-sounding words are cognates**, and the **bias** to interpret L2 words as related to similar-sounding L1 words can be very strong. >> A **false friend** is a word that looks or sounds like a word in another language but means something different. || Spanish “Arnold está embarazado” does not mean that Arnold is embarrassed, because **embarazado** means **pregnant**, so an English speaker relying on the sound gets an embarrassing result. || The link to the familiar L1 word forms before the learner checks the meaning, so the same tendency speeds learning when the meanings match, as with cognates, and **leads to errors** when they do not.
10|**De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** asked in the lab whether people use **cognates** in “second language” learning. They used **paired-associate learning** of made-up words, in which people study pairs and later recall one member of a pair when given the other. >> Dutch speakers were simply presented with Dutch words and their non-word partners and asked to learn the pairs, so Dutch stood in for the first language and the made-up words for a new second language. || The made-up words varied in their **similarity to the Dutch associate** and in **concreteness**, where a concrete word names something that can be seen or touched and an abstract word names an idea. >> The four kinds of pair were **gitaar** with **gettar** (“guitar,” concrete, cognate), **tijger** with **duunze** (“tiger,” concrete, non-cognate), **domein** with **domaan** (“domain,” abstract, cognate) and **herstel** with **groop** (“repair,” abstract, non-cognate). || Participants were then tested a week later on **retention**, meaning how well they remembered the pairs after a delay without study.
11|In the study by **De Groot and Keijzer (2000)**, **cognates were learned more rapidly and also remembered more easily**. Recall was tested across three training sessions and again in a fourth session after a week with no training. >> Recall rose across training for every kind of word, but concrete cognates started far higher than concrete non-cognates, and abstract non-cognates started lowest of all. >> After the week without training, concrete cognates kept most of what was learned while abstract non-cognates fell sharply, and responses were faster for cognates in every session. || The cognate advantage was largest in the first session, so **cognate status can contribute to the very earliest stages of word learning** in a second language. >> Concreteness also had a large effect that adds to the cognate effect, and one explanation for it is called **context availability**, the idea that concrete words are easier to link with a supporting context.
12|The experiment by **De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** is a good one because it tested more than **receptive vocabulary**, which here means how easily participants could produce the Dutch word given its made-up associate. >> A cognate-like made-up word already resembles the Dutch word, so a participant could answer by noticing the resemblance without having learned the pair, and that result alone would not be very impressive. >> The meanings of **gitaar**, **tijger** and **domein** could be guessed without any Dutch training, and made-up cognates such as **gettar**, **riffer** and **vialo** are only slightly changed Dutch words. || The study also tested **expressive vocabulary**, which the authors call **productive testing**, where the learner is given the Dutch word and must produce the made-up word. >> The learner has to recall the exact new form, so a cognate advantage on productive testing reflects **real learning of new forms**.
13|Participants in the study by **De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** really learned the new forms, because the cognate advantage held in **productive testing** as well as in **receptive testing**. Both were measured right after learning and again at retest a week later, and the important measure is how much recall dropped over the week. >> In productive testing, where learners had to produce the new word, cognates lost about half as much as non-cognates, and concrete words were forgotten less than abstract words. >> In receptive testing, where learners gave the Dutch word, scores were higher overall and cognates lost only a little while non-cognates again lost much more. || Cognates and concrete words were **forgotten less in both directions**, so their advantage extends from initial learning to long-term retention. >> Similarity to a known word helped learners form a **lasting memory** of the new word.
14|A central research question is whether **similar L1 words are always activated when processing L2**, and there have been many studies of it. >> If first-language words that sound similar become active even when a bilingual is trying to use only the second language, then the two languages are never fully separate in the mind. || **Marian and Spivey (2003)** give one nice example with a **visual world experiment**, in which people hear spoken instructions while looking at a set of objects and their eye movements are recorded. >> Looks toward an object whose L1 name sounds like the spoken L2 word would reveal that the L1 word became active. >> Eye movements capture that activation without asking for any report, while the person still goes on to choose the correct object.
15|This kind of experiment was used earlier for **categorical perception** by **McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)**, who studied gradient effects of within-category voice onset time (VOT) on **lexical access**. >> Participants saw a lamp, a ship, a bear and a pear and heard a word from a continuum running between “bear” and “pear,” which differ only in their first consonant. || **Eye movements** can tell us about **competition** between the possible responses that participants are considering. >> A person can click on the bear after looking briefly at the pear, and those looks mean that “pear” was active as a competitor. || The amount of competition between BEAR and PEAR seemed to depend on **distance from the category boundary**, with more looks to the competitor for sounds near the boundary between /b/ and /p/. >> The same method can ask whether a word in one language competes with a similar word in another.
16|**Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** found the **cohort effect**, where a **cohort** is the set of words that share the same beginning sounds. >> Participants viewed a grid of pictures and shapes and followed spoken instructions, with a **beaker** as the target, a **beetle** that shares its beginning, a **speaker** that rhymes with it and an unrelated **carriage**. || Looks to the beaker and the beetle **rose together early**, because both fit the first sounds of the word, and looks to the beetle fell once the sounds that distinguish the two words arrived. >> Looks to the speaker rose later and stayed smaller because its overlap comes at the end of the word, and looks to the carriage stayed near zero. || The cohort effect is this **early competition** from words that share a beginning with the target, so words compete as soon as they match the incoming sounds and drop out when the input stops matching them.
17|**Marian and Spivey (2003)** examined the cohort effect with **cross-language competitors**, which are objects whose name in the other language begins like the spoken target word. || They hypothesized that if **L1 words are always activated**, native Russian speakers should look at a book of matches when the target is “speaker” in English. >> English **speaker** begins with the same sounds as Russian **спички (spichki)**, which means matches, so the matches should draw looks if Russian is active during English listening. >> The two words are tied only by **sound**, so looks to the matches during an English task directly measure activation of spichki. || Each English target came with an English competitor and a Russian competitor, as when **speaker** came with **spear** and **spichki**, or **marker** with **marbles** and **marka**, stamp. >> For Russian targets the roles were reversed, so Russian **spichki** came with English **spear** and Russian **spitsi**, knitting needles.
18|**Marian and Spivey (2003)** first had Russian-English bilinguals follow instructions in English, which tests **competition during L2 spoken word recognition**. || When only one competitor was present, participants made eye movements to **within-language and between-language competitors to the same extent**, and far more often than to an unrelated control object. >> The Russian name of an object pulled the eyes as strongly as a similar English word did. || When both competitors were present, participants looked at the within-language competitor more, but they **still looked at the between-language competitor** about twice as often as in the control condition. >> The first language stays active during second-language listening, so bilinguals still choose the correct object while L1 words compete along the way.
19|The same bilinguals in the study by **Marian and Spivey (2003)** then did the experiment in Russian, which tests **competition during L1 spoken word recognition**. >> A Russian within-language competitor still drew extra looks, so the cohort effect appeared in Russian. || **English words do not seem to be activated** when native Russian speakers do the task in Russian. >> An object with a similar English name drew only slightly more looks than the control object, and that small numerical difference is **not statistically significant**, which means it falls within ordinary chance variation. || The two experiments together give an **asymmetric** pattern, because Russian words became active during English tasks and English words did not become reliably active during Russian tasks. >> The asymmetry fits the idea that the first language is more firmly established and more easily activated than the second.
20|The **Revised Hierarchical Model** of **Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** explains interactions between L1 and L2 in terms of **spreading activation**, which means that activating one representation partly activates the representations linked to it. >> The model has an L1 lexicon, or vocabulary, an L2 lexicon and a store of concepts, with **lexical links** joining the two lexicons and **conceptual links** joining each lexicon to the concepts. || The **L1 lexicon** is assumed to be **larger** and **more strongly connected to relevant concepts**, so L2 words have weaker links to their meanings. || The **L2 lexicon** is assumed to have **strong forward connections to the L1 lexicon**, so activation can spread more readily from an L2 word to an L1 word than the other way. >> This occurs because this direction is emphasized in learning, since learners usually attach each new L2 word to its L1 translation.
21|The **Revised Hierarchical Model** of **Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** can explain what happens when the task of **Marian and Spivey (2003)** is done in L2 with the target “speaker.” >> The concept for “matches” **partially activates the Russian word for “matches,”** spichki, because L1 words are strongly connected to their concepts, and the sounds of English speaker also partly activate spichki because the two words begin alike. >> This activity **feeds back to the conceptual activation** for “matches,” and that conceptual activation leads to **eye movements toward the L1 competitor**. || Speaker and spichki are linked only by sound overlap and share no meaning. || When the task is done in L1, the **conceptual links with L2** might be **too weak** to create this kind of interference reliably. >> An English competitor would need a strong link from the English word to its concept, so the unequal looks come from the unequal strength of the links.
22|The **Revised Hierarchical Model** of **Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** makes a **counter-intuitive prediction** even for **balanced bilinguals**, people who are about equally skilled in both languages. >> They do not look like “two monolinguals in one,” with each language running separately. || For a balanced bilingual the **conceptual links are similarly strong for both languages**, so each concept strongly activates its word in both languages. >> **Competitive dynamics**, in which words from the two languages keep competing for selection, and **inhibition**, the suppression of the word from the language not in use, will be engaged all the time. || This prediction has led to a lot of work on the advantages of bilinguals in non-linguistic **cognitive control** tasks, where cognitive control is the ability to manage attention and suppress irrelevant responses. >> Constant practice at selecting one language and suppressing the other might improve control of attention in general.
23|So far second-language learning has followed one **sequence**, in which a person begins learning L1, later learns L2 and continues learning and using L1 the whole time. || The other sequence is one where the person **stops using L1** after L2 is introduced, so L1 is no longer heard or spoken and L2 takes its place. >> In that case L2 can appear to completely replace L1, although some residual knowledge may remain that later learning can unmask. || This situation separates two things that usually go together, which are **learning a language early** and **continuing to use it**. >> If early learning leaves a permanent foundation then traces of L1 should remain without use, and if continued use is needed then L1 might fade or disappear.
24|**Pallier, Dehaene, Poline, LeBihan, Argenti, Dupoux and Mehler** used brain imaging of **language plasticity in adopted adults** to ask whether a second language can replace the first. >> They tested adults in their twenties and early thirties who had been adopted into French homes from Korean orphanages between about three and eight years of age. >> These adults learned Korean as young children and stopped using it entirely after adoption, so they are a natural case of L1 use stopping. || The test was whether the adoptees had any **residual knowledge of Korean**, meaning any remaining trace of the earlier language. >> Monolingual French speakers with no Korean experience were the comparison group, so adoptees doing better on Korean-related tasks would mean that some Korean had survived.
25|Korean has a **three-way stop contrast**, where a **stop** is a consonant made by fully blocking the airflow and then releasing it, such as /p/, /t/ or /k/. >> English and French have two kinds of stop at each place in the mouth, such as /b/ and /p/, while Korean has three, called **aspirated**, **plain** and **tense**. >> An aspirated stop has a strong puff of air, a plain stop has a lighter release and a tense stop is made with a stiffened throat. || The contrast appears in the **bilabial** series **pʰul** “grass,” **pul** “fire” and **p’ul** “horn,” in the **dental** series **tʰal** “mask,” **tal** “moon” and **t’al** “daughter,” and in the **velar** series **kʰo** “nose,” **kok** “music” and **k’ok** “exactly.” || French speakers have no such contrast and tend to hear the three sounds as two, so adoptees who kept any Korean sound knowledge should hear the differences **better than French speakers**.
26|**Ventureyra and colleagues** found that Korean adoptees in France were **no better than monolingual French speakers** at discriminating the Korean contrasts. >> Adoptees, French native speakers and Korean native speakers heard pairs of sounds and judged whether they were different. || The pairs contrasted plain with tense, plain with aspirated and tense with aspirated stops, along with a plain and tense contrast for s. >> Korean speakers heard these differences nearly every time, while adoptees and French speakers rarely heard them. || Two control conditions confirm that the adoptees **could do the task**, because all three groups heard a **vowel** contrast nearly every time and correctly called **identical** sounds the same almost every time. >> The difficulty was specific to the Korean stop contrasts, where early Korean experience left **no measurable advantage**.
27|In a more extensive study, **Pallier and colleagues** found that adoptees were **no better than monolingual French speakers** on three tasks. >> When **distinguishing Korean from a collection of largely unrelated languages**, both groups rated Korean and Japanese sentences as similarly Korean-like and rated Polish, Swedish and Wolof lower. >> When **identifying the correct Korean translation of French words**, both groups scored close to chance. || When **deciding whether a short excerpt was repeated from a recorded sentence they had just heard**, both groups did best on French and equally well on Korean, Japanese and Polish. || These are **not standard psycholinguistic tasks**, because Pallier and colleagues were looking for **any remaining trace of Korean** with tasks meant to catch even weak knowledge. >> They did not find any, so on every measure the adoptees looked like people who had never learned Korean.
28|**Pallier and colleagues** also found that listening to French activated the **classical language network**, including **superior temporal** and **inferior frontal** regions, in both groups. >> The superior temporal region is the upper part of the temporal lobe and is involved in processing speech sounds, and the inferior frontal region is the lower part of the frontal lobe and includes Broca's area. || Activity for Polish, Japanese and Korean was subtracted from activity for French, and French always produced more language-network activity than the foreign language in both the French group and the Korean adoptees. || **Subtracting Korean from French** might have revealed a difference between groups if any **residual processing ability for Korean** were left, because Korean would then activate the language network of the adoptees more. >> It did not, so the brains of the adoptees treated Korean as a foreign language, which agrees with the behavioral tests.
29|Interactions between L2 and L1 can also be thought of in terms of **competitive dynamics**. Imagine that **L1 is represented as a set of connections** within and between different brain regions, which are built and maintained through use. || If those connections are never activated again, they could be **“taken over” by L2**. >> L2 is used constantly, so its connections strengthen and spread, while unused L1 connections weaken and can be claimed by L2. || The same process occurs in vision with **monocular deprivation** in mice. >> The connections of the deprived eye first show increased spine motility, then **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration**, the breakdown of cut-off nerve fibers. >> Later the fibers of the other eye show **axon sprouting and new spine birth**, so the eye that keeps receiving input takes over the lost space.
30|The evidence from the adoptees says that a second language **apparently can replace the first**, because they showed no behavioral or brain sign of their childhood Korean. || What remains open is whether **traces of L1** are left behind that can be **“salvaged,”** meaning recovered or put to use. >> Such a trace might not appear on a test of current ability and could still make **relearning** easier. || Testing current recognition asks what a person can do with the language now, while testing later learning asks whether new learning benefits from something left over. >> Relearning is therefore the measure that could reveal a trace that the adoptee tests could not detect.
31|**Au, Knightly, Jun and Oh**, from the group of Terry Au, studied undergraduates who **overheard Spanish frequently as children** but never learned to speak it. >> All participants started classroom Spanish in their early teens and were tested at the end of a second-year college Spanish course. || On **pronunciation** these childhood overhearers were far ahead of typical late L2 learners and closer to native speakers. >> Their **voice onset time** for voiceless stops matched native speakers almost exactly, while late learners produced much longer, English-like values. >> They also showed more **lenition**, the softening of voiced stops between vowels, their accent ratings fell between the other two groups, and they knew some childhood slang. || On **grammar** they had little or no advantage, since gender agreement, number agreement and grammaticality judgments were about like those of late learners. >> Early listening left traces in the sound system that were **unmasked** by later study.
32|Taking the data from the **Korean adoptees** together with the data from the **overhearers**, two conclusions follow. >> If L1 is removed from the environment entirely, **L2 can appear to completely replace it**, as the adoptees showed no Korean on any test of current ability. >> This replacement is **not necessarily complete**, because there may be some **residual knowledge** that can be “unmasked” with later learning, as with the Spanish pronunciation of the overhearers. || The two populations also differ in potentially important ways. >> The adoptees were tested on current knowledge with no new Korean instruction, while the overhearers were tested after years of Spanish classes that gave hidden knowledge a chance to surface, so the results are hard to compare directly.
33|Several **potentially important differences** between the adoptees and the overhearers might explain why only one group kept traces of an early language. >> **Korean is rarely heard in France** while Spanish is common in Los Angeles, and a **heritage language** spoken by family members differs from a language not spoken by anyone you know. >> **Cognates** are common between Spanish and English and rare between Korean and French, and Spanish and English have a **shared writing system** while Korean uses a different script from French. || This is a **general issue** with work on second language acquisition and bilingualism, because languages and learning situations differ in many ways that are **hard, perhaps impossible, to control experimentally**. >> Researchers cannot assign people to grow up with particular languages, and separating the factors would take groups that differ in only one way.
