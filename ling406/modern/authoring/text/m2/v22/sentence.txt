2|**“The horse raced past the barn fell”** is grammatical, but it feels broken because most readers first take **raced** as the **main verb**, which leaves no room for **fell** when it arrives. >> The sentence describes **two events**, since someone raced the horse past the barn and the horse then fell. || Putting back the missing words **disambiguates** it, as in **“The horse that was raced past the barn fell,”** because “that was” makes “raced past the barn” a description of which horse is meant and leaves fell as the main verb. >> It could fairly be called the sentence that **launched a thousand experiments**, because researchers have used it again and again to study how readers build structure as words come in.
3|**“The horse raced past the barn fell”** is almost as famous as **“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously,”** which is grammatical but meaningless, while the horse sentence is grammatical and meaningful but **very hard to process**. >> A comic jokes that nobody would ever say it, and the reply is that farmers might if their horses kept falling over, which only works because the sentence really is grammatical. || It is the classic case of the **“garden path” effect**, in which a sentence steers the reader into one reading early on and later words reveal that the reading was wrong. >> Raced looks like the main verb and fell then clashes with that structure, so the reader must go back and treat “raced past the barn” as “that was raced past the barn.” >> Garden paths arise because readers build structure word by word and commit to one analysis before the sentence is over. || So **grammaticality and processing difficulty are different things**, because a sentence with an acceptable structure can still be very hard to follow as it unfolds.
4|**Syntactic explanations** of the garden path effect start from a **parser**, a mental process that builds a **syntactic tree** recording how the words of a sentence group into phrases and how the phrases relate. || In the correct tree for the horse sentence, the subject is a large noun phrase, or **NP**, holding “the horse” plus a reduced clause meaning “that was raced past the barn,” and the main verb phrase, or **VP**, contains only **fell**. >> In the failed parse, “raced past the barn” is the main VP with raced as the main verb, so fell has nowhere to attach and the parse breaks down. || The parser runs in a cognitive system with **limited memory** and **time constraints**, so it cannot wait for the end of the sentence before building structure, and it can keep only a limited number of structures in play at once.
5|The **grammatical version** of the horse sentence is much more complex syntactically than the failed parse because it has **many more nodes**. A **node** is a point in a syntactic tree that stands for a grammatical grouping such as an NP or a VP. >> The correct tree needs extra nodes for the embedded clause and the silent “that” and “was,” while the failed parse only makes “raced past the barn” the main VP. || One way to save space is to **initially prefer smaller trees**, a strategy called **“minimal attachment,”** in which each new word is fitted into the existing structure with the fewest new nodes. >> Minimal attachment favors **raced as the main verb** because that analysis needs the fewest nodes, so when fell arrives and requires the larger structure, the reader has to rebuild the tree.
6|**“Late closure”** is another parsing strategy in this framework, and it is a bias to **keep the current clause “open” as long as possible**, so incoming words are attached to the phrase being built. || In **“The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite,”** the phrase “with the dynamite” wants to associate with the big **verb phrase** starting with “blew open,” and **Frazier (1987)** contrasts two structures for it. >> The **preferred** structure attaches the phrase to the VP, so the dynamite is the **instrument** used to blow open the safe. >> The **unpreferred** structure attaches it to the NP “the safe,” so the phrase says which safe was blown open. || Both attachments are grammatical, and here the preferred one is also the minimal attachment and the sensible meaning, so readers reach the right reading easily.
7|Late closure is visible in a sentence that **requires the “unpreferred” parse to be sensible**, such as **“The burglar blew open the safe with the jewels.”** >> Jewels are a poor tool for blowing open a safe, so the sensible meaning is the safe that contained the jewels, which needs the unpreferred attachment to the noun phrase “the safe.” || In careful tests of **reading time** and **eye movements**, people are **slower to read sentences where the sensible reading requires the unpreferred parse**, and the extra time appears right where the parse has to change. >> Readers **momentarily entertain the idea that the jewels were used to blow open the safe**, because the parser first attaches the phrase to the verb phrase. They then have to **retrace their steps** and attach it to the noun phrase.
8|This explanation rests on a **parser that deals exclusively with the abstract syntactic structure** of the sentence, much like the **“lumber mill”** example from speech perception. In a lumber mill, each stage works on its own material and hands a finished product to the next stage. >> The parser builds structure from grammatical categories alone, without checking meaning or context, and meaning is worked out afterward. || The parser has **its own operating characteristics**, because it wants the **simplest tree possible**, which is **minimal attachment**, and it gets there partly by leaving clauses open as long as possible, which is **late closure**. || This deals with ambiguity very efficiently by **ignoring it until it becomes a problem**, since the parser builds only one likely structure and is usually right. >> When a later word does not fit, the structure must be revised, and that revision is the garden path difficulty.
9|A **purely syntactic explanation** has a problem, because **syntactically “difficult” sentences are not always difficult to parse**. >> Published research articles are full of **reduced relative clauses**, which are relative clauses with “that was” or “that were” left out, as in “the horse raced past the barn fell.” || The capitalized verbs in sentences such as “Referential information PROVIDED by the discourse is of no help,” from **Britt, Perfetti, Garrod and Rayner (1992)**, and “Recent research REPORTED by FERREIRA AND CLIFTON,” from **Frazier and Rayner (1987)**, each introduce a reduced relative, **but these are not impossibly difficult sentences**. >> The same holds for examples from **Trueswell, Tanenhaus and Garnsey (1994)** and **Altmann and Steedman (1988)**, which readers follow with no garden path. || **MacDonald, Perlmutter and Seidenberg (1994)** argued instead that **semantic factors and statistical regularities interact with parsing from very early on**. >> Semantic factors concern meaning, and statistical regularities are patterns in how often words occur in particular structures, so familiar words can favor a reduced relative reading from the start.
10|The collection of reduced relatives is **wonderfully passive-aggressive**, because **many of the examples are drawn from papers that use the difficulty of parsing reduced relatives as evidence for an autonomous parser**. An **autonomous parser** builds syntactic structure without help from meaning or context. >> Those authors argued that reduced relatives are hard to process, yet they wrote them in their own articles and readers understood them without trouble. || In “the initial syntactic analysis ASSIGNED to a sentence,” assigned modifies analysis, and in “A person PRESENTED with the evidence EXAMINED by the lawyer,” from **Ferreira and Clifton (1986)**, presented modifies person and examined modifies evidence. || These were **not the only reduced relatives** in those articles, since they turned up at about **one per journal page**. >> Reduced relatives are routine in scientific writing, so in a supportive context a formally complex structure is easy to understand.
11|When we read **“Recent research reported by…,”** several factors besides syntax are at work. >> By **semantics**, research is **inanimate**, so it is an unlikely subject for a verb like report and is easily taken as the thing that was reported. >> By **lexical statistics**, the learned patterns of how often words appear in particular structures, “reported” occurs frequently in reduced relatives, and by **“world knowledge”** it does so very frequently in scientific papers, especially after the word “research.” || These and **many other factors interact with the syntax of the sentence very early on**, so they help decide which structure is chosen in the first place. || **“The burglar arrested in the parking lot was charged today”** is easy because burglars tend to be arrested, so arrested is read as starting a reduced relative. >> **“The policeman arrested in the parking lot was charged today”** is harder because policemen tend to do the arresting, so arrested is first taken as the main verb and “was charged” causes a garden path, even though the grammar is identical.
12|**“World knowledge” can be very local**, because it can come from the visual scene in front of us. **Spivey and colleagues (1995)** tested this with a **visual world** of real objects, which were a pencil, a box, an empty towel and an apple sitting on a towel. >> People heard **“Put the apple on the towel in the box”** and carried it out while their eye movements were recorded. || With **minimal attachment**, “the towel” temporarily seems to end the clause “put the apple on the towel,” so “on the towel” is taken as the **destination** when it really says **which apple** to move. || People **look at the empty towel while planning their movements**, as though considering it as a target, before planning a movement to the box. >> With the unambiguous instruction **“Put the apple that’s on the towel in the box,”** the eyes go from the apple straight to the box.
13|In a **two-referent context** the display holds **two apples**, one on a towel and one elsewhere, along with the empty towel and the box. >> “The apple” alone no longer says which apple to move, so **“on the towel”** now does useful work by picking out one of the two. || Participants usually look at the apple on the towel and then at the box, and looks to the empty towel become much less common. >> **The sequence and the timing of eye movements**, measured against the nouns in the speech, **did not differ** between the ambiguous and the unambiguous instructions. || Listeners take “on the towel” as a description of the apple from the start, so the destination reading never takes hold, and the same sentence gets a different grammatical analysis because the visual context differs.
14|With **two apples** in the display, it suddenly becomes **felicitous**, meaning natural and appropriate, to give more information about **which apple** is meant. >> With one apple there is no need to say which apple, so “on the towel” seems like extra information and is easily taken as a destination. || So **people do not look at the empty towel as much**. >> With one apple, the ambiguous instruction drew looks to the empty towel on a little over half of the trials, and with two apples it did so on far fewer trials, close to the level for the unambiguous instruction. || The words of the instruction were the same in both displays, so this is an effect of **referential context**, the set of objects a phrase could refer to. Referential context influences the grammatical relationship listeners assign while the sentence is still being heard.
15|The results from **Spivey and colleagues (1995)** are **a big problem for purely syntactic accounts**, because **different parses of the same sentence are favored under different semantic and pragmatic conditions, and this effect happens very early**. >> **Semantic** conditions concern meaning, and **pragmatic** conditions concern how language is used in a situation, such as whether a speaker must specify which object is meant. || The same ambiguous sentence misleads listeners often with one apple and rarely with two, so only the visual situation changes the parse the listener adopts first. || The difference appears in **eye movements during the sentence**, before the action is carried out. >> If context only repaired the parse afterward, listeners in both displays would first look at the empty towel, so the drop with two apples means context shaped the **first parse**.
16|More recently, researchers have used **mouse movements** to study these questions, and **Farmer and colleagues (2007)** tracked the path of the computer mouse while participants carried out spoken instructions in a version of the apple and towel display. >> It is not just eye movements but **the actual execution of arm movements** that can be influenced by **syntactic ambiguity** and its interaction with the **visually defined context**. || With one apple, the hand moved from the apple toward the box, but with ambiguous instructions its path **curved toward the empty towel**, most strongly in the middle of the movement. >> With two apples, the ambiguous and unambiguous paths were nearly identical. || The **Euclidean distance**, the straight-line distance between the ambiguous and unambiguous paths at each moment, grew larger with one apple, and with two apples it peaked lower and earlier. >> A competing interpretation bends the path of the hand even when the movement ends at the correct place.

18|**The** is a **determiner**, a word that opens a **noun phrase**, so once the parser meets “the” it expects a noun soon, possibly with an adjective or another modifier first. >> “The” names nothing by itself, but it limits which kinds of words can come next. || The expectation is about a **grammatical category** and not about one particular word, because many nouns could follow, such as pizza, dog or book, or an adjective could come first, as in “the hot pizza.” >> Words that fit the expectation are processed smoothly, while words that break it cause difficulty.
19|**Pizza** is a **noun** that completes the noun phrase opened by “the,” and it is the **head of the noun phrase**, the central word the rest of the phrase is built around. >> “The pizza” can now serve as the subject, so the parser expects a verb or verb phrase saying what the pizza did or what happened to it. || Recognizing the word gives two kinds of information. Its **category**, noun, tells the parser how it can combine with other words, and its **meaning**, a kind of food, sets up expectations about plausible events such as being eaten or baked. >> Both grammatical and meaning-based expectations guide how the next words are processed.
20|**Was** is a past-tense form of **be**, and it can act as an **auxiliary**, a helping verb that joins another verb to mark grammatical information such as tense or voice. >> In “the pizza was eaten,” was helps form a **passive** with the participle eaten, and in “the pizza was in the oven,” it links the subject to a location phrase. || After “was,” the sentence could describe where the pizza was, what it was like or what was done to it, so several structures stay open. >> The parser must keep the developing structure available while more words arrive, because the role of was depends partly on what comes next.
21|**In** is a **preposition**, a word that expresses a relationship such as location or containment, and it begins a **prepositional phrase**. >> “In” does not name the location by itself, so it sets up the expectation of a noun phrase that supplies it, as in “in the oven” or “in the box.” || After “the pizza was,” the word “in” narrows the possible structures, and the sentence is now heading toward a statement about where the pizza was. >> The expectation is grammatical, so the next word must be able to start a noun phrase, as “the,” “an” or a noun can.
22|**The** begins a new **noun phrase** inside “in the …,” the phrase that will name the location. >> “The” does not supply the location by itself, so a noun is still needed, possibly with modifiers before it, as in “in the hot oven.” || The parser now strongly expects a **noun**, and this expectation concerns a **word category** and not only a sensible topic. >> Knowing the sentence is about pizza makes words like oven or box likely, but the grammatical requirement holds whatever the meaning, so any word that cannot be part of a noun phrase will clash with it.
23|**Eaten** is the **past participle** of **eat**, a verb form and not a noun, so it cannot complete the noun phrase that “in the” has set up. >> **“In the eaten”** puts a verb form exactly where a noun is required, which is a **word-category violation**, a word of the wrong grammatical category in a position that demands a different one. || A category error differs from a strange but grammatical meaning, because “The pizza was in the ocean” is odd in meaning but grammatically fine, while no unusual situation can rescue “the pizza was in the eaten.” >> Brain responses to **category errors** and **meaning errors** can be compared to see whether grammar and meaning are processed differently.
24|The **early left anterior negativity**, or **ELAN**, is an **ERP** response elicited by **word class violations**. An ERP, or **event-related potential**, is electrical brain activity recorded at the scalp, averaged over many trials and timed to an event such as the onset of a word. >> Classic stimuli are **“The pizza was in the eaten”** and **“The scientist criticized Max’s of proof the theorem,”** where a participle or the preposition of appears where a noun is expected. || The ELAN is **early**, arising about a tenth to a fifth of a second after the critical word. It is also **left and anterior**, meaning strongest over the left front of the scalp, which raises the question of whether it is specific to language. >> It appears **specifically for word category violations**, such as a noun when a verb is expected, and its speed was taken as evidence for a very quick syntactic check of word categories before meaning is processed.
25|With visually presented words, **the ELAN came from visual cortex**, the region at the back of the brain that processes what we see, as **Dikker, Rabagliati, Farmer and Pylkkänen (2010)** found. >> For nouns ending in a recognizable noun suffix, **“The beautifully princess …”** drew a larger early visual response than **“The beautiful princess …,”** because the adverb beautifully predicts a verb or adjective and princess comes as a surprise. || When a verb is expected, for example, **that prediction can feed back down to the earliest parts of the visual system** and predict visual patterns consistent with **the way verbs are written**. >> The very early response to the error might reflect **a mismatch between that predictive process and what finally appears on the screen**. || A word is **morphologically marked** as a noun or verb when a part of it, such as a suffix, signals the category. Later comparisons answer whether the effect needs such marking, because it also appears for nouns with a typical noun form but not for neutral nouns. >> The other possibility raised is a split between **open-class** content words and **closed-class** function words.
26|Many English words can be either a **noun** or a **verb**, and the rest of the sentence decides which. >> **Perches** is a noun in “the bird perches seem easy to install” and a verb in “the bird perches comfortably in the cage.” >> **Needs** is a noun in “the student needs were not being met” and a verb in “the student needs to be more focused.” || The word form alone does not settle its grammatical role, so the **continuation** after the ambiguous word resolves it. || Words also differ in **phonological form typicality**, which is how closely a word’s sound pattern resembles typical nouns or typical verbs. >> Across several thousand words, nouns generally sound more like other nouns and verbs more like other verbs, but there are also **verb-like nouns** and **noun-like verbs** that sound like one category while belonging to the other.
27|In **form typicality**, **“perches” is noun-like** and **“needs” is verb-like**, which are learned statistical tendencies in how words sound, though both words can still fill either role. || For **perches**, the noun use in “the bird perches seem easy to install” matches its form, and the verb use in “the bird perches comfortably in the cage” goes against it. >> For **needs**, the verb use in “the student needs to be more focused” matches its form, and the noun use in “the student needs were not being met” goes against it. || Each word appears once as a noun and once as a verb, so the same form is compared in a **matching** and a **mismatching** use. >> If form typicality guides parsing, the mismatching uses should be harder to process, even though every sentence is grammatical.
28|Reading is **slower when a word’s required category conflicts with its form typicality**. >> The measure is the extra reading time at **disambiguation**, the point where the continuation makes the category clear, compared with the **point of ambiguity**, the ambiguous word itself, adjusted for word length. || For **noun-like homonyms** such as perches, resolving the word as a noun added only a small cost, while resolving it as a verb added a much larger one. >> For **verb-like homonyms** such as needs, the pattern reversed, because a noun resolution added a large cost and a verb resolution added almost nothing. || The ambiguous word stays the same in each pair and only the continuation changes, so the extra cost comes from a continuation that demands the category the form does not match. >> Parsing uses learned statistics about how word forms sound, along with the structure the sentence finally requires.
29|The **ELAN effect for nouns where a verb is expected** is limited to cases where **syntactic information**, meaning morphemes that indicate noun-ness, or **statistical form-based information**, meaning “nouniness,” is **physically available** in the word. >> A **morpheme** is the smallest meaningful part of a word, such as a noun suffix, and “nouniness” is how much a word’s form resembles typical nouns. || The early visual response peaks about a tenth of a second after a word appears. It was reliably larger for the unexpected version with **bimorphemic nouns**, as in “The beautifully princess …,” and with **typical nouns**, as in “The tastelessly soda …,” than for the expected version. >> **No effect was observed for neutral nouns like “infant” that lack strong cues**, since “The cute infant …” and “The cutely infant …” did not differ. || An abstract category mismatch alone is not enough, because the early response appears only when the arriving word carries physical evidence against the predicted form.
30|**Sentence processing fits in four sentences**, and **“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously”** stands for the idea that **syntax is independent of semantics to some extent**, since a sentence can be grammatically correct but meaningless. || **“The horse raced past the barn fell”** stands for the idea that syntactic structure is often highly **ambiguous**, or really **vague**, though we notice only where things fail. >> Reading times for preferred and unpreferred structures suggest that syntactic processes **take shortcuts to save memory** and do not activate every possible parse. || **“Put the apple on the towel in the box”** stands for the idea that **context, pragmatics and other factors interact with syntactic processing from very early on**. >> **“The bird perches were comfortable”** stands for the idea that the very earliest syntactic effects in the brain are driven in part by **form typicality**, not necessarily by morphosyntactic structures, and seem related to **predictions about the physical forms of words**.
