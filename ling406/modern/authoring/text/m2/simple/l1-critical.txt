2|A **critical period** and a **sensitive period** are different because of how strict the time limit on learning is. This is because a critical period has a **discrete, “programmed” onset and offset** (the window opens and closes at fixed points in development). Learning (change in response to the environment) is **strictly limited to this period**. There is **no sensitivity to the environment before or after**, so experience outside the window does nothing. || A **sensitive or “optimal” period** is looser. **Learning is possible throughout the life span**. Learning is just **optimal during particular windows**, when it is easiest or works best. These windows can have a **gradual onset and offset**, and experience can itself change when they start and end. || The two ideas predict different things for people who learn late. >> A critical period says late learning cannot happen. So if a late learner gets better at all, that goes against a critical period. >> A sensitive period says late learning is harder but still possible. So if early learners keep a lasting advantage, that supports a sensitive period. || Since even the classic critical period in vision fits “sensitive period” better, the same question comes up for language.
3|**Amblyopia** is **poor vision in one eye** even though the eye itself is healthy. The definition says despite normal **peripheral function** (the working of the eye itself). This is because the problem is in how the brain handles the input from that eye. || Three conditions can lead to amblyopia. >> **Strabismus** is when the eyes are misaligned. The two eyes point in different directions, so the brain gets two conflicting images. >> A **cataract** is a clouding of the lens of the eye. It blurs or blocks the light that reaches the back of the eye. >> **Anisometropia** is a large difference in focusing power between the two eyes. One eye's image is much blurrier than the other's. || Poor vision can last **long after the conditions themselves have been remediated** (corrected). This is because the conditions cause **periods of deprivation from normal visual input**. Fixing the eye brings the input back. The brain may still not use that input well. Since a temporary early problem can leave a lasting effect, when the problem happens matters as much as what it is. >> A cataract taken out early may leave little lasting damage. >> A cataract taken out late may leave poor vision even after the lens is clear.
4|**Amblyopia** happens because the two eyes compete in the brain and the weaker eye loses. This is because both eyes send input to **visual cortex**, and the two inputs **compete** to control the neurons there. These are the **competitive dynamics between cortical inputs**. When both eyes work normally, they share the neurons evenly. When one eye has abnormal vision, the other eye can **“colonize”** its cortical representation. The stronger eye takes over neurons that would have served the weaker eye. || **Monocular deprivation** (blocking or degrading the input to one eye) in mice reveals how the takeover happens over time. >> Within **2 to 3 days**, there is **increased spine motility**. **Dendritic spines** (small projections where neurons receive connections) become unstable and move around. >> Within **3 to 4 days**, there is **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration** (the breakdown of nerve fibers cut off from their source). The deprived eye's connections disappear. >> After **more than a week**, there is **axon sprouting and new spine birth**. The open eye's fibers branch out and form connections where the deprived eye lost them. || Fixing the eye later does not fix the vision. This is because the restored eye **remains at a competitive disadvantage**. The other eye now holds the connections. Ordinary viewing with both eyes open keeps favoring the stronger eye. Since the stronger eye keeps winning, **normal cortical responses cannot be restored by normal experience**.
5|The “good” eye gets patched because the patch gives the weak eye a chance to compete again. This is because treatments for amblyopia are **based on competition**. One of the most effective treatments is **intermittently patching the “good” eye**. The patch blocks the stronger eye's input for part of the day. The brain then has to rely on the weaker eye. So the weaker eye's input can win back connections in cortex. || Patching **restores the competitive balance between the eyes**. Then the brain's own **plasticity** (its ability to change its connections in response to experience) can restore normal function. >> The patch does not repair the eye itself. >> The patch changes the experience the brain gets, and plasticity does the rest. || Patching is **most effective early in development** and **may be ineffective by late childhood** (10 years). This is because a young brain is highly plastic, so rebalancing the input leads to fast reorganization. As the visual system matures, the same treatment helps less and less. Since the treatment works through plasticity, when it is given matters as much as the treatment itself.
6|Adults with amblyopia can still get better, even after the critical period. **Levi and Polat (1996)** found that **plasticity is not abolished entirely**, even well into adulthood. Some of the patients were **in their 50s**, an age normally considered past the point where treatment works. || The adults did **long-term, intensive training** on specific **psychophysical tasks** (tasks that measure the link between a physical stimulus and what a person perceives). They used the affected eye. They practiced visual judgments for many **kilotrials** (one kilotrial is a thousand trials). || Getting better was measured with a **threshold** (the smallest stimulus a person can reliably detect). A lower threshold means better vision. >> Over the first **19 kilotrials**, the threshold **went down steadily**, and that change was highly significant. >> From about **20 to 35 kilotrials**, the threshold **stayed flat**. >> Around **36 kilotrials**, the threshold **went down again** to a new lower level and stayed there. || The training made the adults better at the task and also improved their **general visual function**. This is because the adult visual system keeps some **experience-dependent plasticity**. Since the right kind and amount of training can still change the adult visual system, late improvement is possible.
7|Playing video games made vision better in adults with amblyopia. **Li, Ngo, Nguyen, Lam and Levi (2008)** had adults play an action video game with their amblyopic eye. The game was a first-person shooter set in a battle scene. Playing it **enhanced visual acuity and visual attention**. >> **Visual acuity** (sharpness of vision, the ability to see fine detail) got better, so vision through the weak eye was clearer. >> **Visual attention** (the ability to pick out and focus on the important parts of a scene) got better. In a busy battle, the important parts are things like targets. || This is because action games demand fast visual decisions and constant attention. Those demands force the visual system to use the amblyopic eye's input. So the weak eye gets intense, varied practice. || The gains came from the training and not from normal development. This is because these adults were long past childhood visual development. They were also well past the age when patching usually works best. Since a demanding, engaging visual task still changed a mature visual system, adult amblyopia has **plasticity**. An early optimal window can close without removing every chance of improvement.
8|**Amblyopia** is a classic example of a **sensitive period**, not a strict critical period. This is because it has a strong early window, but adults can still change later. A strict critical period would rule out any late improvement. || Three things make the early window strong. >> A **peripheral disturbance in early experience** (an early problem with the eye itself) can lead to **life-long disruption in visual function**. >> The long-term loss of function is the **sequela** (a lasting result of an earlier condition) of a temporary deficit. The deficit happened during **experience-dependent organization of the brain**. The brain organized itself around abnormal input, and that organization stays. >> **Treatment is most effective during a limited developmental window**. Patching works best early and stops working well by late childhood. || However, new evidence suggests that the **right kind of experience can reverse deficits late into adulthood**. Intensive perceptual training made adults' vision better. Video game play did too. Late learning is harder and needs special conditions, but it does happen. Since amblyopia has both a strong early window and some later change, it fits a sensitive period.
9|The vision findings relate to language through two lines of work. || The first is **Lenneberg's** early attempts to synthesize two things. One is **developmental neurology** (the study of how the nervous system develops). The other is a **“language acquisition device”** (a proposed innate capacity for learning language). Lenneberg asked whether changes in the growing brain could open and close a window for language. He linked language milestones to stages of brain development. If there is such a window, language learning depends on the state of the brain and not only on the input. || The second is **studies of early deprivation**. The data come from **neglected children** and **Deaf children with limited input to sign**. These studies ask what happens when language input comes late. This is because these people first met a language they could access unusually late. Neglected children were cut off from ordinary language input. Some Deaf children have no access to sign language in early childhood. Comparing them with typical learners tests whether the age of first exposure changes the final outcome. || Since vision has a window when learning works best, the question is whether language has one too.
10|**Lenneberg (1967)** thought language grows on a biological schedule. In **Biological Foundations of Language**, he tried to synthesize contemporary linguistics with what was known about brain development. This is because studies were starting to find a fairly robust and **stage-like pattern** of language development. || Children reach the stages in the same order. Data from Austrian, British and American children between 8 and 36 months agree. >> **Words** come first, mostly between about 10 and 18 months. >> **Two-word phrases** come next, mostly between about 18 and 26 months. >> **Sentences of five words or more** come last, between about 28 and 36 months. || Lenneberg matched language milestones with motor milestones at each age. >> At **8 months**, infants stand holding on and pick up small objects. **Reduplicated** babbling (repeated syllables) becomes frequent, with intonation that can signal emphasis and emotion. >> At **10 months**, infants creep efficiently and pull to standing. Their vocalizations mix with sound play such as gurgling and bubble-blowing. They try to imitate sounds and start to tell words apart. >> At **12 months**, infants walk with one hand held. Words such as **mama** or **dada** come out, and they understand some words and simple commands. >> At **18 months**, children have more than three but fewer than fifty words. Much babbling continues, and comprehension grows quickly. || Since language milestones line up with motor milestones, both seem to run on one biological timetable. That is why **maturational milestones** (developmental achievements tied to physical growth) were seen as consistent with the **unfolding of a biological program**. Lenneberg proposed that brain maturation decides the pace for both.
11|Lenneberg thought the critical period for language **opens after the fast brain growth of the first two years**. This is because he observed abrupt changes in **neuron size** and **packing density** (how closely neurons are crowded together) before age two. He proposed that these changes might be **permissive** for the beginning of language acquisition. Permissive means they make language acquisition possible without directly causing it. So the brain first has a burst of physical growth. Once that growth settles, the window for learning language opens. || The data behind this were **sparse**. This is because they came almost entirely from **post-mortem** (after death) analyses of tissue. Researchers could not follow the same children's brains as they grew. They had to piece development together from children who had died at different ages. || The neuron size data are an example of how sparse the data were. Neuron size was the cell body volume of neurons in cortical layers III, IV and V. >> Neuron size grows fast in the first two years and then levels off. >> **No data were available between ages 2 and 18**. >> That gap covers much of the period of language acquisition. || Since the measurements had this gap, Lenneberg filled it with a reasonable guess about how the brain matures. Later research would need to test that guess directly.
12|Children can lose the left hemisphere and still develop language, but adults cannot. Lenneberg explained this with **cerebral dominance** (one hemisphere, usually the left, being specialized for language). This is because in young children language is not yet fixed in the left hemisphere. So the right hemisphere can take over. In adults, dominance is established, so the right hemisphere can no longer take over. Since nothing can take over in adults, removing the left hemisphere leaves permanent aphasia. That is why Lenneberg hypothesized that adult-like cerebral dominance is a key factor in **closing the critical period**. || The evidence came from studies of recovery from stroke. **Aphasia is surprisingly rare in children with brain lesions** (areas of damaged brain tissue). In extreme cases a **hemispherectomy** was done. This is surgery that removes or disconnects one whole cerebral hemisphere, typically to relieve seizures in people with epilepsy. || Lenneberg's summary of outcomes from **Basser (1962)** sorts the cases by age. >> For lesions before the teens with the **left** hemisphere removed, **49** had speech not affected or improved. Only 3 had permanent aphasia, and all 3 had aphasia before the operation. >> For lesions before the teens with the right hemisphere removed, 38 had speech not affected or improved. Another 5 had permanent aphasia, and all 5 had aphasia before the operation. >> For one lesion during puberty with left removal, improvement was slow. Some aphasia stayed until the end of life, 27 months after surgery. >> For **adults** with the **left** hemisphere removed, **none** had speech unaffected or improved, and **6 had permanent aphasia**. >> For adults with the right hemisphere removed, 25 had speech unaffected or improved, and none had permanent aphasia.
13|Children with **perinatal strokes** (strokes between the prenatal period and 28 days after birth) do much better than adults. But they do not fully catch up. Systematic studies found that their outcomes are **clearly much better than for adults with similar injuries**. The children still have some effects of the injury. || The first effect is **early language deficits in both right-hemisphere and left-hemisphere patients**. This is different from adults. In adults, language problems mostly come after left-hemisphere damage. In young children, damage to either side causes early language delays. **Some deficits are worse for children with right-hemisphere damage**, which is the opposite of the adult pattern. || The second effect is that language outcomes are generally in the **low-normal range**. That suggests a **persistent delay** rather than a catching up. The children recover far better than adults. They often do not reach the level of children without injury. || This is because a young brain can reorganize to support language, but the reorganized system may not work as well as the original would have. Since recovery is strong but incomplete, early plasticity is **real but limited**.
14|**Genie** is a case of what happens when first-language learning starts in adolescence. **Curtiss and colleagues (1974)** studied her to learn about the **effects of early deprivation**. Genie was born in **April 1957**. When researchers first met her, she was **13 years and 7 months old**. She was so thin that she looked six or seven. || Her early life was extreme isolation and abuse. >> **From the age of 20 months**, she was kept in a small room under more and more physical restraint. >> Most of the time she was harnessed to an infant's potty chair. Otherwise she was kept in a homemade sleeping bag in an infant's crib covered with wire mesh. >> Her mother was rapidly losing her sight and gave her minimal care. Genie was fed only infant food. >> Her father **physically punished her if she made any sounds**. >> Her father and older brother did not speak to her, but at times they barked at her like dogs. >> There was no television or radio, and she had very little contact with other people. || When she was hospitalized for malnutrition, she **could not stand up straight or chew food**. She was **not toilet trained**. She **did not speak, cry or produce any vocal sounds**. || Her case cannot say that missing language alone caused her problems. This is because her lack of language came together with severe physical, social and emotional deprivation. Since all of these happened together, the language deprivation **cannot be separated** from her other hardships.
15|Genie learned quite a bit in the **four years** that her language was observed. >> She learned to **comprehend hundreds of words**. >> She learned to comprehend some aspects of **morphology** (the system of meaningful word parts, such as the plural ending “-s”). She was **particularly good with plurals**, so she could use word endings to tell one from many. >> She learned to **comprehend some prepositions** (words for spatial relationships between objects). || The prepositions were tested with **buttons and plastic boxes** of different colors and sizes. Genie had to move the buttons and boxes the way the spoken instruction said. A sample item was **“Put the red box behind the blue box.”** The test covered **under, over, in, on, behind, in front of, in back of** and **next to**. || She got **more right than wrong for every preposition**, between October 1972 and August 1973. >> **In** had 37 correct and 4 incorrect. **On** had 36 correct and 9 incorrect. >> Over had 11 correct and 2 incorrect. Under had 17 correct and 7 incorrect. >> In front of had 20 correct and 6 incorrect. In back of had 7 correct and 3 incorrect. >> Next to had 7 correct and 1 incorrect. Behind had 12 correct and 5 incorrect. || So **some language can be learned late**. This is because Genie learned all of this after puberty, long after the window Lenneberg proposed. Her learning was uneven across different parts of language.
16|Genie did not learn to speak as well as she understood, and she did not learn much grammar. || Her **production** (what she said) **lagged severely behind her comprehension** (what she understood). This is because of two things. **Some lag is typical of development**, since all children understand more than they can say. Genie was also **punished for making sounds** as a child, which could limit how much she spoke. So her speech revealed less than she actually knew. || Her **grammatical abilities developed much more slowly than her vocabulary**. A test of **conjunctions** put **five familiar objects in a row** in front of her. She was asked to point to one or more of them. The items included **“Show me the fork and pencil”**, **“Show me the crayon or the knife”** and **“Show me either the spoon or the crayon”**. >> For **and**, she had to point to every named object. >> For **or**, she had to point to just one. This is harder because it depends on understanding a logical relationship. || She got and right but or wrong. The testing ran from October 1971 to December 1973. >> In **early responses**, she treated or sentences the same as and sentences, so she pointed to both objects. >> In **later responses**, she could tell and from or, but she still did not understand **either/or**. >> And sentences had **100 correct**, 3 incorrect and 0 unanalyzable responses. >> Or sentences had only **7 correct**, **51 incorrect** and 6 unanalyzable responses. || Since she knew hundreds of words but still failed or, her case fits the idea that **grammar depends more on early exposure than vocabulary does**.
17|Genie's case cannot prove a sensitive period for grammar, but it is widely interpreted as suggesting one. || The interpretation comes from the **disparity between her vocabulary development and her grammatical development**. She learned hundreds of words but made little progress with grammar. So the case is taken as suggesting that **grammar learning in a first language is specifically impaired after a sensitive period**. On this interpretation, words can be learned at any age, while grammar depends on early exposure. || The case cannot prove it. This is because there are **lots of questions** about other causes. >> One question is whether Genie was **learning disabled**. She may have had difficulties from birth that would have hurt her language even without deprivation. >> Another question is what the **residual impacts of neglect and abuse** were on her social development. Severe trauma could affect learning in many ways besides the missing language input. >> Disability, abuse and late exposure could each have affected her grammar, and one case cannot separate them. || There is also a time limit on the evidence. **After four years, funding to study her was pulled**. So nobody can know what she might have learned after a longer time. || Since the other causes cannot be ruled out, her case stays suggestive. Her pattern still became an influential piece of evidence for a sensitive period in first-language learning.
18|Deaf children who learn **American Sign Language (ASL)** late have much more difficulty if ASL is their first language. **Mayberry, Lock and Kazmi (2002)** compared two groups who both learned ASL late. >> The first group were children aged **9 to 15** who were learning a language for the **first time**. They had **difficulty** learning ASL. >> The comparison group were children who **went deaf after first learning a spoken language**. They did **much better** at learning ASL. >> **These differences persist into adulthood**. || This is because the second group already had a language early and the first group did not. Both groups learned ASL late, so lateness alone cannot explain the difference. || These children are a cleaner test than Genie. Many deaf children **do not get sign language input** but have an **otherwise typical upbringing**. They grow up in ordinary, caring homes. So the only unusual thing is the lack of accessible language early in life. || Two tasks measured the adults. >> One task was **recall of complex ASL sentences**. Deaf adults with **no early language** scored about **36%**. Deaf adults with an **early spoken language** scored about **78%**. >> The other task was judging whether **complex English sentences were grammatically correct**, where chance is 50%. Deaf adults with no early language scored about **52%**, which is close to chance. Deaf adults who had ASL in infancy scored about **80%**. Hearing adults with an early spoken language other than English also scored about 80%. || Since early ASL helped later English as much as an early spoken language did, what matters is **having any language early**. Early accessible language, signed or spoken, is the foundation for learning languages later.
19|Age clearly matters for learning a first language (**L1**), but the evidence cannot firmly say whether there is a **“critical period”** or a **“sensitive period”**. || Age matters because **recovery from stroke**, and certainly from **hemispherectomy**, looks **very different in childhood and adulthood**. Children can develop language after damage that would leave an adult with permanent aphasia. So age strongly affects how well the brain can reorganize for language. || **Deprivation of input does not appear to extend the critical period**. One might expect the window to stay open until language input arrives. It does not stay open. Genie and the deaf adults who learned sign late have lasting difficulty, especially with grammar. Since they still had difficulty, the window seems to follow a maturational schedule, whether or not language has been experienced. || It is **difficult to reach firm conclusions**. This is because all of these data come from **highly unusual cases with lots of other things going on**. >> Stroke, brain surgery, abuse and neglect each bring complications besides the timing of language input. >> There is a **lack of animal models**, because animals do not learn human language. >> **Gene knockout experiments** (turning off specific genes to test what they do) cannot be done with people.
