2|**Nativists** say children are born with knowledge of what language is like. This view grew out of **Chomsky's critique of Skinner**. **Skinner** was a behaviorist who explained behavior, including language, with general learning such as reward and punishment. Chomsky said that kind of learning cannot explain how children get grammar. || Chomsky said the important thing about language is **generativity** (being able to make and understand an unlimited number of new sentences). This is because most sentences we hear or say are new. So learning a language means **figuring out the abstract grammar** that makes the sentences of that language. || Behaviorist learning is not up to that job, for two reasons. >> Grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are **not "similar"** in any simple way. One tiny change can make a sentence ungrammatical, and two grammatical sentences can look totally different. >> Learning language does not look like **operant conditioning** (changing behavior with rewards and punishments that come after it). Children are not reliably rewarded for good sentences or punished for bad ones. So the reward and punishment links **do not hold**. || So nativists claim two things instead. Children have innate knowledge called **Universal Grammar** (built-in principles that limit which grammars a child will consider). Children also use **language-specific acquisition processes** (learning tools built just for language). || Chomsky backed this up by writing, "The fact that all normal children acquire essentially comparable grammars of great complexity with remarkable rapidity suggests that human beings are somehow specially designed to do this, with data-handling or 'hypothesis-formulating' ability of unknown character and complexity." Since every typical child gets a complex grammar fast, even with very different experiences, the ability looked built in.
3|At first, **categorical perception** (hearing speech sounds as categories) looked special to humans and to speech. This is because only humans had been tested, and only with speech sounds. So early theories called it **language-specific** (only for language) and **species-specific** (only in humans). || The classic evidence uses fourteen made-up consonant sounds. The upper formant transition changes in equal steps from one sound to the next, and the lower formant stays the same. >> When people **label** the sounds, they switch sharply from **/b/** to **/d/** around sounds 4 and 5. They switch from /d/ to **/g/** around sounds 9 and 10. >> When people **tell pairs apart**, they do best right at those boundaries. They do worse for pairs inside one category. This holds for pairs one, two or three steps apart. || So whether two sounds seem alike depends on the **language categories**, not on the physical difference. This is because two sounds in the same category sound almost the same. Two sounds on opposite sides of a boundary sound clearly different. Both pairs can differ by the same acoustic amount. Since the pattern seemed tied to speech, it was taken as a sign of a special speech system.
4|Researchers find out what infants hear by watching **head turns** and **sucking**. This is because infants cannot tell us, but these behaviors change when an infant notices something new. || In the **conditioned head turn procedure** (used in Janet Werker's infant studies lab), the infant learns to turn toward a sound when it changes. >> The infant sits on a parent's lap, and an experimenter keeps the infant looking forward with toys. >> When the sound changes and the infant turns, a lighted, moving toy rewards the turn. >> Once the infant has learned this, a head turn right after a change means the infant **heard the change**. || In **high-amplitude sucking**, the infant sucks on a pacifier hooked to a pressure recorder. Strong sucks make a sound play. >> When the same sound keeps playing, the infant gets bored and sucks less. This is **habituation** (getting used to a repeated sound). >> If a new sound makes sucking **go back up**, the infant **noticed the change**. || Neither method needs the infant to name or make a sound. The experimenter picks exactly which sound difference to play. Since only the sound difference changes, a head turn or renewed sucking tells us whether infants hear the same differences adults hear.
5|Four-month-olds noticed a change in **voice onset time** only when it crossed an adult category boundary. **Eimas and colleagues (1971)** found this using high-amplitude sucking. **Voice onset time**, or VOT, is the delay between releasing a stop consonant and the start of the vocal folds vibrating. With +100 milliseconds of VOT, voicing starts much later than with +10 milliseconds. || Infants heard one syllable over and over until their sucking dropped. Then the sound changed or stayed the same. >> In **20D**, the change was 20 milliseconds, from +20 to +40. The two sounds belong to **different** adult categories. >> In **20S**, the change was also 20 milliseconds, from −20 to 0 or from +60 to +80. The two sounds belong to the **same** category. >> In **0**, the **control** group, the sound never changed. || Only the 20D infants started sucking more again. In 20D, sucking jumped back up to around 50 per minute. In 20S, sucking barely recovered and kept dropping. In the control, sucking kept dropping to about 15 per minute. || This matters because 20D and 20S were the same size of change. So the only difference was **crossing the category boundary**. Since the control shows what repetition alone does, the jump in 20D must come from hearing a new category.
6|Eimas's result was a big deal because it made categorical perception look **innate**. This is because categorical perception was thought to be the **first step** in turning speech into a **symbolic format**. A symbolic format stores a sound as a category, such as /b/. Later language processes would use that format. So finding it in infants meant the starting point for language was already there. || Four-month-olds are really little. >> Their **gross motor milestones** (big body movements) are things like handling objects with two hands. They are also just holding their head and chest up while lying face down (prone). >> Their color vision and **visual acuity** (sharpness of vision) are still "in progress." >> They are only starting to make **vowel-like sounds** instead of creaky whines. || So this is very, very early for a skill that seems to be **just for language**. This is because infants were sorting speech sounds into categories before they could make those sounds. Their other skills were still immature. Since speech categories showed up so far ahead of everything else, the ability seemed built in, not learned.
7|English-learning infants can hear non-English sound differences at six months, but they mostly lose them by twelve months. This is **perceptual narrowing** (losing sensitivity to sound differences your language does not use). || **Werker and Tees (1984)** tested English-learning infants on two non-English contrasts. One is a **Hindi** contrast between two "ta"-like sounds. The other is a **Salish** contrast between "ki" and "qi" from Nthlakampx, a Salish language. They counted how many infants reached a **discrimination criterion** at each age. >> At 6 to 8 months, about 95% passed for Hindi and about 80% for Salish. >> At 8 to 10 months, about 67% passed for Hindi and about 52% for Salish. >> At 10 to 12 months, only about 20% passed for Hindi and about 10% for Salish. >> Hindi-learning and Salish-learning infants passed for their own contrast close to 100% of the time. || The drop happens because of **language experience**. This is because infants who hear the contrast in their language keep it. English-learning infants never hear it, so they lose it by about their first birthday. This is **perceptual reorganization** in the first year of life. || Narrowing is related to **categorical perception** because infants are forming their native categories. Sounds their language puts in one category get harder to tell apart. So a non-English difference inside one English category ends up heard as a **within-category difference**.
8|The compelling story says babies are born able to hear every speech contrast, and learning just throws out the ones they do not need. This is because it puts the infant findings together in three steps. >> Speech perception depends on **categorical perception** abilities that are a special part of the language system. >> These abilities show up very early in development. >> Children start out as **"citizens of the world"** (able to hear all contrasts). || Being citizens of the world means infants have innate, **language-general** knowledge about speech (knowledge that works for the sounds of every language). So a newborn is ready for any human language. Then children quickly **tune** their hearing to the one language around them. || In this story, learning only **selects**. This is because the contrasts are already there from birth. Learning narrows down the **inventory** (the full set of contrasts the child can hear). It keeps the contrasts that matter in the native language. It drops the ones the native language does not use. Since learning only removes things, the native language just decides which innate categories survive.
9|The citizens-of-the-world story has four problems. Three are about **categorical perception**, and one is about infants. || Categorical perception is not special to language or to humans. >> It is **not limited to language**, because it also happens for non-speech sounds. >> It is **not limited to humans**, because animals without language also hear speech sounds as categories. || Categorical perception is also **task-dependent** (how categorical it looks depends on the task). This is because naming a sound and judging whether two sounds differ can give different results. So it is not always as categorical as the story assumes. || Infant speech perception is **not actually "universal."** This is because infants do not start out hearing every contrast equally well. Some contrasts are hard for young infants to hear, even contrasts that are in their own language. So not every contrast is ready and waiting at birth. || Since some contrasts get better with experience, a full account has to explain gains as well as losses. That means learning must **build** categories, not only remove them.
10|People hear **non-speech** sounds as categories too, so categorical perception does not need a speech system. **Cutting and Rosner (1974)** showed this by changing **rise time** (how fast a sound gets to full loudness after it starts). A **short** rise time sounds like a **plucked** string. A **long** rise time sounds like a **bowed** string. || They used **sawtooth waves**, **speech syllables** and **sine waves**. Each one was made with a 10-millisecond rise and a 70-millisecond rise. A 10-millisecond rise hits full size almost right away. A 70-millisecond rise grows slowly. || For the sawtooth waves, labels switched sharply in the middle. Short rise times got called pluck almost 100% of the time. Long rise times got called bow almost 100% of the time. The switch happened between about 30 and 50 milliseconds. || Telling pairs apart was hardest inside a category and easiest at the switch. This is because pairs 10 milliseconds apart were told apart only about 55 to 60% of the time for most pairs. Near the switch, it **went up to about 75%**. || Pluck and bow are kinds of sound onset, not speech categories. Since the same label boundary and discrimination peak show up without any phonemes, categorical perception can come from **general hearing**.
11|Infants also hear **non-speech** sounds as categories. **Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** found this with high-amplitude sucking. This means infants' early categorical perception is a **general hearing** ability, not a speech ability. || Infants heard one sound over and over until their sucking dropped. Then they got one of three conditions. >> With **no change**, sucking started near 20 per minute, dropped to about 12 and stayed there. >> With a change **within a category**, sucking also stayed near 12. >> With a change **between categories**, sucking jumped back up to about 18. Then it dropped again as the new sound got familiar. || So infants reacted only when the non-speech change **crossed a category boundary**. This is the same pattern Eimas and colleagues found for speech. || This ties to the adult results. Adults label and tell apart plucked and bowed sounds as categories. Infants tell apart non-speech changes as categories. Since neither group needs to know a language to do this, early categorical perception comes from general hearing.
12|Chinchillas hear **speech sounds** as categories, the same way humans do. **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** showed this, following earlier work by **Kuhl and Miller (1975)**. This means categorical perception of speech is not just a human thing. || The chinchillas were trained with **operant conditioning** (a behavior gets more likely when a reward follows it). Each chinchilla learned that either a 0-millisecond lag sound or an 80-millisecond lag sound was an **SD** for food. **SD** means **discriminative stimulus** (a signal that a response will get rewarded). The lag is the **voice onset time**. Humans hear a 0-millisecond lag as /ga/ and an 80-millisecond lag as /ka/. || Then the chinchillas and some humans heard the in-between sounds along the /ga/-/ka/ continuum. >> At short VOTs, they called almost every sound /ga/. >> At long VOTs, they almost never called it /ga/. >> The switch was steep, mostly between about 30 and 70 milliseconds. >> The chinchillas' curves had the **same steep shape** as the humans' curves. || Making the **burst** (the short noise when a stop is released) and **aspiration** (the breathy noise after a voiceless release) louder changed the results. The boundary moved to a **shorter VOT**, the switch got steeper, or both. This happened for one chinchilla and one human listener. Since both species reacted the same way, categorical perception of speech is shared with other animals.
13|Four-month-olds do not easily hear the difference between **/na/** and **/ŋa/**. This means some contrasts are **less salient** (harder to notice) than others for infants. **Narayan and colleagues (2010)** found this. || This is because both contrasts tested use **nasal consonants** in the **onset** (the start of a syllable), but the places differ. They differ in **place of articulation** (where in the mouth the sound is made). The sound /m/ is made with the lips. The sound /n/ is made with the tongue behind the teeth. The sound /ŋ/, the last sound in "sing," is made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate. || English-hearing infants aged 4 to 5 months heard **same** trials and **change** trials. Looking longer on change trials means they noticed the change. >> For **[ma]-[na]**, they looked about 3.3 seconds on same trials and about 5.4 seconds on change trials. So they noticed it. >> For **[na]-[ŋa]**, they looked about 4.5 seconds on same trials and about 4.2 seconds on change trials. So they did not notice it. || So /ma/-/na/ is **relatively salient**, but /na/-/ŋa/ is not readily heard. This is true **no matter what language** the infant hears. Since the difference comes from the sounds themselves, not every contrast starts out equally easy.
14|Hearing the /na/-/ŋa/ contrast in a **Filipino** home makes infants better at hearing it. **Narayan and colleagues (2010)** found this between 6 and 10 months. This means not all speech contrasts are available from birth. || This is because Filipino uses [na] and [ŋa] as different syllables. So Filipino-learning infants hear this contrast a lot. >> At 6 to 8 months, they looked about 3.8 seconds on both same and change trials. So they did **not yet notice** the switch. >> At 10 to 12 months, they looked about 3.3 seconds on same trials and about 4.8 seconds on change trials. So they **noticed** it. >> English-hearing infants at 4 to 5 months did not notice this contrast, though they did notice [ma]-[na]. || So it **cannot be that all speech contrasts are innate**. This is because the contrast starts out weak, even for infants whose language uses it. Hearing it over the first year makes it easier to detect. || So **exposure** to contrasts is **necessary** for learning them in some cases. Since learning can make a contrast stronger, development is not only losing unused contrasts. It also means building up hard ones.
15|The alternative to nativism is the **emergentist** approach. It says general **associative learning** picks up **statistical regularities** in the environment. Associative learning is learning that two things go together because they keep happening together. Statistical regularities are patterns in how often things happen and what happens with what. Language structure then grows out of these patterns in the input. || The simplest case is classical conditioning, with three neurons. One is for a **bell**, one detects **food** and one makes the animal **salivate**. >> The food neuron is already strongly connected to salivation, so food makes the animal salivate. >> When the bell keeps happening along with food, the **synapse** (a connection between neurons) from the bell neuron to the salivation neuron gets **stronger**. >> After enough pairings, the bell alone makes the animal salivate. || The same idea can go beyond conditioning to **pattern recognition** (spotting a repeating structure, such as a speech sound, across many different examples). This is because the same strengthening could connect sound features that tend to happen together. In conditioning, one event comes to predict another. In pattern recognition, many features come to predict a category. || Since connections grow from what keeps happening together, infants could learn speech categories by tracking which sounds go together and how often. They would not need a full set of built-in categories.
16|We might learn each vowel by keeping track of how its sounds are spread out, and the most typical vowel sits at the center. This is what **Kuhl and others** mean by learning the **statistics of distributions** in the environment. A **distribution** tells how often each value turns up across many examples. || The example is the **American vowels** in a **vowel space** made from the **first and second formants**. >> Each time someone says a vowel, that one production has a first formant value and a second formant value. >> Many productions of the same vowel bunch together in one area. >> The areas for different vowels **overlap** a lot. So one pair of formant values can belong to more than one vowel. That is why sorting speech sounds is hard. || So listeners have to learn where each vowel is centered and how far it spreads. This is because over many exposures, they can track where each vowel's productions bunch up. Their categories then match the shape of what they hear. || A **"prototypical" /a/** is then just the **mean** (the average) or maybe the **mode** (the most common value) of all the /a/ sounds we hear. Since the prototype comes from experience, it can be different across languages and listeners.
17|Yes, infants really do learn categories from how often sounds occur. **Maye and colleagues (2002)** had infants **listen passively** (just listen, with no task or reward) to an eight-step **[da]-[ta] continuum** that changes in **voice onset time**. || Two groups heard the same sounds, but how often each sound played was different. >> The **bimodal** group heard sounds 2 and 7 the most, 16 times each, and the middle sounds rarely. Two peaks suggest **two categories**. >> The **unimodal** group heard sounds 4 and 5 in the middle the most, and the ends rarely. One peak suggests **one category**. || At test, every infant heard **sounds 3 and 6**. In **alternating** trials, 3 and 6 switched back and forth. In **non-alternating** trials, one sound repeated. If infants hear 3 and 6 as different, the two kinds of trials should hold their attention differently. || Only the **bimodal** infants treated the two kinds of trials differently. >> Bimodal six-month-olds looked 5.66 seconds on alternating trials and 6.41 seconds on non-alternating trials. Bimodal eight-month-olds looked 5.45 and 6.15 seconds. >> Unimodal six-month-olds looked 4.85 and 4.53 seconds. Unimodal eight-month-olds looked 4.98 and 5.20 seconds, about the same. || This is because the unimodal infants seem to have learned to treat 3 and 6 as the **same category**. Since a few minutes of listening changed how they grouped sounds, infants do use the statistics of what they hear.
18|Infants learn speech sounds better from a **live, responsive person** than from a recording. Two experiments show **social interaction** matters for learning. || In the first, nine-month-old American infants heard **Mandarin Chinese** in twelve 25-minute sessions. >> The **live** group had a Mandarin speaker playing and reading with them in person. >> Other groups got the same kind of Mandarin from audio or TV. >> A control group heard English. || Only the live group learned the Mandarin contrast. We know this because when tested with the **head-turn technique**, live infants got about 65% correct. English controls got about 56%. TV and audio infants scored about the same as controls, near chance. || In the second, eight-month-olds got responses from their mothers when they made sounds. >> In the **contingent** condition, mothers answered right after the infant vocalized (**contingent** means the response depends on the infant's behavior). >> In the **yoked-control** condition, mothers answered on another infant's schedule, so the answers were **noncontingent**. >> Familiarization lasted 30 minutes, then baseline, social response and **extinction** (answers stopped) lasted 10 minutes each. || Contingent answers made infants produce **more syllables**. At baseline, both groups produced few. With social response, the contingent group went up to about 0.32, but the noncontingent group stayed near 0.05. In extinction, the contingent group stayed at about 0.2. || So social responses that depend on the infant's sounds act like a **reinforcing stimulus** (something that makes a behavior more likely). One idea is that humans find social interaction **innately reinforcing**. Another is that we are innately good at using **social cues**.
19|Statistical learning is limited by **constraints**, and constraints can mean three different things. **Statistical learning with constraints** is learning from patterns in the input that is guided and limited. || The first is about which sound properties can be **contrastive** (able to tell words apart, such as voicing in /b/ versus /p/). **Speaker** voice and **speech rate** change the sound a lot, but they do not change which word is said. So they are **not contrastive**. This matters because a learner tracking every pattern would make categories for different voices and speeds. || The second comes from **earlier learning**. This is because what a learner already knows changes what is easy to learn next. Once native categories are set up, new differences that cut across them are harder to notice. This kind of constraint is central to **sensitive periods** (times when earlier experience limits later learning). || The third comes from **social and interactive** settings. This is because sounds are learned better in live, responsive interaction than from a recording. Caregivers' contingent responses also shape the sounds infants make. || Learning depends on the input, on what the learner already knows and on the social setting. Since all three guide it, learning can be fast and stick to the differences that matter.
