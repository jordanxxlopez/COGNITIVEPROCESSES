2|Speech has no physical boundaries between words, but we are still really good at finding the words. Finding where one word stops and the next one starts in continuous speech is called **word segmentation**. || Speech is **messy** in this way. This is because the sound energy of each word flows straight into the next word. In “I hope it’s in my desk,” no silent gap separates the words from one another. || The pauses in speech do not mark the words either. This is because the pauses come from **closures** (moments when the vocal tract is fully blocked, as for /p/, /t/, /d/ and /k/). Very little sound gets out during a closure, so a short silence appears. Closures are **as likely to happen within words as between words**. In “hope” and “it’s,” the silences fall inside words or at their edges. Since the silences do not line up with word boundaries, a listener who used them would split some words and merge others. || Adults still hear separate words in this “sea of sounds” with no effort. They can lean on the words they already know. The **harder question** is **how we learn to do this**. This is because infants do not know the words yet. They have to find the words in the stream before they can learn what the words mean.
3|There are **several cues to word boundaries**, but no single cue works for every word. || The first cue is that some words **appear in isolation**, as in “Hello,” “No,” “Please,” “Go” and “Stop.” A word said alone has clear edges. Not all words are said alone, so this cue only covers part of the vocabulary. || The second cue is **phonotactics** (the patterns for which sound sequences a language allows). This helps because **some sequences of phonemes are very unlikely to occur within words**. In “wordboundary,” the sequence /db/ almost never occurs inside an English word, so it suggests a break between word and boundary. In “speechcode,” the sequence /tʃk/ suggests a break between speech and code. “Movement” needs care, because its /vm/ sequence might suggest a break even though movement is one word. || The third cue is **stress** (one syllable being louder, longer or higher in pitch). This helps because **in English, most words are stressed on the first syllable**. **Jusczyk and colleagues (1999)** let **7.5-month-old infants** get familiar with one word, and then played them passages. One passage kept using “kingdom,” as in “Your kingdom is in a faraway place.” The other passage kept using “device,” as in “Your device can do a lot.” Infants who had heard “kingdom” **preferred the kingdom passage**. Infants who had heard “device” **did not show the same kind of preference**. This is because kingdom is stressed on its first syllable and device is stressed on its second syllable. An infant who starts a new word at every stressed syllable finds kingdom easily but cuts device in the wrong place. || So stress **may help signal the beginnings of words** in connected speech. Since languages put stress in different places, this cue will not work in every language or for every word in English.
4|Two syllables inside one word go together much more often than two syllables from two different words. A learner can use this to find word boundaries, and the cue is called **transitional probabilities**. Using it is a kind of **statistical learning** (picking up patterns in how often things occur together). || Take the phrase “pretty baby.” In English words, the syllable **/ti/** is **very strongly predicted by the syllable /prɪ/**. This is because pretty is a common word, so after “pre” the next syllable is very often “ty.” The pair “ty” and “ba” crosses from one word into the next. This pair is **less predictable**. This is because many different words can come after “ty,” so “ba” only follows it when pretty happens to be followed by baby. || The comparison can be written with frequencies (how often something occurs). The frequency of “pretty” divided by the frequency of “pre” is the chance that “ty” comes after “pre.” The frequency of “tyba” divided by the frequency of “ty” is the chance that “ba” comes after “ty.” The first number is **much bigger** than the second number. || Pairs inside a word keep coming up together, and pairs across a word boundary do not. Since this is true, a learner who counts which syllable follows which can find where the words are.
5|Syllable B is more likely to come after syllable A **within a word than between words**. This likelihood is the **transitional probability** from A to B. This is because inside a word the same syllables always come in the same order, so the probability is high. At a word boundary almost any word can come next, so the probability is lower. Since the probability goes down at a boundary, a drop in transitional probability is a good guess for where a word ends. || This cue is **probabilistic**, which means it works most of the time but not every time. “What’s that in the road a/head?” can be heard as “a head” or as “ahead.” The sounds are the same both ways, so the listener uses the meaning of the whole sentence to decide. “I scream you scream we all scream for ice cream” plays on “I scream” and “ice cream” having almost the same sounds. The same run of sounds can be split into words in two ways. || Transitional probabilities make one way of splitting the stream more likely than another. They work together with stress, phonotactics and words heard alone. When the cues agree, finding the words is easy. When the cues disagree or are missing, as with “a head” and “ahead,” listeners use context.
6|**Saffran and colleagues (1996)** took away every cue to word boundaries except **transitional probabilities**. This is because they wanted to find out if transitional probabilities alone are enough to identify words. So the stimuli were **tightly controlled**. || The speech was **monotone** (no changes in pitch, duration or amplitude, where amplitude is loudness). This took away stress, so stress could not mark where words begin. || The syllables were **simple CV syllables** (one consonant followed by one vowel). This took away **phonotactic cues**, because no unusual consonant clusters ever came up. || The syllables were **presented continuously** (with no pauses). This took away silence, so silence could not mark a boundary. || The researchers made an **artificial language** out of a set of **three-syllable “words,”** such as tokibu, gikoba and gopila. The words were joined in random order into one long stream. The only thing that told where a word began and ended was the transitional probability between syllables. Since every other cue was gone, infants who found the words must have used transitional probabilities alone.
7|The stream that the infant hears has no breaks in it at all. It sounds like “tokibugikobagopilatipolutokibu” and “gopilatipolutokibugikobagopila” and “gikobatokibugopilatipolugikoba.” There are no spaces and no pauses. || There are **four words** hidden in the stream, which are tokibu, gikoba, gopila and tipolu. Each word comes up many times in random order. || The words can still be found. This is because the syllables follow **regular patterns**. Inside **tokibu**, “to” is always followed by “ki,” and “ki” is always followed by “bu.” After “bu,” the stream can go on with “gi,” “go” or “ti.” Which one comes next depends on which word comes next. So some syllable pairs are **fully predictable** and other pairs are not. || Since the order of the syllables is the only signal, a learner has to keep track of which syllables follow which. A learner who does this can find the words from the statistics of the stream.
8|The four words become easy to see when each word is marked in its own color. Tokibu is red, gikoba is green, gopila is pink and tipolu is purple. The infant does not get any marking like this. The infant only hears one unbroken run of syllables. || The marked words make one pattern clear. Each word keeps the **same inner order** every time, but the words next to it keep changing. “To-ki-bu” always comes in that order. After tokibu, the next word can be gikoba, gopila or tipolu. || **Transitional probabilities** (how likely one syllable is to follow another) are **high inside each word** and **lower across word edges**. This is because the syllables inside a word always follow each other. At the end of a word, the next syllable could start any of the other words. || The word edges are exactly the places where the transitional probability drops. The signal has no spaces in it. Since predictability drops at each word edge, a learner who notices the drops can find where one word ends and the next begins.
9|To test for an effect of **transitional probabilities**, the infants had to compare items that they had heard **equally often**. This is because after a long stream of syllables, infants are more familiar with sequences they heard than with ones they did not hear. That is trivial to find. An infant could prefer the heard sequences only because they are familiar, without using transitional probabilities at all. || Two extra details fix this problem. The first is that **half of the words were repeated more frequently** than the other half. The second is that the test compared words with **part-words** (three-syllable sequences that cross a word boundary, made from the end of one word and the start of the next). The part-words were **matched for frequency of occurrence** with real words. || Take the stream “tokibu gikoba gopila gikoba tokibu gopila tokibu gikoba gikoba tokibu.” The word **GOPILA** appears twice and the part-word **BU-GIKO** also appears twice. The probability of GI given BU is **2 out of 4**. This is because BU comes up four times, at the end of each tokibu, and GI comes after it only two of those times. The transitional probabilities inside GOPILA are both **2 out of 2**. This is because PI comes after GO both times, and LA comes after PI both times. || Since gopila and bu-giko are heard equally often, familiarity cannot explain a difference between them. A difference has to come from how predictable the syllables are.
10|Infants **looked longer in the direction of the part-words** than the words. This is called a **novelty effect**. Words got about 6.8 seconds of looking and part-words got about 7.6 seconds. The difference was statistically significant. || Looking longer at the part-words is a **novelty preference** (infants look longer at whatever seems new). This is because the words had become familiar during listening, so they were less interesting. The part-words seemed less familiar to the infants. This can be confusing, because the infants had heard the part-words just as often as the words. || The only thing that was different about the part-words was their transitional probabilities. The syllables in a part-word do not reliably go together. Since frequency was the same and only the transitional probabilities were different, the infants must have noticed the transitional probabilities. || So the infants **had learned the statistical regularities of the speech stream**. They were **not merely picking up on simple frequency of occurrence**. After a short exposure, they pulled units out of a continuous stream using only how predictable the syllables were.
11|Finding a word in speech does not tell a child what the word means. Children can now “find” the words, but they still have to figure out each word’s **referent** (whatever the word refers to). This problem is called **identifying word referents**. || This is because **segmentation** only gives the child a chunk of sound that keeps coming back. The chunk of sound has no information about meaning in it. A child may hear “cat” while looking at a cat. Recognizing the sound pattern “cat” is one achievement. Knowing that “cat” names the furry animal is a different achievement. || Figuring out the meaning is hard because any scene has many objects, parts, properties and actions in it. The child needs more evidence to tell which of these the speaker has in mind. || Since the sound does not give the meaning, the child has to link the word’s form to what the speaker means. The child gets that evidence from the situation in which the word is used.
12|A child who hears a new word once cannot tell what it means, because one experience fits many possible meanings. This is called the **inductive problem of early word learning**. **Induction** is reasoning from particular examples to a general rule, and here the rule is the meaning of a word. || Say a child hears a new word while watching a cat. The word might mean **CAT**, **ANIMAL** or **PET**, which are categories at different levels. It might be **FELIX**, the name of this one cat. It might name a property, such as **FURRY** or **ALIVE**. It might name an action, such as **WALKING**. It might name a part, such as **WHISKERS** or **LEG**. || All of these possible meanings together are the **hypothesis space**. The child cannot pick one from a single scene. This is because every possible meaning fits what the child sees, and nothing in the scene singles out the one the speaker means. || This is a form of **Quine’s “gavagai” problem**. A linguist studying an unknown language hears a speaker say “gavagai” as a rabbit runs by. “Gavagai” might mean rabbit, but it might also mean undetached rabbit parts, a stage of a rabbit or something else. || Children have this problem with every new word, and they still learn words fast. Since they learn fast, something must cut down the possibilities. Joint attention, cues to what the speaker intends and default strategies do this.
13|Children learn what words mean partly through **joint attention** (sharing an attentional focus with another person). In joint attention, two people look at the same object. Each one knows that the other is paying attention to it too. || Joint attention helps with word meaning. This is because when the adult says a word, the object they are both looking at is the most likely referent. So the word gets tied to what the speaker is paying attention to. This makes the **hypothesis space** (the set of possible meanings) smaller. || Infants can join an adult’s focus once they can follow gaze. **Gaze following** (looking where someone else is looking) **emerges around 9 months of age**. When an adult looks over at an object, the infant follows the adult’s eyes to it. || Gaze following **signals an understanding of reference**. This is because an infant who follows gaze understands that the adult’s attention is aimed at something specific. Since the infant knows what the adult is attending to, the infant can link the adult’s word to that object.
14|**Eighteen-month-olds learned a new word** when an adult was interacting with them, but **not** when the word came from a voice behind a screen. **Baldwin and colleagues** found this, and it means that **joint attention optimizes word learning**. || There were two groups of infants. Both groups heard a novel word while playing with an object. In the **joint attention group**, an adult interacting with the baby labeled the object the baby was playing with. In the **no joint attention group**, a voice from behind a screen labeled the object the baby was playing with. || The two groups were the same in most ways. In both groups, the word came while the infant was focused on the same object. The only difference was whether a visible person was engaged with the infant and the object. || The 18-month-olds learned the novel word in the joint attention group but not in the no joint attention group. This is because hearing a word while seeing an object is not enough. Infants need evidence that a speaker means the word to refer to that object. Since the hidden voice gave the same word at the same moment, the difference in learning came from the visible, engaged speaker.
15|Children narrow the **hypothesis space** (the set of possible meanings) for new words by using **intentional cues from the speaker**. An **intentional cue** is a signal of what the speaker means to refer to. One intentional cue is **eye gaze direction**. This works because when a speaker looks toward one toy, the eyes mark which object the speaker is paying attention to. || **Baldwin** taught new words to infants of 16 to 17 months and infants of 18 to 19 months. There were two conditions. In **follow-in labeling**, the adult looked at the toy that the infant was focused on while saying the new word. In **discrepant labeling**, the adult looked at a toy in a bucket while saying the new word. The toy in the bucket was a different toy from the one the infant was focused on. || The two conditions are needed for this reason. In follow-in labeling, the infant and the adult look at the same toy, so both cues point to one toy. In discrepant labeling, the infant’s focus and the speaker’s focus point to different toys. Infants who just attach a new word to whatever they are looking at would link it to their own toy. Infants who follow the speaker’s gaze would link it to the toy in the bucket. Since the two answers are different, comparing the conditions tells whether infants treat the speaker’s gaze as evidence of what the word means.
16|**Gaze direction** helps a child figure out which object a speaker is naming when there are several objects. This is because a speaker who looks at one object while saying a new word ties the word to that object. The child can follow the speaker’s eyes to the **intended referent** (the thing the speaker means). || The speaker’s focus can be different from the child’s own focus. Following the speaker’s focus means using information about someone else’s attention. Since the child uses the speaker’s focus, the child can attach a new word to an object it was not looking at when the word was said. || Gaze makes the possible meanings fewer even though the sound of the word gives no clue. This is because the child puts the new word together with evidence of what the speaker intends. To do this, the child has to understand that a speaker’s eyes point to what the speaker means. Since the eyes pick out one object, the child links the word to that object and not to everything in view.
17|Infants can use an adult’s **facial expression** to figure out which object a new word names. **Akhtar and colleagues** found that infants **used adults’ facial expressions to identify the intended referent of a novel word**. || This works because the adult’s emotional reaction to each object tells whether it is the one the adult meant. A satisfied expression means the speaker found the object the speaker wanted. A disappointed expression means the object is not the one the speaker meant. Since the expression picks out one object, infants linked the new word to that object. || These are intentional cues from the speaker, and there are two kinds here. **Affective cues** are emotional expressions. An expression can tell whether an object is the one the speaker was searching for. **Behavioral cues** are the speaker’s actions. An action, such as reaching for something, can tell what the speaker wants. || Several objects may be there when a word is spoken. The speaker’s reaction can single out one of them. Since the child has to read the reaction, learning a word involves understanding the goal behind the interaction.
18|A speaker’s **emotional reaction** can pick out which object a new word is for, when there are several objects. The reaction is an **intentional cue** (a signal of what the speaker means to refer to). || This is because satisfaction or excitement ties one object to what the speaker was trying to find or do. The learner can use that tie to understand a new label. Infants who do this are following the speaker’s goals. They understand that the speaker is searching for something. They understand that the word names the thing the speaker wants. Since the word names the wanted thing, the word gets attached to the object that meets the goal. Objects that disappoint the speaker are ruled out. || **Intention is conveyed through more than gaze direction**. Facial expressions and actions also tell what a speaker means. A word may be said near several objects, and they are not all equally likely to be the referent. || The useful information is in how the adult responds to each object. Connecting the label to that response makes the word’s **hypothesis space** (the set of meanings it might have) smaller.
19|Children expect a new word to name **a whole object**. This is the **whole object assumption**. It is one of the **word learning strategies**, which are **default strategies that children bring to the word learning situation**. A **default** is a starting assumption that holds until evidence says otherwise. || These strategies are there to **narrow the hypothesis space** (the set of possible meanings) for new words. They give children a place to start when other cues are weak. || With the whole object assumption, the child does not expect a new word to name a part of the object, its color, its texture or something it is doing. A child who hears a new word while looking at a cat will pick “cat” over “whiskers,” “furry” or “walking.” || This makes word learning faster. This is because the assumption gets rid of many unlikely meanings at once. It also fits the way adults usually name objects for children. || The assumption is only a starting preference. Later evidence can override it. Since it can be overridden, children do learn words for parts and properties once the evidence points that way.
20|When a child hears a new word, the child gives it to an object that does not have a name yet. This is **mutual exclusivity**, from **Markman (1989)**. || This is because of a default that **objects can have only one name**. An object that already has a name is not expected to get a second one. So the implication is that **new words map to nameless objects**. Mutual exclusivity **emerges during the second half of the second year**, which is roughly between 18 and 24 months. || Say a child knows the word “dog” but has no word for a parrot, and both animals are in view. The child hears a new word. The dog already has a name, so the child decides the new word means the parrot. The child does not need a gaze cue or an emotional cue to make this choice. || Mutual exclusivity works together with the whole object assumption. The whole object assumption says a new word names an object. Mutual exclusivity says it names an object that has no name yet. || Mutual exclusivity is a default, so later learning can override it. This happens when an object turns out to have two names.
21|With **mutual exclusivity**, a child who hears an unfamiliar word gives it to an object that has no known name yet. This is because an object the child can already name is not likely to be the target of a new word. The child makes the choice by comparing the objects in view. || Which object gets the word depends on the child’s **prior vocabulary** (the words the child already knows). This is because prior vocabulary decides which objects count as already named. Two children in the same scene can make different guesses if they know different words. So the strategy builds on what the child learned earlier. || This makes vocabulary growth feed itself. A child who knows many names can rule out many objects. The objects without names that are left over become the most likely referents for new words. Since known names rule out objects, every word the child knows helps narrow the meaning of the next new word.
22|It is hard to tell how children start putting words together. This is because in production children produce only one, two or three words at a time. Very different processes can make the same short utterance. || **Menn** distinguishes among four kinds of early utterances. The first is **“just a bunch of relevant words.”** Here the child strings together words that fit the situation, and no grammar links them. The second is **formulas**. A formula is a phrase the child memorized as a whole unit, without knowing it has separate parts. The third is **word combinations**. Here the child joins separate words productively to make new combinations. The fourth is **stable constructions**. These are consistent patterns the child uses to build utterances, and they are **not necessarily adult-like**. || A child might say “Up!” or “Pick you up!” or “Mommy up!” “Pick you up!” sounds more advanced, but it may be a formula copied whole from what adults say. “Mommy up!” is shorter, but it may be a real combination of two separate words. So the length of an utterance cannot tell which process made it. || To tell these apart, a **full description of the context** in which the utterance is produced is **necessary, but often not sufficient**. Context is necessary because it tells what the child intends and how the utterance is used across situations. It is not always sufficient because the source of an utterance can stay uncertain even with complete context.
23|Children can understand **basic transitive word order** even when they can only say one word at a time. So **comprehension** is ahead of **production**. The **one-word stage** is when a child speaks only in single words. A **transitive** sentence has a verb with a subject and an object. In English, the actor comes first and the one acted on comes after the verb. || **Gertner and Fisher** explain this with the **structure-mapping account** of early **syntactic bootstrapping** (using sentence structure to help learn meaning). || The child hears the input sentence “The boy is tickling the girl!” and makes a **partial sentence representation** of it. This has the verb tickle, with boy as the first noun and girl as the second noun. || The child also sees the scene, which is a boy tickling a girl. The child makes **conceptual representations** of the events that could be going on. One is giggle with the girl as agent. One is tickle with the boy as agent and the girl as patient. Two more are play with the boy as agent and play with the girl as agent. An **agent** does an action and a **patient** is affected by it. || Then the child uses a **one-to-one mapping between nouns and participant roles**. This is because the sentence has two nouns, so it fits an event with two participants. The two nouns get lined up with the two participants in the tickling event. Since the nouns are matched to the participants, the child can use noun order to tell who did what. This matching also helps the child figure out what the verb means.
24|Two-year-olds can understand a transitive sentence even when the verb is made up. **Gertner and colleagues (2006)** found this with a **generalization test**. || The test works this way. If children have really learned the **transitive structure**, they will **generalize it to new verbs**. A child who only memorized particular sentences will be lost with an unfamiliar verb. A child who knows the structure can use word order to understand any verb. || The sentence was “The duck is gorping the bunny.” The child has to pick which of two novel actions the sentence could depict. “Gorp” is a made-up verb, so its meaning gives no help. The child has to know that the first noun is the doer and the second noun is the one acted on. || Children first heard “Hey look! The duck’s gonna gorp the bunny!” before the scenes. Then two scenes came up side by side. In one scene, a person dressed as a duck pushed a person dressed as a bunny in a wagon. In the other scene, the bunny acted on the duck. Children heard “The duck is gorping the bunny! See?” with the scenes. After a short break they heard “The duck gorped the bunny! Find gorping!” and the scenes came up again. || The two-year-olds **looked significantly longer at the duck acting on the rabbit** than at the reverse. This is because they used the order of the nouns to figure out who was doing the action. || Children at this age typically have a **mean length of utterance (MLU)** of **around 2**. MLU is the average number of morphemes (meaningful words or word parts) in each utterance. Their three-morpheme utterances might be like “Duck gorp bunny.” || Their own speech is this short. So their understanding of the transitive structure is ahead of what they can say.
