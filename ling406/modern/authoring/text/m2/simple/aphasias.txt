2|In the 1870s, German neurologists explained each kind of language loss as a broken link between two **centers**. A center is a brain region that stores one kind of language representation. This is because **Lichtheim** and **Wernicke** began making more systematic observations of **aphasia** (a loss or impairment of language caused by brain damage). They ascribed specific losses of function to **“disconnections”** (damage to the pathway between two centers). When the pathway is broken, information can no longer travel between the two centers. This was reasoning about aphasia before Chomsky. || **Lichtheim’s “House” model** draws the centers and pathways in the shape of a house. **A** stores **sound images** (the stored sound patterns of words). **M** stores **movement images** (the stored motor plans for saying words). **B** stores the **concepts** words stand for. Pathway **a** brings heard speech into A, and pathway **m** carries plans from M out to the speech muscles. Seven numbered sites mark different forms of aphasia, one for each damaged center or pathway. || The place of the break decides what the person loses. >> A break between **A and B** might take away **understanding spoken words**. The sound of the word arrives, but it cannot reach its concept. >> A break between **A and M** might take away **verbatim repetition**. A heard word cannot go straight to the plan for saying it. || This **“box and arrow” model** is still foundational in **aphasiology** (the study of aphasia) and in modeling normal language. The **interactive activation model** of word recognition is built the same way, with levels of representation joined by pathways. This history comes from Eling and Whitaker (2009).
3|In the 1970s, **Norman Geschwind** brought back the box-and-arrow approach, and by then linguistics could split language into separate parts. The result was the **“Geschwind-Wernicke model”**, which ties language functions to particular brain regions and the pathways between them. **Broca’s area** is toward the front of the left hemisphere, in the frontal lobe. **Wernicke’s area** is farther back, in the temporal lobe. The **arcuate fasciculus** (a bundle of nerve fibers) connects Wernicke’s area to Broca’s area. || Two big grooves are used as landmarks. >> The **central sulcus**, also called the **fissure of Rolando**, separates the frontal lobe from the parietal lobe behind it. >> The **lateral sulcus**, also called the **fissure of Sylvius**, separates the temporal lobe from the frontal and parietal lobes above it. || In the century between Wernicke and Geschwind, **linguistics changed dramatically**. Linguists built a model where **syntax was independent** of other parts of language. Syntax also had **its own computational principles**. That made it possible to think about **dissociations** and **double dissociations** of language functions in more sophisticated ways. || A **dissociation** means one ability is impaired while another is spared. A **double dissociation** means one patient loses the first ability and keeps the second, and another patient shows the reverse. A double dissociation is strong evidence that the two abilities use **separate systems**. Since syntax, words and meaning are now separate parts, a patient can be impaired in one part while the others still work.
4|Broca’s aphasia mostly breaks the grammar of speech, while Wernicke’s aphasia keeps speech fluent but fills it with wrong words. This is because of the classic profiles that **Fromkin (1997)** describes. || **Broca’s aphasia** speech has **word-finding pauses**, **loss of grammatical morphemes**, and often **disturbed word order**. Grammatical morphemes are small words or word parts that mark grammar, like “the,” “is” or past-tense “-ed.” Everyday conversation seems to be understood fairly well. But **controlled testing reveals considerable impairments** in comprehension. || **Agrammatism** (difficulty producing or understanding grammatical structure) is almost another name for Broca’s aphasia. But the match is not perfect. >> **Some patients with lesions in Broca’s area are not agrammatic**. >> **Some agrammatic patients would not be classified neurologically as Broca’s aphasics**. >> So Broca’s aphasia names a pattern of behavior, and that behavior does not always come with damage to Broca’s area. || **Wernicke’s aphasia** speech is **fluent**, with **good intonation and pronunciation**. It has **many word substitutions**, some related in meaning to the intended word and some not. It has **neologisms** (invented words) and **phonological errors** (mistakes in the sounds of words). These patients also have **comprehension difficulties**. Fluency only means the speech flows smoothly. It does not mean the speech carries a clear message. || So Broca’s patients keep the meaningful words but lose the small grammar pieces that connect them. Wernicke’s patients keep the grammar frame but fill it with wrong or invented words.
5|The two aphasias seem to split neatly in two, with fluent nonsense on one side and choppy speech made of content words on the other. This is an attractive **dichotomy** (a two-way split). || **Wernicke’s aphasia** is called **“word salad”** or **“Jabberwocky,”** often with **neologisms**, while **syntax is roughly intact**. “Jabberwocky” is Lewis Carroll’s poem of grammatical sentences full of nonsense words. >> Asked about his health, one patient said “I felt worse because I can no longer keep in mind from the mind of the minds to keep me from mind and up to the ear which can be to find among ourselves.” Kriendler and colleagues (1971) reported it. >> Asked about his poor vision, another patient said “My wires don’t hire right.” >> Asked if he was a doctor, an aphasic physician said “Me? Yes, sir, I’m a male demaploze on my own. I still know my tubaboys what for I have that’s gone hell and some of them go.” **Demaploze** and **tubaboys** are neologisms. >> One patient called a fork “a need for a schedule” and a spoon “How many schemes on your throat.” || **Broca’s aphasia** is called **“telegraphic” speech**. It has **difficulty with word order and grammatical particles**, but the **important content words** are mostly spared. >> Asked what brought him to the hospital, one patient said “Yes … ah … Monday … ah … Dad and P.H. (the patient’s name) and Dad … hospital. Two … ah doctors … and ah … thirty minutes … and yes … ah … hospital. And, er Wednesday … nine o’clock and eh Thursday, ten o’clock … doctors. Two doctors … and ah … teeth. Yeah … fine.” Badecker and Caramazza (1985) reported it. >> Words like Dad, hospital and doctors carry the story. The small grammar words that would join them are missing.
6|Broca’s own patients would probably not count as Broca’s aphasics today. They likely had **apraxia of speech** (a disorder in planning and coordinating the movements of speaking). This is because the modern dichotomy is about language, not movement. Wernicke’s aphasia is **problems with activation, or possibly inhibition, in the lexicon** (the mental store of words). Broca’s aphasia is **problems with grammar**. **Activation** makes a candidate word available. **Inhibition** holds back competing words, so weak inhibition could let wrong words slip out. || That is **fairly distant from how Broca described his original cases**. Broca described **Leborgne**, known as **“Tan.”** Tan **could only produce one jargon syllable**, “tan.” Otherwise he **seemed to be in possession of his senses**. He even **seemed to understand what was being said**. || Broca’s description raises three questions. >> His other senses being intact matters because then the loss is only about speech, which makes it a **dissociation**. >> A “capacity for articulate speech” sounds more believable as a job for a brain region than “criminality” or “philoprogenitiveness” (a phrenology term for love of one’s children). >> Testing Tan today would mean measuring his deficits at the phonetic, phonological, lexical and syntactic **levels of description used in modern linguistics**. || This creates a real confusion. Broca’s actual patients were arguably **unable to produce speech at all**. That seems to come from **motor control problems**, not grammar. Apraxia of speech can cut speech down severely without any loss of grammatical knowledge.
7|The aphasia dichotomy is attractive because linguistics also draws a sharp line between **the grammar** and **the lexicon**. This is because late 20th-century formal linguistics separated these two parts. The **grammar** is the set of rules for combining words into sentences. The **lexicon** is the store of single words, which have to be memorized. So Wernicke’s aphasia would be a lexicon disorder, and Broca’s aphasia would be a grammar disorder. || The line starts with **Chomsky’s formulation of the computational problem of language learning**. We do not recognize a new sentence because it matches something familiar in any simple way. We recognize it because **it is generated by the grammar** each person has internalized. So a sentence never heard before can still be understood. || **Chomsky (1955)** also gave **compelling demonstrations of the independence of syntax and semantics**. Neither “colorless green ideas sleep furiously” nor “furiously sleep ideas green colorless” has ever occurred in an English speaker’s past experience. >> “colorless green ideas sleep furiously” makes no sense. Its word order still follows English grammar, so it is **grammatical**. >> “furiously sleep ideas green colorless” uses the same words in an order grammar does not allow, so it is **not grammatical**. >> Both are equally unfamiliar and equally meaningless. Since only the grammar differs, **grammar must be its own system**.
8|The dichotomy also fits the speech production model, where word choice happens at the **functional level** and grammar happens at the **positional level**. This is because the model has separate levels. >> At the **message level**, the speaker chooses a story angle. The speaker also calls up concepts of **referents** (the people, objects or events being talked about) and event structure. >> At the **functional level**, the speaker calls up word meanings and **semantic roles** (the part someone plays in an event, like doer or receiver). >> At the **positional level**, the speaker gets word forms into grammatically appropriate order. >> The **phonological encoding level** supplies the sounds. The **speech gesture level** handles **articulation** (the physical movements of speaking). || At the functional level, **lemmas** (a word’s lexicon entry, linking meaning to grammatical properties) have an **arbitrary** relationship to their referents. Nothing about a dog forces it to be called “dog.” So this is the lexical side of language. || At the positional level, syntactic trees and **affixes** (word parts added to a stem, like “-ed” or “-ly”) come from **abstract syntactic processes**. So this is the grammar side, built by rules. || Someone might object that **HOUSEBOAT** and **DOGHOUSE** are not arbitrary, because they are built from parts. The answer is **speech error evidence** that **derivational morphology** (building new words from parts) happens at the positional level. In the slip “easy enoughly,” the speaker meant “easily enough.” The “-ly” moved by itself and landed on the wrong word. Since the suffix moved as a separate piece, a grammar operation must have added it.
9|Broca’s patients might understand most of what is said even if their grammar is damaged. This is because everyday conversation gives many clues besides grammar. Word meanings, familiar events and context all point to the same message. A listener can follow along using these clues without fully working out the grammar. || Popular theories of **sentence processing** say **syntactic structure is computed independently of meaning** while we listen or read. A **parser** (the process that builds sentence structure) works out the structure first. Meaning gets worked out afterward. || To test these **“parsing” mechanisms**, the test sentences have to be **built carefully**. >> One such sentence is “The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite.” >> In the **preferred** structure, “with the dynamite” attaches to the **verb phrase**, so the dynamite is what he used. >> In the **unpreferred** structure, it attaches to the **noun phrase** “the safe,” so it says which safe. >> The words stay the same and only the attachment changes. So this kind of sentence tests parsing without changing word meanings. || Since meaning can give away the answer, a real grammar test needs sentences where the roles cannot be guessed from the likeliest event. **Reversible sentences**, where either person could plausibly do the action, force the listener to use grammar.
10|To show that a patient’s problem is really with **syntax**, researchers test **comprehension**. This is because a comprehension test lets the patient answer without speaking. So motor problems cannot be the reason for mistakes. If the patient still misunderstands grammatical relationships, the problem **goes beyond articulation**. || Broca did not have any of this theory in the 1860s. For him, it was interesting enough to **dissociate “articulate speech” from comprehension**. Leborgne could say only one syllable, yet he seemed to understand what people said to him. That showed speaking and understanding can come apart, which was the key finding back then. || The modern question is different. It asks whether some patients have a difficulty that is **more generally with syntax**. A **syntactic impairment** (a problem with organizing grammatical relationships) should hurt both speaking and understanding sentences. Since speaking is already hurt by motor problems, understanding is the part that can show it.
11|It was interesting to find comprehension problems in Broca’s patients because their broken speech alone could just be a motor problem. This is because **Broca’s aphasia almost always comes with some motor dysfunction**. So agrammatic speech might **merely reflect an attempt to communicate despite motor limitations**. When speaking is very hard, a patient might drop the small grammar words and keep the most informative words. || **Hughlings Jackson** said **we are observing what the individual can do despite the impairment**. So agrammatic speech after an injury does not literally mean **the grammar itself is injured**. Caramazza and Zurif (1976) cite Lenneberg (1973) for a version of this. On that view, speaking is such a strain that the patient **speaks asyntactically to economize effort**. || **Chomsky’s distinction between competence and performance** helps here. **Competence** is a person’s underlying knowledge of language. **Performance** is actually using that knowledge, which memory limits, distraction or motor problems can disrupt. When motor problems clearly mess up performance, **comprehension may give a clearer window into competence**. || So **Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** tested comprehension in Broca’s patients. If the problem is only motor, understanding grammar should be normal. If the grammar is damaged, understanding should suffer too. Both explanations predict telegraphic speech, so only a comprehension test can tell them apart.
12|Special psycholinguistic tests are needed because ordinary sentences can be understood correctly without using grammar at all. This is because conversation has lots of **redundant cues** (separate clues that all point to the same meaning). “Man bites dog” is the newspaper example of a surprising event. || “The dog bit the man” gives many ways to get the right meaning. >> The listener can just put DOG, BITE and MAN into the **most likely event**, with or without syntax. Dogs bite people far more often than people bite dogs. >> The sentence uses **canonical word order** (agent, verb, undergoer, which is subject, verb, object). The **agent** is the doer, and the **undergoer** is the one the action happens to. >> In English the first noun is usually the agent. >> The **discourse context** (the surrounding conversation) also clears up a lot. || Broca’s patients might understand by leaning on these **compensatory heuristics** (mental shortcuts that make up for a weakness somewhere else). A patient could guess the most plausible event. A patient could also guess that the first noun is the doer. Either shortcut gets “The dog bit the man” right with no grammar. || So a better test sentence is “The dog that the man is biting is angry.” Here the **man** is doing the biting. The dog comes first and dogs are likelier biters, so both shortcuts give the wrong answer. Only working out the grammar gets it right.
13|**Caramazza and Zurif** wrote exactly this kind of test sentence, plus **control sentences**, in four groups of eight. This is because each group changes how much meaning helps. A **center-embedded** sentence has a relative clause in the middle of the main clause. In every one of them, the first noun is acted on and the doer is inside the clause. || **Control sentences**, group A, are simple and in normal order, like “The girl is kicking a green ball,” “The cat is chasing a blue bird” and “The boy is riding a new bicycle.” || **Center-embedded sentences with semantic constraints**, group B, let meaning give away the roles. Examples are “The apple that the boy is eating is red,” “The wagon that the horse is pulling is green” and “The balloon that the clown is holding is round.” Apples cannot eat boys. || **Reversible center-embedded sentences**, group C, have two participants who could each do the action. Examples are “The cow that the monkey is scaring is yellow,” “The horse that the bear is kicking is brown” and “The girl that the boy is tickling is happy.” Only grammar gives the roles. || **Improbable center-embedded sentences**, group D, describe unlikely events. Examples are “The bird that the worm is eating is blue,” “The man that the horse is riding is fat” and “The policeman that the robber is chasing is skinny.” Common sense points to the wrong answer. || A listener who relies on meaning should pass groups A and B but fail C and D. A listener who parses normally should pass all four.
14|Broca’s patients got sentences wrong only when grammar was the only way to tell who did what to whom. This is because **Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** used a **sentence-picture matching task** (hear a sentence, then choose which picture fits it). Accuracy shows whether the listener understood the roles, with no speaking needed. Healthy listeners and patients with Broca’s, conduction and Wernicke’s aphasia all heard the four sentence types. || **Healthy controls** were **more or less equally good** on every type, near the top score. || **Wernicke’s patients** were **poor overall**, even on simple control sentences. That is probably because of their **comprehension deficits**, which affect words as well as grammar. || **Broca’s patients** did **well** on control and semantically constrained sentences, where meaning gives away the answer. They did **poorly** on reversible and improbable sentences. In those, **syntax conflicted with common sense** or with the likely roles of the nouns. || This **specific comprehension deficit** was taken as evidence that **Broca’s aphasia affects syntax specifically**. Since the problem shows up in understanding as well as speaking, it seems to be in the grammar itself. The paper’s title is “Dissociation of algorithmic and heuristic processes.” Broca’s patients lose the **algorithmic** route (working out the grammar) but keep the **heuristic** shortcuts (guessing from meaning).
15|A detailed theory of the parts of language lets researchers separate grammar problems from word problems. This is because it opens new ways to study **structure-function relationships** (links between a brain structure and the job it does). Before, aphasics could only be sorted into good or poor comprehension, or more or less fluent. Now researchers can **isolate problems with particular aspects of language**. || Two patients can look the same on fluency and still differ. >> One patient might know word meanings well but assign the wrong roles in complex sentences. >> Another patient might produce a fluent grammar frame filled with words that miss the message. >> A single fluency score would hide that difference. || The key split is **the grammar versus the lexicon**. **Grammar** is the rules that relate words to each other in a sentence. The **lexicon** is the stored words and the ability to pull up the right one. || Each kind of damage predicts a different result. A **grammar deficit** predicts trouble with reversible sentences but not with word meanings. A **lexical deficit** predicts trouble with word meanings and naming but not with grammar. So controlled tasks can tie one damaged operation to one brain region.
16|**Dick and colleagues** found **problems with this dichotomy** by describing one event with four kinds of sentences. This is because only two of the four sentence types put the doer first. Participants heard each sentence and had to **indicate which animal is doing the action**. A picture of a dog and a picture of a cat appeared on a computer screen. Participants pressed a button on a response box to choose. The event was always the dog biting the cat. || Two types put the **doer first**. >> An **active** sentence is “The dog is biting the cat.” >> **Subject cleft** is “It’s the dog that is biting the cat.” A **cleft** splits a simple sentence with “It’s … that” to focus on one part. Here the focused noun is the doer. >> For both, picking the first animal mentioned gives the **right** answer. || Two types put the **receiver first**. >> **Object cleft** is “It’s the cat that the dog is biting.” Here the focused noun is the one being bitten. >> **Passive** is “The cat is bitten by the dog.” The receiver becomes the grammatical subject. >> For both, picking the first animal mentioned gives the **wrong** answer. || Since the first-animal shortcut fails on object clefts and passives, only a listener who **parses the grammar** gets all four types right.
17|Broca’s patients did worst on the hard sentences, object clefts and passives, but they were not great on any sentence type. We know this because aphasia patients and **healthy aging controls** did Dick’s task, and the pattern looked **much like what Caramazza and Zurif observed**. Patients were grouped by aphasia type with the Western Aphasia Battery. || Each group had its own profile. >> **Elderly controls** scored close to 100% on all four types. >> **Anomic** patients (whose main problem is finding words) scored about 89% on actives and subject clefts. They scored about 69% on object clefts and about 73% on passives. >> **Conduction** patients scored about 75% on actives and subject clefts but about 42% on object clefts. >> **Wernicke’s** patients scored low on every type, around 43% to 50%. || **Broca’s** patients scored about 61% on actives and 65% on subject clefts. They dropped to about 42% on object clefts and about 54% on passives. Object clefts and passives are **noncanonical** (they break the usual doer-first order). So they are the **syntactically complex** sentences. || Broca’s patients had two problems at once. Even on simple actives, they were far below the elderly controls. On complex sentences, they lost even more. So their pattern is a **general drop** plus an **extra cost** for complex structures.
18|Healthy students under stress make the same kind of errors that Broca’s patients make, so the patients’ problem may be **performance** and not lost grammar. This is because researchers gave the task to **healthy undergraduates** under **conditions that stress the language system**. >> **Compressed** speech is speech played faster than normal. >> A **low-pass filter** cuts the higher frequencies, so speech sounds muffled. >> Noise was also added. >> **Auditory digits** and **visual digits** were number lists to remember while listening, which uses up memory. || With **one stressor**, accuracy stayed high on actives and subject clefts. Object clefts suffered most. With noise, accuracy fell from about 97% on actives to about 86% on object clefts. Passives dropped to about 91%. || With **two stressors**, the drops got bigger. With muffled and sped-up speech together, accuracy was about 91% on actives and 90% on subject clefts. It fell to about 66% on object clefts and 68% on passives. || So **object clefts and passives are the most vulnerable** to every kind of stress. These students have no brain damage and no loss of grammar knowledge. Stress alone gave them an aphasia-like pattern. Complex sentences may need more processing resources, so any loss of resources hurts them first. Since that is true, Broca’s patients may still have the grammar but lack the resources to use it.
19|Broca’s aphasics’ comprehension deficits do not seem to be specific to language. This is because **Saygin and colleagues (2004)** asked patients to **match an action to an object**, with and without words. >> In the **non-linguistic** version, a drawing had a boy pantomiming an action, like licking. Participants picked the matching object from pictures like an ice cream cone, a cake, broccoli and a rooster. >> In the **linguistic** version, the action was given in words instead. || **Broca’s aphasics had difficulty with this task whether the stimuli were linguistic or not**. >> Without words, controls scored **98.5%** and aphasic patients **93.4%**. >> With words, controls scored **99.4%** and aphasic patients **87.5%**. || Accuracy also depended on the **distractor** (the wrong choice offered). >> With **unrelated** distractors, controls scored 99.8% and patients 93.3%. >> With **semantic** distractors (related in meaning to the right object), controls scored 99.2% and patients dropped to **86.3%**. >> With **affordance-based** distractors, controls scored 97.7% and patients 91.6%. An **affordance** is an action an object allows, like another object that can also be licked. || The pantomime version takes language out but keeps meaning in. The patient still has to understand the action and link it to the right object, just as a verb links to its object. A purely grammar deficit predicts no trouble here. Since the patients still struggled, the problem seems to include **action meanings** in general.
20|Doing badly on the pantomime version went with damage to **Broca’s area**, so Broca’s area does more than syntax. This is because **performance on the non-verbal version was significantly correlated with damage to Broca’s area**. Lesion maps marked the brain areas linked to trouble with **non-linguistic pantomime interpretation**. The maps show three horizontal slices of the brain. Red and yellow mark the strongest links between damage and poor performance. The strongest links are in the left frontal region in front of the **central sulcus**, which includes Broca’s area. || The task used no language at all. >> Participants only had to understand a pantomimed action and pick an object. >> They did not have to say a sentence. >> They did not have to work out the grammar of a spoken instruction. || Since no language was involved, patients with damage there struggled to connect an action with its meaning. So Broca’s area supports **understanding actions** as well as grammar. Any account of Broca’s area has to explain both the grammar findings and this trouble with actions.
21|Wernicke’s aphasics’ comprehension deficits do not seem to be specific to language either. This is because **Saygin and colleagues** asked patients to **match sounds to pictures**, with and without words. >> In the **nonverbal** version, a speaker played an environmental sound, like a cow mooing. Participants chose between pictures like a cow and a sheep. >> In the **verbal** version, the speaker played a spoken phrase, like “cow mooing.” Participants chose between pictures like a cow and a violin. || **Wernicke’s patients were particularly bad at this task**. >> **Controls** scored 99.2% with words and 98.1% without. >> Patients with **right hemisphere damage** scored 99.1% and 98.2%. >> **Anomic** patients scored 98.6% and 96.7%. >> **Broca’s** patients scored 96.3% and 95.2%. >> **Wernicke’s** patients scored **90.0%** with words and **91.3%** without. || **No significant difference was observed between the verbal and nonverbal versions**. Wernicke’s patients had as much trouble with a cow’s moo as with the words “cow mooing.” So their problem goes beyond understanding spoken words. Since both kinds of sound gave trouble, the problem is **linking any meaningful sound to what it means**.
22|Trouble with sound-picture matching, with or without words, comes from damage in the same place, around **Wernicke’s area**. This is because lesion overlays marked where the brain was damaged in patients who failed each version. **Eight** patients were impaired on the **nonverbal** version. **Ten** patients were impaired on the **verbal** version. Red and yellow mark the areas damaged in the most patients. || For both groups, the most overlap is in the **posterior temporal area of the left hemisphere**, around Wernicke’s area. So the verbal and nonverbal problems come from damage to the **same general region**. || The two versions use different sounds but ask for the same thing. >> A spoken phrase gives the meaning through words. >> An animal call gives the meaning without language. >> Both require connecting a meaningful sound to what it stands for. || Since damage around Wernicke’s area breaks that connection in both cases, Wernicke’s area seems to handle **meaningful sounds in general**. Language is one important case of that.
23|Nobody fully agrees yet on how language-specific the deficits in aphasia are, so it is **still a controversy**. This is because the findings point two ways. Some patients have special trouble with complex grammar, which suggests some parts of language are handled in **specialized** ways. The same patients also struggle with action meanings or environmental sounds when no language is involved. That suggests language **shares brain systems** with other kinds of meaning. A full explanation has to cover both. || What is clear is **how the terms of the debate have evolved**. The field now has three kinds of tools. >> **Relatively sophisticated tests that tap specific aspects of language**, like reversible and improbable sentences, cleft sentences and nonverbal matching tasks. >> **Quantitative analyses of behavior**, measuring accuracy across sentence types, patient groups and stress conditions. >> **Quantitative analyses of structure-function relationships**, linking patterns of brain damage to performance across many patients. || So the field has come **a long way from the 19th century**. Wernicke and Lichtheim drew boxes and arrows from a few single cases. Modern research can test which specific operation is damaged. It can also map that damage onto brain tissue across large groups of patients.

25|Across many patients, damage toward the front goes with **fluency** problems and damage toward the back goes with **comprehension** problems. This is because **voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping** uses MRI data to formalize **structure-function relationships**. It comes from Bates, Wilson, Saygin, Dick, Sereno, Knight and Dronkers. || The method works one small spot at a time. >> A **voxel** is a tiny three-dimensional unit of an MRI brain image, like a 3D pixel. >> At each voxel, patients with damage there are compared with patients without damage there. >> If the damaged patients score much worse, that voxel is linked to the symptom. >> Colors go from blue for a weaker link to red for a stronger link. || The **“symptoms”** here are **defined very broadly**. **Fluency deficits** link most strongly to the front part of the left hemisphere. **Comprehension deficits** link most strongly to the posterior temporal region, farther back. || So data from many patients fits a **rough double dissociation**. **Posterior regions** go with **perception**, or comprehension. **Anterior regions** go with **production**, or fluency. Since each patient adds a damage pattern and a score, many single cases become one statistical map.
