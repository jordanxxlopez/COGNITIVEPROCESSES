2|Lenneberg (1967) said the **sensitive period** closes because language settles into one side of the brain. This is because his theory ties the sensitive period to the development of **cerebral dominance** (one hemisphere, usually the left, becoming specialized for language). He also called this developmental changes in **language laterality** (how much language depends on one side of the brain instead of both). Once language is fixed in the left hemisphere, the window for learning language closes. || Two kinds of evidence led him to the theory. >> The first was that brain development roughly lined up with language milestones. Cortical neurons grow quickly for about two years and then level off. There were no measurements between ages 2 and 18. Lenneberg linked the end of fast brain growth to the start of language learning. >> The second was a review of **resilience after brain injury or surgery**. Children who lost the left hemisphere often went on to develop language. Adults did not get language back. || The theory is about the brain's **maturational state** (its stage of physical development). A young brain is less specialized, so it can move language to other tissue. A mature brain is specialized, so it has fewer ways to do that. Since a mature brain has fewer options, language learning gets harder. || **Second-language (L2) learning** is used as a **model system** for research on the sensitive period. A model system is a common, easily studied situation used to answer questions about a process that is hard to study directly.
3|L2 learning gives a bigger comparison than first-language cases do. This is because most of what is known about **first-language (L1) sensitive periods** comes from **idiosyncratic** (particular to one individual) cases or relatively rare phenomena. The cases include children recovering from surgery that removed a hemisphere. They also include Genie, who was cut off from language until age 13. Each case comes with other unusual circumstances, such as injury, illness or abuse. So it is hard to figure out which factor caused the outcome. || In contrast, many people learn **second languages** at different times in their lives, often with somewhat **limited success**. L2 learners are common, so researchers can study large groups. They start at every age from early childhood to late adulthood. Their final success can be measured. So researchers can compare early starters with late starters. || Those limits on L2 success are used to figure out the **timing** of the sensitive or critical period. This is because later starters might reliably end up at lower levels. If they do, the age where the drop appears could mark when the sensitive period closes. || There is one complication, which is the **first language**. Every L2 learner already knows a language. That knowledge shapes how the new language is learned. Since L2 learners are common and start at every age, they still give a large comparison on timing.
4|People who arrived in the United States younger ended up with better English grammar. **Johnson and Newport (1989)** found this when they tested for **critical period effects** in second-language learning. Their paper is about the influence of **maturational state** on learning English as a second language. || They ran the study because of **Lenneberg (1967)**. He hypothesized that language could be acquired only within a **critical period** (from early infancy until puberty). In its basic form, that idea is only about first-language acquisition. If it also applies to a second language, young children should learn a second language better than adults. They should also reach higher final proficiency. || To test this, they compared the English proficiency of **46 native Korean or Chinese speakers**. These people had arrived in the United States between the ages of 3 and 39. They had lived there between 3 and 26 years when they were tested. They did a **grammaticality judgment task** on a wide variety of structures of English grammar. || Earlier arrivals had a **clear and strong advantage** over later arrivals. Up to puberty, test performance was **linearly related** to age of arrival, so each extra year of age at arrival meant a lower score. After puberty, performance was **low but highly variable** and was unrelated to age of arrival. || The age effect was not an accident. This is because it did not come from differences in amount of experience with English, motivation, self-consciousness or American identification. It also appeared on every grammatical structure tested, even though later learners mastered some structures far better than others. Since the age effect held up, the authors concluded that a critical period for language acquisition **extends its effects to second-language acquisition**.
5|Each example set tests one rule of English grammar. This is because every item on the **grammaticality judgment test (GJT)** had a grammatical version and an ungrammatical version. **Johnson and Newport (1989)** devised the test. Participants listened to recorded sentences and said whether each sentence was grammatical or not. So each answer was a **grammaticality judgment** (a decision about whether a sentence follows the rules of the language). The test was **untimed**, so they could take as long as they needed. || Two sets test the form of a noun or a verb. >> **(1a) “The farmer bought two pigs at the market”** and **(1b) “The farmer bought two pig at the market”** test **plural marking** on nouns. >> **(2a) “The little boy is speaking to a policeman”** and **(2b) “The little boy is speak to a policeman”** test the **progressive -ing** form of the verb. || Three sets test articles and other **determiners**. >> **(6a) “Tom is reading a book in the bathtub”** and **(6b) “Tom is reading book in the bathtub”** test whether a needed article is there. >> **(7a) “The boys are going to the zoo this Saturday”** and **(7b) “A boys are going to the zoo this Saturday”** test whether the determiner matches the noun in number. >> **(8a) “Larry went home after the party”** and **(8b) “Larry went the home after the party”** test a place where English does not allow an article. || Two sets test questions. >> **(23a) “When will Sam fix his car?”** and **(23b) “When Sam will fix his car?”** test **wh-question** word order, where the helping verb must come before the subject. >> **(24a) “What do they sell at the corner store?”** and **(24b) “What they sell at the corner store?”** test the need for the helping verb “do” in a question. || Since each pair tests one rule, the whole test covers many different rules of English grammar.
6|Two patterns are in these data. For people who arrived as children, a later arrival goes with a lower score. For people who arrived as adults, age of arrival says very little about the score. || The data set each person's score on the grammar test, out of 276 items, against their **age of arrival (AOA)**. The people are split at puberty, because puberty is the end of the critical period in Lenneberg's proposal. Splitting there tests whether the age effect changes after puberty. || The strength of each pattern is given by a **correlation** (written r, a number from −1 to +1 for how closely two things are related). A value near −1 means one thing goes down steadily as the other goes up. A value near 0 means there is little relationship. || For **early arrivals** (aged 3 to 15), the correlation was **−0.87**. This is because children who arrived between about 3 and 7 scored near the maximum, around 270. Scores then went down steadily, to around 250 near age 10 and around 210 to 230 near age 15. Since −0.87 is close to −1, age of arrival predicts much of the score in this group. || For **late arrivals** (aged 17 to 39), the correlation was only **−0.16**. This is because scores were lower overall and widely scattered, from about 160 to 250. Some adults scored around 250, while others of a similar age scored below 180. Since −0.16 is close to 0, age of arrival predicts little about which adults do well. || So there is a **strong age effect in childhood** and **lower, highly variable performance in adulthood**.
7|The **Johnson and Newport (1989)** data have three main features. || The first is a **strong relationship between AOA and accuracy**. AOA is age of arrival. People who arrived earlier got higher scores. || The second is that **early learners**, up to about age 8, are often **near-perfect** (native-like, with scores close to native English speakers). Children who arrived after that, between about 8 and 15, scored progressively lower. Since the scores are already going down by then, the decline starts well before puberty. || The third is a **nearly random distribution of accuracy** for late arrivals. This does not mean the late arrivals were guessing. This is because late arrivals scored well above chance. “Nearly random” means that among late arrivals, age of arrival and score are only weakly related. Scores varied greatly from person to person. Two adults who arrived at the same age could end up with very different scores. Since age of arrival barely predicts an adult's score, the adult scores look nearly random. || Near-perfect scores for the youngest arrivals are the clearest sign of an early advantage. Adult arrivals scored lower still, with wide differences between individuals.
8|Johnson and Newport (1989) say their data are consistent with a **sensitive period** for L2 learning. They actually call it a **“critical period.”** They give two reasons. || The first reason is that **ultimate attainment** (the final level a learner reaches) depends on AOA. This is because scores go down steadily as the age of arrival in childhood goes up. A steady drop like that is consistent with a **loss of language-learning ability over development**. || The timing is not entirely consistent with Lenneberg's hypothesis. This is because Lenneberg put the end of the critical period at puberty, but the decline in these data starts around age 7 or 8. Lenneberg was working with a large gap between early childhood and adolescence. He had no brain measurements between ages 2 and 18. Since he had that gap, his timing was only a rough estimate. || The second reason is that attainment in late acquisition is **all over the place**. Adults who arrived at the same age reached very different levels. This is because, after the sensitive period closes, attainment depends on **idiosyncratic learning abilities** (individual differences in things such as memory, motivation or analytical skill). It no longer depends on the usual language acquisition processes. Children all learn through the same natural acquisition process, so they reach similar levels at similar ages. Adults no longer have that process, so they have to rely on their own abilities. Since those abilities differ a lot between people, adult scores are spread out.
9|Johnson and Newport (1989) see the **maturational state hypothesis** as more consistent with their data. This is because all of their participants had learned a first language normally. They had already used their capacity for acquiring languages. Later starters still learned the L2 less well. Since using the capacity did not protect it, the data fit the maturational state hypothesis, which is also named in the title of their paper. || The two versions of the **“critical period” hypothesis** start the same way. Both say that early in life, humans have a superior capacity for acquiring languages. >> The **exercise hypothesis** says that if the capacity is not exercised (used) during this time, it will disappear or decline with maturation. If the capacity is exercised, further language-learning abilities will remain intact throughout life. So an adult who learned an L1 normally should learn an L2 as well as a child does. >> The **maturational state hypothesis** says the capacity disappears or declines with maturation, whether or not it has been used. So even a person with a normal L1 should learn an L2 less well as an adult. || Yes, this changes the interpretation of the **L1 critical period data**. This is because in cases like Genie, the capacity was never exercised. Both versions predict poor learning when the capacity was never exercised. Since both predict the same thing, the L1 cases cannot tell the two versions apart. Only second-language data can tell them apart, because only L2 learners exercised the capacity before learning another language.
10|The **grammaticality judgment task** may not measure the same knowledge that people use in fluent language. This is because the task is very different from fluent language use. In fluent use, people speak and understand quickly without stopping to think about rules. In the task, people consider each sentence and decide whether it is correct. So people can stop and use **“metalinguistic”** or **“explicit” knowledge** (conscious knowledge about language, such as knowing that English plurals add “-s”). || **Ellis (2005)** compares **implicit knowledge** and **explicit knowledge** on several characteristics. >> For **awareness**, implicit knowledge is an intuitive awareness of linguistic norms, and explicit knowledge is a conscious awareness. >> For **type of knowledge**, implicit knowledge is **procedural** (knowing how to do something) and explicit knowledge is **declarative** (knowing facts that can be stated). >> For **systematicity**, implicit knowledge is variable but systematic, and explicit knowledge is often anomalous and inconsistent. >> For **accessibility**, implicit knowledge comes through **automatic processing**, and explicit knowledge comes through **controlled processing**, which takes effort. >> For **use of L2 knowledge**, implicit knowledge is used during fluent performance, and explicit knowledge is used when a speaker is planning and having difficulty. >> For **self-report**, implicit knowledge is **nonverbalizable** (people cannot explain it) and explicit knowledge is **verbalizable**. >> For **learnability**, implicit knowledge is potentially only learnable within the **critical period**, and explicit knowledge is learnable at **any age**. || This is a problem of test design for an untimed GJT. One learner can get the right answer by consciously applying a taught rule. Another learner can get the same answer from automatic knowledge. The score is the same either way. Since the score cannot tell the two apart, the untimed test may miss the implicit knowledge that the critical period is supposed to affect.
11|The **untimed GJT** may be a better measure of explicitly learned **“metalinguistic” knowledge** than of grammar acquisition. This is because of what the task asks people to do. **Ellis (2005)** compared the design features of five tests. >> **Imitation** and **oral narrative** rely on feel, have time pressure and focus on meaning. They require no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **timed GJT** relies on feel and has time pressure, but it focuses on form. It requires no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **untimed GJT** relies on rules, has no time pressure and focuses on form. It does allow metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **metalanguage test** asks people to state rules. It relies on rules, has no time pressure, focuses on form and requires metalinguistic knowledge. || Untimed GJT performance goes with **years of formal education in L2**. Ellis related each test to the age people started learning and to their years of formal instruction. Only two relationships were significant. >> The age people started was related to the **timed GJT**, with a correlation of **−0.23**. >> Years of formal instruction were related to the **untimed GJT with ungrammatical items**, with a correlation of **0.27**. >> The other tests had only weak relationships with either measure. || This is because formal classroom instruction teaches explicit rules, and explicit rules help on the untimed test. The timed test limits the use of explicit rules, so starting age matters there instead. Since taught rules help on the untimed test, an age effect found with an untimed GJT may partly come from explicit, taught knowledge and not from natural grammar acquisition.
12|If the languages were more similar, the learners would get more help from **transfer**, and the age pattern might look different. This is because transfer (using first-language knowledge while learning a second language) helps more when the two languages are alike. Languages that are **typologically more similar** (more similar in structure, such as word order and word forms) can benefit more from transfer. A learner whose first language shares grammar and vocabulary with the second language can reuse much of what they already know. || Familiar words are one kind of help. Cognates and loan words, such as French **auto**, **rivière** and **bicyclette**, and Japanese **korokke** and **monoreru**, give learners familiar words to build on. || **Johnson and Newport (1989)** tested people with **English as L2** and **Chinese or Korean as L1**. Chinese and Korean are very different from English in grammar and vocabulary. So these learners got little help from transfer. || Speakers of a language close to English would get much more help. Their scores might relate to age in a different way. Since transfer depends on the language pair, the age pattern in Johnson and Newport's data would depend on the particular languages involved as well as on age. That is why transfer is both a methodological and a theoretical issue.
13|Not all aspects of the grammar are equally difficult for late learners. **Determiners** and **plurals** are the hardest, and **word order** and **progressive -ing** are the easiest. || Johnson and Newport (1989) compared the errors on **12 types of English rules**. They compared native speakers with groups who arrived at ages 3 to 7, 8 to 10, 11 to 15, 17 to 24 and 25 to 39. >> The rule types are determiners, plurals, **verb subcategorization** (the rules for which kinds of words or phrases can follow a verb), past tense, pronouns, particles and auxiliaries. The rest are third-person singular, wh-questions, yes/no questions, word order and progressive -ing. >> Native speakers and the youngest arrivals made very few errors on any rule. >> The latest arrivals (aged 17 to 24 and 25 to 39) made the most errors on determiners, around 65 to 68%, and on plurals, around 45 to 55%. >> Errors on word order and progressive -ing were low for every group, under about 10%. || Some of this looks like the **order in which children learn language**. This is because progressive -ing and word order are among the first structures children master. They are also the easiest for late learners. || Some of it could be **transfer** (using first-language knowledge in the second language). >> Determiners are hard because **Chinese has no determiners**. Chinese speakers have no L1 category for “a” and “the.” Since they have nothing to carry over, English articles are especially hard for them. >> Questions do not match the child order. Children learn questions very late, but questions are among the **easiest structures for late learners**. This may be because adults can use general cognitive skills or knowledge from their L1 when they learn question forms. || Since both things matter, how hard a structure is depends on the structure itself and on what the learner already knows from the first language.
14|The consequence is that AOA effects on GJT performance do not reflect only **maturational constraints** (limits on learning caused by the brain's development). They also depend on the language pairing. We know this because **Birdsong and Molis (2001)** repeated the Johnson and Newport study with **Spanish speakers learning English** and got almost the reverse pattern. || For **Chinese and Korean speakers learning English**, age mattered most in childhood. Early arrivals (aged 3 to 15) had a strong correlation between age of arrival and score, **−0.87**. Late arrivals (aged 17 to 39) had a weak one, **−0.16**. || For **Spanish speakers learning English**, age mattered most in adulthood. Early arrivals (aged 3 to 16) had only a weak correlation, **−0.23**. They scored near the maximum regardless of age, with only a slight drop for those arriving around 16. Late arrivals (aged 17 to 44) had a strong correlation, **−0.69**. Their scores went down from around 260 for those arriving near 18 to around 160 for those arriving after 40. || If a fixed maturational schedule controlled learning, the pattern should look the same for every first language. Instead, the pattern changed with the learners' first language. This may be because Spanish is much closer to English than Chinese or Korean is. Spanish speakers can get more help from **transfer**, so they can reach native-like grammar even when they arrive late in childhood. Since the pattern changes with the first language, the age effect depends on the language pairing as well as on age.
15|The one thing that holds across studies is that **L2 attainment is lower for older learners** overall. Even so, these findings may not really count as “conclusions.” This is because how much lower depends on several things, and several factors are hard to separate. || How much lower depends on three things. >> It depends on **what constructions are tested**. Some structures, like determiners, are much harder for late learners than others, like word order. >> It depends on **how they are tested**. A timed test and an untimed test can give different results, because the untimed test lets people use explicit knowledge. >> It depends on **language pairings**. Spanish speakers and Chinese or Korean speakers have different age patterns when they learn English. || Three pairs of things are **hard to dissociate** (hard to separate, because they usually go together). >> **Age of acquisition** is hard to separate from the **amount and quality of experience**. This is because people who arrive younger usually have more years of exposure and more immersion, such as schooling in the language. >> **Transfer effects** are hard to separate from **L2 acquisition**. This is because L2 performance reflects what was learned and also what carries over from the L1. >> **Performance** (how well knowledge is used on a particular task) is hard to separate from **competence** (the underlying knowledge of the language). This is because a test score measures performance, so it may not reveal competence directly. || Since so many factors go together, the age effect is real, but it depends on many factors that are hard to separate.
