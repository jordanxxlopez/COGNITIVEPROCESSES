2|**Klatt (1979)** describes a very old and very general model in which speech is recognized in a row of stages. This is because each stage takes the output of the stage before it and changes it. >> The **speech waveform** goes into the **peripheral auditory system**, which does a **spectral analysis** (the **cochlea** splits the sound into its frequencies). >> **Acoustic property detectors** respond to particular properties of the sound. >> **Phonetic feature detectors** figure out how likely features such as voicing are at each moment. >> **Segmental analysis** puts the features together into a sequence of speech segments. >> **Lexical search** compares that sequence with stored words and comes up with **lexical hypotheses** (candidate words). || **Categorical perception** fits this model. This is because **information is lost** somewhere between the **analog signal** from the cochlea and the **activation of phonemes**. Many physically different sounds end up as the same phoneme. This **loss of information is helpful**, because it lets the listener reach higher levels of representation. So the model assumes that to identify a word, you **first identify its phonemes**. || Two problems make this simple view harder to keep. >> The first is **cascaded processing** (partial information moves on to the next level before the earlier decision is done). >> The second is **feedback** (a higher level, such as words, sends influence back down to a lower level, such as phonemes).
3|On the beach and peach continuum, the same size change in sound is easy to hear only when it crosses the boundary. This is because the two words differ in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**. VOT is the delay between opening the lips and the start of vocal fold vibration. Beach starts with /b/ and peach starts with /p/. VOT gets shorter in equal steps from “peach” to “beach”. A long delay sounds like peach, and a short delay sounds like beach. || In **identification**, one listener's labels switch suddenly. >> Stimuli 1 through 5 always get one label. >> Stimulus 6 gets mixed labels, near 0.33. >> Stimuli 7 through 9 always get the other label. >> The **category boundary** is the narrow spot where the label switches. || In **discrimination**, the same listener can only tell pairs apart across the boundary. >> Pairs inside one category are almost never heard as different. >> Pairs 4 and 6 and 5 and 7 cross the boundary, so they are heard as different every time. >> Pair 6 and 8 is in between, near 0.33, and pair 7 and 9 drops back to 0. || Since a step across the boundary changes the word and a step inside a category does not, equal VOT steps sound very different.
4|How does categorical perception happen? One answer is a **threshold model**, where categorical perception happens at phoneme identification. This is because activity only passes from one level to the next once it crosses a **threshold**. A threshold is a set level that has to be reached first. Activity below the threshold can go up and down without changing the next level at all. || Take the voicing evidence for a sound between /b/ and /p/. >> At the **feature level**, the voicing evidence goes up and down between a **+voiced** threshold and a **−voiced** threshold. >> At the **phoneme level**, /b/ and /p/ both stay silent while the evidence is still going back and forth. >> When the evidence finally crosses the +voiced threshold, **/b/ turns on all at once**. || An ambiguous sound either crosses a threshold or it does not. So the word level gets a finished /b/ or a finished /p/. The earlier uncertainty stays behind at the feature level. Two different sounds that both become /b/ look exactly the same to word recognition. Since the switch is all or none, categorical perception happens right at the phoneme level.
5|How does categorical perception happen in a **cascaded model**? It might happen later on. It might even be an **artifact of the task structure** (a result made by how something is measured, not by the process being studied). This is because activity is passed forward continuously over time, while decisions are still forming. || Feature activity and phoneme activity go up and down together. >> When evidence for **+voiced** grows, **/b/** activation grows. >> When the evidence moves toward **−voiced**, activation moves toward **/p/**. >> When the evidence is mixed, /b/ and /p/ each get some activation, matching how much evidence each one has. || So graded activation at the phoneme level passes graded activation to words. A sound near the boundary **partly activates both beach and peach**. Two sounds that both get labeled /b/ can still differ in how much they supported /p/ along the way. || The answer still looks categorical because of the task. An identification task makes the listener click beach or peach, even if both words were partly active. Since the response choices are categorical, the final answer is categorical too.
6|**Making furniture** is a way to think about **thresholded activity**. This is because each operation takes one specific input and turns it into one specific output. >> The **lumber mill** turns **trees** into **lumber**. >> The **furniture factory** turns **lumber** into **unassembled furniture**. >> The **IKEA customer** turns **unassembled furniture** into finished **furniture**. || Each step has to wait for a finished product it can use. Trees have to become lumber before any furniture is made. Lumber has to become furniture parts before anyone can put them together. No part of one stage's work moves on until the whole job is done. || **Thresholded processing** works the same way. A higher level waits until the lower level hands it the representation it needs. Since nothing moves until it is finished, information moves forward **only as a completed product**.
7|In the furniture example, each **“layer”** can only take an input that the layer before it has **completely transformed**. This is because the factory has to wait for the lumber shipment before it can start making anything. Many computer programs work this way. A lower stage can keep working while the higher stage just sits and waits. || In speech, a strict threshold account means **word processing depends on an identified phoneme**. A partly figured-out sound stays at the lower level until it is identified as a phoneme. Then the word level gets only the finished segment. || This setup also decides **what information is lost**. When different sounds become the same phoneme, their earlier differences never reach word recognition. That helps the listener treat varied sounds the same way. Since only the finished output is passed on, later stages never find out how uncertain the earlier stages were.
8|A **food web** is a way to think about **cascaded processing**. This is because its **“levels” continuously interact** with one another. The desert food web connects desert plants, insects, kangaroo rats, small lizards, large lizards, scorpions, snakes, desert foxes and hawks by who eats whom. >> A drought that cuts down the plants **immediately** affects the health of kangaroo rats. >> The change in kangaroo rats then affects the snakes that eat them. >> No animal waits for the level below it to be finished before it is affected. || Cascaded processing works the same way. When the feature evidence changes, phoneme activity changes right away. When phoneme activity changes, the support for words changes right away. So each level reacts to partial information as it comes in. || The food web also has clear **feedback loops**. What does adding more hawks do? More hawks change the numbers of snakes, lizards and rats. Those changes then affect the hawks’ own food supply. In a recognition model, **feedback** is influence going from a higher level back down to a lower level. || Cascading and feedback are not the same thing. Cascading sends developing evidence **forward**. Feedback lets a higher level **change an earlier representation**.
9|**Phoneme identification for word recognition** works more like the food web than the lumber mill, so it is **cascaded** and not **thresholded**. This is because listeners' eye movements show a competing word getting support before the final choice. Only a cascaded system predicts that. The two accounts make different predictions before the final choice. >> In a **thresholded account**, word recognition starts only after the phoneme decision is finished. >> In a **cascaded account**, a word can get support while the phoneme is still uncertain. || Take two sounds that are both labeled **/b/**, where one sounds a bit more like **/p/**. >> A thresholded system turns both into the same finished /b/. So pear should **not compete** with bear. >> A cascaded system passes along more /p/ support for the less clear sound. So pear should **compete with bear for a while**. || Both accounts can predict the same final word. They only differ before the final choice. Since that is where they differ, the deciding test has to measure **temporary support for a competing word** during recognition.
10|**McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)** tested whether phoneme identification is thresholded or cascaded by tracking eye movements. They looked for **gradient effects of within-category VOT on lexical access**. >> **Lexical access** means getting a word out of memory during recognition. >> **Gradient** means changing by degrees. >> **Within-category VOT** means differences in voice onset time between sounds that are all heard as the same phoneme. || Listeners identified sounds from a continuum between /b/ and /p/ in a **“visual world” display**. **Eye-tracking** recorded where they looked among the pictures while they heard speech. Each display had a **target**, such as pear, and a **competitor**, such as bear. It also had two unrelated **fillers**, a lamp and a ship. The target and competitor differ only in their first consonant. || This method works because eye movements happen fast and continuously while a word is being recognized. Listeners look at objects as they think about them, often before they know they have decided. So a listener can click bear after a quick look at the pear. Since the looks come before the click, the number and timing of looks show **how active the competitor was**.
11|The clicks alone make perception look completely categorical. This is because participants used the **mouse to select** whichever of the pictures best matched what they heard, such as a “bear” or a “pear”. Each trial had one sound from the VOT continuum and the four pictures. The click shows which word the listener finally settled on. || The clicks gave a **typical, step-like identification function**. >> From 0 to about 15 milliseconds of VOT, /p/ responses stay near 0, so listeners almost always clicked bear. >> Between about 15 and 20 milliseconds, /p/ responses jump up steeply. >> From about 20 to 40 milliseconds, /p/ responses stay near 1, so listeners almost always clicked pear. >> The **category boundary** is at **17.5 milliseconds**, plus or minus 0.83 milliseconds. || On its own, this sudden switch would seem to support the threshold model. But a click only records the word chosen at the very end. Eye movements record which other words got support before that choice. Since the two measure different moments, a sharp click curve can still go along with gradual differences in competition.
12|**Eye movements reveal that ambiguity is gradient**. There are **more looks to the competitor close to the boundary**. This is because a sound near the boundary keeps the other word active longer, even when the final click is the same. || The comparison only uses trials with the same final click. >> **When listeners clicked bear**, the measure is looks to the **pear**, at VOTs of 0, 5, 10 and 15 milliseconds. Looks to the pear go up after the word starts, peak around 400 to 600 milliseconds, and then drop as bear wins. >> The 15 millisecond sound, closest to the boundary, gets more looks to the pear, and they last longer than for 0 milliseconds. >> **When listeners clicked pear**, the measure is looks to the **bear**, at VOTs of 20, 25, 30, 35 and 40 milliseconds. The 20 millisecond sound, just past the boundary, gets the most looks to the bear. The 40 millisecond sound, the clearest /p/, gets the fewest. || Since every trial in a comparison ended with the same click, the differences cannot come from listeners changing their answer. VOT mostly changes the **duration of activation** (how long the competitor stays active). So the system keeps track of how ambiguous each sound was. A threshold model would throw that information away.
13|Looks to the competitor go up as the VOT gets closer to the boundary, from both sides. This is because sounds near the **category boundary** give more support to the rival word. || The comparison runs across the whole **VOT** range, with the boundary in the middle. >> **On the bear side**, listeners clicked bear, and the measure is how often they looked at the pear. >> **On the pear side**, listeners clicked pear, and the measure is how often they looked at the bear. || Looks were measured in two time windows. >> The **early** window is 300 to 1100 milliseconds after the word starts. >> The **late** window is 1100 to 1900 milliseconds after the word starts. || Both windows show the same pattern. >> On the bear side, late looks to the pear go up from about 0.02 at 0 milliseconds to about 0.045 near 15 milliseconds. >> On the pear side, late looks to the bear are about 0.04 near 20 milliseconds and drop to about 0.01 by 40 milliseconds. >> Early looks are higher overall, but they follow the same pattern. The rise is gradual, not sudden. || Since the effect shows up in both windows, uncertainty about the first sound affects looking through the whole recognition process. A sound can support the winning word and still support its rival.
14|The eye-movement result fits the idea that **activation flows in a cascaded way from features to phonemes**. This is because even sounds that are identified as /b/ **100% of the time** can differ in how strongly the **“voiced” feature** is activated. A clear /b/ turns on voicing strongly. A /b/ near the boundary turns it on only weakly. || /b/ and /p/ share almost all their features. >> Both are **bilabial** (the two lips close). >> Both are **stops** (the air is fully blocked and then let go). >> A sound that is obviously **+bilabial** and **+stop** supports /b/ and /p/ equally. >> Only voicing tells them apart, so weak voicing makes a **momentary ambiguity between /b/ and /p/**. >> That ambiguity shows up in the **eye movements** while the participant decides where to click. || The **TRACE** model shows this cascade with three layers. >> The **feature layer** takes a **pseudo-spectral input representation** (a simplified version of the sound’s frequencies over time). Its detectors are POW, VOC, DIFF, ACU, GRD, VOI and BUR, for power, vocalic, diffuseness, acuteness, gradualness of onset, voicing and burst. >> The **phoneme layer** has units such as /b/, /p/, /r/ and /t/, repeated at every point in time. >> The **word layer** has word units, also repeated across time. >> Activity passes upward, and units in the same layer compete through **inhibition**.
15|**Andruski and colleagues (1994)** found that **partial activation can reach all the way to the lexical level** (the level of stored words and their meanings). We know this because they got **semantic priming** (a faster response to a word after hearing a related word) even from **nonwords**. >> The nonword “beace” primes “war”. Beace is peace with a voiced first consonant, and peace is related to war. >> The nonword “ging” primes “queen”. Ging is king with a voiced first consonant, and king is related to queen. >> In **word to word** items, the changed prime is another real word, so “bill” primes “drug” because bill is a changed form of pill. || Participants did a **lexical decision task** (deciding as fast as possible whether a target is a real word). Primes were **unaltered**, had VOT cut by **one third**, had it cut by **two thirds**, or were **unrelated**. A response faster than after an unrelated prime counts as priming. || The response times went like this. >> Unrelated primes gave the slowest responses, near 950 to 970 milliseconds. >> Unaltered primes gave much faster ones, around 810 to 860 milliseconds. >> With one third cut, responses stayed fast, around 825 to 855 milliseconds. >> With two thirds cut, word to nonword items stayed near 820 milliseconds. Word to word items slowed to about 890 milliseconds, because the changed prime also partly activates a competing real word. || Since beace matches peace closely enough to partly activate it, **incomplete or imperfect input can still activate words**. That activation spreads to meaning and speeds up the response to war, which fits **cascaded processing**.
16|A **thresholded model** has a hard time explaining the bear and pear eye-tracking result, so the result is evidence for **cascaded processing**. A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in one sound, such as bear and pear. This is because looks to the other member of the minimal pair go up gradually toward the boundary. That happens even when the final click is the same, so it comes from graded activation inside one category. || In a thresholded model, graded feature activity never gets to the phoneme level. >> Feature activation goes back and forth between +voiced and −voiced. >> Phoneme activation stays flat until /b/ suddenly turns on. >> So every sound in a category sends the **same finished phoneme** to the word level. >> Then word-level competition would be the same for every /b/, and nothing could make competitor looks change gradually. || In a cascaded model, graded information does get to the word level. More support for the competing phoneme passes on more support for its word. Since that support grows near the boundary, competitor looks grow near the boundary too.
17|The **TRACE** model also has **feedback from the lexical layer to the phoneme layer**. This means activity goes up from phonemes to words and also back down from words to phonemes. **Lexical feedback** (an active word sends support back to its own phonemes) works one step at a time. An unclear sound partly activates a word. Then that word strengthens the reading of the sound that fits it. || So influence goes both ways. >> The sound evidence changes which words are active. >> The active words change the balance between possible phonemes while the phoneme decision is still forming. >> This helps listeners deal with noisy or ambiguous input. || **Feedback is not the same as cascading**. >> Cascading sends incomplete evidence forward, from features to phonemes and from phonemes to words. >> Feedback sends influence from words back down to phonemes. >> One interactive model can have both. || The reason to add feedback comes from experiments where knowing words changes how sounds are heard, such as the **Ganong effect**.
18|**Ganong (1980)** tested whether knowing which sound sequences are real words changes how an ambiguous sound is heard. This is the **Ganong effect**. The line “Gisses and kifts for Valentine’s day” plays on it by swapping the voicing of the first sounds in “kisses and gifts”. || He made **word and nonword pairs** such as “corpse/gorpse”. >> Hearing /k/ makes corpse, which is a real word. >> Hearing /g/ makes gorpse, which is not a word. >> The continua crossed the phoneme boundary in **5 millisecond steps** of VOT. || This setup works because it combines an **uncertain sound** with a **difference in being a word**. The rest of the word, “orpse”, stays the same at every step. So the ending tells the listener which first sound would make a real word. If word knowledge matters, an unclear first consonant should be heard more often as the sound that makes a word. Since word knowledge has the most room to act where the sound is unclear, the important test is the **ambiguous middle** of the continuum.
19|One prediction is a **late “correction” process**, where the listener categorizes the sound as usual but then leans toward reporting a word. This is because in this account **categorical perception works as usual**, and the word knowledge only comes in at the response stage. The result is a **bias to report having heard a word**. || Three kinds of continua are compared. >> In **word-nonword** continua, the voiced end makes a word. >> In **nonword-word** continua, the voiceless end makes a word. >> In **neutral** continua, neither end has a word advantage. || A bias added after perception pushes answers toward the word by the same amount everywhere. >> For word-nonword, voiced answers stay near 0.25 at the long VOT end and never fall to 0. >> For nonword-word, voiced answers start near 0.75 at the short VOT end, not at 1. >> So even a clear sound would sometimes be reported as the word. || The **lexical effect** (the difference in voiced answers between the two word conditions) would be **the same size at every step**, near 0.25. Since the bias only nudges the reported answer, perception and word knowledge stay separate.
20|A second prediction is that **word knowledge shifts the category boundary**. Then the word effect should be **strongest for the items closest to the boundary**. This is because those are the sounds where knowing the word can help clear up which first phoneme it was. || In this account, word knowledge changes where the listener switches from one phoneme to the other. Near an unclear start, the reading that makes a real word gets extra support. So the categorization itself changes, not just the reported answer. || The conditions should only differ in the middle. >> At both ends, the sound is clear, so voiced answers start near 1 and end near 0 in every condition. >> Near the boundary, the word-nonword condition switches later, and the nonword-word condition switches earlier. || The **lexical effect** makes a **peak**. It is near 0 at the ends and goes up to about 0.35 for the items next to the boundary. At the ends, the strong sound evidence already settles the consonant, so word knowledge has little room to help. || Since the two accounts differ in shape, a **flat effect** points to a reporting bias, and a **peaked effect** points to a boundary shift.
21|**Ganong’s results** match the **second model**, the boundary shift, very closely. This is because the word effect was biggest right at the boundary. >> At the short and long ends of the VOT continuum, the word-nonword and nonword-word conditions give almost the same answers. >> Near the boundary, the two conditions pull apart the most. >> The **lexical effect** goes from about 0.1 at the ends up to a peak near **0.3** at the boundary. || A peak at the boundary rules out a simple reporting bias. A reporting bias would be the same size everywhere. Here word knowledge matters most where the sound evidence is weakest. At the boundary, the word that fits tips the balance. At the ends, the sound decides the answer by itself. || Ganong raised several ideas for how this could happen. He focused on different kinds of **guessing strategies**, where listeners use word knowledge only when they are unsure of the sound. || Later models put more weight on **feedback**. In **interactive models**, an active word sends support back to its own phonemes. So the whole word can change how an unclear part of it is heard during recognition.
22|A **TRACE simulation of the Ganong effect** shows the activation of words and phonemes changing as an unclear start turns into the word **plug**. This is because the model records **phoneme-level and word-level activations** at four moments, called frames, one as each new sound comes in. The input starts with a sound **ambiguous between /b/ and /p/**, then /l/, the vowel /ʌ/ and /g/. || In the **first frame**, only the unclear first sound has arrived. Both /b/ and /p/ are a little active, at about the same level, near 0.15 to 0.2. Word activation is still very low. || In the **second and third frames**, /l/ and the vowel arrive. >> Partial /b/ evidence supports words starting with bl, and partial /p/ evidence supports words starting with pl. >> **Plug**, **blood** and **blush** all go up together, because all three fit what has been heard so far. >> /p/ starts to pull ahead of /b/. || In the **fourth frame**, the final **/g/** arrives. The /g/ fits plug but does not fit blood or blush. So **plug** becomes the most active word, at about 0.45, and the others fall away. Since plug wins, **/p/ ends up more active than /b/**.
23|In TRACE, the final /g/ makes **“plug”** win, and plug then pushes the unclear first sound toward /p/. This is because activity builds up for plug as the input comes in, most of all when the **final /g/** arrives. The /g/ does not fit “blood” or “blush”, which were also active in Frame 3. Blood and blush fit the early part, so they were possible for a while. Once /g/ arrives, their endings do not match, so they lose support. || The model explains **word effects on phoneme category boundaries** with **top-down processing** (higher-level knowledge changing lower-level analysis). >> Activity at the lexical level sends **feedback to the phoneme level**. >> The feedback gives **more activation to the phoneme that fits a word**. >> Plug is the winning word, so /p/ gets the extra support. || Since /p/ belongs to the word the whole sequence supports, the unclear first sound ends up heard as /p/. So information at the end of a word can change how an earlier sound is heard.
24|The **TRACE** model is a version of the **Interactive Activation model** for **written word recognition**, from **McClelland and Rumelhart (1981)**. Many of the principles are the same. This is because both models connect levels in the same way. >> **Feature** units connect to **letter** units, and letter units connect to **word** units such as WEAR, WORK, WORD and WEAK. >> Units that fit together at different levels support each other. >> Units competing in the same level inhibit each other. >> Active words send support back down to their own letters. || Say a reader sees **W, O and R** clearly, but ink blots cover part of the last letter. >> The visible part of the last letter fits both **R** and **K**. >> W, O and R activate words such as WORK and WORD. >> Only WORK fits the last letter, so WORK becomes the strongest word. >> WORK sends support back to K, so the unclear letter is **seen as K**, just like plug supports /p/ in speech. || The big difference is that **speech unfolds over time**. A written word is all there at once. A spoken word comes in one sound at a time. So TRACE repeats feature, phoneme and word units at every point in time. That way a word can fit the beginning, get active, and then fade when the ending does not fit.
25|**Categorical perception** still holds, but spoken word recognition is more interactive than a lumber mill. This is because given the same physical difference, it is **harder to tell apart sounds from the same category** than sounds from different categories. Nothing about cascading or feedback changes that basic finding. || But two findings about the **lexical-phonetic interface** add to it. >> **Ambiguity about phoneme identity does affect word recognition**. Sounds with the same label still differ in how strongly they activate a competing word. >> **Word-level information seems to affect phoneme categorization**. In the Ganong effect, word knowledge moves the category boundary, and TRACE does this with lexical feedback. || So the word recognition system **cannot just sit there waiting** for the phoneme system to finish its job. It does not work the way a factory waits for the lumber mill. Partial information moves forward while uncertainty is still being worked out. Active words send support back down at the same time. Since information flows both ways, spoken word recognition works more like the food web. Labels can be categorical while the processing behind them is graded.

27|**Spoken word recognition is also very fast**, and words become active while the word is still being heard. We know this because **Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** used a visual world eye-tracking task and found looks to the target starting before the word ended. Participants saw a grid of pictures and geometric shapes and followed spoken instructions to move objects. || The pictures were picked for how much they sound like **beaker**. >> **Beaker** is the **referent** (the target). >> **Beetle** is a **cohort competitor** (it shares the beginning of the word). >> **Speaker** is a **rhyme competitor** (it shares the ending of the word). >> **Carriage** is **unrelated** (it shares no sounds). || The measure is the chance of looking at each item over the first 1,000 milliseconds. On average the spoken word ends a little before 400 milliseconds. >> Looks to beaker and beetle go up together from around 200 milliseconds, because both fit the beginning. >> Looks to beetle peak near 0.2 around 500 milliseconds and then drop once the sounds that separate the words arrive. >> Looks to beaker keep climbing, past 0.8 by about 800 milliseconds and close to 1 by 1,000 milliseconds. >> Looks to speaker go up later and stay small, near 0.1. Looks to carriage stay near zero. || Since words that match the input get active and drop out when they stop matching, recognition happens while the word unfolds.
28|A **TRACE simulation** predicts the eye movements moment by moment. So the looks make it possible to **see cognitive processing before a final response is issued**. This is because **eye movements give an idea of the changes in “activity”** for different competitors over time. The comparison is between real looks and the looks TRACE predicts over 1,000 milliseconds. >> For the **referent**, real and predicted looks both go up steadily from about 300 milliseconds and get close to 1 by 1,000 milliseconds. >> For the **cohort** competitor, both go up early, peak near 0.17 to 0.2 around 500 milliseconds, and then drop. >> For the **rhyme** competitor, both go up later and stay small, near 0.1. >> So the TRACE predictions closely match the real looks. || The **granularity** of the simulation is fine (it predicts activity moment by moment, not just the final choice). Since the model and the eyes both track activity over time, the two can be compared at every moment. || This is like the **graded effects of category goodness** in the categorical perception task. Sounds that are better or worse examples of a category make different amounts of competitor activity. That happens even when the final label is the same.
