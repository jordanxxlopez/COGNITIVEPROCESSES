2|“The horse raced past the barn fell” describes **two events**. Someone raced the horse past the barn, and then the horse fell. || It feels broken at first even though it is grammatical. This is because most readers take **raced** as the **main verb** (the verb that says what the subject did). Then “the horse raced past the barn” looks like a whole event where the horse did the racing. When **fell** shows up, there is no place left for it. So the sentence seems to fall apart. || The real meaning is “The horse that was raced past the barn fell”. Here “raced past the barn” tells which horse is meant. **Fell** is the main verb, and it says what happened to that horse. || You can **disambiguate** it (make only one reading possible) by putting back the missing words “that was”. This is because “that was” makes raced part of the description of the horse. So raced can no longer be read as the main verb. Since understanding depends on how the words relate, knowing each word’s meaning is not enough. || It could be called **the sentence that launched a thousand experiments**. This is because researchers have used it again and again to study how readers build structure as words come in.
3|The horse sentence is the classic case of the **garden path effect** (getting led into one reading early and then finding out it was wrong). The name comes from “to lead someone down the garden path”, which means to mislead someone. || This is because readers build structure **word by word**. They pick one analysis before the sentence is over. In the horse sentence, raced looks like the main verb. Then fell clashes with the structure already built. So the reader has to go back and read “raced past the barn” as “that was raced past the barn”. If readers waited until the end, they would never be misled. || The horse sentence is almost as famous as “Colorless green ideas sleep furiously”. The two are hard in different ways. “Colorless green ideas sleep furiously” is grammatical but makes no sense. The horse sentence is grammatical and makes sense, but it is very hard to process. || So **grammaticality** and **processing difficulty** are different things. A sentence can have a fine structure and still be hard to follow as it unfolds. A dinosaur comic jokes that nobody would say the horse sentence. The reply is that farmers might, if their horses kept falling over. The joke only works because the sentence really is grammatical.
4|Syntactic explanations of the garden path start with a **parser** (the mental process that builds a **syntactic tree** for each sentence we hear or read). A syntactic tree records how words group into phrases and how the phrases fit together. || The correct tree for the horse sentence is big. The subject is a large **NP** (noun phrase). It holds “the horse” plus a shortened clause meaning “that was raced past the barn”. The words “that” and “was” are silent, and the clause has an empty subject spot. Inside it, raced takes the **PP** (prepositional phrase) “past the barn”. The main **VP** (verb phrase) is just **fell**. || The failed parse is small. “The horse” is a plain NP, and “raced past the barn” is the main VP. This is why fell has nowhere to attach, so the parse breaks down. || The parser has to work with **limited memory** and **time constraints**. This is because words come in fast, so the parser cannot wait for the end of the sentence. It also can only keep a few structures going at once. Since it works under these limits, it needs some way to deal with ambiguous sentences.
5|The parser first prefers the smaller tree, and this strategy is called **minimal attachment**. It means fitting each new word in with the fewest new **nodes**. Nodes are points in a tree that stand for a grouping, such as an NP, a VP or a sentence. || This strategy saves space. Fewer nodes means less structure to build. It also means less to hold in memory at that moment. || The correct version of the horse sentence is much more complex. This is because it has many more nodes. It needs extra nodes for the embedded clause. It also needs nodes for the silent “that” and “was”. The failed parse just makes “raced past the barn” the main VP, which takes far fewer nodes. || So minimal attachment picks **raced as the main verb**. This is because that reading needs the fewest nodes. The reading where “raced past the barn” describes the horse needs an embedded relative clause. So the parser passes it over at first. When fell arrives, it needs the bigger structure. Since the parser chose the small tree, the reader has to rebuild the tree, and that is the garden path.
6|**Late closure** is a second parsing strategy. It is a bias to keep the current clause **open** as long as possible. Keeping a clause open means adding new words to it instead of closing it and starting something new. || In “The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite”, the phrase “with the dynamite” joins the big verb phrase starting with “blew open”. **Frazier (1987)** gives two trees for this sentence. In the **preferred** tree, the PP attaches to the VP. This is **verb-phrase attachment**, so the dynamite is the **instrument** (the tool) used to blow open the safe. In the **unpreferred** tree, the PP attaches to the NP “the safe”. This is **noun-phrase attachment**, so the phrase tells which safe, the one that had the dynamite. || Both are grammatical, but they mean different things. The parser picks the VP attachment. This is because late closure keeps the verb phrase open, so the new phrase joins it. Minimal attachment also likes the VP attachment, because it needs fewer nodes than an extra NP around “the safe with the dynamite”. Since the preferred parse is also the sensible one here, readers get the right meaning easily.
7|People read “The burglar blew open the safe with the jewels” more slowly. This is because the sensible meaning needs the **unpreferred parse**. || Jewels are not a tool for blowing open a safe. The sensible meaning is that the burglar blew open **the safe that had the jewels**. That means “with the jewels” has to attach to the noun phrase “the safe”. That is the unpreferred NP attachment. || Careful tests of **reading time** and **eye movements** find the slowdown. Reading time is how long readers spend on each part of a sentence. Eye movements show where readers look and when they jump back to reread. The extra time shows up right where the parse has to change. || Readers **briefly think** the jewels were used to blow open the safe. This is because late closure makes the parser attach the phrase to the verb phrase first, just like with the dynamite. Then the meaning of jewels clashes with the tool reading. So the parser moves the attachment to the noun phrase, and readers **retrace their steps**. Since that repair takes time, reading is slower even though the final meaning is easy.
8|The syntactic explanation assumes a parser that works **only on abstract syntactic structure**. It is like the **“lumber mill”** example from speech perception. In a lumber mill, each stage works on its own material and passes a finished product to the next stage. In the same way, the parser builds structure from grammatical categories alone. It does not check meaning or context. Meaning gets worked out afterward from the finished structure. || The parser has **its own operating characteristics**. It wants the simplest tree possible, which is **minimal attachment**. It gets there partly by leaving clauses open as long as possible, which is **late closure**. Both of these keep down how much structure it has to build and hold at once. || This is a very efficient way to handle ambiguity, because the parser **ignores ambiguity until it becomes a problem**. This is because it commits to one likely structure and builds only that one. Most of the time the first choice is right, so no extra work is needed. When a later word does not fit, the structure has to be fixed. Since that fix is the garden path, garden paths are the price of being efficient.
9|Syntactically “difficult” sentences are **not always hard to read**, and that is a problem for a purely syntactic explanation. The difficult structure here is the **reduced relative clause** (a relative clause with “that was” or “that were” left out, as in the horse sentence). || Research articles contain many reduced relatives that are easy to read. The verb that starts each one is in capitals. Trueswell, Tanenhaus and Garnsey (1994) wrote “a noun phrase FOLLOWED by a MORPHOLOGICALLY AMBIGUOUS VERB … will be temporarily ambiguous”. Britt, Perfetti, Garrod and Rayner (1992) wrote “Referential information PROVIDED by the discourse is of no help”. Frazier and Rayner (1987) wrote “Recent research REPORTED by FERREIRA AND CLIFTON (1986) has demonstrated that syntactic processing is quite independent and that the initial syntactic analysis ASSIGNED to a sentence is little influenced by the semantic information ALREADY ANALYZED”. Altmann and Steedman (1988) wrote “The pattern of results OBTAINED with global reading times is reflected by differences LOCATED on the critical disambiguating phrase”. || These have the same structure as the horse sentence, but they are not impossibly hard. In “recent research reported by Ferreira and Clifton”, research is the thing that got reported. || **MacDonald, Perlmutter and Seidenberg (1994)** argue that **semantic factors** (meaning) and **statistical regularities** (how often words show up in certain structures) affect parsing from very early on. So familiar word uses can make the reduced relative reading easy from the start.
10|The table of examples is **wonderfully passive-aggressive**. This is because many examples come from papers that use the difficulty of reduced relatives as evidence for an **autonomous parser**. That is a parser that builds structure without help from meaning or context. Those authors said reduced relatives are hard to process. Yet they wrote reduced relatives in their own papers, and readers understood them easily. || In each example, the capitalized verb describes the noun right before it. In “a noun phrase FOLLOWED by a morphologically ambiguous verb”, followed describes noun phrase. In “the initial syntactic analysis ASSIGNED to a sentence”, assigned describes analysis. In “differences LOCATED on the critical disambiguating phrase”, located describes differences. || Ferreira and Clifton (1986) wrote “A person PRESENTED with the evidence EXAMINED by the lawyer may interpret the evidence as a theme, not an agent, because examined requires an animate agent”. Presented describes person, and examined describes evidence. || These were **not the only reduced relatives** in those papers. They showed up about **once per journal page** in this sample. So reduced relatives are normal in scientific writing. Since readers handle them easily in context, a complex structure can be easy when the context supports it.
11|“Recent research reported by…” is easy because several things besides syntax help from **very early on**. || The first is **semantics** (meaning). Research is **inanimate** (not a living thing). So it is unlikely to be the one doing the reporting. That means it gets read as the thing that was reported. || The second is **lexical statistics** (learned patterns of how often words show up in certain structures). “Reported” shows up a lot in reduced relatives. || The third is lexical statistics plus **world knowledge**. In scientific papers, “reported” shows up very often in reduced relatives, especially right after “research”. World knowledge here means knowing how people actually write in that setting. These and many other factors help decide which structure gets picked in the first place. || “The burglar arrested in the parking lot was charged today” is easy, but “The policeman arrested in the parking lot was charged today” causes a garden path. This is because burglars usually get arrested. So arrested is read as describing the burglar. Policemen usually do the arresting. So arrested is read as the main verb, and “was charged” then breaks that reading. Since the grammar is the same, the difference comes from what we know about burglars and policemen.
12|Listeners briefly treat the empty towel as the place to put the apple. **Spivey and colleagues (1995)** found this when they tested whether **world knowledge** can come from the scene right in front of you. || People saw a pencil, a box, an empty towel and an apple sitting on a towel. They heard “Put the apple on the towel in the box” and did it while their eyes were tracked. The clear version was “Put the apple that’s on the towel in the box”. || This is because of **minimal attachment**. With it, “the towel” briefly seems to end the clause “put the apple on the towel”. So “on the towel” sounds like the **destination** (where the apple should go). In the real meaning, “on the towel” tells **which apple** to move, and “in the box” is the destination. || With the unclear instruction, people looked at the apple, then the empty towel, then the box. The look to the apple came near 1,000 milliseconds, and the look to the box came near 2,000 milliseconds. With the clear instruction, they looked from the apple straight to the box. Since every word was heard correctly, the mistake was only in how the location phrase got attached.
13|With **two apples** in the display, people mostly stop looking at the empty towel. This display is the **two-referent context** (two possible things “the apple” could mean). One apple sits on a towel and the other sits somewhere else. The empty towel and the box are still there. || This is because “the apple” alone no longer tells which apple to move. So “on the towel” now has a job, which is picking out one apple. Since a phrase that picks out the apple is expected, listeners read “on the towel” that way from the start. The destination reading never takes hold. || People usually looked at the apple on the towel and then at the box. Looks to the empty towel were much less common. The **order and timing of eye movements**, measured against the nouns in the speech, **did not differ** between the unclear and the clear instruction. || So the unclear instruction was handled just like the clear one with “that’s on the towel”. Since only the scene changed, the scene changed how the same sentence got parsed.
14|People look at the empty towel much less when there are **two apples**. This is because with two apples it becomes **felicitous** (natural and appropriate) to say which apple you mean. With one apple, there is no need to say which apple. So “on the towel” seems like extra information and gets heard as a destination. With two apples, “on the towel” has a clear job, which is picking the right apple. || The measure was how often people looked at the wrong destination, the empty towel. With one apple, the unclear instruction led to these looks on about **55%** of trials. The clear instruction led to almost none. With two apples, the unclear instruction led to these looks on only about **17%** of trials. That is close to the roughly 12% for the clear instruction. || The words were exactly the same in both displays. Since only the scene changed, the scene alone changed how often listeners were misled. This is an effect of **referential context** (the set of objects a phrase could be about). It affects which grammatical relationship listeners pick while they are still hearing the sentence.
15|The apple results are **a big problem for purely syntactic accounts**. This is because the same sentence gets parsed differently under different **semantic** (meaning) and **pragmatic** (how language is used in a situation) conditions. One pragmatic condition is whether the speaker needs to say which object they mean. || The same unclear sentence misled people on about 55% of trials with one apple. It misled them on only about 17% of trials with two apples. The words never changed. Only the scene changed, and the scene changed the first parse. || The effect also happens **very early**. This is because it shows up in eye movements during the sentence, before the action starts. So the eye movements catch the parse while it is being built. If context only fixed the parse afterward, people in both displays would first look at the empty towel. They do not, because those looks drop with two apples. Since context shaped the first parse, a parser that always starts with the same structure cannot explain the results.
16|Ambiguous instructions bend the **hand’s path** toward the wrong spot, not just the eyes. **Farmer and colleagues (2007)** found this by tracking **mouse movements** while people followed spoken instructions in an apple and towel display. || This is because mouse tracking records the whole path of the hand. So a competing reading can pull the hand toward another place before it reaches the target. The actual **arm movements** are affected by syntactic ambiguity and how it interacts with the **visual context**. || With one apple, the hand moved from the apple toward the box. On unclear instructions, its path **curved toward the empty towel**. The two paths split most in the middle of the movement. The hand still ended in the right place. With two apples, the unclear and clear paths were nearly the same. This is because the second apple gives the location phrase a job, so it is not heard as a destination. || They also measured the **Euclidean distance** (straight-line distance) between the unclear and clear paths at each moment. With one apple, it peaked at about 200 pixels around 40% of the way through. With two apples, it peaked lower and earlier, at about 140 pixels around 30%. Since the hand bends while moving, understanding and planning the action happen at the same time.

18|After “the”, the parser expects a noun to come. This is because **the** is a **determiner** (a word that starts a **noun phrase**). “The” does not name anything by itself. It limits what kinds of words can come next. An adjective or other describing word might come before the noun. || The expectation is about a **grammatical category** (a word type such as noun), not one particular word. Many nouns could follow, such as pizza, dog or book. An adjective could come first, as in “the hot pizza”. So the parser guesses the form of the phrase while its meaning is still open. || Since each word sets up an expectation for the next one, words that fit go through smoothly. Words that break the expectation cause trouble.
19|After “the pizza”, the parser expects a verb. This is because **pizza** is a **noun** that finishes the noun phrase started by “the”. It is the **head of the noun phrase** (the main word the phrase is built around). “The pizza” can now be the subject of the sentence. So the parser expects a verb or verb phrase that says what the pizza did or what happened to it. || Recognizing the word gives two kinds of information. Its **category**, noun, tells the parser how it can combine with other words. Its **meaning**, a kind of food, makes certain events likely, such as being eaten or baked. || Since both kinds of information are available, grammar-based and meaning-based expectations both guide how the next words get handled.
20|After “was”, the sentence can still go several ways. This is because **was** is a past-tense form of **be**, and it can be an **auxiliary** (a helping verb that joins another verb to mark things like tense or voice). || In “the pizza was eaten”, was helps make a **passive** (a sentence about what was done to the subject) with eaten. In “the pizza was in the oven”, was links the pizza to a place. || So the sentence could say where the pizza was, what it was like, or what was done to it. A place would come next with a preposition. A passive would come next with a participle such as eaten. An adjective could also come next. || Since the role of was depends on what comes after it, the parser has to keep the growing structure ready while more words arrive.
21|After “in”, the parser expects a noun phrase that names a place. This is because **in** is a **preposition** (a word for a relationship such as location or being inside something). It starts a **prepositional phrase**. “In” does not name the place by itself. So it needs a noun phrase to supply the place, as in “in the oven” or “in the box”. || After “the pizza was”, the word “in” narrows down what the sentence can be. Now it is heading toward saying where the pizza was. || The expectation is about grammar. So the next word has to be able to start a noun phrase, such as “the”, “an” or a noun. Since the phrase is only complete with that noun phrase, understanding it means putting the preposition and the noun phrase together.
22|After “in the”, the parser strongly expects a **noun**. This is because this second **the** starts a new **noun phrase** inside “in the …”. That noun phrase will name the place. “The” does not give the place by itself. So a noun is still needed, maybe with a describing word in front, as in “in the hot oven”. || The expectation is about **word category**, not just a sensible topic. Knowing the sentence is about pizza makes words like oven or box likely. But the grammar rule holds no matter what the meaning is. Whatever comes next has to fit inside a noun phrase. || Since the grammatical frame is now very specific, any word that cannot be part of a noun phrase will clash with it.
23|“The pizza was in the eaten” breaks the sentence’s structure. This is because **eaten** is the **past participle** (a verb form, as in “was eaten”) of eat, not a noun. “In the” needs a noun to finish its noun phrase. So eaten lands exactly where a noun is required. This is a **word-category violation** (a word of the wrong type in a spot that needs a different type). || This is different from a sentence that is grammatical but strange. “The pizza was in the ocean” is odd in meaning, but the grammar is fine, because ocean is a noun. “The pizza was in the eaten” cannot be fixed by imagining some unusual situation. This is because the problem is the type of word, not the meaning. || Since one is a grammar error and the other is a meaning error, these sentences are useful for brain research. Researchers can compare the brain’s responses to the two errors to see whether grammar and meaning are handled differently.
24|The brain gives a fast response to a word of the wrong type, called the **early left anterior negativity** (**ELAN**). It is an **ERP** (event-related potential, the brain’s electrical activity recorded at the scalp, averaged over many trials and timed to something like a word appearing). It shows up for **word class violations** (a word of the wrong grammatical category, such as noun, verb or preposition). || Classic ELAN sentences have the wrong word type. “The pizza was in the eaten” has a participle where a noun should be. “The scientist criticized Max’s of proof the theorem” has the preposition **of** where a noun should come after “Max’s”. || The ELAN has three features. It is **early**, about **100 to 200 milliseconds** after the word. It is **left and anterior**, strongest over the left front of the scalp. That location raises the question of whether it is specific to language. It shows up **specifically for word category violations**, such as a noun where a verb is expected or a preposition in place of a noun. || Wrong sentences give this early negative response at left frontal sites, and correct sentences do not. Since it comes so fast, it was taken as evidence that the brain checks word categories before it works out meaning.
25|With written words, the early response to a wrong word type comes from **visual cortex**. That is the part at the back of the brain, in the **occipital** lobe, that handles what we see. **Dikker, Rabagliati, Farmer and Pylkkänen (2010)** found this. Their paper is called “Early occipital sensitivity to syntactic category is based on form typicality”. || They compared “The beautiful princess …” with “The beautifully princess …”. Princess is a **bimorphemic noun** (a noun ending in a noun suffix). Beautiful is an adjective, so a noun is expected next. Beautifully is an adverb, so a verb or adjective is expected next. So princess is a surprise after beautifully. The visual response was **bigger** for that surprise at about 120 to 130 milliseconds. || This suggests the prediction **reaches down to the earliest parts of the visual system**. When you expect a verb, you also expect visual patterns that fit how verbs are written, such as typical endings. So the early response might come from a **mismatch** between that prediction and what actually shows up on the screen. || This raises a question. Does it work only for words **morphologically marked** (with a part, like a suffix, that signals the category) as nouns or verbs, or for **open-class** and **closed-class** words? Open-class words are content words that keep gaining members. Closed-class words are a small fixed set of function words such as prepositions. The effect shows up for nouns with a noun suffix and for typical-looking nouns, but not for neutral nouns like “infant”.
26|The same word can be a **noun** or a **verb**, and the rest of the sentence decides which. || In “Chris and Ben are glad that the bird perches seem easy to install”, perches is a noun (the bars birds sit on), and seem is the verb. In “Chris and Ben are glad that the bird perches comfortably in the cage”, perches is a verb (what the bird does). In “The teacher told the principal that the student needs were not being met”, needs is a noun (what the student requires). In “The teacher told the principal that the student needs to be more focused”, needs is a verb. || This is because the word form alone does not settle its role. The **continuation** (the words after the ambiguous word) settles it. || Words also differ in **phonological form typicality** (how much a word sounds like typical nouns or typical verbs). A corpus analysis measured this for **3,158 words**. Each word got a distance from all nouns and from all verbs in sound features. A small distance means it sounds like that group. Nouns mostly sound closer to nouns, and verbs closer to verbs. But some do not. Besides **noun-like nouns** and **verb-like verbs**, there are **verb-like nouns** and **noun-like verbs**. So a word can sound like one category while belonging to the other.
27|**“Perches” sounds noun-like** and **“needs” sounds verb-like**, so each word has one use that fits its sound and one that clashes. These are **form typicality** tendencies (learned patterns in how words sound). Both words can still be used either way. || For perches, “the bird perches seem easy to install” uses it as a noun, which **fits** its noun-like sound. “The bird perches comfortably in the cage” makes it a verb, which **goes against** its sound. || For needs, it is flipped. “The student needs to be more focused” uses it as a verb, which **fits** its verb-like sound. “The student needs were not being met” makes it a noun, which **goes against** its sound. || These four sentences make a fair test. This is because each word shows up once in a fitting use and once in a clashing use. So the test separates what the word’s sound suggests from what the sentence’s grammar requires. If sound patterns guide parsing, the clashing uses should be harder to read. Since all four sentences are grammatical, any extra difficulty would come from the sound pattern.
28|Reading slows down when the sentence needs the word type that does **not match** how the word sounds. || For **noun-like homonyms** such as perches, reading the word as a noun added only about **50 milliseconds**. Reading it as a verb added about **120 milliseconds**. So perches was harder to read as a verb. For **verb-like homonyms** such as needs, reading the word as a noun added about **110 milliseconds**. Reading it as a verb added almost nothing, under 10 milliseconds. So needs was harder to read as a noun. || The measure was reading time at **disambiguation** minus reading time at the **point of ambiguity**. Disambiguation is where the next words make the word type clear, such as “seem” or “comfortably”. The point of ambiguity is the ambiguous word itself. The times were adjusted for word length. A bigger increase means more difficulty. || This is because the ambiguous word stays the same in each pair, and only the rest of the sentence changes. So the extra cost comes from the rest of the sentence demanding the type that clashes with the word’s sound. Since that clash slows people down, parsing uses learned patterns about how words sound, along with the structure the sentence finally needs.
29|The early brain response to an unexpected noun only shows up when the word itself **physically looks like a noun**. This **ELAN effect** for nouns where a verb is expected needs one of two cues. One is **morphemes** (the smallest meaningful word parts, such as a noun suffix) that mark the word as a noun. The other is **“nouniness”** (how much the word’s form looks like typical nouns, even without a suffix). || The response measured was the **M100** (a brain response that peaks about 100 milliseconds after a word appears). Three kinds of nouns were tested. For **bimorphemic nouns**, “The beautifully princess …” gave a significantly bigger response than “The beautiful princess …”. Princess has a noun suffix. For **typical nouns**, “The tastelessly soda …” gave a significantly bigger response than “The tasteless soda …”. Soda has a typical noun form. For **neutral nouns**, “The cutely infant …” and “The cute infant …” did not differ. || The neutral nouns show **no effect** because infant has no strong cue. It has no noun suffix and no typical noun form. The brain predicts what the next word should look like. The early response only happens when the word carries physical evidence against that prediction. Since infant has no such evidence, a category mismatch alone is not enough.
30|Sentence processing can be summed up in **four sentences**, each with one idea. || “Colorless green ideas sleep furiously” means **syntax is independent of semantics** to some extent. This is because a sentence can be grammatically correct but meaningless. The words fill a grammatical structure without describing a sensible event. || “The horse raced past the barn fell” means syntactic structure is often highly **ambiguous** (actually **vague**), but we only notice when things fail. Reading times for “preferred” and “unpreferred” structures suggest that parsing **takes shortcuts to save memory**. It does not build every possible parse. So when a shortcut picks the wrong structure, the reader goes down the garden path. || “Put the apple on the towel in the box” means **context, pragmatics and other factors** affect parsing from very early on. With two apples in view, listeners hear “on the towel” as telling which apple, not where to put it. || “The bird perches were comfortable” means the very earliest syntactic effects in the brain come partly from **form typicality**. They do not necessarily come from **morphosyntactic** structure (grammar signaled by word parts and word order). They seem tied to **predictions about how words physically look**. Perches has a noun-like form, so using it as a noun here fits what its form predicts.
