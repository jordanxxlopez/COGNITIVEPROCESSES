2|Listeners get one clear message out of a speech signal that is messy and that changes from speaker to speaker. This is because **speech perception** (getting a language message out of the sound of speech) has to undo two problems. || The first problem is that the sounds run together. In the signal for “I can see you,” the energy of each sound runs into the next one. Nothing in the signal cleanly marks where letters or even words begin and end. Real-time MRI of the **vocal tract** (the passage from the throat to the lips) from USC SPAN explains why. The tongue, lips and other articulators keep moving without a pause. The movement for one sound overlaps the movement for the next, so their sounds overlap too. || The second problem is that one word sounds different from each speaker. People have different voices and different vocal tract sizes, so the same word comes out with different acoustic properties. Across **76 speakers**, each of ten vowels covers a wide range of first and second formant values. These ranges **overlap** with each other. So one pair of formant values can be one vowel for one speaker and a different vowel for another speaker. || Since the signal varies so much, recognizing speech means finding a **stable linguistic identity** inside all that physical variation.
3|Listeners have to turn a changing, **analog signal** (one that varies continuously) into something more abstract, so the language system can work with it. This is because the raw signal can shift in frequency, loudness and timing by tiny amounts. It has no built-in markers of where one speech sound ends and the next starts. Many small changes still leave the speech sound the same. || The abstract form is a **symbolic representation** (a label for a language unit that ignores the fine physical detail). The unit is the **phoneme** (a sound category that tells words apart in a language). The /b/ in “bat” and the /p/ in “pat” are different phonemes, because swapping them changes the word. Many slightly different acoustic versions of /b/ still all count as one /b/. || **Categorical perception** is thought to reflect this **“translation” from physical to symbolic**. Listeners are worse at hearing differences inside one category. They are better at hearing differences of the same size that cross from one category to another. So a change is easy to notice when it changes which phoneme the listener hears. The same size of change is easy to miss when both sounds stay the same phoneme. || Categorical perception is only one idea about how this translation happens, and there are other ideas.
4|The shape of the vocal tract decides which frequencies get made louder, and those louder frequencies are the formants. This is because, following **Ladefoged (1996)**, the shape of the **vocal tract** is tied in a regular way to **which resonant frequencies get amplified**. The vocal tract is the passage from the larynx through the throat and mouth to the lips. || A **resonant frequency** is a frequency that a space naturally makes stronger. A tube of one shape boosts some frequencies and weakens others. The tongue, jaw and lips change the shape of the vocal tract. So moving them changes which frequencies get the biggest boost. These boosted frequencies are the **formants**, and each vowel has its own set. || For **heed, hid, head and had**, the tongue goes from high and front to low. In heed, the second formant is far above the first. Through hid, head and had, the two formants get closer together. || For **hod, hawed, hood and who’d**, the tongue moves back and the lips get more rounded. The boosted frequencies gather at the low end, so the first and second formants sit close together. || A vowel is defined by the **relationship among its resonances**, which stays similar across speakers even when the exact frequencies differ. || A **formant transition** (a formant’s frequency changing over time) happens when the mouth moves from a consonant into a vowel. The steady part of a vowel tells you about the vowel. The moving part tells you how the neighboring sound is made.
5|Vowels look different on a spectrogram mainly because their two lowest formants are spaced differently. This is because a **spectrogram** (a display of sound energy over time and frequency) shows each vowel as several bands of strong energy. These bands are the vowel’s **formants**. **F1** is the lowest formant and **F2** is the next one up. Both run through the whole vowel at the same time. || The words **bead, bid, bade, bed, bad, bod, baud, bode, “bood” and booed** come from Rob Hagiwara’s English vowel recordings. They all start with /b/ and end with /d/. Since the consonants are the same, the differences between the words come mainly from the vowels. || In **bead**, F1 is very low and F2 is very high, above 2,000 Hz. So the two bands are far apart. || Going through **bid, bade, bed and bad**, F1 goes up and F2 comes down. So the two bands get closer together. || In the back rounded vowels **bode, “bood” and booed**, F2 drops low, near 1,000 Hz or below. So F2 ends up sitting near F1. || Bead has a low F1 with a high F2, while booed has a low F1 with a low F2. Since F1 and F2 change this way from vowel to vowel, these two formants carry most of what tells vowels apart.
6|A speech sound can be split into a source and a filter. This recording of /u/ has a low first and a low second formant. This is because **source-filter synthesis** builds speech sounds from two parts. The **source** is the sound energy the voice makes. The **filter** is how the vocal tract shapes that energy. Splitting them lets you change one part while keeping the other the same. || The work is done in **Praat** (free speech analysis software from praat.org). Praat can show recordings, measure formants and rebuild sounds. || The recording is a speaker saying **/u/**, the vowel in “booed.” The **waveform** (a record of air pressure changing over time) shows timing and loudness. The **spectrogram** splits the same sound into its frequencies, so it shows the resonance pattern. || In this /u/, the **first formant is at about 300 Hz**. The **second formant is very low, around 900 Hz**. Both run through the whole vowel. A low F1 together with a low F2 fits the back rounded sound of /u/. || If only the filter is changed and the vowel changes, then the vowel’s identity must come from the filter. Since the filter is the pattern of formants, these measured formants become the starting point for making new filters.
7|Praat finds five formants in the /u/, but only the first two are changed in this example. This is because **Praat can find formants automatically**. It follows each resonance through the recording and marks its frequency at each moment. In this /u/, it finds **five formants** running at the same time. Nobody has to guess the frequencies by eye. || **Only the first three formants are usually important for phonemic processing** (telling phonemes apart). The numbers give the order from low to high. F1 is the lowest, F2 is next and F3 is third. F1, F2 and F3 carry most of the information that separates phonemes. The higher formants depend more on the particular person’s voice. || The example only changes **the first two formants**, which sit near 300 Hz and 900 Hz in this /u/. These two formants carry most of the vowel information. So changing just these two is enough to turn one vowel into another. || F3 and the higher formants are kept the same. Since nothing else changes, any change listeners hear has to come from F1 or F2.
8|Taking the filter out of the recorded /u/ leaves the **source**, and the source sounds like a buzz. This is because Praat builds a model of the filter from the measured formant frequencies. It then uses that model to remove the filter’s effect from the recording. What is left is a **more-or-less “unfiltered” version of the voice**, the sound energy the vocal folds made. It is only “more or less” unfiltered, because the split is an estimate. || The buzz still has energy and pitch, but it no longer sounds like /u/. Its energy is spread across many frequencies, without the strong low formants of /u/. The pitch stays, because pitch comes from the vocal folds vibrating. The vowel sound goes away, because the vowel sound comes from the filter. || The split is **a little bit like doing a multiple regression analysis**. In multiple regression, you estimate how much one predictor contributes and take it out, so you can look at what is left. Here the filter’s contribution is estimated and taken out, and what is left is treated as the source. || Since the source is now on its own, new filters can be put on it. Keeping the same source while swapping filters lets you test what the resonance pattern does.
9|Three different filters are put on the same source, and each one changes only one part of the resonance pattern. This is because a **filter** says which frequencies get more boost and which get less, so each filter is a resonance pattern. || The **first filter** is just the first four formants from the original /u/ recording. So putting it on the source should bring back the original /u/. || The **second filter** raises **the first formant a little**. The other formants stay about where they were. || The **third filter** raises **the second formant a lot**. F1 and the higher formants stay about where they were. || In the original filter, F1 and F2 are both low, which fits /u/. Raising F1 moves only the lowest resonance up. Raising F2 moves only the second resonance up, by a much bigger amount. So the filters do not shift every frequency together. || The **sound source stays the same** for all three filters, and no new speaker or recording is needed. Since only the filter changes, any change in the vowel sound comes from the filter. Comparing the three results shows what changing the lowest resonance does and what changing the second resonance does.
10|Putting one source through three different filters makes three different vowels. This is because **multiplying the source by the filters gives new vowels**. The output is the source shaped by the filter. The source gives the raw sound energy. The filter decides how much of that energy stays strong in each frequency range. Each filter boosts a different set of frequencies, so each one makes a different vowel. || With the **original filter**, F1 and F2 stay low. So the result still sounds like /u/. || With **F1 raised a little**, the lowest resonance goes up. So the vowel changes. || With **F2 raised a lot**, the second resonance goes up far. So the vowel changes in a different way, and the back rounded /u/ moves toward a more front vowel. || **Source-filter synthesis** pulls apart two things that normal speech combines. The voice makes the sound, and the vocal tract resonances shape what vowel it is. All three outputs have the same buzz, pitch and timing, yet they sound like different vowels. Since only the filter changed, a researcher can change the vowel just by swapping the filter. Nobody has to record each version.
11|Formant values change from speaker to speaker, but they still describe a careful talker’s vowels pretty well. This is because the vowel a speaker means to say only **partly** decides the formant values. Three other things change them too. || **Anatomical features** differ between speakers of the same language. Vocal tracts differ in length and shape, and a longer one gives lower resonances overall. || **Different rates of speech** matter. Fast speech gives the tongue and lips less time to reach their targets. || **Context** matters, meaning the **phones** (particular spoken versions of speech sounds) made before and after the vowel. The tongue and lips are already heading to the next sound or coming from the last one. || So across 76 speakers, each of ten vowels covers a wide range of formant values, and the ranges overlap. The same F1 and F2 values can be different vowels for different speakers. || Even so, formant values give a pretty good description of **vowel space** (how a language’s vowels are laid out by their formant frequencies). This works best for **one talker speaking carefully**, because that talker’s vowels separate much more cleanly. So, all else being equal, you can make **reasonable approximations of vowels** by changing **steady-state formants** (the resonances in the stable middle of a vowel).
12|The **pattern playback synthesizer** turns a painted spectrogram back into sound. This is because researchers paint bands on a clear sheet wherever they want sound energy in time and frequency. The machine then plays that painted pattern out loud. So changing the painting changes the sound. || The machine works by shining light through the painted pattern. A light source shines through a **cylindrical lens** onto a spinning **tone wheel**. The rings on the tone wheel make the light flicker at many frequencies at once. A lens and a **mirror** send that light onto the painted **spectrogram**. The painted parts let the matching frequencies through. A **light collector** and a **photocell** turn the light into an electrical signal. An **amplifier** and a **loudspeaker** turn that signal into sound. || This let researchers make controlled, speech-like sounds instead of using natural recordings. Natural recordings differ in many ways at once that nobody controls. With the machine, a researcher can change just one formant band or one transition and hear the result right away. Everything else in the pattern stays exactly the same. || Since only one thing changed, any change in what listeners hear comes from that one painted change. That is how series of patterns that differed in tiny steps showed how formant transitions signal consonants.
13|Stop consonants are told apart by their **formant transitions** (the way their formants glide over time). This is because **/b/, /d/ and /g/** are made by closing the vocal tract for a moment and then opening it. The part of a /b/, /d/ or /g/ that you can hear happens **while the jaw is opening**. During that opening, the shape of the vocal tract changes fast. So the formants glide from the consonant’s position to the vowel’s position. Since the consonant is heard during this glide, its identity is in how the formants move. || **Liberman and colleagues (1957)** used the **pattern playback synthesizer** to make speech-like sounds that copied these formant transitions. Each painted pattern had two formants. The **lower formant** was nearly the same in every stimulus. The **upper formant** started at a different frequency in each stimulus. It then settled at the same steady vowel level. || In some stimuli, the upper formant started low and **rose** into the vowel, and listeners heard /b/. In some, it was nearly **flat**, and listeners heard /d/. In others, it started high and **fell** into the vowel, and listeners heard /g/. || The fourteen patterns kept the vowel the same and changed only where the upper transition started. Since only the transition differed, what listeners heard could be tied directly to the transition.
14|The speech continuum changes one thing in equal steps, so every neighboring pair of sounds differs by the same physical amount. This is because a **speech continuum** (a series of sounds that change step by step along one property) was built by **keeping F1 constant**. Only the **F2 onset frequency** (the frequency where the second formant starts its glide) was changed. It was **varied linearly** from stimulus 1 to stimulus 13, meaning it moved by the same step every time. So **adjacent stimuli differ by the same amount across the entire continuum**. || Along the series, the F2 glide slowly changes. At the start, F2 begins well below the vowel frequency, so it rises. As the starting point goes up, the rise gets smaller until the glide is nearly flat. Later, F2 starts above the vowel frequency, so it falls. || Equal steps make a fair test of whether hearing follows the sound or the categories. Some neighboring pairs fall inside one consonant category. Other pairs cross a boundary between two categories. A listener who only tracked the sound would hear every step as the same size. A listener with categorical perception would hear steps inside a category as alike and steps across a boundary as different. Since all steps are physically equal, any advantage for boundary pairs has to come from the boundary.
15|If perception were not categorical, the labels would change slowly and smoothly from /b/ to /d/ to /g/. This is because a simple **ideal observer model** assumes perception is **non-categorical**. An ideal observer model is a prediction of how a listener would respond under one idea of how perception works. That means hearing follows the sound gradually, with no sharp category boundaries. The model is a baseline, so real data can be compared with it. || The first prediction is that **categorization gets “blurry” as you move away from category prototypes**. A **prototype** is the clearest example of a category. The ends of the continuum are /b/ and /g/, with /d/ in the middle. Between these points, **stimulus ambiguity should scale with the distance from the prototype**. So the farther a sound is from the /b/ prototype, the less it sounds like /b/ and the more it sounds like /d/. || **Stimulus 2** would be labeled **75% /b/ and 25% /d/**. This is because it sits a quarter of the way from the /b/ prototype toward the /d/ prototype. || Under this idea, **labeling** changes smoothly. The /b/ labels go down in a straight slope to 0 at stimulus 6, where /d/ reaches its peak. The /d/ labels then go down to 0 at stimulus 10, where /g/ reaches the top. Halfway between two prototypes, the two labels are equally likely. No label ever jumps suddenly.
16|If perception were linear, people would be equally good at telling apart every pair, no matter how they label the sounds. This is because a **linear** ideal observer hears equal sound differences as equally big. So it predicts that **discrimination performance should be independent of labeling**. || **Labeling** asks a listener to name a sound as /b/, /d/ or /g/. **Discrimination** only asks whether two sounds are the **same or different**. These are two separate tasks, even when they use the same sounds. || Labeling **has to have a “peaked” function**. This is because there are three categories and listeners have to pick between labels. So each category’s labels go up toward its prototype and go down away from it. These peaks come from how the labeling task is set up. || Discrimination works in a different way. The pairs are two steps apart, from **1 and 3** through **9 and 11**, so every pair has the same physical gap. If people heard F2 onset differences equally well across the whole continuum, accuracy would be the same for every pair. So the prediction is a flat line, at about 0.8. || A **flat** result would mean category boundaries do not affect hearing differences. A **peaked** result would mean the boundaries make some differences easier to hear.
17|**Liberman and colleagues (1957)** found that labels switch suddenly and that people are best at telling sounds apart right at the switch. This pattern is **categorical perception**, and it is very different from the linear prediction. Listeners labeled each sound on the /b/, /d/ and /g/ continuum. They also judged pairs that were **one step**, **two steps** or **three steps** apart. || The labels switched **sharply**. Stimuli 1 through about 3 were labeled /b/ almost 100% of the time. Around stimuli 4 and 5, /b/ dropped steeply and /d/ climbed to nearly 100%. /d/ stayed on top through about stimulus 8. Around stimuli 9 and 10, /d/ dropped steeply and /g/ rose to near 100%. The narrow spot where the label flips is the **category boundary**. || **Listeners were best at discriminating sounds near the category boundaries**. Accuracy peaked at the /b/ to /d/ boundary and again at the /d/ to /g/ boundary. Pairs inside one category were close to **chance** (the score you get by guessing). Bigger steps raised accuracy overall, but the peaks stayed at the boundaries. || Liberman and colleagues also predicted discrimination from the labels, assuming people only hear a difference when they give two different labels. This prediction matched much of the real pattern. Real accuracy was often a little higher, especially inside categories. So listeners still hear some sound differences beyond the labels.

19|Each listener is best at telling sounds apart near the point where that listener’s own label switches. This is because students in prior years were tested on a **beach to peach continuum**, where the words differ only in /b/ or /p/. In **identification**, each listener labeled every sound from 1 to 9. In **discrimination**, each listener judged pairs two steps apart, from 1 and 3 through 7 and 9. || The first listener says one word for the early sounds and then **switches to the other word** around the middle. Answers are mixed only in a small area near the switch. So the sound changes slowly, but the label changes in a narrow spot. || That first listener is best at the pair 4 and 6 and the pair 5 and 7. These pairs cross that listener’s boundary. Pairs well inside one category are hard to tell apart, even though the physical gap is the same. The pair 6 and 8 falls in between, because stimulus 6 itself got mixed labels. || The second listener **switches later** along the continuum. That listener is best at telling sounds apart near that later switch. || Since the two switches are in different places, different people can have their boundaries at different points. For each person, the best discrimination still lands near that person’s **own identification boundary**.
20|Not everyone has the same category boundary, but people are generally best at telling apart sounds they hear as different categories. This is because a sound near the beach to peach boundary for one listener can be well inside a category for another listener. So a pair that crosses one person’s boundary may stay inside one category for someone else. When people’s boundaries differ, averaging the group can flatten the peak at the boundary. || **In general, discrimination is best for stimuli perceived as being in different categories**. Listeners can miss a physical difference between two versions of the same phoneme. The same size of difference is much easier to hear when the two sounds are heard as different words. || The discrimination test had **only “different” trials**, so the lab could be done in a short time. Every pair had two sounds that really were physically different. So the correct answer on every trial was **“different,”** if the listener could hear the difference. Saying “same” meant missing a real difference. || Accuracy here is how often a listener heard the pair as different. Someone who always said “different” would get a perfect score. Since every pair really differed, low accuracy on pairs inside one category means listeners often missed differences that were really there.
21|The data from prior years bounce around because real experiments are noisy and this one had weak spots. This is because of the one iron-clad law of experimental research, which is that **stuff happens**. Even with a carefully built continuum, answers can jump up and down. || For the **bear to pear** continuum, the labels switch fairly clearly from one word to the other. But discrimination jumps between 0 and 1 from one pair to the next. It even misses a pair that crosses the boundary. || Another listener’s **beach to peach** data bounce even more. The labels zigzag instead of switching once. Discrimination jumps between high and low from pair to pair. || Identification (which word a listener picks) and discrimination (whether a difference is heard) can vary in different ways across trials and people. So a single spike or dip in discrimination reflects noise in a few answers. || The experiment was not the best in two ways, and it could be done differently. Only “different” trials were used, so nothing measured how often people said “different” when the sounds were identical. The short session gave each listener **few trials per pair**, so one answer could move accuracy a lot. A better version would add trials with identical sounds and more trials for each pair. Then you could check whether better discrimination lines up with where the labels change across the whole continuum.
22|Being worse at hearing some differences helps because it keeps word recognition stable. **Categorical perception** makes a listener worse at hearing some differences between sounds, and that looks like a weakness because the listener throws away sound detail. || It helps because every time someone says a phoneme, it comes out physically a little different. Speakers, speaking rates and neighboring sounds all change it. If every small change were heard as a new sound, everyday versions of one word would sound like unrelated sounds. Being less sensitive inside a category lets all those versions count as **one phoneme**. || Differences **between categories** stay easy to hear. So the differences that change one word into another are kept. Differences inside a category are ignored, because they do not change the word. || Since the listener ignores the small differences that do not matter, the listener can focus on the differences that change meaning. That is the advantage of being worse at hearing some differences.

24|Researchers test **categorical perception in infants** by watching a behavior that changes when the infant notices something new. This is because infants cannot name sounds, so they cannot do a labeling task. But infants react to new things, and that reaction can be measured. The experimenter controls exactly which sound difference the infant hears. || The **conditioned head turn procedure** comes from Janet Werker’s infant studies lab. An infant learns to turn toward a sound when the sound changes. A correct turn gets an interesting visual reward, such as a moving toy. Once the infant learns this, a head turn after a change means the infant noticed the change. || The results that matter here come from **high-amplitude sucking**. The infant sucks on a pacifier hooked to a recording device, and strong sucks make a sound play. When the same sound keeps repeating, the infant gets used to it and sucks less. Getting used to a repeated sound is **habituation**. If a new sound makes the infant suck **more again**, the infant noticed the change. || Since these methods need no labels or words, they work for infants who are still months away from talking.
25|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** found that **4-month-old infants** notice a voicing change only when it crosses the adult category boundary. We know this because they measured **high-amplitude sucking** to sounds that differed in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**. VOT is the delay between releasing a consonant and the vocal folds starting to vibrate. Adults hear a short VOT as a voiced consonant like /b/. Adults hear a longer VOT as a voiceless consonant like /p/. With a VOT of +100 milliseconds, voicing starts much later than with +10 milliseconds. || Infants heard one synthetic syllable again and again until their sucking slowed down. Then the sound changed or stayed the same. In group **20D**, the VOT changed from **+20 to +40 milliseconds**, which crosses the adult boundary between two consonants. In group **20S**, the VOT also changed by **20 milliseconds**, from −20 to 0 or from +60 to +80. Both of those sounds are in the **same** adult category. In group **0**, the control group kept hearing the same sound. || In every group, sucking went up at first and then down as the sound got familiar. In 20D, sucking **jumped back up** after the change. In 20S, sucking kept going down with little recovery. In 0, sucking kept going down steadily. || Both changes were the same 20 milliseconds. So the only reason 20D was different is that its change crossed the boundary. Since infants noticed only the crossing change, they already hear voicing **categorically** at four months.
26|English-learning infants lose the ability to hear some non-English sound differences during their first year. This is called **perceptual narrowing** (experience tuning hearing to the sounds of the language around you). Along the way, infants get worse at some differences from other languages. || **Werker and Tees (1984)** tested two **non-English contrasts**. The **Hindi contrast** is two “ta”-like sounds that English does not tell apart. The **Salish contrast** is “ki” versus “qi” in Nthlakampx, a Salish language, which English also does not use. They measured the percentage of infants who reached a **discrimination criterion** at each age. || English-learning infants got worse with age. At **6 to 8 months**, about 95% passed for Hindi and about 80% for Salish. At **8 to 10 months**, about 67% passed for Hindi and about 52% for Salish. At **10 to 12 months**, only about 20% passed for Hindi and about 10% for Salish. || **Hindi-learning and Salish-learning infants** passed close to 100% of the time for the contrast in their own language. So the drop does not happen just from getting older. It depends on which language the infant hears. || This is described as **perceptual reorganization during the first year of life**. Young infants can hear these nonnative differences. Since English never uses them, English-learning infants mostly stop hearing them by about their first birthday.
27|The “very compelling narrative” says categorical perception is a special language ability that babies have early and then tune to their own language. This is because the earlier findings seemed to support three claims. || The first claim is that speech perception depends on **categorical perception abilities that are a special property of the language system**. So the machinery that sorts sounds into categories would be built just for language. || The second claim is that these abilities are **present very early in development**. The infant sucking and head-turn studies suggest they are there long before children talk. || The third claim is that children **start off as “citizens of the world,”** able to hear **all** contrasts. They then **rapidly tune their perceptual systems to their specific language**. “Citizens of the world” means a baby could learn any human language and is ready to hear the differences in every one. Tuning means the differences in the baby’s own language stay, while differences from other languages fade, as in the Werker and Tees results. || So the narrative explains both where babies start (the **starting state**) and how they change (**developmental change**). It says babies start with broad language abilities and mostly narrow down from there. Each early result seems to fit this story.
28|The compelling narrative has **some issues**, and they weaken the idea that categorical perception is special to language and starts out universal. This is because each issue goes against one part of the narrative. || Categorical perception is **not limited to language**. It also happens with sounds that are not speech. So it does not look special to language. || Categorical perception is **not limited to humans**. Animals also hear speech sounds categorically. So it does not look special to humans. || Categorical perception is **task-dependent**. How categorical the results look depends on what the listener is asked to do. Picking between labels makes answers look more categorical than a task that measures small sound differences. || **Infant speech perception** is **not actually “universal.”** Some contrasts are harder for infants to hear than others right from the start. So development includes differences infants start with, not just changes from experience. || Since all four issues hold, the strong claim fails. That claim says every speech difference starts out equally easy to hear in a system built only for language.
29|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** found categorical perception for sounds that are not speech. This is because they used **non-speech sounds** that differed in **rise time** (how fast a sound reaches full loudness after it starts). A **rapid rise time** sounds **plucked**, like a plucked string. A **slow rise time** sounds **bowed**, like a bowed string. Pluck and bow are names for two kinds of sound starts, not phonemes. || The sounds included **sawtooth waves**, **sine waves** and **speech syllables**, made with short and long rise times such as 10 and 70 milliseconds. With a 10-millisecond rise, the sound is at full strength almost right away. With a 70-millisecond rise, the sound builds up slowly. || For the sawtooth waves, short rise times were called pluck almost 100% of the time. Long rise times were called bow almost 100% of the time. The label switched steeply around 30 to 50 milliseconds. || For telling apart pairs 10 milliseconds apart, accuracy stayed around 55 to 60% for most pairs, just a bit above chance. Accuracy **peaked near 75%** for pairs around 30 to 50 milliseconds, right where the label switched. || Since this happened for plucked and bowed sounds, the link between a label switch and better discrimination does not need language at all.
30|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** found that infants also hear non-speech sounds categorically. This is because they used **high-amplitude sucking** with **infants** and **non-speech sounds**. Infants heard one sound over and over until their sucking slowed down. Then the sound was shifted in one of three ways. || With **no change**, sucking stayed low. || With a change **within categories**, sucking also stayed low. So the infants showed no sign of noticing that change. || With a change **between categories**, sucking jumped back up. It then went down again as the new sound got familiar. So the infants noticed that change. || This is the same pattern **Eimas and colleagues (1971)** found with speech sounds. But infants cannot be using what they know about words or phonemes when they hear plucked and bowed sounds. So their categorical response has to come from more general hearing. Since general hearing works this way, the same process could also explain their categorical response to speech.
31|**Chinchillas** hear speech sounds categorically, just like humans. **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** found this, following the earlier work of **Kuhl and Miller (1975)**. The animals were trained with **operant conditioning** (learning where rewards change how often a behavior happens). Each chinchilla learned that either the **0-millisecond lag** sound or the **80-millisecond lag** sound was an **SD** for food. **SD** stands for **discriminative stimulus** (a signal that a response will be rewarded). The lag is **voice onset time**. Humans hear a 0-millisecond lag as /ga/ and an 80-millisecond lag as /ka/. || After training, chinchillas and humans were tested on the VOTs in between, along the **/ga/ to /ka/** continuum. Short VOTs were called /ga/ almost every time. Long VOTs were almost never called /ga/. The switch happened in a narrow range, mostly between about 30 and 70 milliseconds. The chinchilla curves had the same steep shape as the human curves. || The study also changed the **burst** (the short noise when a stop consonant is released). It also changed the **aspiration noise** (the breathy noise after a voiceless release). Making them louder moved the boundary to shorter VOTs, made the switch steeper, or both. This happened for one chinchilla and one human, so both species reacted in a similar way. || Since animals without human language sort speech sounds into categories, this argues against the view that categorical perception is special to human language.
32|Some sound differences are hard for infants to hear at first, even ones in their own language, and experience makes them easier. This is because some contrasts are **less salient** (less easy to notice) than others. The contrasts here differ in **place of articulation** (where in the mouth a sound is made). In **[ma]**, the nasal is made with the lips. In **[na]**, it is made with the tongue near the ridge behind the teeth. In **[ŋa]**, it is made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate, like the sound at the end of “sing.” || Researchers measured **looking time** on **same** trials, where the syllable stayed the same, and **change** trials, where it switched. Longer looking on change trials means the infant noticed the change. || English-hearing infants at **4 to 5 months** looked longer on change trials for the native **[ma] and [na]** contrast, about 5.4 seconds against 3.3 seconds. For **[na] and [ŋa]**, they looked about the same, about 4.2 seconds against 4.5 seconds. So they did not hear that change, and English never starts a syllable with [ŋ]. || **Filipino-learning infants** have [na] and [ŋa] in their own language. At **6 to 8 months**, they looked about 3.8 seconds on both kinds of trials, so they did not hear the change yet. By **10 to 12 months**, they looked about 4.8 seconds on change trials and about 3.3 seconds on same trials. || Since hearing the language made this contrast easier, development includes **gains** as well as losses.
