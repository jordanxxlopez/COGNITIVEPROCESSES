2|Babies are overrated as language learners, so the idea of **sensitive periods** for a second language (**L2**) should not be taken too far. This is because toddlers actually learn words slowly. By **18 months**, a typical child uses at least **20 words**. An adult who knew only about 20 words after 18 months of classes would be a very poor learner. || A typical 18-month-old also does several other things. >> The child understands "in and out" and "off and on" and points to body parts when asked. >> The child answers "Where's teddy?" and "What's that?" with words or gestures and pretends to give teddy a drink. >> The child makes at least four consonant sounds, such as b, n, d, g, w and h. The child enjoys being read to and points to pictures with one finger. || **Adults learn faster at the start** than children do. This is because adults have two advantages. The first is **transfer from L1** (using what they already know from their first language). The second is **better general cognitive skills**, such as memory, attention and reasoning. || Adults do worse on **ultimate attainment** (the final level of skill a learner ends up with). This is because adults rarely end up sounding native-like. That may also partly come from transfer. Since early speed and final level are different things, children and adults can each come out ahead on one of them.
3|Adult learners already have many of the ideas that toddlers are still figuring out. This is because some toddler **language milestones** are really **general conceptual abilities**. Understanding "in and out" and "off and on" depends on understanding **spatial relations between objects** (where things are relative to each other). **Pretend play**, like giving teddy a drink, depends on **event representations** (mental models of who does what in an event). || So a later learner has a smaller job. This is because an adult learning Spanish already has the ideas behind "in" and "on". The adult only has to learn how the **target language** (the language being learned) expresses those ideas. A toddler has to build the ideas and the words at the same time. || Later learners also already know another language, and that changes how they learn the new one. This is because the first language gives ready-made ways to say things. Some of those ways fit the new language, so they help. Other ways clash with it, so they lead to mistakes.
4|**Transfer** is using your first language (**L1**) processes and representations for a second language (**L2**). This is because a learner already has a full working language system. So the learner carries old habits for hearing sounds, building sentences and finding words into the L2. || Transfer can help or hurt. >> **Positive transfer** happens when an L1 pattern matches the L2. Then the learner gets a head start. >> **Negative transfer** happens when an L1 pattern clashes with the L2. Then the learner makes mistakes where the two languages differ. || A cartoon gives an example of negative transfer in grammar. A man in a pub asks "Do you not be happy with me as the translator of the books of you?" This is because he is translating word by word. The words are English, but the word order and phrasing come from another language. Since every first-language habit comes along, it helps where languages match and hurts where they differ.
5|English speakers have trouble learning a Hindi sound difference because English lumps the two sounds together. This is because of **categorical perception**, which leads to **acquired similarity** (sounds in one category come to sound more alike with experience). || **Minimal pairs in Hindi** (two words that differ in only one sound) make this clear. >> Two words both sound like "tap" to an English speaker. One means hot, and the other means a kind of beat. >> Two words both sound like "dab". One means pressure, and the other means a kind of fruit. >> Two words both sound like "ari". One means saw (the tool), and the other means crooked. >> In each pair, one word has a **dental** consonant (tongue against the teeth). The other has a **retroflex** consonant (tongue curled back toward the roof of the mouth). || Native English speakers use their **alveolar** category for both sounds. An alveolar consonant, like English /t/ or /d/, is made with the tongue on the ridge behind the teeth. This is because English only has the alveolar category. So both Hindi sounds fall into it and seem the same. || So here **transfer makes learning harder**. The English category works fine for English. But it hides a difference that Hindi uses to tell words apart. Since the first language taught learners to treat these sounds as one, they must now learn to pull them apart.
6|English speakers learning Chinese have a hard time with **classifiers**. This is because English never makes them pay attention to what kind of thing a noun names. A **classifier** is a word that goes between a number or demonstrative and a noun. Which classifier you use depends on the kind of thing the noun names. Classifiers are a somewhat unusual feature across languages. || The order is demonstrative pronoun, then classifier, then noun. In **yí gè rén** ("one person"), gè is the classifier. Each classifier goes with one kind of noun. >> **Gè** is the general classifier. **Bǎ** is for objects with a handle. **Běn** is for books and similar objects. >> **Kuài** is for objects divided into pieces. **Liàng** is for wheeled vehicles. **Tái** is for devices and gadgets. >> **Xiē** is for an indeterminate amount, or for the plural of pronouns and demonstratives. >> **Zhāng** is for flat objects. **Zhī** is for long, thin objects. || In English, saying "a chair", "that dog", "the car" or "one book" needs no thought about noun meaning. In Chinese, different nouns need different classifiers. So English speakers have learned to ignore the cues that classifiers depend on. Since they learned to ignore shape and function, they now have to start noticing them again.
7|**Grammatical gender** causes transfer problems for English speakers. This is because English nouns have no gender, so English speakers have no habit of tracking it. Grammatical gender sorts nouns into classes such as masculine and feminine. It is different from social or cultural gender. || There is nothing "male" about a book or "female" about liberty. Still, French **le livre** (the book) is masculine, and **la liberté** (liberty) is feminine. >> Other masculine nouns are le cheval (the horse), le chien (the dog) and le bruit (the noise). >> Other feminine nouns are la colombe (the dove), la chemise (the shirt) and la maison (the house). || Gender matters because of **agreement** (related words have to match the noun). Pronouns, adjectives and some verb forms all have to match the noun's gender. || Gender has to be learned word by word. This is because French has **synonyms with different genders**. >> **Auto** and **voiture** both mean car, and one is masculine while the other is feminine. >> **Rivière** (feminine) and **fleuve** (masculine) both mean river. >> **Vélo** (masculine) and **bicyclette** (feminine) both mean bicycle. || Word endings give hints. >> Masculine nouns often end in -age (le fromage), -r (le professeur), -t (le chat) or -isme (le capitalisme). >> Feminine nouns often end in -ie (la boulangerie), -ion (la nation), -ité or -tié (la fraternité) or -ance (la balance). >> Feminine nouns also often end in -nne, -mme or -lle (la fille, l’indienne).
8|Transfer is not all bad, because similar words make vocabulary easier to learn. This is because the learner already knows a word that looks and means about the same. >> French **auto** looks like English "auto" and means a car. >> French **rivière** looks like "river", and French **bicyclette** looks like "bicycle". >> Japanese **コロッケ (korokke)** means croquette, and **モノレール (monoreru)** means monorail. || Words can be similar for two reasons. >> One reason is **shared etymology** (a shared word history). Words with a shared origin are called **cognates**, like rivière and river. >> The other reason is more recent **borrowing**. A **loan word** is a word taken from another language. Japanese korokke and monoreru came from European languages, with their sounds changed to fit Japanese. || Both kinds give **positive transfer** for vocabulary. Someone who knows river can link it to rivière right away. Since the new word hooks onto a familiar one, it is easier to store and remember.
9|Not all similar-sounding words are cognates. This is because some words sound alike but mean different things. Those are called **false friends** (words that sound like a word in another language but mean something else). || "Arnold está embarazado" does not mean Arnold is embarrassed. Spanish **embarazado** means **pregnant**. An English speaker hears embarrassed in it. So the speaker gets the meaning badly wrong, and the result is embarrassing. || The mistake happens because the pull to link L2 words to similar L1 words is very strong. The link forms before the learner checks the meaning. >> When the meanings match, as with cognates, the link speeds up learning. >> When the meanings differ, the same link causes mistakes. || Since the same link drives both, a helpful cognate and a false friend differ only in whether the meanings really match.
10|**De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** tested in the lab whether cognates help people learn new second-language words. They used **paired-associate learning** (studying pairs, then recalling one when given the other) with made-up words. || Participants were Dutch speakers. So Dutch played the first language, and the made-up words played a new second language. Participants were simply shown each Dutch word with its made-up partner and asked to learn the pairs. Then they were tested **a week later** on **retention** (how well they still remembered). || The made-up words differed in two ways. >> Some were similar to their Dutch partner (cognate) and some were not (non-cognate). >> Some were **concrete** (something you can see or touch) and some were **abstract** (an idea). || Mixing the two gave four kinds of pairs. >> Gitaar and gettar ("guitar") is concrete and cognate. >> Tijger and duunze ("tiger") is concrete and non-cognate. >> Domein and domaan ("domain") is abstract and cognate. >> Herstel and groop ("repair") is abstract and non-cognate. || Since every pair was one of each kind, the effect of each factor could be measured on its own.
11|Yes, people use cognates when learning a second language. In De Groot and Keijzer (2000), **cognates were learned faster and remembered better**. We know this because recall started higher for cognates and dropped less after a week. Recall was tested in three training sessions, then in session 4 after a week with no training. || Recall rose from session 1 to session 3 for every kind of word. >> Concrete cognates went from 69.7% to 93.4%. >> Concrete non-cognates went from 43.7% to 92.0%. >> Abstract cognates went from 42.7% to 86.0%. >> Abstract non-cognates went from 19.0% to 72.7%. || Recall dropped after the week off, most of all for abstract non-cognates. >> Concrete cognates dropped to 78.4%, and concrete non-cognates dropped to 69.0%. >> Abstract cognates dropped to 68.4%, and abstract non-cognates fell hard to **36.0%**. >> Responses were also faster for cognates in every session. || So **cognate status helps from the very first stage** of word learning. The cognate advantage was biggest in session 1 and showed up again after the week. || **Concreteness** also mattered a lot, and the two factors add up. Abstract non-cognates had neither kind of help, so they were hardest. One explanation for concreteness effects is **context availability** (concrete words are easier to link to a helpful context).
12|Yes, it is a good experiment, because it tested people in both directions. This is because a receptive test alone would have been too easy. **Receptive** testing gives the made-up word and asks for the Dutch word. A cognate made-up word gives the answer away. || The meanings of gitaar, tijger and domein could be guessed without any Dutch. This is because they look like English guitar, tiger and domain. The concrete cognates are very close to their Dutch partners. >> Broer and breur, brood and brodde, gitaar and gettar, jongen and jange, lepel and lippel. >> Lichaam and lechem, naald and nadel, paraplu and parredu, rivier and riffer, schaar and skare. >> Stier and stire, trein and trinen, varken and vark, viool and vialo, vrouw and vrauk. >> Each made-up word is a slightly changed Dutch word. || The study also tested **expressive** vocabulary, which the authors call **productive** testing. Productive testing gives the Dutch word and asks for the made-up word. This direction matters because the learner then has to recall the exact new form, like gettar. Since there is no shortcut, a cognate advantage here means real learning.
13|The participants really learned the new words, and cognates and concrete words were forgotten less. This is because they dropped less over the week in **both** test directions. Recall was measured right after learning (session 3) and a week later (session 4). || In **productive** testing, learners had to produce the made-up word. >> Concrete words went from 92.7% to 73.7%, a drop of 19.0 points. Abstract words went from 79.3% to 52.2%, a drop of 27.1. >> Cognates went from 89.7% to 73.3%, a drop of 16.4. Non-cognates went from 82.3% to 52.5%, a drop of 29.8. || In **receptive** testing, learners had to give the Dutch word. >> Concrete words went from 97.7% to 88.9%, a drop of 8.8. Abstract words went from 91.5% to 64.9%, a drop of 26.6. >> Cognates went from 98.5% to 90.7%, a drop of 7.8. Non-cognates went from 90.7% to 63.0%, a drop of 27.7. || Receptive scores were higher overall. This is because recognizing a familiar word is easier than producing a new one. || The cognate advantage held up in productive testing too. Since the learner had to recall the new form itself, similarity to a known word must have helped build a lasting memory.
14|The question is whether similar first-language words always become active when someone processes a second language. There have been many, many studies of this. This is because a bilingual hearing an L2 word might also activate L1 words that sound like it. That could happen on its own, even when the person is trying to use only the L2. If it does, the two languages are never fully separate. || **Marian and Spivey (2003)** gave one clear example with a **visual world experiment**. In this kind of experiment, people hear spoken instructions while looking at objects. Their eye movements are recorded. || Eye movements can answer the question. This is because a look toward an object whose L1 name sounds like the spoken L2 word means the L1 word was active. The person never has to report it. The person still picks the right object in the end.
15|Eye movements show which words a person is considering, even when the final choice is right. The same method was used to study categorical perception. **McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)** used it to study how within-category **VOT** (voice onset time) affects **lexical access** (finding the word in memory). || Participants saw a lamp, a ship, a bear and a pear. They heard a word from a continuum between "bear" and "pear". Bear and pear differ only in the first consonant. The lamp and ship were unrelated fillers. || A participant could click the bear after glancing at the pear. This is because "pear" was active as a competitor. Those glances measure **competition**. || The amount of competition between BEAR and PEAR depended on **distance from the category boundary**. >> Sounds near the /b/ and /p/ boundary got more looks to the competitor. >> Sounds far from the boundary got fewer. || Since eye tracking measures how active another word is, it can also test whether a word in one language competes with one in another.
16|**Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** found the **cohort effect**. Words that start like the target compete early in recognition. A **cohort** is the set of words that share the same beginning sounds. || Participants saw a grid of pictures and shapes and followed spoken instructions. >> The **beaker** was the target. >> The **beetle** starts like beaker. >> The **speaker** rhymes with beaker. >> The **carriage** is unrelated. || Looks were tracked for the first 1,000 milliseconds after the word began. >> Looks to the beaker and the beetle went up together early. This is because both fit the first sounds. >> Looks to the beetle peaked near 0.2 around 500 milliseconds. They dropped once the sounds that tell the words apart came in. >> Looks to the beaker kept going up toward 1 by about 1,000 milliseconds. >> Looks to the speaker rose later and stayed small, near 0.1. This is because its overlap is at the end. >> Looks to the carriage stayed near zero. || So words compete as soon as they match the incoming sounds. They drop out once the sounds stop matching. Since eyes follow this, eye movements track competition moment by moment.
17|**Marian and Spivey** checked whether a word in the other language competes just because it starts with the same sounds. They called these **cross-language competitors** (objects whose name in the other language starts like the target). || Their hypothesis was that if L1 words are always active, Russian speakers hearing English "speaker" should look at matches. This is because English **speaker** starts like Russian **спички (spichki)**, which means matches. || Each English target had an English and a Russian competitor. >> Speaker had spear and spichki (matches). Boot had book and buben (tambourine). Shark had shovel and sharik (balloon). >> Chair had chess set and cherepaha (turtle). Marker had marbles and marka (stamp). Barbed wire had bark and barhat (velvet). >> Plug had plum and platye (dress). Gun had gum and gaika (nut). Lock had lobster and lak dlea nogtei (nail polish). || For Russian targets, it was flipped. >> Spichki (matches) had English spear and Russian spitsi (knitting needles). >> Marka (stamp) had English marker and Russian marlya (cheese cloth). >> Platye (dress) had English plum and Russian plashch (raincoat). || The target and the competitor share only **sound**. Speakers and matches have nothing to do with each other in meaning. Since the overlap only exists if you know Russian, looks to the matches mean spichki was active.
18|Yes, the first language stays active while bilinguals listen in their second language. We know this from Russian and English bilinguals who followed instructions in **English**. They looked at objects with similar Russian names far more than at unrelated objects. || The **between-language competitor** had a similar Russian name. The **within-language competitor** had a similar English name. Looks were counted as the percent of trials with a look at each object. >> With only the Russian-name competitor, looks were **18%**. >> With only the English-name competitor, looks were also **18%**. >> With both, the Russian-name one got 13% and the English-name one got 19%. >> With no competitor, an unrelated control object got only **7%**. || With one competitor, both kinds drew looks **to the same extent**. So the Russian name pulled the eyes as strongly as a similar English word. || With both, the English-name competitor got more looks. The Russian-name competitor still got about **twice as many as the control**. Since bilinguals still picked the right object, the L1 words competed along the way without causing mistakes.
19|No, English words did not reliably become active when the same bilinguals listened in **Russian**. We know this because looks to objects with similar English names were barely above the unrelated control. || Looks were counted as the percent of trials with a look at each object. >> With only the English-name competitor, looks were **8%**. >> With only the Russian-name competitor, looks were 14%. >> With both, the English-name one got 8% and the Russian-name one got 13%. >> With no competitor, the control object got **5%**. || The difference between 8% and 5% is **not statistically significant** (it is small enough to be chance). The Russian-name competitor still drew extra looks, so the cohort effect showed up in Russian. || So the result goes one way only, which is called **asymmetric**. >> Russian words became active during English tasks. >> English words did not reliably become active during Russian tasks. || Since the first language interferes with the second but not the reverse, the first language seems more firmly set up and easier to activate.
20|In the **Revised Hierarchical Model** of **Kroll and Stewart**, activation flows more easily from an L2 word to an L1 word than the other way. This is because learners usually learn each L2 word by tying it to its L1 translation. || The model works by **spreading activation** (turning on one representation partly turns on the ones linked to it). It has three parts. >> One part is the L1 vocabulary. >> Another part is the L2 vocabulary. >> The third part is a store of **concepts** (meanings). || **Lexical links** connect L1 and L2 words directly. **Conceptual links** connect each vocabulary to the concepts. || The L1 vocabulary is assumed to be **larger**. It is also **more strongly linked to concepts**. L2 words are linked to concepts more weakly. || The L2 vocabulary has strong forward links to the L1 vocabulary. The link from L2 to L1 is strong, and the link from L1 to L2 is weak. Since learning practices the L2 to L1 direction, that path gets strong. The direct path from L2 words to concepts stays weaker, at least early on.
21|The **Revised Hierarchical Model of Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** explains why the Russian competitor drew looks in the English task. This is because L1 words are strongly linked to their concepts. || In the L2 task with the target "speaker", the model works in four steps. >> The concept for "matches" partly activates the Russian word for matches, spichki. >> The sounds of speaker also partly activate spichki, because the two words start the same way. >> This activity feeds back and boosts the concept for "matches". >> The boosted concept for "matches" makes the eyes move toward the L1 competitor, the matches. || Speaker and spichki share only **sound overlap**. Speakers and matches share no meaning. So the looks come from sound alone. || The effect does not happen when the task is done in L1. This is because the conceptual links for L2 words might be too weak. An English competitor would need a strong link from its English word to its concept. L2 words only have weak links. Since those links are weak, English words cannot reliably pull the eyes during a Russian task.
22|The model predicts that **balanced bilinguals** (people about equally good at both languages) are not "two monolinguals in one". This prediction is counter-intuitive. You might expect each language to run on its own. || The two languages compete all the time instead. This is because a balanced bilingual has similarly strong **conceptual links** for both languages. Each concept strongly activates its word in both languages. So both vocabularies light up whenever either is used. || Two processes are engaged all the time. >> **Competitive dynamics** means the words of the two languages keep competing to be picked. >> **Inhibition** means holding back the word from the language not in use. || This prediction led to a lot of work on bilinguals' advantages in non-linguistic **cognitive control** tasks. Cognitive control is managing attention and holding back responses that do not fit. Since bilinguals constantly practice picking one language and holding back the other, they might get better at controlling attention in general.
23|The question is what happens when someone **stops using their first language** after learning a second. This is because the usual sequence keeps both languages in use. >> The person begins learning L1. >> Later the person learns L2. >> The person keeps learning and using L1 the whole time. || In the other sequence, the person begins learning L1 and later learns L2. But the person stops using L1. So L1 is no longer heard or spoken, and L2 takes over. || This second sequence pulls apart two things that usually go together. One is **learning a language early**. The other is **continuing to use it**. >> If early learning leaves a permanent foundation, traces of L1 should still be there without use. >> If ongoing use is needed, L1 might fade or disappear.
24|**Pallier, Dehaene, Poline, LeBihan, Argenti, Dupoux and Mehler** asked whether a second language can replace the first. They used brain imaging of language plasticity in adopted adults. || They tested adults aged **20 to 32**. These adults were adopted into French homes from Korean orphanages between ages **3 and 8**. They learned Korean as young children and stopped using it completely after adoption. This is because they then heard only French at home. So they are a real case of L1 use stopping. || The researchers looked for **residual knowledge** of Korean (any leftover trace of it). Monolingual French speakers who never knew Korean were the comparison group. If the adoptees did better than them on Korean tasks, some Korean must have survived. Since any surviving Korean would come only from early childhood, it would show what early learning leaves behind.
25|Korean has a **three-way stop contrast**, so it makes a good test of whether adoptees kept any Korean. A **stop** is a consonant made by fully blocking the air and then letting it go, like /p/, /t/ or /k/. || English and French have two kinds of stop at each place in the mouth, like /b/ and /p/. Korean has three kinds at each place. >> An **aspirated** stop is let out with a strong puff of air. >> A **plain** stop has a lighter release. >> A **tense** stop is made with a stiffened, tight throat. || The UCLA Phonetics Archive has recordings of three series. >> The **bilabial** series (made with the lips) is pʰul ("grass"), pul ("fire") and p’ul ("horn"). >> The **dental** series (tongue at the teeth) is tʰal ("mask"), tal ("moon") and t’al ("daughter"). >> The **velar** series (back of the tongue) is kʰo ("nose"), kok ("music") and k’ok ("exactly"). || In each series, the three words differ only in the kind of stop. French speakers tend to hear the three sounds as two. This is because French has no such contrast. Korean speakers hear three. Since French speakers struggle, adoptees who kept Korean sound knowledge should do better than them.
26|No, the Korean adoptees heard the Korean stop differences no better than French speakers. **Ventureyra and colleagues** found this. Listeners heard pairs of sounds and said whether they were different. || Each set of numbers below gives how often Koreans, adoptees and French speakers said "different", in that order. >> For plain and tense p and k, it was about 96%, 8% and 3%. >> For plain and aspirated p and k, it was about 90%, 19% and 26%. >> For tense and aspirated p and k, it was about 95%, 23% and 20%. >> For plain and tense s, it was about 79%, 26% and 23%. || The adoptees could do the task. We know this because two control conditions came out fine for everyone. >> On a **vowel** difference, all three groups said "different" about 97 to 99% of the time. >> On **identical** pairs, all groups said "different" only about 3 to 13% of the time. || So the problem was only with the Korean stops. Since the adoptees scored like people who never heard Korean, early Korean left no measurable advantage for these sounds.
27|No, the adoptees showed no trace of Korean on any of three unusual tasks. In a more extensive study, **Pallier and colleagues** found adoptees were no better than monolingual French speakers on all three. This is because the adoptees scored like French speakers even on tasks built to catch weak knowledge. Pallier and colleagues were looking for any remaining trace at all, so they used tasks that are not standard psycholinguistic tasks. || The first task was telling Korean apart from mostly unrelated languages. People rated from 1 to 7 how Korean each sentence sounded. >> Both groups rated Korean around 4 and Japanese around 4 to 5. >> Polish got about 2, and Swedish and Wolof got about 2.5 to 3. >> Adoptees were no better at picking out Korean. || The second task was picking the correct Korean translation of French words. Adoptees got about 55% and French speakers about 50%. Both are close to chance. || The third task was deciding whether a short piece came from a recorded sentence they just heard. >> Both groups got about 95% on French. >> Both got about 80% on Korean, Japanese and Polish. >> Adoptees had no edge on Korean. || Since every measure matched French speakers, the adoptees looked like people who never learned Korean.
28|The adoptees' brains treated Korean like a foreign language, just like French speakers' brains did. **Pallier and colleagues** found this with brain imaging. || Listening to French turned on the **classical language network** in both groups. >> This includes **superior temporal** regions (the upper temporal lobe, which handles speech sounds). >> It also includes **inferior frontal** regions (the lower frontal lobe, including Broca's area). || The researchers subtracted the activity for another language from the activity for French. >> They did French minus Polish, French minus Japanese and French minus Korean. >> They did each one for the French group and for the Korean group (the adoptees). >> French always turned on the language network more than the foreign language. || French minus Korean could have come out different for the adoptees. This is because adoptees who still processed Korean would show more language activity for Korean. Then the French minus Korean difference would be smaller for them. But it was not different. Since the brain results match the behavior results, no Korean processing seems to be left.
29|An unused first language could have its brain connections taken over by the second language. This is the idea of **competitive dynamics**. Imagine L1 as a set of connections within and between brain regions. Using a language keeps those connections in place. || The same thing happens in vision with **monocular deprivation** (one eye gets no input) in mice. >> After 2 to 3 days, the deprived eye's connections show **increased spine motility** (they become unstable). >> After 3 to 4 days, there is **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration** (cut-off nerve fibers break down). >> After more than a week, the other eye shows **axon sprouting and new spine birth**. It takes over the space. || So the eye that keeps getting input wins the connections the other eye loses. || Language could work the same way. If L1 connections are never used again, L2 could take them over. This is because L2 is used all the time, so its connections get stronger and spread. L1 is not used, so its connections weaken. Since both compete for the same brain resources, L2 could end up replacing L1.
30|Apparently yes, a second language can replace the first. This is because the adoptees showed no sign of their childhood Korean in behavior or in the brain. As far as these tests could tell, French had fully replaced Korean. || The next question is whether traces of L1 are left that can be **"salvaged"**. Salvaging a trace means getting back or using some leftover effect of the old language. A trace might not show up on a test of what you can do now. It could still make relearning easier. || These are two different kinds of test. >> A recognition test asks what the person can do with the language now. >> A relearning test asks whether new learning gets help from something left over. || Since a hidden trace might only help new learning, **relearning** is the measure that could reveal it.
31|Yes, some traces can be salvaged. Students who overheard Spanish as kids later pronounced it much more like native speakers. **Terry Au and colleagues** at UCLA (Au, Knightly, Jun and Oh) found this. || They studied undergraduates who **overheard Spanish** often as children but never learned to speak it. All started Spanish classes around age 14. All were tested after a second-year college Spanish course. They were compared with native speakers and typical late learners. || Pronunciation was much better for overhearers. Each set of numbers below is in the order natives, overhearers, then late learners. >> **Voice onset time** (the delay before voicing starts) for word-initial voiceless stops was 19.3, 19.6 and 36.2 milliseconds. Word-medial values were 22.4, 23.4 and 31.2. >> **Lenition** (softening voiced stops between vowels) was 43%, 24% and 1% word-initially, and 72%, 63% and 23% word-medially. >> Accent ratings out of 5 were 4.3, 3.5 and 2.2 for voiceless stops, and 4.4, 3.3 and 2.7 for voiced. >> Childhood slang production was 79%, 17% and 3%, and comprehension was 88%, 22% and 3%. || Grammar was not better. >> Gender agreement was 93%, 70% and 68%, and number agreement was 92%, 88% and 85%. >> Grammaticality judgment accuracy was 94%, 64% and 63%. Reaction times were 1,042, 2,475 and 2,209 milliseconds. || Since sounds improved but grammar did not, early listening left traces in the sound system that later classes unmasked.
32|Losing all contact with a first language can make it look fully replaced, but some knowledge may still be there. This is because the two groups gave different results. >> The Korean adoptees had no Korean exposure at all. L2 seemed to completely replace L1, since they showed nothing on any test of current ability. >> The Spanish overhearers took Spanish classes later. Their pronunciation showed leftover knowledge that got "unmasked" by the new learning. || So the replacement is **not necessarily complete**. There may be some **residual knowledge** (a leftover trace) that comes back with later learning. || The two groups also differ in ways that could matter. >> The adoptees were tested on what they knew, with no new Korean classes. >> The overhearers were tested at the end of a second-year college Spanish course. That gave hidden knowledge a chance to show up.
33|The adoptees and the overhearers differ in many ways, so it is hard to say why only the overhearers kept traces. L1 can seem fully replaced, but residual knowledge may be unmasked later. This is because several other differences could explain the results. >> Korean is rarely heard in France, but Spanish is common in Los Angeles. So overhearers may have kept hearing Spanish all their lives. >> A **heritage language** (spoken by family members) is different from a language nobody you know speaks. Overhearers often heard Spanish from relatives, while adoptees lost all contact with Korean speakers. >> **Cognates** are common between Spanish and English but rare between Korean and French. Shared words could help overhearers relearn. >> A **shared writing system** also helps. Spanish and English use the same alphabet, while Korean uses a different script from French. || This is a general problem in research on second language acquisition and bilingualism. Languages and learning situations differ in many, many ways. Those ways are hard, maybe impossible, to control in experiments. This is because researchers cannot assign people to grow up with certain languages. Since real groups never differ in just one way, these factors are hard to pull apart.
