2|**Nature and nurture in child language** is the question of how much language comes from built-in capacities and how much comes from experience. **Nativist positions** grew out of **Chomsky's critique of Skinner**. >> **Skinner** was a behaviorist. He explained behavior, including language, with general learning mechanisms such as reward and punishment. >> **Chomsky** argued that those mechanisms could not explain how children acquire grammar. || For Chomsky, the important feature of language is its **generativity**. Generativity is the ability to produce and understand an unlimited number of new sentences. >> Most sentences a person hears or says are new. >> So acquiring a language means **identifying the abstract grammar** that produces the sentences of the language. || The general learning mechanisms studied by behaviorists were **not up to this task**, for two reasons. >> Grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are **not "similar"** in any straightforward way. A tiny change can make a sentence ungrammatical. Two grammatical sentences can look completely different. >> The context for language acquisition does not look like **operant conditioning**. Operant conditioning shapes behavior with rewards and punishments that follow it. Children are not reliably rewarded for grammatical sentences or punished for ungrammatical ones. So the **reward and punishment contingencies do not hold**. || Nativist approaches assert two things instead. >> Children have **innate knowledge of what language is like**, called **Universal Grammar**. Universal Grammar is a built-in set of principles that limits which grammars a child will consider. >> Children use **language-specific acquisition processes**. These are learning mechanisms built specifically for language. || Chomsky argued for this position by writing, "The fact that all normal children acquire essentially comparable grammars of great complexity with remarkable rapidity suggests that human beings are somehow specially designed to do this, with data-handling or 'hypothesis-formulating' ability of unknown character and complexity." >> Every typical child reaches a complex grammar quickly, even though children's experiences differ widely. >> That is why nativists say innate limits narrow the possible grammars, while experience decides which language the child ends up with.
3|**Categorical perception** is the pattern in which listeners hear differences between speech sounds in terms of categories. Early theories treated it as a **language-specific** and **species-specific** phenomenon. >> Language-specific means it was thought to happen only for language. >> Species-specific means it was thought to happen only in humans. >> At the time, **only humans** were known to show categorical perception, and **only for speech sounds**. || The classic evidence comes from a synthetic consonant continuum of fourteen stimuli. The upper formant transitions change in equal steps from one stimulus to the next. The lower formant stays the same. >> In **labeling**, listeners switch sharply from **/b/** to **/d/** around stimuli 4 and 5. They switch from /d/ to **/g/** around stimuli 9 and 10. >> In **discrimination**, accuracy **peaks near those boundaries**. Accuracy falls inside each category. This holds whether the pairs are one, two or three steps apart. || Categorical perception is a compelling example of how similarity between stimuli is defined in **linguistic, rather than physical, terms**. >> Two sounds in the **same category** can sound almost identical. >> Two sounds on **opposite sides of a category boundary** can sound clearly different. >> This happens even when the two pairs differ by the same acoustic amount. || So what counts as similar depends on the language categories. That is why categorical perception was taken as a sign of a specialized speech system.
4|Infant studies measure behaviors that change when an infant notices something new. **Head turns** and **sucking** reveal what an infant hears without the infant saying anything. || The **conditioned head turn procedure** trains an infant to turn toward a sound when it changes. It is used in Janet Werker's infant studies lab. >> The infant sits on a parent's lap. An experimenter keeps the infant's attention forward with toys. >> When the sound changes, a correct head turn earns an interesting visual reward, such as a lighted, moving toy. >> Once the infant has learned this link, a head turn after a sound change means the infant **heard the change**. || **High-amplitude sucking** uses the infant's natural interest in something new. >> The infant sucks on a pacifier connected to a pressure recorder. Strong sucks make a sound play. >> As one sound repeats, the infant gets used to it, so sucking slows down. Getting used to a repeated sound is called **habituation**. >> If a new sound makes sucking **increase again**, the infant has **noticed the change**. || Neither method asks the infant to name or produce a sound. The experimenter controls exactly which acoustic difference is presented. So both methods test whether infants hear the same distinctions that adults hear.
5|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** tested categorical perception in **4-month-old infants** with **high-amplitude sucking**. The sounds varied in **voice onset time (VOT)**. VOT is the delay between the release of a stop consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration. >> With a VOT of +100 milliseconds, voicing starts much later after the release than with a VOT of +10 milliseconds. || Infants heard one syllable repeatedly until their sucking declined. Then the sound either changed or stayed the same. There were three conditions. >> In **20D**, the two sounds differed by 20 milliseconds of VOT, +20 versus +40 milliseconds. They came from **different adult phonemic categories**. >> In **20S**, the two sounds also differed by 20 milliseconds, either −20 versus 0 or +60 versus +80 milliseconds. They came from the **same** category. >> In **0**, the **control** condition, the same sound continued throughout. || In every condition, sucking first rose above the baseline rate and then declined before the change. >> **For 20D**, sucking **jumped back up** after the change, to around 50 responses per minute. >> **For 20S**, sucking showed **little recovery** and kept falling. >> **For the control**, sucking kept declining, down to about 15 responses per minute. || The two changed conditions were matched in acoustic size, so the difference comes from crossing the category boundary. The control establishes what happens with repetition alone. So infants responded to a change that **crossed an adult phoneme boundary** but not to an equally large change **within a category**.
6|Eimas's result was **a big deal** because of what categorical perception was thought to do. Categorical perception was considered the **first step in turning speech into a symbolic format** that later linguistic processes could use. >> A **symbolic** format represents a sound as a category, such as /b/. >> Finding this ability in infants suggested that the starting point for language processing was already in place. || **Four-month-olds** are really little. >> Their **gross motor milestones** include manipulating objects with two hands and holding their head and chest up while prone. Prone means lying face down. Gross motor skills are large body movements. >> Their **color vision** and general **visual acuity** are still "in progress." Visual acuity is the sharpness of vision. >> They are just starting to produce **vowel-like sounds** instead of creaky whines. They cannot yet produce speech sounds well. || This is very, very early in development for an apparently **language-specific** ability to emerge. >> Infants sort speech sounds into categories before they can produce them. >> They do this while their other abilities are still immature. || So speech categorization ran far ahead of other skills. That is why the result **seemed like evidence for innateness**.
7|**Perceptual narrowing** for speech categories is the decline in sensitivity to sound distinctions that the child's language does not use. At around **six months**, English-learning infants discriminate speech contrasts they have **never heard before**. As development goes on, the ability to perceive these contrasts **declines**. || **Werker and Tees (1984)** tested English-learning infants on two non-English contrasts. >> The **Hindi contrast** is a distinction between two "ta"-like sounds. >> The **Salish contrast** is a distinction between "ki" and "qi" from Nthlakampx, a Salish language. >> They measured the percentage of infants at each age who reached a **discrimination criterion**. || The percentage fell with age for English-learning infants. >> **At 6 to 8 months**, about 95% discriminated the Hindi contrast and about 80% discriminated the Salish contrast. >> **At 8 to 10 months**, about 67% and about 52% did. >> **At 10 to 12 months**, about 20% and about 10% did. >> **Hindi-learning and Salish-learning infants** discriminated their native contrast close to 100% of the time. || This is **perceptual reorganization during the first year of life**. >> Without experience of a contrast, English-learning infants lose much of their ability to hear it by about their first birthday. >> Infants who hear the contrast in their language keep it, so the decline **depends on language experience**. || Perceptual narrowing is related to categorical perception through the native categories. >> As infants form the categories of their native language, sounds that the language groups together become harder to tell apart. >> So a nonnative distinction inside one native category comes to be heard as a **within-category difference**.
8|A **very compelling narrative** was built from these findings. It has three parts. >> Speech perception depends on **categorical perception abilities** that are a **special property of the language system**. >> These abilities are present **very early in development**, as the infant studies suggest. >> Children start off as **"citizens of the world."** || A citizen of the world can hear **all contrasts**. >> This means children have **innate, language-general knowledge** about speech. Language-general knowledge applies to the speech sounds of every language. >> So a newborn is ready to learn any human language. >> Children then **rapidly tune** their perceptual systems to the specific language in their environment. || In this narrative, the role of learning is to **select** among the many possible innately specified phonetic contrasts. >> Learning narrows down the **inventory** to the contrasts that are meaningful in the child's native language. The inventory is the full set of phonetic contrasts the child can perceive. >> Learning removes the contrasts that the native language does not use. >> Learning keeps the innate categories that the native language needs. || So learning is a process of elimination, and the native language decides which innate categories survive.
9|The citizens-of-the-world view has **some problems**. Three of them concern **categorical perception**. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to language**. It also occurs for nonspeech sounds. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to humans**. Animals without language also perceive speech sounds categorically. >> Categorical perception is **task-dependent**. Results look more or less categorical depending on what the listener is asked to do. The task could be naming a sound or judging whether two sounds differ. || The fourth problem concerns **infant speech perception**, which is **not actually "universal."** >> Infants do not start out able to hear every contrast equally well. >> Some contrasts are hard for young infants to detect, even contrasts that occur in their own language. || Each problem targets a different part of the narrative. >> The nonspeech and animal findings question whether categorical perception is special to language. >> The task effects question how categorical perception really is. >> The uneven infant sensitivity questions whether all contrasts are innately available. || So a full account must explain contrasts that improve with experience as well as contrasts that are lost. That is why learning must **build categories** as well as remove them.
10|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** tested categorical perception with **non-speech sounds**. The sounds varied in **rise time**. Rise time is how quickly a sound grows to full loudness after it starts. >> A **short rise time** makes a sound like a **plucked** string. >> A **long rise time** makes a sound like a **bowed** string. || The stimuli were **sawtooth waves**, **speech syllables** and **sine waves**. Each kind was made with a 10-millisecond rise time and a 70-millisecond rise time. >> With a 10-millisecond rise, the wave reaches full size almost immediately. >> With a 70-millisecond rise, the wave grows gradually. || For the sawtooth waves, listeners identified each sound as pluck or bow. They also discriminated pairs that were 10 milliseconds apart. >> **For short rise times**, pluck responses are near 100%. >> **For long rise times**, bow responses are near 100%. >> The labels **switch steeply** between about 30 and 50 milliseconds. >> Discrimination stays near 55 to 60% for most pairs. It **peaks near 75%** for pairs around 30 to 50 milliseconds, where the labels switch. || Pluck and bow are labels for kinds of sound onset, not speech categories. So a label boundary goes with better discrimination for a distinction that has nothing to do with phonemes. That is why categorical perception can arise in **general auditory processing**, without a mechanism specific to language.
11|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** tested whether **infants** show categorical perception of **non-speech sounds**. They used high-amplitude sucking. >> Infants heard one sound repeatedly until their sucking declined. >> The sound was then changed, and three conditions were compared. || The three conditions were **no change**, a change **within categories** and a change **between categories**. In all three, sucking started near 20 per minute and declined to about 12 before the change. >> **After no change**, sucking stayed near 12. >> **After a within-category change**, sucking also stayed near 12. >> **After a between-category change**, sucking **jumped back up** to about 18. It then declined again as the new sound became familiar. || So infants responded more strongly to a non-speech change that **crossed a category boundary** than to a change within a category. >> This is the same pattern Eimas and colleagues found for speech. >> Adults show categorical labeling and discrimination for plucked and bowed sounds. Infants show categorical discrimination for nonspeech changes. >> Neither group needs language knowledge to show the pattern. || That is why early categorical perception reflects a **general auditory ability**, not only a part of speech.
12|**Chinchillas** also show categorical perception of **speech sounds**. This finding comes from **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and the earlier work of **Kuhl and Miller (1975)**. || The animals were trained with **operant conditioning**. Operant conditioning is learning in which a behavior becomes more likely when a reward follows it. >> Each chinchilla learned that either the **0-millisecond lag** stimulus or the **80-millisecond lag** stimulus was an **SD** for food. >> SD stands for **discriminative stimulus**, a signal that a response will be rewarded. >> The lag is the **voice onset time**. Humans hear a 0-millisecond lag as /ga/ and an 80-millisecond lag as /ka/. || After training, the animals were tested with **intermediate VOTs** along the /ga/-/ka/ continuum. Human listeners heard the same sounds. The measure was the proportion of stimuli labeled /ga/. >> **For short VOTs**, /ga/ responses stay near 1. >> **For long VOTs**, /ga/ responses fall to near 0. >> The fall is steep and happens mostly between about 30 and 70 milliseconds. >> The chinchillas' labeling has the **same steep, categorical shape** as the humans' labeling. || **Increasing the intensity of the burst and aspiration noise** shifted the category boundary to a **shorter VOT**, made the switch steeper, or both. This held for one chinchilla and one human listener. >> The **burst** is the brief noise at the release of a stop. **Aspiration** is the breathy noise that follows a voiceless release. >> So both species responded to these acoustic properties in a similar way. || Animals without language sort a speech continuum into categories. That is why categorical perception of speech is **shared with other species**.
13|**Narayan and colleagues (2010)** found that **some contrasts are less salient than others for infants**. A **salient** contrast is one that is easy to notice. || The contrasts they tested use **nasal consonants** in the **onset**, which is the start of a syllable. The consonants differ in **place of articulation**, which is where in the mouth the sound is made. >> **/m/** is made with the lips. >> **/n/** is made with the tongue behind the teeth. >> **/ŋ/**, the final sound in "sing," is made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate. || Infants heard **same** trials, where the syllable did not change, and **change** trials, where it switched. Longer looking on change trials means the infant noticed the change. The infants were English-hearing and 4 to 5 months old. >> **For the native [ma]-[na] contrast**, they looked about 3.3 seconds on same trials and about 5.4 seconds on change trials. So they **noticed** the switch. >> **For the nonnative [na]-[ŋa] contrast**, they looked about 4.5 seconds on same trials and about 4.2 seconds on change trials. So they **did not notice** the switch. || A place contrast for nasals in onset, **/na/-/ŋa/**, is **not readily perceived** by four-month-olds, independent of language experience. In contrast, **/ma/-/na/** is **relatively salient**. || So the difference lies in the sounds themselves, not in experience. That is why the idea that every contrast starts out equally available runs into trouble.
14|**Narayan and colleagues (2010)** also tested infants in a **Filipino** language environment. In Filipino, [na] and [ŋa] are different syllables, so Filipino-learning infants hear this contrast regularly. || Between **6 and 10 months**, exposure to the contrast **increases its salience**. >> **At 6 to 8 months**, Filipino-learning infants looked about 3.8 seconds on both same and change trials. So they did **not yet notice** the switch. >> **At 10 to 12 months**, looking rose from about 3.3 seconds on same trials to about 4.8 seconds on change trials. So they **noticed** the switch. >> English-hearing infants at 4 to 5 months did not respond to this contrast, although they did respond to [ma]-[na]. || So it **cannot be that all speech contrasts are available innately**. >> Sensitivity to [na]-[ŋa] is weak at the start, even for infants whose language uses it. >> Hearing the contrast over the first year makes it easier to detect. || **Exposure to contrasts in the environment is necessary for acquisition** in some cases. >> Learning can strengthen a contrast as well as preserve it. >> That is why development includes building sensitivity to hard contrasts, not only losing sensitivity to unused ones.
15|The **emergentist approach** is the alternative to the nativist approach. Emergentist approaches assume that **associative learning mechanisms** are sensitive to **statistical regularities** in the environment. >> **Associative learning** is learning that two things go together because they repeatedly occur together. >> **Statistical regularities** are patterns in how often things occur and which things occur together. >> On this view, language structure emerges from general learning mechanisms working on these patterns in rich input. || Classical conditioning is the simplest case of associative learning. It can be described with three neurons, a **bell** neuron, a **food detector** neuron and a **salivation** neuron. >> The food detector already connects strongly to salivation, so food produces salivation. >> When the bell repeatedly occurs with food, the **synapse** between the bell neuron and the salivation neuron is **strengthened**. A synapse is a connection between neurons. >> After enough pairings, the bell alone can produce salivation. || This can extend beyond conditioning to support **pattern recognition**. Pattern recognition is identifying a recurring structure, such as a speech sound, across many varied examples. >> The same strengthening could link acoustic features that tend to occur together. >> Repeated co-occurrence in speech could then build categories that were never specified in advance. >> In conditioning, one event comes to predict another. In pattern recognition, many features come to predict a category. || So infants could learn speech categories by tracking which sounds occur together and how often. They would not have to start with a full set of built-in categories.
16|The **American vowels** are an example of statistical regularities. When **Kuhl and others** talk about learning the **statistics of distributions** in the environment, they mean patterns like these. A **distribution** describes how often each value occurs across many examples. || A **vowel space** is determined by the **first and second formants**. Each production of a vowel has one first formant value and one second formant value. >> Many productions of one vowel cluster together in one region of the space. >> The regions of different vowels **overlap** heavily. >> So a single pair of formant values can belong to more than one vowel. >> That is why speech categorization is difficult. Listeners must learn where each vowel is centered and how far it spreads. || Maybe we learn the vowels of our language by **extracting the statistics** of these distributions. >> Over many exposures, the listener tracks where the productions of each vowel cluster. >> The categories then reflect the shape of the input. || A **"prototypical" /a/** is then the **mean** (or perhaps the **mode**) of the distribution of all the /a/ sounds we hear. >> The mean is the average of all the values. >> The mode is the most frequent value. || So the prototype comes from experience. That is why it can differ across languages and listeners.
17|**Maye and colleagues (2002)** tested whether infants really learn categories from distributions. Infants **listened passively** to sounds from a **[da]-[ta] continuum** of eight stimuli that differed in **voice onset time**. Passive listening means the infants heard the sounds with no task or reward. || Two groups heard different distributions of the same continuum. >> **For the bimodal group**, stimuli 2 and 7 were presented most often, 16 times each, and the middle stimuli were rare. Two peaks give **evidence for two different categories**. >> **For the unimodal group**, stimuli 4 and 5 in the middle were presented most often, and the ends were rare. One peak gives **evidence for a single category**. || At test, infants listened to **stimuli 3 and 6** in **alternating** or **non-alternating** trials. >> In alternating trials, the two stimuli switched back and forth. In non-alternating trials, one stimulus repeated. >> If infants treat 3 and 6 as different sounds, the two kinds of trials should hold their attention differently. || **Only infants pre-exposed to the bimodal distribution** were sensitive to the difference between the two kinds of trials. >> **Bimodal six-month-olds** looked 5.66 seconds on alternating trials and 6.41 seconds on non-alternating trials. **Bimodal eight-month-olds** looked 5.45 and 6.15 seconds. >> **Unimodal six-month-olds** looked 4.85 and 4.53 seconds. **Unimodal eight-month-olds** looked 4.98 and 5.20 seconds. So unimodal infants looked about equally long on both. || So the infants in the unimodal condition acted as if they had learned to treat stimuli 3 and 6 as **members of the same category**. That is why a few minutes of exposure to a distribution supports **statistical learning** of speech categories.
18|Experiments suggest that **social interaction is important for learning**. || First, **nine-month-old American infants** were exposed to **Mandarin Chinese** in twelve 25-minute sessions. >> **For live exposure**, a Mandarin speaker played and read with the infants in person. >> **For auditory or audiovisual exposure**, infants heard or watched Mandarin on audio or television. >> A control group had English exposure. >> Afterward, infants were tested on a **Mandarin phonetic contrast** with the **head-turn technique**. || Only live exposure led to phonetic learning. >> Infants with **live** Mandarin exposure scored about 65% correct, above the English control group at about 56%. >> Infants with **TV or audio** Mandarin exposure scored about the same as controls, close to chance. >> So the same exposure is **more effective in person** (and interactive) than as a video. || Second, **eight-month-old infants** received social feedback from their mothers in response to their vocalizations. >> **For the contingent condition**, mothers responded right after the infant vocalized. **Contingent** means the response depends on and follows the infant's behavior. >> **For the yoked-control condition**, mothers responded on a schedule copied from another infant, so the responses were **noncontingent**. >> A 30-minute familiarization phase came first, then a 10-minute baseline, a 10-minute social response phase and a 10-minute **extinction** phase. In extinction, the responses stopped. || The measure was the proportion of syllables among the infants' vocalizations. >> At baseline, both groups produced about 0.05 to 0.08. >> During social response, the contingent group rose to about **0.32**, while the noncontingent group stayed near 0.05. >> During extinction, the contingent group stayed higher at about 0.2, against about 0.1 for the noncontingent group. >> So **contingent social feedback increased the quantity and complexity** of infants' vocalizations. || Social interactions contingent on the production of different sounds seem to work much like a **reinforcing stimulus**. A reinforcing stimulus makes a behavior more likely when it follows that behavior. >> One idea is that humans find social interaction with other humans **innately reinforcing**. >> Another idea is that we are innately good at using **social cues** to learn from other people.
19|**Statistical learning with constraints** is learning from patterns in the input that is guided and limited in several ways. **Constraints** can mean a lot of things. || The first kind limits **which stimulus properties can be contrastive**. A **contrastive** property is one that distinguishes words, such as voicing in /b/ versus /p/. >> **Speaker** identity and **speech rate** change the sound a great deal. They do not change which word is said, so they are **not contrastive**. >> A learner who tracked every statistical pattern would form categories for different voices and speeds. >> So learning has to focus on the properties that matter for meaning. || The second kind is **based on earlier learning**. >> What a learner has already acquired shapes what is easy to learn next. >> Once native categories are in place, new distinctions that cut across them become harder to notice and learn. >> Constraints of this kind are central to **sensitive periods**, in which earlier experience limits later learning. || The third kind comes from **social and interactive contexts** for learning. >> The same sounds are learned more effectively in live, responsive interaction than from a recording. >> Contingent responses from caregivers shape the sounds infants produce. || So learning depends on the patterns in the input, on what the learner already knows and on the social situation. That is why learning is fast and focuses on the distinctions that matter.
