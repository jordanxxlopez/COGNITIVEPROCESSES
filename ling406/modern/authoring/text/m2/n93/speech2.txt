2|**Klatt (1979)** describes a very old and very general model of speech perception. Recognition runs through a series of stages. Each stage transforms the output of the stage before it. >> The **speech waveform** enters the **peripheral auditory system**, which does a **spectral analysis**. The **cochlea** in the inner ear splits the sound into its frequency components. >> **Acoustic property detectors** respond to particular properties of the sound. >> **Phonetic feature detectors** estimate the probability of features such as voicing at each moment. >> **Segmental analysis** combines the features into a sequence of speech segments. >> **Lexical search** compares that sequence with stored words and produces **lexical hypotheses**, which are candidate words. || **Categorical perception** fits this model. **Information is lost** somewhere between the **analog signal** from the cochlea and the **activation of phonemes**. >> Many physically different sounds become the same phoneme. >> This **loss of information is helpful**, because a phoneme sequence can make contact with higher levels of representation. >> One assumption follows from this model. To identify words, listeners must **first identify their constituent phonemes**. || Two problems challenge this simple view. >> The first problem is **cascaded processing**. Partial information moves on to the next level before the earlier decision is finished. >> The second problem is **feedback**. A higher level, such as words, sends influence back down to a lower level, such as phonemes. || So the simple view, in which listeners identify every phoneme before finding the word, is challenged by both cascading and feedback.
3|The **beach and peach continuum** is a familiar **voicing continuum**. The two words differ in their first consonant, which is /b/ in beach and /p/ in peach. >> These consonants differ mainly in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**. VOT is the delay between the release of the lips and the start of vocal fold vibration. >> VOT gets shorter in equal steps along the continuum from “peach” to “beach”. >> A **long delay** before voicing sounds like peach. A **short delay** sounds like beach. || In **identification**, one listener labels each stimulus as one of the two words. >> Stimuli 1 through 5 are labeled consistently as one word. >> Stimulus 6 gets mixed labels, with a proportion near 0.33. >> Stimuli 7 through 9 are labeled consistently as the other word. >> The **category boundary** is the narrow region where the preferred label switches. || In **discrimination**, the same listener's results follow the boundary. >> Pairs inside one category are almost never heard as different. >> Pairs 4 and 6 and 5 and 7 straddle the boundary, so they are heard as different every time. >> Pair 6 and 8 is intermediate, near 0.33, and pair 7 and 9 falls back to 0. || So equal changes in VOT are **much easier to detect across the boundary** than inside one category. A step across the boundary changes the word. A step inside one category leaves the word unchanged.
4|A **threshold model** is one answer to how categorical perception happens. In this model, **activity is only passed forward** from one level to the next when an activity **threshold** is crossed. >> A threshold is a set level of activity that must be reached before anything is sent onward. >> Activity below the threshold can rise and fall without any effect on the next level. || In this kind of model, **categorical perception arises at phoneme identification**. Consider the voicing evidence for a sound between /b/ and /p/. >> **At the feature level**, the voicing evidence swings up and down between a **+voiced** threshold and a **−voiced** threshold. >> **At the phoneme level**, /b/ and /p/ both stay silent while the evidence wavers below threshold. >> When the evidence finally crosses the +voiced threshold, **/b/ switches on all at once**. || An ambiguous stimulus with mixed responses either crosses a threshold or does not. >> The word level receives a finished /b/ or a finished /p/. >> The earlier uncertainty stays behind at the feature level. >> Two acoustically different sounds that both end up as /b/ are identical by the time they reach word recognition. || That is why this **all-or-none switch** would produce categorical perception directly at the phoneme level.
5|A **cascaded model** gives a different answer to how categorical perception happens. In this model, **activity is passed forward continuously** over time from one level to the next. >> Each level responds to whatever activity is present at that moment. >> The next level responds while the earlier decision is still forming. || Feature activity and phoneme activity rise and fall together. >> When evidence for **+voiced** grows, **/b/** activation grows. >> When the evidence shifts toward **−voiced**, activation shifts toward **/p/**. >> When the voicing evidence is mixed, /b/ and /p/ each get activation in proportion to their evidence. || Graded phoneme activation then passes graded activation to words. >> A sound near the boundary **partly activates both beach and peach**. >> Two sounds that both end up labeled /b/ can still differ in how much they supported /p/. || In this kind of model, categorical perception **might happen later on**. It might even be an **artifact of the task structure**. An artifact is a result created by the way something is measured, not by the process being studied. >> An identification task makes the listener click beach or peach, even if both words were partly active. >> The answer looks categorical because the response choices are categorical. || So the processing before the final choice can keep fine-grained information about how ambiguous the sound was.
6|**Making furniture** is a metaphor for **thresholded activity**. Each operation takes one specific input and turns it into one specific output. >> The **lumber mill** takes **trees** as input and produces **lumber**. >> The **furniture factory** takes **lumber** as input and produces **unassembled furniture**. >> The **IKEA customer** takes **unassembled furniture** as input and produces finished **furniture**. || Each step needs a **finished product** in a form it can use. >> Trees must become lumber before any furniture is made. >> Lumber must become furniture parts before any assembly begins. >> No part of the work at one stage passes forward until the whole job is done. || **Thresholded processing** depends on its inputs in the same way. >> A higher level waits until the lower level supplies the representation it needs. >> So information moves forward **only as a completed product**.
7|In the furniture metaphor, each **“layer”** of the system can only take a specific input. That input must be **transformed completely by the previous layer**. >> The factory has to wait for the shipment of lumber before manufacturing can start. >> Many computer programs work this way. Each step finishes before the next one starts. >> A lower stage can keep working while the higher stage sits idle and waits. || In speech recognition, a strict threshold account makes **word processing depend on an identified phoneme**. >> A partly resolved acoustic signal stays at the lower level until it is identified as a phoneme. >> The word level receives only the finished segment. || This design also decides **what information is lost**. >> When different sounds become the same phoneme, their earlier differences never reach word recognition. >> This helps the listener treat varied sounds alike. >> The cost is that later stages never learn how uncertain the earlier stages were. || So in a thresholded system, each stage hands on only its finished output.
8|A **food web** is a metaphor for **cascaded processing**. In a food web, the **“levels” continuously interact** with one another. >> The desert food web links desert plants, insects, kangaroo rats, small lizards, large lizards, scorpions, snakes, desert foxes and hawks. The links are who eats whom. >> A drought that reduces the number of plants **immediately** affects the health of kangaroo rats. >> The change in kangaroo rats then affects the snakes that eat them. >> No population waits for the level below it to reach a finished state. || **Cascaded processing** has the same continuous dependence. >> Changing feature evidence changes phoneme activity as it happens. >> Changing phoneme activity changes the support for words. >> So each level responds to partial information as it arrives. || The food web also has clear **feedback loops**. >> Adding more hawks changes the populations of snakes, lizards and rats. Those changes then affect the hawks’ own food supply. >> In a recognition model, **feedback** is influence that travels from a higher level back down to a lower level. || Cascading and feedback are different ideas. Cascading sends developing evidence **forward**. Feedback lets a higher level **change an earlier representation**.
9|The central question is how **phoneme identification for word recognition** works. It could work like **thresholded processing**, as in the lumber mill. It could work like **cascaded processing**, as in the food web. >> In a **thresholded account**, word recognition begins only after a phoneme decision is finished. >> In a **cascaded account**, a word can gain support while the phoneme is still uncertain. || The two accounts differ in how much developing acoustic evidence reaches candidate words. Two sounds can both be labeled **/b/** and still differ in how much they resemble **/p/**. >> **For a thresholded system**, both sounds become the same finished /b/. So pear should **not compete** with bear. >> **For a cascaded system**, the more ambiguous sound passes along more /p/ support. So pear should **compete with bear for a while**. || Both accounts can predict the same final word. They differ in what happens before the final choice. >> That is why the deciding test measures **temporary support for a competing word** during recognition. >> The evidence from eye movements favors the cascaded account.
10|**McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)** tested whether phoneme identification is thresholded or cascaded. They looked for **gradient effects of within-category VOT on lexical access**. >> **Lexical access** is the retrieval of a word from memory during recognition. >> **Gradient** means that an effect changes by degrees. >> **Within-category VOT** means differences in voice onset time among sounds that are all heard as the same phoneme. || Listeners identified sounds from a continuum between /b/ and /p/ in a **“visual world” display**. **Eye-tracking** recorded where they looked among the pictures while they heard speech. >> Each display had a **target**, such as pear, and a **competitor**, such as bear. >> Each display also had two unrelated **fillers**, a lamp and a ship. >> The target and the competitor differ only in their first consonant. || Eye movements happen quickly and continuously while a word is being recognized. Listeners look at objects as they think about them, often before they know they have decided. >> A listener can choose bear in the end after briefly looking at the pear. >> A look at the pear means that pear was being considered. || So the number and timing of looks to the competitor measure **how active the competitor was** before the final click.
11|Participants used the **mouse to select** whichever of the pictures was most consistent with what they heard, such as a “bear” or a “pear”. >> Each trial presented one sound from the VOT continuum with the four pictures. >> The click records which word the listener finally settled on. || The clicks gave a **typical, step-like identification function**. >> **For VOTs from 0 to about 15 milliseconds**, /p/ responses stay near 0, so listeners almost always clicked bear. >> **Between about 15 and 20 milliseconds**, /p/ responses jump up steeply. >> **For VOTs from about 20 to 40 milliseconds**, /p/ responses stay near 1, so listeners almost always clicked pear. >> The **category boundary** falls at **17.5 milliseconds**, plus or minus 0.83 milliseconds. || Judged by the clicks alone, perception looks fully **categorical**. A sound well inside the /b/ category and a sound just short of the boundary both produce bear. >> Taken alone, this sharp switch would seem to support the threshold model. >> A click records only the word chosen at the end of recognition. >> Eye movements record which other words received support before that choice. || So a sharp identification function can exist together with gradual differences in competition.
12|**Eye movements reveal that ambiguity is gradient**. There are **more looks to the competitor close to the boundary**. Gradient means that the uncertainty varies by degree. >> The analysis holds the final response constant. It compares only trials with a bear click, or only trials with a pear click. >> Every trial in a comparison ended with the same click. So differences in competitor looks cannot come from listeners changing their answer. || **For trials where the response was bear**, the measure is looks to the **pear**. The VOTs were 0, 5, 10 and 15 milliseconds. >> Looks to the pear rise after the word begins and peak around 400 to 600 milliseconds. Then they decline as bear wins. >> A VOT near the boundary, such as 15 milliseconds, gets more looks to the pear, and they last longer. >> A VOT of 0 milliseconds gets the fewest looks to the pear. || **For trials where the response was pear**, the measure is looks to the **bear**. The VOTs were 20, 25, 30, 35 and 40 milliseconds. >> A VOT of 20 milliseconds, just past the boundary, gets the most looks to the bear. >> A VOT of 40 milliseconds, the clearest /p/, gets the fewest. || The effect of VOT is mainly on the **duration of activation**, which is how long the competitor stays active. || So the recognition system keeps track of how ambiguous each sound was. A threshold model would throw that information away.
13|The gradient effect is clearest when competitor looks are compared across the whole **VOT** range. The **category boundary** sits in the middle of that range. >> **On the bear side** of the boundary, listeners clicked bear. The measure is how often they looked at the pear. >> **On the pear side** of the boundary, listeners clicked pear. The measure is how often they looked at the bear. || Looks were measured in two time windows. >> The **early** window covers **300 to 1100 milliseconds** after the word begins. >> The **late** window covers **1100 to 1900 milliseconds** after the word begins. || In both windows, competitor looks **increase as VOT approaches the boundary from either side**. >> **On the bear side**, late looks to the pear rise from about 0.02 at 0 milliseconds to about 0.045 near 15 milliseconds. >> **On the pear side**, late looks to the bear are about 0.04 near 20 milliseconds. They fall to about 0.01 by 40 milliseconds. >> Early looks are higher overall, but they follow the same pattern. >> The rise is gradual and not sudden. || The effect appears in both windows, so uncertainty about the first sound affects looking throughout recognition. || So a sound can support the eventual winner while still supporting its rival. The closer the sound is to the boundary, the stronger that rival support is.
14|The eye-movement finding is consistent with **activation flowing in a cascaded way from features to phonemes**. Changing features affect phonemes while recognition is still going on. Changing phonemes affect words in the same way. >> Even stimuli identified as /b/ **100% of the time** can vary in how strongly and unambiguously the **“voiced” feature** is activated. >> A clear /b/ activates voicing strongly. >> A /b/ near the boundary activates voicing only weakly. || **/b/ and /p/** share most of their features. >> Both are **bilabial**, which means the two lips form the closure. >> Both are **stops**, which means the airflow is completely blocked and then released. >> A stimulus that is obviously **+bilabial** and **+stop** supports /b/ and /p/ equally. >> Only voicing separates them, so weak voicing evidence creates **momentary ambiguity between /b/ and /p/**. >> That ambiguity is reflected in the **eye movements** while the participant decides where to click. || The **TRACE** model represents this cascade with three layers. >> The **feature layer** receives a **pseudo-spectral input representation**, a simplified version of the sound’s frequency content over time. >> Its detectors are POW, VOC, DIFF, ACU, GRD, VOI and BUR. They stand for power, vocalic, diffuseness, acuteness, gradualness of onset, voicing and burst. >> The **phoneme layer** has units for phonemes such as /b/, /p/, /r/ and /t/, repeated at every point in time. >> The **word layer** has units for words, also repeated across time. >> Activity passes up from features to phonemes and from phonemes to words. Units within a layer compete through **inhibition**. || So in TRACE, partial evidence about voicing reaches the phoneme and word layers before any decision is final.
15|**Andruski and colleagues (1994)** gave evidence that **partial activation can reach all the way to the lexical level**. The lexical level is the level of stored words and their meanings. >> They found **semantic priming** even with **nonwords**. Semantic priming is the speed-up in responding to a word after hearing a word related in meaning. >> The nonword “beace” primes “war”. Beace is peace with a voiced first consonant, and peace is related in meaning to war. >> The nonword “ging” primes “queen”. Ging is king with a voiced first consonant, and king is related in meaning to queen. >> In the **word to word** items, the altered prime is another real word. The word “bill” primes “drug” because bill is an altered form of pill. || Participants did a **lexical decision task**. They decided as quickly as possible whether a target was a real word. >> The primes were **unaltered**, altered by reducing VOT by **one third**, altered by **two thirds**, or **unrelated** to the target. >> Reaction times faster than in the unrelated condition count as priming. || The reaction times differed by condition. >> **For unrelated primes**, responses were slowest, near 950 to 970 milliseconds. >> **For unaltered primes**, responses were much faster, around 810 to 860 milliseconds. >> **For one third of VOT removed**, responses stayed fast, around 825 to 855 milliseconds. >> **For two thirds removed**, word to nonword items stayed fast, near 820 milliseconds. Word to word items slowed to about 890 milliseconds. >> In word to word items, the altered prime also partly activates a competing real word, which reduces the benefit. || So **lexical representations can be activated even by incomplete or imperfect input**. Beace never becomes a real word, but it matches peace closely enough to partly activate it. That activation spreads to meaning and speeds the response to war, which fits **cascaded processing**.
16|The bear and pear eye-tracking result is one piece of evidence for **cascaded processing** in speech perception. A **thresholded model** has a hard time explaining it. >> The number of looks to the other member of the **minimal pair** increases gradually toward the category boundary. >> A minimal pair is two words that differ in only one sound, such as bear and pear. >> The increase holds even when the final response is the same. So it comes from graded activation within one category. || **In a thresholded model**, graded feature activity never reaches the phoneme level. >> Feature activation keeps swinging between +voiced and −voiced. >> Phoneme activation stays flat until /b/ suddenly switches on. >> So the phoneme level cannot know how close a sound came to the boundary. >> Every token in a category sends the **same finished phoneme** to the word level. >> Word-level competition would then be the same for every /b/. Nothing would produce gradually changing competitor looks. || **In a cascaded model**, graded information reaches the word level. >> More support for the competing phoneme passes along more support for its word. || That is why the gradual rise in competitor looks favors cascaded processing.
17|The **TRACE** model also includes **feedback from the lexical layer to the phoneme layer**. Activity runs up from phonemes to words and back down from words to phonemes. >> **Lexical feedback** means that an active word sends support back to its own phonemes. >> An uncertain sound can partly activate a word. That word can then strengthen the reading of the sound that fits it. || So influence runs in both directions. >> Acoustic evidence changes which words are active. >> Active words change the balance between possible phonemes while the phoneme decision is still forming. >> This helps listeners recover from noisy or ambiguous input. || **Feedback is different from cascading**. >> **Cascading** passes incomplete evidence forward, from features to phonemes and from phonemes to words. >> **Feedback** sends influence from words back down to phonemes. >> An interactive model can include both. || Some experiments motivate adding this kind of feedback. In them, knowledge of words changes how sounds are heard, as in the **Ganong effect**.
18|**Ganong (1980)** looked at how **phoneme identification is affected by word knowledge**. The finding is called the **Ganong effect**. >> Ganong tested whether knowing which sound sequences are real words changes how an ambiguous sound is heard. >> “Gisses and kifts for Valentine’s day” plays on the effect. It swaps the voicing of the first consonants in “kisses and gifts”. || Ganong made **word and nonword pairs** such as “corpse/gorpse”. One end of each continuum is a real word and the other end is a nonword. >> **For the /k/ interpretation**, the result is corpse, which is a familiar word. >> **For the /g/ interpretation**, the result is gorpse, which is not a word. >> The continua spanned the relevant phoneme boundary in **5 millisecond steps** of VOT. || The design combines **uncertain phonetic evidence** with a **difference in lexical status**. >> The rest of the word, “orpse”, is the same at every step. It tells the listener which beginning would complete a real word. >> If word knowledge affects identification, an ambiguous first consonant should more often be heard as the sound that makes a word. || So the important test is in the **ambiguous middle** of the continuum. Word knowledge has the most room to act where the sound is ambiguous.
19|A **late “correction” process** is one prediction for Ganong’s continua. In this account, **categorical perception functions as usual**, but there is a **bias to report having heard a word**. >> The listener first categorizes the sound with no help from word knowledge. >> At the response stage, the listener leans toward the option that forms a real word. || Three kinds of continua are compared. >> **For word-nonword continua**, the voiced end forms a word. >> **For nonword-word continua**, the voiceless end forms a word. >> **For neutral continua**, neither end has a lexical advantage. || This account predicts a **general reporting bias**. A bias added after perception pushes responses toward the word by the same amount everywhere. >> The word-nonword responses are shifted up everywhere. At the long VOT end, voiced responses stay near 0.25 and do not fall to 0. >> The nonword-word responses are shifted down. At the short VOT end, voiced responses start near 0.75 and not at 1. >> So even a clearly voiceless sound would sometimes be reported as voiced when the voiced version makes a word. || The **lexical effect** is the difference in voiced responses between the two lexical conditions. >> In this account, the lexical effect is **the same size at every step**, near 0.25. >> So perception and word knowledge stay separate, and word knowledge only nudges the reported answer.
20|A second prediction is that **word knowledge shifts the categorical boundary**. In this account, word knowledge changes where the listener switches from one phoneme category to the other. >> Near an ambiguous onset, the interpretation that forms a real word gets extra support. >> So the effect changes categorization itself, not only the reported answer. || This account predicts that the lexical conditions differ only in the middle of the continuum. >> **At both ends**, the sound is clear, so every condition gives the same answer. Voiced responses start near 1 at short VOT and end near 0 at long VOT. >> **Near the boundary**, the word-nonword condition crosses later and the nonword-word condition crosses earlier. || The **lexical effect** forms a **peak**. >> It is near 0 at the endpoints and rises to about 0.35 for the items closest to the boundary. >> The effect is **strongest for the items closest to the category boundary**. Those are the stimuli for which lexical information can help resolve ambiguity about the initial phoneme. >> At the endpoints, strong acoustic evidence already settles the consonant, so word knowledge has little room to help. || So where the effect occurs matters. A **flat effect** points to a reporting bias. A **peaked effect** points to a shift in the boundary itself.
21|**Ganong’s results** are strikingly consistent with the **second model**, which is the boundary-shift account. >> **At the short and long ends** of the VOT continuum, the word-nonword and nonword-word conditions give nearly the same answers. >> **Near the boundary**, the two conditions separate the most. >> The **lexical effect** rises from about 0.1 at the endpoints to a peak near **0.3** at the boundary. || A peak at the boundary rules out a simple reporting bias. Word knowledge matters most where the acoustic evidence is weakest. >> At the boundary, the word that fits tips the balance. >> At the endpoints, the acoustic evidence decides the answer alone. || Ganong raised a number of possibilities for how this could happen. >> He focused on different kinds of **guessing strategies**. >> A guessing strategy would let listeners use word knowledge only when they were unsure about the sound. || Later models emphasize the potential role of **feedback** in these shifts. >> In **interactive models**, an active word sends support back to its own phonemes. >> So the whole-word representation can affect how an ambiguous part of the word is interpreted during recognition.
22|A **simulation of the Ganong effect in the TRACE model** tracks **phoneme-level and word-level activations** as the input unfolds. >> The input begins with a sound **ambiguous between /b/ and /p/**. >> Then come /l/, the vowel /ʌ/ and /g/, which together make the word **plug**. >> Activations are recorded at four moments, called frames. || **In the first frame**, only the ambiguous first sound has arrived. >> /b/ and /p/ are both weakly active at about the same level, near 0.15 to 0.2. >> Word activation is still very low. || **In the second and third frames**, /l/ and the vowel arrive. >> Partial /b/ evidence supports words beginning with bl. Partial /p/ evidence supports words beginning with pl. >> **Plug**, **blood** and **blush** gain activation together, because all three fit the beginning heard so far. >> Phoneme activity for /p/ starts to pull ahead of /b/. || **In the fourth frame**, the final **/g/** arrives. >> The /g/ fits plug but does not fit the endings of blood or blush. >> **Plug** becomes the most active word, at about 0.45, while the alternatives fall away. >> So **/p/ ends up more active than /b/** at the phoneme level.
23|In the TRACE simulation, **activity accumulates for “plug”** as the input unfolds over time. The biggest gain comes when the **final /g/** arrives. >> The /g/ is highly inconsistent with “blood” and “blush”, which were also activated in Frame 3. >> Blood and blush fit the early part of the input, so they were plausible candidates for a while. >> Once /g/ arrives, they lose support because their endings do not match. >> Plug wins because only plug matches the full sequence of sounds. || The model explains **word effects on phoneme category boundaries** in terms of **top-down processing**. Top-down processing means that higher-level knowledge influences lower-level analysis. >> **Activity from the lexical level produces feedback to the phoneme level**. >> The feedback gives **greater activation to the phoneme consistent with a word**. >> Plug is the winning word, so /p/ receives extra support. || So the ambiguous first sound comes to be heard as /p/. Information that arrives at the end of a word can affect how an earlier sound is interpreted.
24|The **TRACE** model is a version of the **Interactive Activation model** for **written word recognition**. That model comes from **McClelland and Rumelhart (1981)**, and many of the principles are the same. >> **Feature** units connect to **letter** units, and letter units connect to **word** units such as WEAR, WORK, WORD and WEAK. >> Compatible units at different levels support each other. >> Competing units at the same level inhibit each other. >> Active words send support back down to their own letters. || Suppose a reader sees **W, O and R** clearly, while ink blots cover part of the last letter. >> The visible features of the last letter are consistent with both **R** and **K**. >> W, O and R activate words such as WORK and WORD. >> Only WORK fits the visible features of the last letter, so WORK becomes the strongest word. >> WORK sends support back to K, so the ambiguous letter is **perceived as K**. This parallels plug supporting /p/ in speech. || A key issue in speech perception is that **the stimuli unfold over time**. >> A written word is all there at once. A spoken word arrives one sound after another. >> TRACE handles this by repeating feature, phoneme and word units at every point in time. >> So a candidate can fit the beginning, gain activation and then weaken when the ending conflicts.
25|**Categorical perception** means that perception is shaped by **category boundaries**. Given the same physical difference, it is **harder to distinguish stimuli from the same category** than from different categories. >> Equal acoustic steps have different perceptual effects depending on whether they cross a boundary. >> Cascading and feedback do not undermine this basic finding. || Two findings about the **lexical-phonetic interface** add to it. >> **Ambiguity about phoneme identity does have an impact on word recognition**. Sounds with the same label still differ in how strongly they activate a competing word. >> **Word-level information seems to have an impact on phoneme categorization**. In the Ganong effect, word knowledge shifts the category boundary. >> In TRACE, that shift happens through lexical feedback to the phoneme level. || So the word recognition system **cannot just sit there waiting** for the phoneme recognition system to finish its job. A lumber mill chain would work that way. >> **Spoken word recognition is more interactive** than this, more like the food web. >> Partial information passes forward while uncertainty is still being resolved. >> Active words send support back down at the same time. >> So labels can be categorical while the processing behind them is graded.

27|**Spoken word recognition is also very fast**. **Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** used a visual world eye-tracking task to measure how quickly candidate words become active. >> Participants saw a grid of pictures and geometric shapes. They followed spoken instructions to move objects. >> The display included a **beaker**, a **beetle**, a **speaker** and a **carriage**. || The pictures were chosen for their sound overlap with the target word beaker. >> **Beaker** is the **referent**, which is the target. >> **Beetle** is a **cohort competitor**, because it shares the beginning of the word. >> **Speaker** is a **rhyme competitor**, because it shares the ending of the word. >> **Carriage** is **unrelated** and shares no sounds with beaker. || The measure is the probability of fixating each item over the first 1,000 milliseconds after the word begins. The spoken word ends a little before 400 milliseconds on average. >> **For beaker and beetle**, looks rise together from around 200 milliseconds, because both fit the beginning. >> **For beetle**, looks peak near 0.2 around 500 milliseconds. Then they fall once the distinguishing sounds arrive. >> **For beaker**, looks keep climbing, past 0.8 by about 800 milliseconds and close to 1 by 1,000 milliseconds. >> **For speaker**, looks rise later and stay smaller, with a peak near 0.1. >> **For carriage**, looks stay near zero. || So recognition begins **while the word is still unfolding**. Words that match the input become active, and they drop out when they stop matching.
28|The eye-tracking data can be compared with a **TRACE simulation** of the same task. The comparison is between real fixation probability and the fixation probability that TRACE predicts over 1,000 milliseconds. >> **For the referent**, the predicted and real looks both rise steadily from about 300 milliseconds. Both approach 1 by 1,000 milliseconds. >> **For the cohort competitor**, both rise early, peak near 0.17 to 0.2 around 500 milliseconds, and then fall. >> **For the rhyme competitor**, both rise later and stay small, with a peak near 0.1. >> So the TRACE predictions closely match the eye movements. || The **granularity** of the simulation is fine. It predicts activity moment by moment, and not only the final choice. >> **Eye movements give an idea of the changes in “activity”** for different competitors over time. >> That is why it is possible to **see cognitive processing before a final response is issued**. || This parallels the **graded effects of category goodness** in the categorical perception task. >> Sounds that are better or worse examples of a category produce different amounts of competitor activity. >> This happens even when the final label is the same.
