2|**Word segmentation** is the job of finding where one word ends and the next begins in continuous speech. Speech is **messy** because **there are no physical boundaries between words** in the signal. >> In “I hope it’s in my desk,” the sound energy of each word flows straight into the next word. >> No silent gap separates the words from one another. || The pauses that do occur come from **closures**. A **closure** is a moment when the vocal tract is fully blocked, as for /p/, /t/, /d/ and /k/. >> Very little sound escapes during a closure, so a brief silence appears. >> Closures are **as likely to happen within words as between words**. >> In “hope” and “it’s,” the silences fall inside words or at their edges, so they do not match the word boundaries. >> A listener who treated every silence as a boundary would split some words and merge others. || Listeners are still **really good at finding words** in this “sea of sounds.” >> Adults hear separate words with no effort, because they can lean on the words they already know. >> The **harder question** is **how we learn to do this**. Infants have to discover the words in the stream before they can learn what those words mean. || So silence does not mark the words, and infants have to find them some other way.
3|Listeners have **several cues to word boundaries**. No single cue works for every word. || Some words **appear in isolation**, as in “Hello,” “No,” “Please,” “Go” and “Stop.” >> A word that is a whole utterance has obvious boundaries. >> **Not all words** are heard alone, so this cue covers only part of the vocabulary. || **Phonotactics** is the set of patterns for which sound sequences a language allows. **Some sequences of phonemes are very unlikely to occur within words**. >> In “wordboundary,” the sequence /db/ almost never occurs inside an English word, so it suggests a break between word and boundary. >> In “speechcode,” the sequence /tʃk/ suggests a break between speech and code in the same way. >> “Movement” calls for caution. Its /vm/ sequence might suggest a break, but movement is one word. || **Stress** is the emphasis that makes one syllable louder, longer or higher in pitch. **In English, most words are stressed on the first syllable**. >> **Jusczyk and colleagues (1999)** first familiarized **7.5-month-old infants** with one word. Then the infants heard passages. >> The kingdom passage kept using “kingdom,” as in “Your kingdom is in a faraway place.” The device passage kept using “device,” as in “Your device can do a lot.” >> For infants familiarized with **“kingdom,”** which has first-syllable stress, there was a **preference for the kingdom passage**. >> For infants familiarized with **“device,”** which has second-syllable stress, there was **not the same kind of preference**. >> An infant who starts a new word at every stressed syllable finds kingdom easily but cuts device in the wrong place. || So in connected speech, **stress may help signal the beginnings of words**. It will **not work in every language or for every word in English**, because languages put stress in different places.
4|**Statistical learning** means picking up patterns in how often things occur together. **Transitional probabilities** give the learner one more cue to word boundaries through statistical learning. || The phrase “pretty baby” has one pair of syllables inside a word and one pair across two words. >> In English words, the syllable **/ti/** is **very strongly predicted by the syllable /prɪ/**. After “pre,” the next syllable is very often “ty,” because pretty is a common word. >> Syllable pairs **across words** are less predictable. Many different words can follow “ty,” so “ba” is poorly predicted. || The comparison can be written with frequencies of occurrence. >> The frequency of “pretty” divided by the frequency of “pre” gives the chance that “pre” is followed by “ty.” >> The frequency of “tyba” divided by the frequency of “ty” gives the chance that “ty” is followed by “ba.” >> The first value is **far larger** than the second. || Syllables inside a word predict each other **strongly**. Syllables that straddle two words predict each other **weakly**. >> Pairs inside a word recur together again and again. >> Pairs that cross a word boundary recur much less reliably, because each word can be followed by many others. || So a learner who counts how often each syllable follows each other syllable can find the joints in speech.
5|The **transitional probability** from syllable A to syllable B is the likelihood that B follows A. That likelihood is **greater within words than between words**. >> Inside a word, the same syllables always come in the same order, so the probability is high. >> At a word boundary, almost any word can come next, so the probability is lower. >> A drop in transitional probability is a good guess for a word boundary. || The cue is **probabilistic**, so it works most of the time but not every time. >> “What’s that in the road a/head?” can be heard as “a head” or as “ahead.” The sounds are identical, so the meaning of the whole sentence decides the boundary. >> “I scream you scream we all scream for ice cream” plays on “I scream” and “ice cream” having almost the same sounds. One sequence can be split into words two ways. || Transitional probabilities tip the odds toward one way of splitting the stream. They work alongside stress, phonotactics and words heard alone. >> When the cues agree, segmentation is easy. >> When the cues clash or are missing, as with “a head” and “ahead,” listeners fall back on context. || So transitional probability is a cue to a boundary, and it is not a marked boundary.
6|**Saffran and colleagues (1996)** tested whether **transitional probabilities** alone are enough to identify words. They separated **statistical information** from every other cue, so the stimuli were **tightly controlled**. || Each control removed one cue. >> The speech was **monotone**, with no changes in pitch, duration or amplitude. Amplitude means how loud the speech is. Without those changes, stress could not mark where words begin. >> Every syllable was a **simple CV syllable**, which is one consonant followed by one vowel. No unusual consonant clusters occurred, so there were **no phonotactic cues** to word boundaries. >> The syllables were **presented continuously** with **no pauses**, so silence could not signal a boundary. || The researchers built an **artificial language** from a set of **three-syllable “words,”** such as tokibu, gikoba and gopila. >> The words were joined in random order into one long stream. >> The only clue to where each word began and ended was the transitional probability between syllables. || So infants who find the words in this stream must be using transitional probabilities alone, because every other cue was removed.
7|The stream of the artificial language has **no breaks at all**. It runs like “tokibugikobagopilatipolutokibu” and “gopilatipolutokibugikobagopila” and “gikobatokibugopilatipolugikoba.” >> The infant hears exactly this, with no spaces and no pauses. >> **Four words** are buried in the stream. They are tokibu, gikoba, gopila and tipolu. >> Each word recurs many times in random order. || The syllables still follow **regular patterns**. >> Inside **tokibu**, “to” is always followed by “ki.” Then “ki” is always followed by “bu.” >> After “bu,” the stream may go on with “gi,” “go” or “ti.” The next syllable depends on which word comes next. || Some syllable pairs are **fully predictable** and others are not. >> A learner who keeps track of which syllables follow which can discover the difference. >> The order of the syllables is the only signal of where words begin and end. || So everything needed to find the words is in the statistics of the stream.
8|Marking each of the four words separately makes its position in the stream visible to a reader. With one color for each word, tokibu is red, gikoba is green, gopila is pink and tipolu is purple. >> **Tokibu**, **gikoba**, **gopila** and **tipolu** each appear many times. >> The infant gets no such marking. The infant hears only one unbroken run of syllables. || Each word keeps the **same inner order** every time, but its **neighbors keep changing**. >> “To-ki-bu” always comes in that order. >> Tokibu may be followed by gikoba, gopila or tipolu. >> So transitional probabilities are **high inside** each word and **lower across word edges**. || The word edges are exactly where **transitional probability falls**. >> The signal itself contains no spaces. >> The learner notices where predictability drops. || That is why the learner can recover where one word ends and the next begins.
9|A test for an effect of **transitional probabilities** has to rule out plain familiarity. After a long stream of syllables, infants are trivially more familiar with sequences they have heard than with ones they have not. >> Infants could prefer heard sequences only because they are familiar. >> That preference would not involve transitional probabilities at all. || **Two additional details** remove that possibility. >> **Half of the words were repeated more frequently** than the other half. >> The familiarity test compared words against **“part-words”** that were **matched for frequency of occurrence** with real words. >> A **part-word** is a three-syllable sequence that spans a word boundary. It joins the end of one word to the start of the next. || Take the stream “tokibu gikoba gopila gikoba tokibu gopila tokibu gikoba gikoba tokibu.” >> The word **GOPILA** and the part-word **BU-GIKO** each occur **twice**, so they are equally frequent. >> For **BU-GIKO**, the probability of GI given BU is **2 out of 4**. BU occurs four times, at the end of every tokibu, and GI follows only two of them. >> For **GOPILA**, both transitional probabilities are **2 out of 2**. GO is always followed by PI, and PI is always followed by LA. || So frequency is equal and only predictability differs. Any difference between gopila and bu-giko must come from transitional probabilities.
10|The test produced a **novelty effect**. Infants **looked longer in the direction of the “part-word” stimuli**. >> Words got about 6.8 seconds of looking. Part-words got about 7.6 seconds. >> The difference was statistically significant. || Longer looking at part-words is a **novelty preference**. Infants tend to look longer at whatever seems new. >> The words had grown familiar during listening, so they were less interesting. >> The part-words felt less familiar, even though infants had heard them just as often. || Frequency was equal for words and part-words. Only their transitional probabilities could separate them. >> The infants noticed that the syllables in part-words do not reliably go together. >> After a brief exposure, infants pulled units out of a continuous stream using only how predictable the syllables were. || So the infants **had learned the statistical regularities of the speech stream**. They were **not merely picking up on simple frequency of occurrence**.
11|A **referent** is whatever a word refers to. **Identifying word referents** is the problem that comes after segmentation. >> Children can now “find” the words in speech. They still have to learn what the words mean. >> A child may hear “cat” while looking at a cat. >> Recognizing the sound pattern “cat” is one achievement. Knowing that it names the furry animal is a separate one. || **Segmentation** delivers a recurring chunk of sound, but it gives **no information about meaning**. >> Any scene holds many objects, parts, properties and actions. >> The learner needs more evidence to tell which of these the speaker has in mind. || Children learn what a word means by **linking its form to the speaker’s intended meaning**. >> The child gathers that evidence from the situation in which the word is used. || So finding a word and learning its meaning are two separate achievements.
12|**Induction** is reasoning from particular examples to a general rule. In word learning, the rule is the meaning of a word. The **inductive problem of early word learning** is that a single experience fits many possible meanings. || A child hears a new word while watching a cat. >> The word might name a category, such as **CAT**, **ANIMAL** or **PET**. These are categories at different levels. >> The word might be **FELIX**, the name of this particular cat. >> The word might name a property, such as **FURRY** or **ALIVE**. >> The word might name an action, such as **WALKING**. >> The word might name a part, such as **WHISKERS** or **LEG**. || All the candidate meanings together make up the **hypothesis space**. >> Each candidate fits what the child sees. >> Nothing in one scene singles out the meaning the speaker intends. || This is a form of **Quine’s “gavagai” problem**. >> A linguist studying an unknown language hears a speaker say “gavagai” as a rabbit runs by. >> “Gavagai” might mean rabbit. It might also mean undetached rabbit parts, a stage of a rabbit or something else. || Children meet this problem with every new word and still learn words fast. So something must narrow the possibilities. Joint attention, cues to the speaker’s intention and default strategies do that narrowing.
13|**Joint attention** is sharing an attentional focus with another person. It is one way children acquire word meanings. >> Two people look at the same object. >> Each person knows that the other is attending to it too. || **Gaze following** is looking where someone else is looking. It **emerges around 9 months of age**. >> An adult looks over at an object. >> The infant follows the adult’s eyes to that object. || Gaze following **signals an understanding of reference**. >> The infant grasps that the adult’s attention is aimed at something specific. >> If the adult then says a word, the shared object becomes the most likely referent. || So joint attention shrinks the **hypothesis space**, which is the set of meanings a word might have. It ties the word to what the speaker is attending to.
14|**Joint attention optimizes word learning**. **Baldwin and colleagues** tested this with two groups of infants who heard a novel word while playing with an object. || The two groups differed in who said the word. >> In the **joint attention group**, an adult interacting with the baby labeled the object the baby was playing with. >> In the **no joint attention group**, a voice from behind a screen labeled the object the baby was playing with. || The two conditions matched closely. >> In each one, the word came while the infant was focused on the same object. >> They differed only in whether a visible person was engaged with the infant and the object. || **Eighteen-month-olds learned the novel word in the joint attention group**. They did **not** learn it in the no joint attention group. >> The hidden voice gave the same word at the same moment. So the difference in learning came from the visible, engaged speaker. >> Hearing a word while seeing an object was not enough. || So infants need evidence that a speaker means the word to refer to that object.
15|An **intentional cue** is a signal of what the speaker means to refer to. Children use **intentional cues from the speaker** to narrow the **hypothesis space** for new words. >> **Eye gaze direction** is one such cue. >> When a speaker looks toward one toy, the direction of the eyes marks the object of the speaker’s attention. || **Baldwin** taught new words to infants of **16 to 17 months** and infants of **18 to 19 months** in two conditions. >> In **follow-in labeling**, the adult looked at the toy the infant was focused on while saying the new word. Both cues pointed to one toy. >> In **discrepant labeling**, the adult looked at a toy in a bucket while saying the new word. That toy was different from the infant’s toy. || The discrepant condition pulls the infant’s focus apart from the speaker’s focus. >> Infants who attach a new word to whatever they are looking at would link it to their own toy. >> Infants who follow the speaker’s gaze would link it to the toy in the bucket. || So comparing the two conditions reveals whether infants treat the speaker’s gaze as evidence of meaning.
16|**Gaze direction** helps a child work out which object a speaker is naming when several objects are present. >> A speaker who looks at one object while saying a new word ties the word to that object. >> The child can follow the speaker’s eyes to the **intended referent**. || The speaker’s focus can be **different from the child’s own focus**. >> Following the speaker’s focus means using information about someone else’s attention. >> The child can then attach a new word to an object it was not looking at when the word was spoken. || The sound of the word gives no clue to its meaning. Gaze still narrows the possible meanings. >> The child combines the new word with evidence of what the speaker intends. >> Following gaze rests on understanding that a speaker’s eyes point to what the speaker means. || That is why gaze gives a far **more specific mapping** than linking the word to everything in view.
17|**Intentional cues from the speaker** include **affective and behavioral cues**. **Affective cues** are emotional expressions. **Behavioral cues** are the speaker’s actions. >> An expression can reveal whether an object is the one the speaker was searching for. >> An action, such as reaching for something, can reveal what the speaker wants. || **Akhtar and colleagues** found that infants **used adults’ facial expressions to identify the intended referent of a novel word**. >> The adult’s emotional reaction to each object signaled whether it was the one meant. >> Infants linked the new word to the object that the adult’s expression picked out. || Several objects may be present when a word is spoken. The speaker’s reaction can single out one of them. >> For a **satisfied** expression, the speaker has found the desired object. >> For a **disappointed** expression, the object is not the one the speaker meant. || So word learning involves grasping the goal behind the interaction.
18|A speaker’s **emotional reaction** works as an **intentional cue**. It can pick out the intended object from several candidates. >> Satisfaction or excitement can tie one object to what the speaker was trying to find or do. >> The learner can use that tie to interpret a new label. || **Intention is conveyed through more than gaze direction**. >> Facial expressions and actions also reveal what a speaker means. >> A word may be said near several objects that are not all equally likely referents. || The helpful information lies in **how the adult responds to each object**. >> Connecting the label to that response shrinks the word’s **hypothesis space**, the set of meanings it might have. >> Infants who use emotional reactions are following the speaker’s goals. They understand that the speaker is searching for something and that the word names what the speaker wants. || So the word attaches to the object that meets the goal. Objects that disappoint the speaker drop out.
19|**Word learning strategies** are **default strategies that children bring to the word learning situation**. A **default** is a starting assumption that holds until evidence says otherwise. >> These strategies **function to narrow the hypothesis space for new words**. >> They give children a place to start when other cues are weak. || The **whole object assumption** is one such strategy. It leads a child to expect a new word to name **a whole object**. >> The child does not expect the word to name a part of the object, its color, its texture or something it is doing. >> A child who hears a new word while looking at a cat will favor “cat” over “whiskers,” “furry” or “walking.” || The assumption rules out many unlikely meanings at once. It also fits the way adults usually name objects for children. >> It is only a starting preference, so later evidence can override it. >> Children do eventually learn words for parts and properties once the evidence points that way. || So the whole object assumption speeds learning by removing many unlikely meanings before the child considers them.
20|**Mutual exclusivity**, from **Markman (1989)**, is another word learning strategy. It says that, **as a default, objects can have only one name**. >> The implication is that **new words map to nameless objects**. >> Mutual exclusivity **emerges during the second half of the second year**, roughly between 18 and 24 months. || Suppose a child knows the word “dog” but has no word for a parrot, and both animals are in view. >> The child hears a new word. >> The dog already has a name, so the child takes the new word to mean the parrot. >> No gaze cue or emotional cue is needed for this choice. || Mutual exclusivity combines with the **whole object assumption**. >> The whole object assumption says a new word names an object. >> Mutual exclusivity says it names an object that has no name yet. || Mutual exclusivity is a default. So later learning can override it when an object turns out to have two names.
21|With **mutual exclusivity**, a child who hears an unfamiliar word gives it to an object that has **no known name yet**. >> An object the child can already name is an unlikely target for a new word. >> The choice comes from comparing the objects in view. || **Prior vocabulary** determines which objects count as already named. >> Two children in the same scene can guess differently if they know different words. >> The strategy builds directly on earlier learning. || Vocabulary growth feeds itself. >> A child who knows many names can rule out many objects. >> The unnamed objects left over become the likeliest referents for new words. || So every word the child knows helps narrow the meaning of the next new word.
22|Children start putting words together in ways that are hard to tell apart. In production, it is difficult to tell what is going on when children produce only one, two or three words at a time. >> Very different processes can produce the same short utterance. || **Menn** distinguishes among four kinds of early utterances. >> **“Just a bunch of relevant words”** means the child strings together words that fit the situation, with no grammar linking them. >> **Formulas** are phrases memorized as whole units. The child does not know they have separate parts. >> **Word combinations** are separate words that the child joins productively to make new combinations. >> **Stable constructions** are consistent patterns the child uses to build utterances. They are **not necessarily adult-like**. || A child might say “Up!” and “Pick you up!” and “Mommy up!” in the same kind of situation. >> “Pick you up!” sounds more advanced, but it may be a formula copied whole from adult speech. >> “Mommy up!” is shorter, but it may be a real combination of two separate words. >> Utterance length alone cannot tell which process produced each one. || A **full description of the context** in which an utterance is produced is **necessary but often not sufficient** to distinguish among these. >> Context reveals what the child intends and how the utterance is used across situations. >> Even with complete context, the source of a given utterance can remain uncertain. || So a short utterance alone does not tell you whether a child is really combining words.
23|**Comprehension** and **production** develop at different rates. Even children at the **one-word stage** in production can use **basic transitive word order** in comprehension. >> In the one-word stage, a child speaks only in single words. >> A **transitive** sentence has a verb with a subject and an object. >> English transitive order puts the actor first and the one acted on after the verb. || **Gertner and Fisher** describe how children do this with the **structure-mapping account** of early **syntactic bootstrapping**. Syntactic bootstrapping means using sentence structure to help learn meaning. >> The input sentence is “The boy is tickling the girl!” and the child builds a **partial sentence representation** from it. It records the verb tickle with boy as the first noun and girl as the second noun. >> The observed scene is a boy tickling a girl. From it, the child builds **conceptual representations** of possible events. >> One event is giggle with the girl as agent. Another is tickle with the boy as agent and the girl as patient. Two more are play with the boy as agent and play with the girl as agent. >> An **agent** performs an action, and a **patient** is affected by it. >> A **one-to-one mapping between nouns and participant roles** lines up the two nouns with the two participants in the tickling event. Two nouns fit an event with two participants. || So comprehension can get ahead of production. A child who says only single words can still use noun order to tell who did what. Matching nouns to the participants in a scene also helps the child figure out what the verb means.
24|A **generalization test** checks whether children have really learned the **transitive structure**. If they have, they will **generalize it to new verbs**. >> A child who has only memorized particular sentences will be lost with an unfamiliar verb. >> A child who knows the structure can use word order to interpret any verb. || The test sentence is “The duck is gorping the bunny.” The child has to select which of two novel actions it could depict. >> **“Gorp”** is invented, so the meaning of the verb gives no help. >> The child needs to know that the first noun is the doer and the second noun is the one acted on. || **Gertner and colleagues (2006)** ran this test with **two-year-olds**. >> Children first heard “Hey look! The duck’s gonna gorp the bunny!” before any scene appeared. >> Two scenes then appeared side by side. In one scene, a person dressed as a duck pushed a person dressed as a bunny in a wagon. In the other scene, the bunny acted on the duck. >> Children heard “The duck is gorping the bunny! See?” with the scenes. After a short break they heard “The duck gorped the bunny! Find gorping!” and the scenes appeared again. >> The two-year-olds **looked significantly longer at the duck acting on the rabbit** than at the reverse. So they understood the sentence from noun order alone. || Children at this age typically have a **mean length of utterance (MLU)** of **around 2**. MLU is the average number of **morphemes** in each utterance, and morphemes are meaningful words or word parts. >> Their three-morpheme utterances might be like “Duck gorp bunny.” >> That is why their own speech is short and incomplete while they already understand the full transitive structure.
