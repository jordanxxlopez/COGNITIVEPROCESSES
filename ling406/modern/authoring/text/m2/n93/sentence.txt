2|“The horse raced past the barn fell” is a grammatical English sentence that usually feels broken on first reading. The opening words push the reader toward the wrong structure. >> Most readers first take **raced** as the **main verb**. Then “the horse raced past the barn” seems to be a complete event in which the horse did the racing. >> When **fell** arrives, that structure has no place for it. The sentence seems to fall apart. || The sentence describes **two events**. Someone raced the horse past the barn, and the horse then fell. >> The intended meaning is “The horse that was raced past the barn fell”. >> In that reading, “raced past the barn” tells which horse is meant. **Fell** is the main verb, and it says what happened to that horse. || Putting back the missing words **disambiguates** the sentence. >> Adding “that was” makes the modifying relationship explicit. Raced can no longer be taken as the main verb. >> Understanding a sentence depends on assigning relationships among its words, not only on knowing what each word means. || Researchers have used this sentence again and again to study how readers build structure as words arrive. That is why it could be called **the sentence that launched a thousand experiments**.
3|“The horse raced past the barn fell” is almost as famous as “Colorless green ideas sleep furiously”. >> “Colorless green ideas sleep furiously” is grammatical but meaningless. >> “The horse raced past the barn fell” is grammatical and meaningful, but it is very hard to process. || The horse sentence is the classic case of the **“garden path” effect**. A **garden path sentence** leads the reader into one interpretation early. Later words reveal that this reading is wrong. >> The name comes from the expression “to lead someone down the garden path”, which means to mislead them. >> Raced looks like the main verb. Fell then conflicts with the structure already built. >> The reader has to go back and reinterpret “raced past the barn” as “that was raced past the barn”. || Garden paths happen because readers build structure **word by word**. >> Readers choose one analysis before the sentence is complete. >> If they waited until the end of the sentence, they would never be led astray. || A dinosaur comic jokes about the horse sentence. One dinosaur insists nobody would ever say it. The other replies that farmers could say it if their horses kept falling over. >> The joke works only because the sentence really is grammatical, even though it sounds wrong. || So **grammaticality and processing difficulty are different things**. A sentence can have an acceptable structure and still be very hard to follow as it unfolds.
4|**Syntactic explanations** of the garden path effect start with a **parser**. The parser is a mental process that builds a **syntactic tree** for each sentence we hear or read. >> A syntactic tree records how words group into phrases and how those phrases relate to one another. || **In the correct tree** for the horse sentence, the subject is a large noun phrase, or **NP**. It contains “the horse” plus a reduced clause meaning “that was raced past the barn”. >> “That” and “was” are silent in the actual sentence. The clause also has an empty subject position. >> Inside the clause, raced takes the prepositional phrase, or **PP**, “past the barn”. >> The main verb phrase, or **VP**, contains only **fell**. || **In the failed parse**, “the horse” is an ordinary NP. The main VP is “raced past the barn”, with raced as the main verb. >> **Fell** is then left with nowhere to attach, so the parse breaks down. || The parser runs inside a cognitive system with **limited memory** and **time constraints**. >> Words arrive quickly, so the parser cannot wait for the end of the sentence before building structure. >> It can keep only a limited number of structures available at once. || That is why an account of parsing has to explain how the parser handles an ambiguous sentence under these limits.
5|The **grammatical version** of the horse sentence is much more complex syntactically than the failed parse. It has **many more nodes**. >> A **node** is a point in a syntactic tree that stands for a grammatical grouping, such as an NP, a VP or a sentence. >> The correct tree needs extra nodes for the embedded clause and for the silent “that” and “was”. >> The failed parse makes “raced past the barn” the main VP, which takes far fewer nodes. || **“Minimal attachment”** is a strategy in which the parser **initially prefers smaller trees**. >> The parser fits each new word into the existing structure with the fewest possible new nodes. >> Fewer nodes means less structure to build and hold in memory at that moment, so the strategy saves space. || In the horse sentence, minimal attachment favors **raced as the main verb**, because that analysis needs the fewest nodes. >> The reading where “raced past the barn” modifies horse needs an embedded relative clause. So the parser passes it over at first. >> When fell arrives, it has no place in the small structure. The parser has to switch to the larger structure and rebuild the tree. || So the smaller tree lowers the immediate load, but it leads the reader down the garden path.
6|**“Late closure”** is another parsing strategy in this framework. It is a bias to **keep the current clause “open” as long as possible**. >> Keeping a clause open means adding incoming words to it instead of closing it and starting a new structure. >> So new words attach to the phrase currently being built. || **Frazier (1987)** contrasts two structures for “The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite”. The phrase “with the dynamite” wants to associate with the big **verb phrase** starting with “blew open”. >> **In the preferred structure**, the PP attaches to the **VP**. This is **verb-phrase attachment**, and it makes the dynamite the **instrument** the burglar used to blow open the safe. >> **In the unpreferred structure**, the PP attaches to the **NP** “the safe”. This is **noun-phrase attachment**. It makes the phrase describe which safe was blown open, the one that had the dynamite. || Both attachments are grammatical, but they express different relationships. >> Late closure keeps the verb phrase open, so the incoming phrase joins it. >> Minimal attachment prefers the same attachment, because it needs fewer nodes than building an extra NP around “the safe with the dynamite”. || So both strategies favor the VP attachment. For this sentence the preferred parse is also the sensible one, so readers reach the right meaning easily.
7|Late closure can be seen at work in a sentence that needs the **“unpreferred” parse** to be sensible. The sentence is “The burglar blew open the safe with the jewels”. >> Jewels are an implausible tool for blowing open a safe. >> The sensible meaning is that the burglar blew open **the safe that contained the jewels**. >> That meaning requires attaching “with the jewels” to the noun phrase “the safe”, which is the unpreferred NP attachment. || Careful tests of **reading time** and **eye movements** find that people are **slower** to read sentences where the sensible reading requires the unpreferred parse. >> Reading time measures how long readers spend on each part of a sentence. >> Eye movement recordings track where readers look and when they jump back to reread earlier words. >> The extra time appears at the point where the parse has to change. || Readers **momentarily entertain the idea** that the jewels were used to blow open the safe. Then they have to **retrace their steps**. >> The parser first attaches the phrase to the verb phrase, just as it did for the dynamite. >> The meaning of jewels conflicts with the instrument reading, so the parser moves the attachment to the noun phrase. || So the final meaning is easy to understand, but reaching it carries a measurable **processing cost**.
8|This syntactic explanation rests on a **parser that deals exclusively with the abstract syntactic structure** of the sentence. It parallels the **“lumber mill”** example from speech perception. >> In a lumber mill, each stage works on its own material and hands a finished product to the next stage. >> In the same way, the parser builds structure from grammatical categories alone, without consulting meaning or context. >> Meaning is worked out afterward from the finished structure. || The parser has **its own operating characteristics**. >> It wants to make the simplest tree possible, which is **minimal attachment**. >> It accomplishes this partly by leaving clauses open as long as possible, which is **late closure**. >> Both preferences limit how much structure must be built and held in memory at once. || This is a very efficient way of dealing with ambiguity. The parser **ignores ambiguity until it becomes a problem**. >> The parser commits to one likely structure and builds only that one. Most of the time the first choice is right, so no extra effort is needed. >> When a later word does not fit, the structure has to be revised. That revision is the source of garden path difficulty. || So garden paths are the price of an efficient strategy.
9|A **purely syntactic explanation** has a problem. Syntactically “difficult” sentences are **not always difficult to parse**. >> A **reduced relative clause** is a relative clause with “that was” or “that were” left out, as in “the horse raced past the barn fell”. >> Published research articles contain many reduced relatives. In each example here, the verb that introduces the reduced relative is written in capitals. || The examples come from four articles. >> Trueswell, Tanenhaus and Garnsey (1994) wrote “a noun phrase FOLLOWED by a MORPHOLOGICALLY AMBIGUOUS VERB … will be temporarily ambiguous”. >> Britt, Perfetti, Garrod and Rayner (1992) wrote “Referential information PROVIDED by the discourse is of no help”. >> Frazier and Rayner (1987) wrote “Recent research REPORTED by FERREIRA AND CLIFTON (1986) has demonstrated that syntactic processing is quite independent and that the initial syntactic analysis ASSIGNED to a sentence is little influenced by the semantic information ALREADY ANALYZED”. >> Altmann and Steedman (1988) wrote “The pattern of results OBTAINED with global reading times is reflected by differences LOCATED on the critical disambiguating phrase”. || Each capitalized verb begins a reduced relative with the same structure as the horse sentence. **But these are not impossibly difficult sentences**. Readers understand them easily, with no garden path. >> In “recent research reported by Ferreira and Clifton”, reported describes research that someone reported. Research is not the one doing the reporting. || **MacDonald, Perlmutter and Seidenberg (1994)** argue that **semantic factors and statistical regularities interact with “parsing” from very early on**. >> Semantic factors concern meaning. >> Statistical regularities are patterns in how often words appear in particular structures. >> So particular words and their familiar uses can support a reduced relative reading from the start, which makes the sentence easy to read.
10|The collection of reduced relative examples is **wonderfully passive-aggressive**. Many of the examples are drawn from papers that use the difficulty of parsing reduced relatives as evidence for an **autonomous parser**. >> An autonomous parser builds syntactic structure without help from meaning or context. >> The authors of those papers argued that reduced relatives are hard to process. >> Yet they wrote reduced relatives in their own articles, and readers understood them easily. || In each example, the capitalized verb modifies the noun before it. >> In “a noun phrase FOLLOWED by a morphologically ambiguous verb”, followed modifies noun phrase. >> In “the initial syntactic analysis ASSIGNED to a sentence”, assigned modifies analysis. >> In “differences LOCATED on the critical disambiguating phrase”, located modifies differences. >> Ferreira and Clifton (1986) wrote “A person PRESENTED with the evidence EXAMINED by the lawyer may interpret the evidence as a theme, not an agent, because examined requires an animate agent”. Presented modifies person, and examined modifies evidence. || These examples were **not the only reduced relatives** in those articles. >> Reduced relatives appeared at a rate of about **one per journal page** in this sample of articles. >> So reduced relatives are common in ordinary scientific writing. || Readers attach each modifier to the correct noun easily in context. That is why a formally complex structure can be easy to understand in a supportive context.
11|When we read “Recent research reported by…”, several factors besides syntax come into play. >> **Semantics** matters because research is an **inanimate** object, which means it is not a living thing. Research is unlikely to be the subject of a verb like report. So it is read as the thing that was reported. >> **Lexical statistics** matter because “reported” occurs frequently in reduced relatives. Lexical statistics are learned patterns of how often particular words appear in particular structures. >> Lexical statistics and **“world knowledge”** matter together. In scientific papers, “reported” occurs very frequently in reduced relatives, especially when following the word “research”. World knowledge here means knowing how people actually write in that setting. || These and many other factors **interact with the syntax of the sentence very early on**. >> They act from the first moments of reading. >> They help decide which structure is chosen in the first place. || Two sentences with identical grammar differ in difficulty. >> **For the burglar**, the sentence is “The burglar arrested in the parking lot was charged today”. Burglars are likely to be arrested, so arrested is easily read as a reduced relative describing the burglar. >> **For the policeman**, the sentence is “The policeman arrested in the parking lot was charged today”. Policemen are likely to do the arresting, so arrested is first taken as the main verb. Then “was charged” causes a garden path. || So the difference in difficulty comes from what we know about burglars and policemen, not from the grammar.
12|**“World knowledge” can be very local**. It can come from the visual scene in front of the listener as well as from general knowledge. || **Spivey and colleagues (1995)** tested this with a visual world task. >> Participants saw real objects, which were **a pencil, a box, an empty towel and an apple sitting on a towel**. >> They heard the instruction “Put the apple on the towel in the box”. They carried it out while their eye movements were recorded. >> The unambiguous comparison instruction was “Put the apple that’s on the towel in the box”. || With **minimal attachment**, “the towel” would temporarily seem like the end of the clause “put the apple on the towel”. >> In that reading, “on the towel” names the **destination**, the place the apple should go. >> In the intended reading, “on the towel” tells **which apple** to move, and “in the box” is the destination. || People **do look at the empty towel** while planning their movements, as though they are considering it as a target. >> **For the ambiguous instruction**, a typical sequence of looks goes from the apple to the empty towel and then to the box. The look to the apple comes near 1,000 milliseconds, and the look to the box comes near 2,000 milliseconds. >> **For the unambiguous instruction**, the looks go from the apple straight to the box. || So the listener recognizes every word correctly but briefly takes the location phrase as the destination. That briefly changes the planned action.
13|A **two-referent context** is a display that contains **two apples**. One apple sits on a towel, and the other sits elsewhere. The empty towel and the box are still present. >> Now “the apple” alone does not say which apple to move. >> The phrase “on the towel” becomes useful, because it picks out one apple from the two. || In this context, participants usually look first at the apple on the towel and then at the box. >> Looks to the empty towel are much less common. >> The **sequence and timing of eye movements**, measured relative to the nouns in the speech, **did not differ** between the ambiguous and unambiguous instructions. || The ambiguous instruction is processed just like the explicit instruction with “that’s on the towel”. >> With two apples present, the listener needs to know which apple, so a phrase that picks one out is expected. >> Listeners treat “on the towel” as a description of the apple from the start. The destination reading never takes hold. || So the same sentence gets a different grammatical analysis because the visual context is different.
14|With **two apples** in the display, it becomes **felicitous** to give more information about which apple the speaker means. Felicitous means appropriate and natural in the situation. >> **With one apple**, there is no need to say which apple. So “on the towel” seems like extra information and is easily taken as a destination. >> **With two apples**, “on the towel” has a clear job, which is to identify the right apple. || So people **do not look at the empty towel as much**. The measure is the proportion of trials with eye movements to the incorrect destination, the empty towel. >> **In the one-referent context**, ambiguous instructions led to such looks on about **55%** of trials. Unambiguous instructions led to almost none. >> **In the two-referent context**, ambiguous instructions led to such looks on only about **17%** of trials. That is close to the roughly 12% for unambiguous instructions. || The words of the instruction were identical in both contexts. So the visual context alone changed how often listeners were misled. >> This is an effect of **referential context**, which is the set of objects a phrase could refer to. >> Referential context influences which grammatical relationship listeners assign while the sentence is still being heard.
15|The Spivey results are **a big problem for purely syntactic accounts**. Different parses of the same sentence are favored under different **semantic and pragmatic conditions**, and the effect happens **very early**. >> **Semantic** conditions concern meaning. >> **Pragmatic** conditions concern how language is used in a situation, such as whether a speaker needs to specify which object is meant. || The same ambiguous sentence misleads listeners often with one apple and rarely with two apples. >> With one apple, it led to looks at the empty towel on about 55% of trials. With two apples, it led to them on only about 17%. >> The words are identical in both conditions. Only the visual situation changes, and it changes the parse the listener adopts first. || The difference appears in **eye movements during the sentence**, before the action is carried out. So the eye movements capture the parse while it is being built. >> If context only corrected the parse afterward, listeners in both contexts should first look at the empty towel. >> Those looks drop with two apples, so context must have shaped the **first parse**. || That is why a parser that always starts with the same structure cannot predict these results.
16|Researchers have more recently used **mouse movements** to study these questions. **Farmer and colleagues (2007)** tracked the path of the computer mouse while participants carried out spoken instructions. The display was a version of the apple and towel display. >> It is not just eye movements but **the actual execution of arm movements** that syntactic ambiguity can influence. The influence depends on how the ambiguity interacts with the **visually defined context**. >> Mouse tracking records the whole path of the hand. So a competing interpretation can pull the movement toward another location before the hand reaches its target. || **In the one-referent context**, the hand moved from the apple toward the box. On ambiguous instructions, its path **curved toward the empty towel**, compared with the path on unambiguous instructions. >> The two paths separated most in the middle of the movement. >> The hand still ended at the correct place. || **In the two-referent context**, the ambiguous and unambiguous paths were nearly identical. >> The second apple makes the location phrase useful for picking out an apple. So the phrase is no longer taken as a destination. || The researchers also measured the **Euclidean distance** between the ambiguous and unambiguous paths at each moment. Euclidean distance is the straight-line distance between two points. >> With one apple, the distance rose to a peak of about 200 pixels around 40% of the way through the movement. >> With two apples, it peaked lower, at about 140 pixels, and earlier, around 30%. || So comprehension and action planning interact while both are underway. A competing interpretation bends the path of the hand even when the movement ends correctly.

18|**The** is a **determiner**, which is a word that introduces a **noun phrase**. When the parser meets “the”, it expects a noun to follow. An adjective or other modifier may come first. >> “The” does not name anything by itself. >> It limits which kinds of words can come next, so the parser can anticipate the shape of the phrase. || The expectation is about a **grammatical category**, not about one particular word. >> Many different nouns could follow, such as pizza, dog or book. >> An adjective could also come first, as in “the hot pizza”. >> The parser predicts the form of the phrase while its meaning is still open. || So each word sets expectations for the next word. Words that fit the expectation are processed smoothly, and words that break it cause difficulty.
19|**Pizza** is a **noun**, and it completes the noun phrase begun by “the”. It is the **head of the noun phrase**, the central word that the rest of the phrase is organized around. >> “The” combines with “pizza” to form “the pizza”. That phrase can now serve as the subject of the sentence. >> With a complete noun phrase in place, the parser expects a verb or verb phrase. The verb would tell what the pizza did or what happened to it. || Recognizing the word makes two kinds of information available. >> Its **category**, noun, tells the parser how it can combine with other words. >> Its **meaning**, a kind of food, sets up expectations about plausible events, such as being eaten or baked. || So both grammatical expectations and meaning-based expectations guide how the next words are processed.
20|**Was** is a past-tense form of the verb **be**. It can act as an **auxiliary**, which is a helping verb that combines with another verb to mark grammatical information such as tense or voice. >> In “the pizza was eaten”, was helps form a **passive** with the participle eaten. >> In “the pizza was in the oven”, was links the subject to a phrase giving a location. || When “was” arrives, the sentence could continue in several ways. It could describe where the pizza was, what it was like or what was done to it. >> A location phrase would follow with a preposition. >> A passive would follow with a participle such as eaten. >> An adjective could also follow. || The grammatical role of was depends partly on what comes next. That is why the parser has to keep the developing structure available while more words arrive.
21|**In** is a **preposition**, which is a word that expresses a relationship such as location or containment. It begins a **prepositional phrase**. >> “In” does not name the location by itself. >> It sets up the expectation of a noun phrase that supplies the location, as in “in the oven” or “in the box”. || After “the pizza was”, the word “in” narrows the possible structures. The sentence is now heading toward a statement about where the pizza was. >> The parser expects a noun phrase to complete the prepositional phrase. >> The expectation is grammatical, so the next word must be able to start a noun phrase. Words like “the”, “an” or a noun would fit. || So understanding the phrase requires combining the preposition with the noun phrase that follows it.
22|**The** begins a new **noun phrase** inside the prepositional phrase “in the …”. It starts the phrase that will name the location. >> “The” does not supply the location by itself. >> A noun is still needed, possibly with modifiers before it, as in “in the hot oven”. || The parser now strongly expects a **noun**. The expectation concerns a **word category**, not just a sensible topic. >> Whatever comes next must work grammatically as part of a noun phrase. >> Knowing the sentence is about pizza makes words like oven or box likely. The grammatical requirement holds regardless of meaning. || So the grammatical frame is now very specific. Any word that cannot be part of a noun phrase will clash with the expectation.
23|**Eaten** is the **past participle** of the verb **eat**. It is a verb form, not a noun, so it cannot complete the noun phrase that “in the” has started. >> “In the eaten” puts a verb form exactly where a noun is required. >> This is a **word-category violation**. A word of the wrong grammatical category appears in a position that demands a different category. || A category error differs from a strange but grammatical meaning. >> **For “The pizza was in the ocean”**, the meaning is odd but the grammar is fine, because ocean is a noun. >> **For “The pizza was in the eaten”**, no unusual situation can fix the sentence. The problem lies in the grammatical type of the word, so the structure itself breaks. || That is why such sentences are useful for studying the brain. Brain responses to category errors and meaning errors can be compared to see whether grammar and meaning are processed differently.
24|The **early left anterior negativity**, or **ELAN**, is an **ERP** response elicited by **word class violations**. >> An **ERP**, or **event-related potential**, is electrical brain activity recorded at the scalp. It is averaged over many trials and timed to an event such as the start of a word. >> A **word class** is a grammatical category such as noun, verb or preposition. || Classic ELAN sentences contain word-category violations. >> In “The pizza was in the eaten”, a participle appears where a noun is expected. >> In “The scientist criticized Max’s of proof the theorem”, the preposition **of** appears where a noun is expected after “Max’s”. || The ELAN has three main features. >> It is **early**, appearing about **100 to 200 milliseconds** after the critical word. >> It is **left and anterior**, which means it is strongest over the left front of the scalp. That location raises the question of whether it is specific to language. >> It occurs **specifically for word category violations**, such as a noun when a verb is expected, or a preposition in place of a noun. || Incorrect sentences produce an early negative response over left frontal sites soon after the critical word. Correct sentences do not. >> So the ELAN was taken as evidence for a very fast syntactic analysis that checks word categories before meaning is processed.
25|With visually presented words, **the ELAN came from visual cortex**. Visual cortex is the region at the back of the brain that processes what we see. It sits in the **occipital** lobe. || **Dikker, Rabagliati, Farmer and Pylkkänen (2010)** reported this finding. Their paper is titled “Early occipital sensitivity to syntactic category is based on form typicality”. >> They compared “The beautiful princess …” with “The beautifully princess …”. Princess is a **bimorphemic noun**, which is a noun that ends in an identifiable noun suffix. >> Beautiful is an adjective, which correctly predicts a noun. Beautifully is an adverb, which predicts a verb or an adjective, so princess is unexpected. >> The visual response was **larger** for “The beautifully princess …” at about 120 to 130 milliseconds after the noun appeared. || The finding suggests that a prediction can **feed back down to the earliest parts of the visual system**. >> When we expect a verb, for example, the prediction covers visual patterns that are consistent with the way verbs are written. A predicted category implies likely visual forms, such as typical word endings. >> The very early response to the error might reflect a **mismatch** between that prediction and what finally appears on the screen. || This raises a question. Does this work only for items that are **morphologically marked** as nouns or verbs, or also for **open-class** and **closed-class** words? >> A word is morphologically marked when a part of it, such as a suffix, signals its category. >> Open-class words are content words such as nouns and verbs, which keep gaining new members. Closed-class words are function words such as prepositions and determiners, which form a small fixed set. >> The early effect appears for nouns with a noun-marking morpheme and for nouns with a typical noun form. It does not appear for neutral nouns like “infant” that lack strong cues. || So the early visual response depends on how much a word’s written form looks like its category.
26|Many English words can function as either a **noun** or a **verb**. The rest of the sentence decides which. >> **For perches as a noun**, the sentence is “Chris and Ben are glad that the bird perches seem easy to install”. Perches means the bars that birds sit on, and seem is the verb. >> **For perches as a verb**, the sentence is “Chris and Ben are glad that the bird perches comfortably in the cage”. Perches describes what the bird does. >> **For needs as a noun**, the sentence is “The teacher told the principal that the student needs were not being met”. Needs means the student’s requirements. >> **For needs as a verb**, the sentence is “The teacher told the principal that the student needs to be more focused”. || Recognizing the word form alone does not settle its grammatical role. The **continuation** after the ambiguous word resolves it. || Words also differ in **phonological form typicality**. This is how much a word’s sound pattern resembles the sound patterns of typical nouns or typical verbs. >> A corpus analysis of **3,158 words** measured each word’s mean distance in phonological feature space from all nouns and from all verbs. A small distance means the word sounds similar to that category. >> Nouns tend to sound closer to other nouns, and verbs tend to sound closer to other verbs. >> Some words go against the trend. There are **noun-like nouns** and **verb-like verbs**, but also **verb-like nouns** and **noun-like verbs**. || So a word can sound like one category while actually belonging to the other.
27|In **form typicality**, **“perches” is noun-like** and **“needs” is verb-like**. >> These are learned statistical tendencies in how words sound. >> Both words can still appear in either role. || **For perches**, the noun-like form matches one sentence and clashes with the other. >> In “the bird perches seem easy to install”, perches is a noun, which matches its form. >> In “the bird perches comfortably in the cage”, perches must be a verb, which goes against its form. || **For needs**, the pattern is reversed. >> In “the student needs to be more focused”, needs is a verb, which matches its form. >> In “the student needs were not being met”, needs must be a noun, which goes against its form. || Each word appears once as a noun and once as a verb. So the same word form can be compared in a matching use and a mismatching use. >> The comparison separates what a word’s form suggests from what the grammar of the sentence requires. || So the four sentences set up a test of form typicality. If form typicality guides parsing, the mismatching uses should be harder to process, even though all four sentences are grammatical.
28|Reading is **slower when a word’s required category conflicts with its form typicality**. >> The measure is reading time at **disambiguation** minus reading time at the **point of ambiguity**. The times are adjusted for word length. >> The point of ambiguity is the ambiguous word itself, such as perches or needs. >> Disambiguation is the point where the continuation makes the intended category clear, such as “seem” or “comfortably”. >> A larger increase from the point of ambiguity to disambiguation signals more difficulty. || **For noun-like homonyms** such as perches, noun-resolved sentences added only about **50 milliseconds**. Verb-resolved sentences added much more, about **120 milliseconds**. >> So a noun-like form is harder to resolve as a verb. || **For verb-like homonyms** such as needs, noun-resolved sentences added about **110 milliseconds**. Verb-resolved sentences added almost nothing, under 10 milliseconds. >> So a verb-like form is harder to resolve as a noun. || The ambiguous word stays the same in each pair, and only the continuation changes. >> The extra cost appears when the continuation demands the category that does not match the form. || So parsing uses learned statistics about how word forms sound, along with the structure the sentence finally requires.
29|The **ELAN effect** for nouns where a verb is expected is limited to certain cases. It appears only when **syntactic** or **statistical form-based** information is **physically available** in the word. >> Syntactic information means **morphemes** that indicate noun-ness. A morpheme is the smallest meaningful part of a word, such as a suffix that marks a noun. >> Statistical form-based information means **“nouniness”**. This is how much a word’s form resembles typical nouns, even without a noun suffix. || The early response measured is the **M100**, a brain response that peaks about 100 milliseconds after a word appears. Three kinds of nouns were compared. >> **For bimorphemic nouns**, the pair was “The beautiful princess …” and “The beautifully princess …”. Princess has a noun-marking suffix, and the unexpected version produced a significantly larger response. >> **For typical nouns**, the pair was “The tasteless soda …” and “The tastelessly soda …”. Soda has a statistically noun-like form, and the unexpected version again produced a significantly larger response. >> **For neutral nouns**, the pair was “The cute infant …” and “The cutely infant …”. Infant has neither a suffix nor a typical noun form, and the two versions did not differ. || So **no effect** was observed for neutral nouns like “infant” that lack strong cues. An abstract category mismatch alone is not enough to produce this earliest visual response. >> An expected category generates predictions about what the next word will look like. >> The early response appears only when the arriving word carries physical evidence that conflicts with the prediction.
30|Sentence processing can be summed up in **four sentences**, and each one carries one idea. || **For “Colorless green ideas sleep furiously”**, the idea is that **syntax is independent of semantics** to some extent. >> In the limit, a sentence can be grammatically correct but meaningless. >> Words can fill a grammatical structure without describing a sensible event. || **For “The horse raced past the barn fell”**, the idea is that syntactic structure is often highly **ambiguous**, or actually **vague**. We do not notice except where things fail. >> Reading times for “preferred” and “unpreferred” structures suggest that syntactic processes **take shortcuts to save memory**. They do not activate every possible parse of a sentence. >> When the shortcut picks the wrong structure, the reader is led down the garden path. || **For “Put the apple on the towel in the box”**, the idea is that **context, pragmatics and other factors** interact with syntactic processing from very early on. >> With two apples in view, listeners take “on the towel” as a description of the apple, not as a destination. >> So the visual situation shapes the parse while the sentence is still unfolding. || **For “The bird perches were comfortable”**, the idea is that the very earliest syntactic effects observable in the brain are driven in part by **form typicality**. They are not necessarily driven by **morphosyntactic** structures, which are grammatical structures signaled by word parts and word order. >> These effects seem to be related to **predictions about the physical forms of words**. >> Perches has a noun-like form, so its use as a noun here matches the expectation its form creates.
