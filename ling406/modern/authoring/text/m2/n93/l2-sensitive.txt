2|**Second-language (L2) learning** works as a **model system** for research on the sensitive period. A model system is a common, easily studied situation that answers questions about a process that is hard to study directly. || **Lenneberg (1967)** proposed that the sensitive period comes from the development of **cerebral dominance**. >> **Cerebral dominance** means one hemisphere, usually the left, becomes specialized for language. >> He also described it as developmental change in **language laterality**. Language laterality is how much language relies on one side of the brain instead of both. >> On this theory, the window for learning language closes once language is fixed in the left hemisphere. || Two kinds of evidence motivated the theory. >> The first was a rough alignment of **brain development** with **language milestones**. Cortical neurons grow quickly for about two years and then level off. No measurements existed between ages 2 and 18. Lenneberg linked the end of fast brain growth to the start of language learning. >> The second was a review of **resilience after brain injury or surgery**. Children who lost the left hemisphere often developed language. Adults did not recover it. || So the theory is about the brain's **maturational state**, which is its stage of physical development. >> A young, less specialized brain can shift language to other tissue. >> A mature, specialized brain has fewer options, so language learning becomes harder.
3|Research on **first-language (L1) sensitive periods** relies mostly on **idiosyncratic cases** or relatively rare phenomena. Idiosyncratic means particular to one individual. >> One kind of case is children recovering from surgery that removed a hemisphere. >> Another case is Genie, who was isolated from language until age 13. >> Each case comes with other unusual circumstances, such as injury, illness or abuse. So it is hard to tell which factor caused the outcome. || **Second-language (L2) learning** gives a much larger comparison. Many people learn second languages at different times in their lives, often with somewhat **limited success**. >> L2 learners are common, so researchers can study large groups instead of single rare cases. >> They start at every age from early childhood to late adulthood. >> Their final success can be measured. || The **limits of L2 success** are used to reason about the **timing** of the sensitive or critical period. >> If later starters reliably reach lower levels, the age where the decline appears could mark when the sensitive period closes. >> The **first language** complicates the comparison. Every L2 learner already knows a language, and that knowledge shapes how the new one is learned. || That is why L2 learning is used as a model system for sensitive period research.
4|**Johnson and Newport (1989)** tested whether **critical period effects** reach **second-language learning**. Their paper is about the influence of **maturational state** on the acquisition of English as a second language. >> **Lenneberg (1967)** hypothesized that language could be acquired only within a **critical period**. That period runs from early infancy until puberty. >> In its basic form, the critical period hypothesis only has consequences for first-language acquisition. >> If it also extends to second-language acquisition, young children should be better second-language learners than adults. They should also reach higher final proficiency. || They compared the **English proficiency** of **46 native Korean or Chinese speakers**. >> The speakers had arrived in the United States between the ages of **3 and 39**. >> By the time of testing, they had lived in the United States between 3 and 26 years. >> A **grammaticality judgment task** tested a wide variety of structures of English grammar. || Earlier arrivals had a **clear and strong advantage** over later arrivals. >> **Up to puberty**, test performance was **linearly related** to age of arrival. Each extra year of age at arrival meant a lower score. >> **After puberty**, performance was **low but highly variable** and unrelated to age of arrival. || The age effect was not an accidental result of other differences between people. >> It did not come from amount of experience with English, motivation, self-consciousness or American identification. >> It appeared on **every grammatical structure tested**, although later learners mastered some structures far better than others. || So Johnson and Newport concluded that a critical period for language acquisition **extends its effects to second-language acquisition**.
5|**Johnson and Newport (1989)** devised a **grammaticality judgment test**, or **GJT**. A grammaticality judgment is a decision about whether a sentence follows the rules of the language. >> Participants listened to recorded sentences and said whether each sentence was grammatical or not. >> The test was **untimed**, so participants could take as long as they needed. || Each item had a grammatical version and an ungrammatical version, and each pair tests **one rule of English**. >> **(1a) “The farmer bought two pigs at the market”** and **(1b) “The farmer bought two pig at the market”** test **plural marking** on nouns. >> **(2a) “The little boy is speaking to a policeman”** and **(2b) “The little boy is speak to a policeman”** test the **progressive -ing** form of the verb. || Several pairs test articles and other **determiners**. >> **(6a) “Tom is reading a book in the bathtub”** and **(6b) “Tom is reading book in the bathtub”** test whether a needed article is present. >> **(7a) “The boys are going to the zoo this Saturday”** and **(7b) “A boys are going to the zoo this Saturday”** test whether determiner and noun agree in number. >> **(8a) “Larry went home after the party”** and **(8b) “Larry went the home after the party”** test a spot where English does not allow an article. || Other pairs test how **English questions** are built. >> **(23a) “When will Sam fix his car?”** and **(23b) “When Sam will fix his car?”** test **wh-question** word order, where the helping verb comes before the subject. >> **(24a) “What do they sell at the corner store?”** and **(24b) “What they sell at the corner store?”** test the need for the helping verb “do” in a question. || So the test covers many different rules of English grammar, with one rule for each pair.
6|The data relate each participant's score on the grammar test to **age of arrival**, or **AOA**. Each participant is one point, and the score is items correct out of 276. >> The participants are split at **puberty**, which is the end of the critical period in Lenneberg's proposal. Splitting there tests whether the age effect changes after puberty. >> A **correlation**, written r, measures how closely two variables are related, from −1 to +1. A value near −1 means one variable falls steadily as the other rises. A value near 0 means little relationship. || For **early arrivals**, aged 3 to 15, the correlation was **−0.87**. >> Children who arrived between about 3 and 7 scored near the maximum, around 270. >> Scores fell steadily with each later year of arrival, to around 210 to 230 near age 15. >> The strong negative correlation means age of arrival **predicts much of the score** in this group. || For **late arrivals**, aged 17 to 39, the correlation was only **−0.16**. >> Scores were lower overall and widely scattered, from about 160 to 250. >> Some adults scored around 250, while others of similar age scored below 180. >> The weak correlation means age of arrival **predicts little** about which adults do well. || So the pattern has two parts, a **strong age effect in childhood** and **lower, highly variable performance in adulthood**.
7|The **Johnson and Newport (1989)** data have three main features. >> There is a **strong relationship between AOA and accuracy**. AOA is age of arrival, and earlier arrival goes with higher scores. >> **Early learners**, up to about age 8, are often **near-perfect**, which means **native-like**. Their scores sit close to those of native English speakers. >> **Late arrivals** have a **nearly random distribution of accuracy**. Among adults, age of arrival barely predicts the score. || The first two features describe learners who arrived as children. >> For the **youngest arrivals**, grammar reaches a native-like level. >> For **arrivals between about 8 and 15**, scores are progressively lower. So the decline starts well before puberty. >> For **adult arrivals**, scores are lower still, with wide differences between individuals. || A “nearly random distribution” has a specific meaning here. >> It means that age of arrival and score are only weakly related among late arrivals. >> It does not mean guessing, because late arrivals scored well above chance. >> Individual scores varied greatly, so two adults who arrived at the same age could end up far apart. || That is why near-perfect scores for the youngest arrivals are the clearest sign of an early advantage.
8|**Johnson and Newport (1989)** argue that their data are consistent with a **sensitive period** for L2 learning. They themselves call it a **“critical period.”** Their argument has two parts. || First, **ultimate attainment** depends on AOA. Ultimate attainment is the final level a learner reaches. >> Scores fall steadily as age of arrival in childhood rises. That is consistent with a **loss of language-learning ability over development**. >> The timing is **not entirely consistent with Lenneberg's hypothesis**. Lenneberg put the end of the critical period at puberty, but the decline here starts around age 7 or 8. >> Lenneberg was working with a large gap in the data between early childhood and adolescence. He had no brain measurements between ages 2 and 18, so his timing was a rough estimate. || Second, attainment in late acquisition is **all over the place**. Adults who arrived at the same age reached very different levels. >> After the sensitive period closes, attainment depends on **idiosyncratic learning abilities**, not on the usual language acquisition processes. >> Idiosyncratic learning abilities are individual differences in things such as memory, motivation or analytical skill. >> **Children** all learn through the same natural acquisition process, so they reach similar levels at similar ages. >> **Adults** no longer have that process, so they rely on their own abilities, which vary greatly between people. || So a steady decline in childhood and scattered adult scores both fit a sensitive period that closes.
9|The **“critical period” hypothesis** comes in **two versions**. Both start from the same claim that early in life humans have a superior capacity for acquiring languages. || The **exercise hypothesis** says the capacity must be used early to be kept. >> If the capacity is **not exercised** during this time, it will disappear or decline with maturation. >> If the capacity **is exercised**, further language-learning abilities will remain intact throughout life. >> So an adult who learned an L1 normally should learn an L2 as well as a child does. || The **maturational state hypothesis** says the capacity disappears or declines with maturation, whether or not it has been used. >> So even a person with a normal L1 should learn an L2 less well as an adult. || Johnson and Newport (1989) see the **maturational state hypothesis** as more consistent with their data. >> All of their participants had a normal first language, so all had exercised the capacity. >> Later starters still learned the L2 less well. >> That result fits the maturational state hypothesis, which is the idea named in the title of their paper. || This changes how the **L1 critical period data** should be read. >> In cases like Genie, the capacity was never exercised. Both hypotheses predict poor learning for such cases, so those cases cannot separate them. >> Only **second-language data** can separate the two versions. That is because only L2 learners exercised the capacity before learning another language.
10|**Test design** raises a methodological issue for the grammaticality judgment task. The task is **very different from fluent language use**. >> In fluent use, people speak and understand quickly without stopping to think about rules. >> In a grammaticality judgment task, people consider each sentence and decide whether it is correct. || One worry is the use of **“metalinguistic”** or **“explicit” knowledge**. **Metalinguistic knowledge** is conscious knowledge about language, such as knowing that English plurals add “-s.” || **Ellis (2005)** contrasts **implicit knowledge** and **explicit knowledge** on several characteristics. >> For **awareness**, implicit knowledge is intuitive awareness of linguistic norms. Explicit knowledge is conscious awareness. >> For **type of knowledge**, implicit knowledge is **procedural**, which means knowing how to do something. Explicit knowledge is **declarative**, which means knowing facts that can be stated. >> For **systematicity**, implicit knowledge is variable but systematic. Explicit knowledge is often anomalous and inconsistent. >> For **accessibility**, implicit knowledge is reached through **automatic processing**. Explicit knowledge is reached through **controlled processing**, which takes effort. >> For **use of L2 knowledge**, implicit knowledge is used during fluent performance. Explicit knowledge is used when a speaker is planning and having difficulty. >> For **self-report**, implicit knowledge is **nonverbalizable**, so people cannot explain it. Explicit knowledge is **verbalizable**. >> For **learnability**, implicit knowledge is potentially only learnable **within the critical period**. Explicit knowledge is learnable at **any age**. || So an **untimed GJT** may let people use explicit knowledge. >> A learner who consciously applies a taught rule gets the same score as one who knows the rule implicitly. >> That is why the test may miss the implicit knowledge that the critical period is supposed to affect.
11|One suggestion is that the **untimed GJT** measures explicit learning of **“metalinguistic” knowledge** better than it measures grammar acquisition. The reason is the task demands of the test. || **Ellis (2005)** compared the design features of five tests. >> **Imitation** and **oral narrative** rely on feel, are done under time pressure and focus on meaning. They require no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **timed GJT** relies on feel and is done under time pressure, but it focuses on form. It requires no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **untimed GJT** relies on rules, has no time pressure and focuses on form. It does allow metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **metalanguage test** asks people to state rules. It relies on rules, has no time pressure, focuses on form and requires metalinguistic knowledge. || Ellis then related each test to **starting age** and to **years of formal instruction**. Only two relationships were significant. >> For the **timed GJT**, starting age had a correlation of **−0.23**. >> For the **untimed GJT with ungrammatical items**, years of formal instruction had a correlation of **0.27**. >> The other tests had only weak relationships with either measure. || Consistent with this, untimed GJT performance is correlated with **years of formal education in L2**. >> Formal classroom instruction teaches explicit rules, and explicit rules help on the untimed test. >> Starting age matters for the timed test, which limits the use of explicit rules. || So an age effect found with an untimed GJT may partly reflect **explicit, taught knowledge** instead of natural grammar acquisition.
12|**Transfer** is the use of first-language knowledge in learning a second language. It raises another methodological and theoretical issue for the age findings. >> Languages that are **typologically more similar** can benefit more from transfer. **Typological similarity** is similarity in how languages are structured, such as word order and word forms. >> Cognates and loan words give learners familiar words to build on. The examples are French **auto**, **rivière** and **bicyclette**, and Japanese **korokke** and **monoreru**. || **Johnson and Newport (1989)** tested people with **English as L2** and **Chinese or Korean as L1**. >> Chinese and Korean are very different from English in grammar and vocabulary. >> So these learners got little help from transfer. || If the languages were more similar, transfer would help much more. >> A learner whose L1 shares grammar and vocabulary with English can reuse much of what they already know. >> Those learners might have a different relationship between age and attainment. || So the age pattern in Johnson and Newport's data may depend on the **particular languages involved** as well as on age.
13|**Johnson and Newport (1989)** compared errors on **12 types of English rules**. They compared native speakers with five arrival groups. The groups arrived at ages 3 to 7, 8 to 10, 11 to 15, 17 to 24 and 25 to 39. >> The rule types are **determiners**, **plurals**, **verb subcategorization**, **past tense**, **pronouns**, **particles**, **auxiliaries**, **third-person singular**, **wh-questions**, **yes/no questions**, **word order** and **progressive -ing**. >> **Verb subcategorization** is the set of rules for which kinds of words or phrases can follow a verb. || Not all aspects of the grammar are **equally difficult**. >> For **native speakers and the youngest arrivals**, errors are very few on every rule. >> For **the latest arrivals**, errors are highest on determiners, around 65 to 68%, and on plurals, around 45 to 55%. >> For **every group**, errors on word order and progressive -ing stay low, under about 10%. || Some of the pattern looks like the **order in which children learn language**. >> Progressive -ing and word order are easiest for late learners. They are also among the first structures children master. || Some of the pattern could be **transfer**. >> **Chinese has no determiners**, so Chinese speakers have no L1 category for “a” and “the.” That makes English articles especially hard for them. >> Questions do not follow the child order. Children learn questions very late, but questions are among the **easiest structures for late learners**. >> Adults may use general cognitive skills or L1 knowledge when they learn question forms. || So how hard a structure is depends on the structure itself and on what the learner's first language provides.
14|**Birdsong and Molis (2001)** repeated the Johnson and Newport study with **Spanish speakers learning English**. The comparison is between two language pairs. || For **Chinese and Korean speakers**, the age effect was in childhood. >> Early arrivals, aged 3 to 15, had a strong correlation with age of arrival, **−0.87**. >> Late arrivals, aged 17 to 39, had a weak correlation, **−0.16**. || For **Spanish speakers**, the pattern was almost the reverse. >> Early arrivals, aged 3 to 16, had only a weak correlation, **−0.23**. They scored near the maximum regardless of age, with only a slight decline for those arriving around 16. >> Late arrivals, aged 17 to 44, had a strong correlation, **−0.69**. Scores fell from around 260 for those arriving near 18 to around 160 for those arriving after 40. || The results are a problem for the idea that AOA effects on GJT performance reflect **maturational constraints**. Maturational constraints are limits on learning caused by the brain's development. >> If a fixed maturational schedule controlled learning, the pattern should look the same for every first language. >> Instead, the pattern changed with the learners' first language. >> Spanish is much closer to English than Chinese or Korean is. So **transfer** may help Spanish speakers reach native-like grammar even when they arrive late in childhood. || So the age effect depends on the **language pairing** as well as on age.
15|These findings may not even count as **“conclusions.”** One thing holds across studies. Overall, **L2 attainment is lower for older learners**. || How much lower depends on three things. >> It depends on **what constructions are tested**. Some structures, such as determiners, are much harder for late learners than others, such as word order. >> It depends on **how they are tested**. Timed and untimed tests can give different results, because the untimed test allows explicit knowledge. >> It depends on **language pairings**. Spanish speakers and Chinese or Korean speakers have different age patterns when learning English. || Three pairs of factors are **hard to dissociate**, which means hard to separate. >> **Age of acquisition** is hard to separate from the **amount and quality of experience**. People who arrive younger usually have more years of exposure and more immersion, such as schooling in the language. >> **Transfer effects** are hard to separate from **L2 acquisition**. L2 performance reflects what was learned and also what carries over from the L1. >> **Performance** is hard to separate from **competence**, generally speaking. Competence is underlying knowledge of a language. Performance is how well that knowledge is used on a particular task. A test score measures performance, so it may not reveal competence directly. || So the age effect in second-language learning is real, but it depends on many factors that are hard to separate.
