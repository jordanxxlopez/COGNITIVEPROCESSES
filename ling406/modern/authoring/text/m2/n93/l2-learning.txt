2|**Sensitive periods** for a second language (**L2**) are often taken to mean that infants, toddlers and children learn language better than adults. That idea should not be carried too far, because **babies are overrated** as language learners. >> A typical child by **18 months** uses at least **20 words**. The child also understands "in and out" and "off and on" and points to several body parts when asked. >> The child answers simple questions such as "Where's teddy?" and "What's that?" with words or gestures. The child also has some pretend play, such as giving teddy a drink. >> The child makes at least four different consonant sounds, such as b, n, d, g, w and h. The child enjoys being read to and points to pictures with one finger. >> An adult who knew only about 20 words after 18 months of language instruction would count as a very poor learner. || **Adults initially learn more quickly** than children. >> One reason is **transfer from L1**, the first language. Adults build on what they already know about how language works. >> The other reason is **better general cognitive skills**, such as memory, attention and reasoning. || **Adults do worse on ultimate attainment**, which is the final level of skill they eventually reach. >> Adults **rarely become native-like** in a second language. >> That may also be due in part to transfer. The first language gives adults a fast start, but it can also keep them from native-like skill. || So early learning speed and final success are separate measures. Children and adults rank differently on each.
3|Several toddler **language milestones** are really **general conceptual abilities**. >> Understanding "in and out" and "off and on" depends on the ability to deal with **spatial relations between objects**. >> **Pretend play**, such as giving teddy a drink, depends on **event representations**. Event representations are mental representations of who does what in an event. >> A toddler who meets these milestones is building new ideas about the world along with new words. || **Later learners of a second language** already have much more of this in place. >> An adult learning Spanish already has the concepts behind "in" and "on". >> So the adult can focus on how these concepts are represented in the **target language**, which is the language being learned. >> That is a smaller job than building concepts and words at the same time. || Later learners also **already know another language**. That knowledge affects how the second language is learned. >> The first language offers ready-made ways to express concepts. >> Some of those ways fit the second language, so they help. Others clash with it, so they lead to errors. || So a later learner mainly has to learn how the new language expresses ideas the learner already has.
4|**Transfer** is the use of native language (**L1**) processes and representations for a second language (**L2**). >> A learner brings L1 habits for hearing sounds, building sentences and finding words into the L2. >> The learner comes to the L2 with a complete working language system already in place. || Transfer can have both **positive** and **negative** impacts on second-language learning and performance. >> **Positive transfer** happens when an L1 pattern matches what the L2 needs. Shared sounds, words and structures then give the learner a head start. >> **Negative transfer** happens when an L1 pattern conflicts with the L2. Errors appear where the languages differ, because the learner's habits point the wrong way. || A cartoon gives a case of negative transfer in grammar. A man in a pub asks "Do you not be happy with me as the translator of the books of you?" >> The words are English, but the structure follows the grammar of another language. >> Translating word by word carries over the first language's word order and phrasing. || So every habit built for the first language comes along into the second, whether it helps or hurts.
5|**Transfer** affects **phonetic and phonological learning**, which is the learning of speech sounds. >> **Categorical perception** leads to **acquired similarity** for sounds in the same category. >> Acquired similarity means that sounds a language groups into one category come to sound more alike with experience. || **Minimal pairs in Hindi** make the problem clear. A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in only one sound. >> Two Hindi words both sound like "tap" to an English speaker. One means hot, and the other means a kind of beat. >> Two words both sound like "dab" to an English speaker. One means pressure, and the other means a kind of fruit. >> Two words both sound like "ari" to an English speaker. One means saw (the tool), and the other means crooked. >> In each pair, one word has a **dental** consonant, made with the tongue against the teeth. The other has a **retroflex** consonant, made with the tongue curled back toward the roof of the mouth. || Native English speakers tend to use the English **alveolar** category to interpret both dental and retroflex consonants. >> An alveolar consonant, such as English /t/ or /d/, is made with the tongue on the ridge behind the teeth. >> English has only the alveolar category, so both Hindi sounds fall into it and seem the same. || So in this case **transfer makes learning the L2 contrast more difficult**. >> The English category was learned successfully, but it now hides a difference that Hindi uses to tell words apart. >> The learner has to separate sounds that the first language taught them to treat as one.
6|**Transfer** also affects **grammar learning**. **Classifiers** are a somewhat unusual grammatical feature across languages. >> A **classifier** is a word that must come between a number or demonstrative and a noun. >> The classifier depends on the kind of thing the noun names. >> The order is demonstrative pronoun, then nominal classifier, then noun. In **yí gè rén** ("one person"), gè is the classifier. || Chinese has many classifiers, and each one goes with a kind of noun. >> **Gè** is the general classifier. **Bǎ** goes with objects that have a handle. **Běn** goes with books and similar objects. >> **Kuài** goes with objects that are divided into pieces. **Liàng** goes with wheeled vehicles. **Tái** goes with devices and gadgets. >> **Xiē** marks an indeterminate amount, or goes with the plural of pronouns and demonstratives. >> **Zhāng** goes with flat objects. **Zhī** goes with long, thin objects. || **Native English speakers learning Chinese have difficulty** learning classifiers. >> In English, saying "a chair", "that dog", "the car" or "one book" does not require much attention to noun meaning. >> In Chinese, different nouns require different classifiers. So the learner must notice properties such as shape and function. || One way to think about this is that English speakers have **learned to ignore the cues** for classifiers as part of learning English grammar. That is why learning Chinese means paying attention again to cues that English taught them to disregard.
7|**Grammatical gender** is another place where **transfer causes problems**. Grammatical gender sorts nouns into classes, often called masculine and feminine. >> Grammatical gender is different from social or cultural gender. >> There is nothing "male" about a book or "female" about liberty. Yet French **le livre** (the book) is masculine, and **la liberté** (liberty) is feminine. >> Other masculine nouns are le cheval (the horse), le chien (the dog) and le bruit (the noise). >> Other feminine nouns are la colombe (the dove), la chemise (the shirt) and la maison (the house). || Grammatical gender matters for **agreement**. Agreement means that related words must match the gender of the noun. >> Pronouns, adjectives and some forms of verbs all need to be coordinated with noun gender. >> English nouns have no grammatical gender, so English speakers have no habit of tracking it. || French is famous for **synonyms with different genders**. >> **Auto** and **voiture** both mean car. One is masculine and the other is feminine. >> **Rivière** and **fleuve** both mean river. Rivière is feminine and fleuve is masculine. >> **Vélo** and **bicyclette** both mean bicycle. Vélo is masculine and bicyclette is feminine. >> So gender has to be learned one word at a time. || **Word endings** give clues to gender. >> Masculine nouns often end in -age (le fromage, the cheese), -r (le professeur, the teacher), -t (le chat, the cat) or -isme (le capitalisme). >> Feminine nouns often end in -ie (la boulangerie, the bakery), -ion (la nation), -ité or -tié (la fraternité, brotherhood) or -ance (la balance, the scales). >> Feminine nouns also often end in -nne, -mme and -lle, as in la fille (the girl) and l’indienne.
8|**Transfer is not all bad**. In some L1 and L2 situations, the similarity of words across languages helps **vocabulary learning**. >> French **auto** resembles English "auto" and means a car. >> French **rivière** resembles English "river". >> French **bicyclette** resembles English "bicycle". >> Japanese **コロッケ (korokke)** means a croquette. Japanese **モノレール (monoreru)** means a monorail. || Sometimes the similarity comes from **shared etymology**. Etymology is the history of where a word comes from. >> Words with a shared origin are called **cognates**. >> French rivière and English river both come from the same older word. || Sometimes the similarity comes from more recent **borrowing**. >> A **loan word** is a word that one language takes from another. >> Japanese korokke and monoreru were borrowed from European languages. Their sounds were changed to fit Japanese. || Cognates and loan words give **positive transfer** for vocabulary. >> The learner already knows a similar form with a similar meaning. >> A learner who knows river can link it to rivière right away. So the new word is easier to store and recall.
9|Not all similar-sounding words are **cognates**. A **false friend** is a word that looks or sounds like a word in another language but means something different. >> Learners have a bias to interpret L2 words as related to similar-sounding L1 words. >> That bias can be **very strong**, and sometimes the result is embarrassing. || The Spanish sentence "Arnold está embarazado" does not mean that Arnold is embarrassed. >> Spanish **embarazado** means **pregnant**. >> Embarazado sounds like embarrassed, so an English speaker assumes it means embarrassed. >> That speaker gets the meaning badly wrong. || The link to the familiar word forms before the learner checks the meaning. >> When the meanings match, the link speeds up learning, as with cognates. >> When the meanings differ, the same link leads to errors. || So a helpful cognate and a misleading false friend differ only in whether the meanings really match.
10|**De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** tested in the lab whether people use **cognates** in "second language" learning. >> They used **paired-associate learning** of made-up words. In paired-associate learning, people study pairs and later recall one member when given the other. >> The participants were Dutch speakers. So Dutch stood in for the first language, and the made-up words stood in for a new second language. || The made-up words varied in two ways. >> They varied in their **similarity to their Dutch associates**. Some resembled the Dutch word, so they were cognates, and some did not. >> They also varied in **concreteness**. A concrete word names something that can be seen or touched. An abstract word names an idea. || Crossing the two factors gave four kinds of pairs. >> The pair gitaar and gettar ("guitar") is concrete and cognate. >> The pair tijger and duunze ("tiger") is concrete and non-cognate. >> The pair domein and domaan ("domain") is abstract and cognate. >> The pair herstel and groop ("repair") is abstract and non-cognate. || Participants were presented with pairs of Dutch words and their non-word associates and asked to learn the pairs. >> They were then tested **a week later on retention**. Retention is how well they remembered the pairs after a delay without study. || So the four kinds of pairs let the effect of each factor be measured separately.
11|In **De Groot and Keijzer (2000)**, **cognates were learned more rapidly** and also **remembered more easily**. >> Recall was tested in three training sessions. It was tested again in session 4, after one week with no training. || Recall rose across the three training sessions for every kind of word, but the starting points differed. >> For **concrete cognates**, recall was 69.7% in session 1 and 93.4% by session 3. >> For **concrete non-cognates**, recall was 43.7% in session 1 and 92.0% by session 3. >> For **abstract cognates**, recall was 42.7% in session 1 and 86.0% by session 3. >> For **abstract non-cognates**, recall was 19.0% in session 1 and 72.7% by session 3. || The week without training separated the kinds of words again. >> Concrete cognates dropped to 78.4%, and concrete non-cognates dropped to 69.0%. >> Abstract cognates dropped to 68.4%, and abstract non-cognates fell sharply to **36.0%**. >> Responses were also faster for cognates in every session. || So **cognate status** can contribute to the very earliest stages of word learning in a second language. >> The cognate advantage was largest in session 1, when learning had just begun. It reappeared after a week, when non-cognates were forgotten more. >> **Concreteness** also had a large effect, and the two factors add together. Abstract non-cognates have neither source of support, so they were hardest. >> One explanation for concreteness effects is **context availability**. It is the idea that concrete words are easier to link with a supporting context.
12|**De Groot and Keijzer** tested vocabulary in two directions, and that is what makes the experiment a good one. >> **Receptive** testing gives the made-up word and asks for the Dutch word. >> **Expressive** testing, which the authors call **productive** testing, gives the Dutch word and asks for the made-up word. || A receptive test alone would not be very impressive. >> A cognate new word already resembles the Dutch word. So a participant could answer just by noticing the resemblance, without learning the pair. >> The meanings of gitaar, tijger and domein could be guessed without any Dutch training. They resemble English guitar, tiger and domain. || The **concrete cognates** in the study are very close to their Dutch partners. >> Five pairs are broer and breur, brood and brodde, gitaar and gettar, jongen and jange, lepel and lippel. >> Five more are lichaam and lechem, naald and nadel, paraplu and parredu, rivier and riffer, schaar and skare. >> The last five are stier and stire, trein and trinen, varken and vark, viool and vialo, vrouw and vrauk. >> Each made-up word is a slightly changed Dutch word. || Productive testing removes the shortcut. >> The learner must recall the exact new form, such as gettar, out of many similar possibilities. >> So a cognate advantage in productive testing reflects **real learning of new forms**.
13|The comparison of **productive** and **receptive** testing tells what participants actually learned. Recall was measured immediately after learning (session 3) and at retest a week later (session 4). >> The measure that matters is how much recall dropped over the week. || In **productive testing**, learners had to produce the new word. >> **Concrete** words fell from 92.7% to 73.7%, a drop of 19.0 points. **Abstract** words fell from 79.3% to 52.2%, a drop of 27.1 points. >> **Cognates** fell from 89.7% to 73.3%, a drop of 16.4 points. **Non-cognates** fell from 82.3% to 52.5%, a drop of 29.8 points. || In **receptive testing**, learners had to give the Dutch word. >> Concrete words fell from 97.7% to 88.9%, a drop of 8.8 points. Abstract words fell from 91.5% to 64.9%, a drop of 26.6 points. >> Cognates fell from 98.5% to 90.7%, a drop of 7.8 points. Non-cognates fell from 90.7% to 63.0%, a drop of 27.7 points. >> Receptive scores were higher overall, because recognizing a familiar word is easier than producing a new one. || Cognates and concrete words were **forgotten less in both directions**. >> The cognate advantage held in productive testing, where the learner had to recall the new form itself. >> So similarity to a known word helped learners form a **lasting memory** of the new word.
14|A central research question is whether **similar L1 words are always activated** when a person processes L2. There have been many, many studies of this question. >> When a bilingual hears an L2 word, L1 words that sound similar might also become active. >> That activation could happen automatically, even when the person is trying to use only the L2. >> If it does, the two languages are never fully separate in the mind. || **Marian and Spivey (2003)** gave one clear example with a **visual world experiment**. >> In a visual world experiment, people hear spoken instructions while they look at a set of objects. Their eye movements are recorded. >> Looks toward an object whose L1 name sounds like the spoken L2 word would mean the L1 word became active. >> Eye movements reveal that activation without asking the person to report it. The person still goes on to choose the correct object. || So eye movements can answer whether L1 words are active during L2 listening.
15|**McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)** used the same kind of eye movement experiment to study categorical perception. They studied gradient effects of within-category **VOT** on **lexical access**. >> Participants saw a lamp, a ship, a bear and a pear. They heard a word from a continuum between "bear" and "pear". >> Bear and pear differ only in their first consonant. The lamp and the ship are unrelated fillers. || **Eye movements** tell about **competition** between the possible responses that participants are considering. >> A participant can click on the bear after looking briefly at the pear. >> Those looks mean that "pear" was active as a competitor. || The amount of competition between BEAR and PEAR depended on **distance from the category boundary**. >> Sounds closer to the boundary between /b/ and /p/ drew more looks to the competitor. >> Sounds farther from the boundary drew fewer looks to the competitor. || So eye tracking measures how active an alternative word is, even when the final choice is correct. That is why the same method can ask whether a word in one language competes with a similar word in another.
16|**Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** found the **cohort effect**. A **cohort** is the set of words that share the same beginning sounds. >> Participants viewed a grid with pictures and shapes and followed spoken instructions. >> The display had a **beaker**, which was the target. >> It had a **beetle**, which shares its beginning with beaker. >> It had a **speaker**, which rhymes with beaker. >> It had a **carriage**, which is unrelated. || The probability of looking at each object changed over the first 1,000 milliseconds after the target word began. >> Looks to the beaker and the beetle rose together early, because both fit the first sounds of the word. >> Looks to the beetle peaked near 0.2 at around 500 milliseconds. They fell once the sounds that separate the two words arrived. >> Looks to the beaker kept rising and approached 1 by about 1,000 milliseconds. >> Looks to the speaker rose later and stayed smaller, peaking near 0.1. Its overlap comes at the end of the word. >> Looks to the carriage stayed near zero. || The **cohort effect** is this early competition from words that share a beginning with the target. >> Words compete as soon as they match the incoming sounds. >> They drop out when the input stops matching them. || So eye movements track the rise and fall of competition moment by moment.
17|**Marian and Spivey** examined the **cohort effect** with **cross-language competitors**. A cross-language competitor is an object whose name in the other language begins like the spoken target word. || They hypothesized that L1 words are always activated. If so, native Russian speakers should look at a book of matches when the target is "speaker" in English. >> English **speaker** begins with the same sounds as Russian **спички (spichki)**, which means matches. >> If Russian is active during English listening, the matches should draw looks. || For **English targets**, each target came with an English competitor and a Russian competitor. >> For speaker, they were spear and spichki (matches). For boot, they were book and buben (tambourine). For shark, they were shovel and sharik (balloon). >> For chair, they were chess set and cherepaha (turtle). For marker, they were marbles and marka (stamp). For barbed wire, they were bark and barhat (velvet). >> For plug, they were plum and platye (dress). For gun, they were gum and gaika (nut). For lock, they were lobster and lak dlea nogtei (nail polish). || For **Russian targets**, the roles were reversed. >> For spichki (matches), the competitors were English spear and Russian spitsi (knitting needles). >> For marka (stamp), the competitors were English marker and Russian marlya (cheese cloth). >> For platye (dress), the competitors were English plum and Russian plashch (raincoat). || The target and its cross-language competitor are tied only by **sound**. >> Speakers and matches share no meaning. >> The overlap exists only for someone who knows Russian. So looks to the matches during an English task measure activation of spichki.
18|Russian and English bilinguals first followed instructions in **English**. This tests **competition during L2 spoken word recognition**. >> The **between-language competitor** was an object with a similar Russian name. >> The **within-language competitor** was an object with a similar English name. || The measure was the percent of trials with a look to each object. >> With only the between-language competitor present, participants looked at it on **18%** of trials. >> With only the within-language competitor present, participants looked at it on **18%** of trials. >> With both present, they looked at the between-language competitor on 13% of trials and the within-language competitor on 19%. >> With no competitor present, they looked at a control filler object on only **7%** of trials. || When only one competitor was present, participants looked at within-language and between-language competitors **to the same extent**. >> The Russian name of the matches pulled the eyes as strongly as a similar English word did. || When both were present, participants looked at the within-language competitor more. They still looked at the between-language competitor a lot, about **twice as often as the control**. || So the **first language stays active** during second-language listening. Bilinguals still choose the correct object, but L1 words compete along the way.
19|The same bilinguals then followed instructions in **Russian**. This tests **competition during L1 spoken word recognition**. >> Here the between-language competitor was an object with a similar **English** name. || The measure was again the percent of trials with a look to each object. >> With only the between-language competitor present, participants looked at it on **8%** of trials. >> With only the within-language competitor present, participants looked at it on 14% of trials. >> With both present, they looked at the between-language competitor on 8% of trials and the within-language competitor on 13%. >> With no competitor present, they looked at the control filler on **5%** of trials. || **English words do not seem to be activated** when native Russian speakers do this experiment in Russian. >> The small numerical difference between the between-language and control conditions is **not statistically significant**. >> A difference that is not statistically significant is within the range of chance variation. >> The within-language competitor still drew extra looks, so the cohort effect appeared in Russian. || So the two experiments give an **asymmetric** result. >> Russian words became active during English tasks. >> English words did not become reliably active during Russian tasks. >> That fits the idea that the first language is more strongly established and more easily activated than the second.
20|**The Revised Hierarchical Model** of **Kroll and Stewart** explains interactions between L1 and L2 in terms of **spreading activation**. Spreading activation means that activating one representation partly activates the representations connected to it. >> The model has an L1 vocabulary, an L2 vocabulary and a store of **concepts**, which are meanings. >> **Lexical links** connect L1 words and L2 words directly. >> **Conceptual links** connect each vocabulary to the concepts. || The **L1 lexicon** is assumed to be larger and more strongly connected to relevant concepts. >> For **L1 words**, the links to meanings are strong. >> For **L2 words**, the links to meanings are weaker. || The **L2 lexicon** is assumed to have strong forward connections to the L1 lexicon. >> The lexical link from L2 to L1 is strong. The lexical link from L1 to L2 is weak. >> So activation spreads more readily **from an L2 word to an L1 word** than the other way. || This happens because that direction is **emphasized in learning**. >> Learners usually learn each new L2 word by connecting it to its L1 translation. That builds a strong route from L2 words to L1 words. >> The direct link from L2 words to concepts stays weaker, at least early in learning. It can grow stronger as proficiency rises.
21|The **Revised Hierarchical Model of Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** explains the Marian and Spivey results through **spreading activation** along links of different strength. || Consider the task in **L2**, with the English target "speaker". >> The concept for "matches" partially activates the Russian word for matches, spichki. This happens because L1 words are strongly connected to their concepts. >> The sounds of English speaker also partly activate spichki, because the two words begin alike. >> This activity feeds back to the conceptual activation for "matches". >> Conceptual activation for "matches" leads to **eye movements toward the L1 competitor**, the matches. || Speaker and spichki are linked only by **sound overlap**. >> Speakers and matches share no meaning. >> The extra activation of spichki strengthens the concept of matches, so the eyes are drawn to the matches. || Now consider the task in **L1**. The conceptual links with L2 might be **too weak** to create this kind of interference reliably. >> In the Russian task, an English competitor would need strong links from the English word to its concept. >> In the model, L2 words have only weak conceptual links. Those links are too weak to drive looks to their objects. || That is why the effect appears in one direction and not the other.
22|The Revised Hierarchical Model makes a **counter-intuitive prediction** even for **balanced bilinguals**. A balanced bilingual is a person who is about equally skilled in both languages. >> One might expect a balanced bilingual to work like "two monolinguals in one", with each language running separately. >> The model predicts the opposite. || For a balanced bilingual, the **conceptual links are similarly strong for both languages**. >> Each concept strongly activates its word in both languages. So both vocabularies become active whenever either one is used. >> **Competitive dynamics** will be engaged all the time. Competitive dynamics means the words of the two languages keep competing for selection. >> **Inhibition** will also be engaged all the time. Inhibition is the suppression of the word from the language that is not in use. || This prediction has led to a lot of work on bilinguals' advantages in **non-linguistic cognitive control tasks**. >> **Cognitive control** is the ability to manage attention and suppress irrelevant responses. >> Bilinguals constantly practice selecting one language and suppressing the other. So they might become better at controlling attention in general, even on tasks unrelated to language.
23|Second-language learning has so far been considered in one particular **sequence**. >> A person begins learning L1. >> Later, the person learns L2. >> The person also continues learning and using L1, so both languages stay in use. || A second sequence asks what happens when someone **stops using L1** after L2 is introduced. >> The person begins learning L1 and later learns L2, as before. >> This time the person does not continue learning or using L1. >> L1 is no longer heard or spoken, and L2 takes over. || The second sequence separates two things that usually go together. One is **learning a language early**, and the other is **continuing to use it**. >> If early learning leaves a permanent foundation, traces of L1 should remain even without use. >> If continued use is needed, L1 might fade or disappear. || So comparing the two sequences tells whether early learning alone leaves a lasting mark.
24|**Pallier, Dehaene, Poline, LeBihan, Argenti, Dupoux and Mehler** asked whether a **second language can replace the first**. Their study used brain imaging of language plasticity in adopted adults. >> They tested adults aged **20 to 32**. >> These adults had been adopted into French homes from Korean orphanages between the ages of **3 and 8**. >> They learned Korean as young children and then grew up in homes where only French was spoken. >> They stopped using Korean entirely after adoption. So they are a natural case of L1 use stopping. || The researchers tested whether the adoptees had any **residual knowledge of Korean**. Residual knowledge is any remaining trace of the earlier language. >> Monolingual French speakers with no Korean experience served as the comparison group. >> If the adoptees did better than the French speakers on Korean tasks, some Korean knowledge must have survived. || So any surviving Korean would reveal what early learning leaves behind once a language is no longer used.
25|Korean has a **three-way stop contrast**. A **stop** is a consonant made by fully blocking the airflow and then releasing it, such as /p/, /t/ or /k/. >> English and French have two kinds of stop at each place in the mouth, such as /b/ and /p/. >> Korean has three kinds at each place, which are **aspirated**, **plain** and **tense**. >> An aspirated stop is released with a strong puff of air. A plain stop has a lighter release. A tense stop is made with a stiffened, tightened throat. || The contrast appears in three series, recorded in the UCLA Phonetics Archive. >> For the **bilabial** series, made with the lips, the words are pʰul ("grass"), pul ("fire") and p’ul ("horn"). >> For the **dental** series, made with the tongue at the teeth, the words are tʰal ("mask"), tal ("moon") and t’al ("daughter"). >> For the **velar** series, made with the back of the tongue, the words are kʰo ("nose"), kok ("music") and k’ok ("exactly"). || The three words in each series are made at the same place in the mouth. They differ only in the type of stop. >> French has no such contrast, so French speakers tend to hear the three Korean sounds as two. >> Korean speakers hear three different sounds. || That is why the contrast is a good test of retained Korean. Adoptees who kept any Korean sound knowledge should hear these differences better than French speakers do.
26|**Ventureyra and colleagues** found that **Korean adoptees in France were no better than monolingual French speakers** at the Korean contrasts. >> Listeners heard pairs of sounds and judged whether they were different. >> Three groups were compared, which were adoptees, French native speakers and Korean native speakers. || Four conditions used Korean contrasts. >> For **plain and tense** p and k, Korean speakers said "different" on about 96% of trials. Adoptees did so on about 8% and French speakers on about 3%. >> For **plain and aspirated** p and k, Korean speakers scored about 90%, adoptees about 19% and French speakers about 26%. >> For **tense and aspirated** p and k, Korean speakers scored about 95%, adoptees about 23% and French speakers about 20%. >> For **plain and tense** s, Korean speakers scored about 79%, adoptees about 26% and French speakers about 23%. || Two control conditions checked general performance. >> For a **vowel** contrast, all three groups said "different" on about 97 to 99% of trials. >> For pairs of **identical** sounds, all groups rarely said "different", at about 3 to 13%. || So the adoptees could do the task. The difficulty was specific to the Korean stop contrasts. >> On those contrasts, the adoptees heard the differences at the same level as people who had never heard Korean. >> Their early Korean experience left **no measurable advantage** for these sounds.
27|In a more extensive study, **Pallier and colleagues** found that **adoptees were no better than monolingual French speakers** on three tasks. Each task tested a different kind of knowledge. || The first task was distinguishing Korean from a collection of largely unrelated languages. It tested familiarity with the overall sound of Korean. >> Participants rated from 1 to 7 how much each sentence sounded like Korean. >> Both groups rated Korean sentences around 4, and they rated Japanese sentences around 4 to 5. >> Polish sentences got about 2, and Swedish and Wolof sentences got about 2.5 to 3. >> Adoptees did not pick out Korean any better than French speakers did. || The second task was identifying the correct Korean translation of French words. It tested memory for vocabulary. >> Adoptees scored about 55% correct and French speakers about 50%. Both scores are close to chance. || The third task was deciding whether a short excerpt was repeated from a recorded sentence just heard. It tested sensitivity to sound sequences. >> For French sentences, both groups were right about 95% of the time. >> For Korean, Japanese and Polish sentences, both groups scored about 80%. Adoptees had no advantage for Korean. || These are not standard psycholinguistic tasks. Pallier and colleagues were looking for **any remaining trace of Korean**, and they **did not find any**. >> The tasks were designed to catch even weak knowledge. >> On every measure, the adoptees looked like people who had never learned Korean. || So the Korean the adoptees heard early in life left no measurable trace on these tasks.
28|Pallier and colleagues also used **brain imaging**. In both groups, listening to French activated the **classical language network**, including **superior temporal** and **inferior frontal** regions. >> The superior temporal region is the upper part of the temporal lobe. It is involved in processing speech sounds. >> The inferior frontal region is the lower part of the frontal lobe. It includes Broca's area. || The comparisons subtracted brain activity for another language from activity for French. >> The three comparisons were French minus Polish, French minus Japanese and French minus Korean. >> Each was computed for the French group and for the Korean group, the adoptees. >> In every comparison, French produced more activity in the language network than the foreign language did. || **Subtracting Korean from French** might have revealed a difference between the groups, if any residual processing ability for Korean were left. >> Suppose adoptees still processed Korean as a language. Then Korean would activate their language network more than it did for French speakers. >> The French minus Korean difference would then be smaller in adoptees. >> But the subtraction revealed **no difference** between the groups. || So the adoptees' brains responded to Korean like a foreign language, much as they responded to Polish and Japanese. The imaging agrees with the behavioral tests.
29|Interactions between L2 and L1 can also be thought of in terms of **competitive dynamics**. >> Imagine that L1 is represented as a set of **connections** within and between different brain regions. >> These connections are built and maintained through use. || **Monocular deprivation** in vision, where one eye is deprived, follows this kind of competition. The same process could apply to language. >> In mice, after 2 to 3 days, the deprived eye's connections have **increased spine motility**. >> After 3 to 4 days, there is **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration**. Wallerian degeneration is the breakdown of cut-off nerve fibers. >> After more than a week, the other eye's fibers have **axon sprouting and new spine birth**. They take over the lost space. >> So the eye that keeps receiving input wins the connections that the deprived eye loses. || If the L1 connections are never activated again, they could be **"taken over" by L2**. >> L2 is used constantly, so its connections strengthen and spread. >> L1 is not used, so its connections weaken and can be claimed by L2. || So on this account, L2 replaces L1 by winning a competition for the same neural resources.
30|A second language can **apparently replace** the first. >> The adoptees had no behavioral or brain sign of their childhood Korean. >> As far as these tests could measure, French had completely replaced Korean. || The remaining question is whether **traces of L1** are left behind that can be **"salvaged"**. >> Salvaging a trace means recovering or using some leftover effect of the earlier language. >> A trace might not appear on a test of current ability. It could still make relearning easier. || A test of current recognition and a test of later learning ask different questions. >> The first asks what the person can do with the language now. >> The second asks whether new learning benefits from something left over from the past. || That is why **relearning** is the measure that could reveal such a trace.
31|**Terry Au and colleagues** at UCLA examined whether traces can be salvaged. The paper is "Overhearing a language during childhood" by **Au, Knightly, Jun and Oh**. >> They studied undergraduates who **overheard Spanish** frequently as children but never learned to speak it. >> All participants started classroom Spanish at around age 14. They were tested at the end of a second-year college Spanish course. >> The overhearers were compared with **native speakers** and **typical late L2 learners**. || On **pronunciation**, the overhearers were far ahead of the late learners. >> For **voice onset time** in word-initial voiceless stops, native speakers had 19.3 milliseconds and overhearers had 19.6. Late learners had 36.2, a longer and English-like value. >> For word-medial voiceless stops, the three values were 22.4, 23.4 and 31.2. >> **Lenition** is a softening of a consonant, which Spanish applies to voiced stops between vowels. Word-initial voiced stops were lenited 43% by natives, 24% by overhearers and 1% by late learners. >> Word-medial voiced stops were lenited 72%, 63% and 23%. >> For **accent rating** on a 5-point scale, voiceless stops scored 4.3, 3.5 and 2.2. Voiced stops scored 4.4, 3.3 and 2.7. || On childhood slang, overhearers knew some that late learners did not. >> Production was 79% for natives, 17% for overhearers and 3% for late learners. Comprehension was 88%, 22% and 3%. || On **grammar**, the overhearers had little or no advantage. >> Gender agreement was 93% for natives, 70% for overhearers and 68% for late learners. Number agreement was 92%, 88% and 85%. >> Grammaticality judgment accuracy was 94%, 64% and 63%. Reaction time was 1,042, 2,475 and 2,209 milliseconds. || So childhood overhearing helped **pronunciation but not grammar**. Early listening left traces in the sound system that were unmasked by later study.
32|The data from **Korean adoptees** and the data from **overhearers** can be taken together. >> If L1 is removed from the environment entirely, L2 can **appear to completely replace it**. The adoptees had no trace of Korean on any test of current ability. >> But this replacement is **not necessarily complete**. There may be some **residual knowledge** that can be "unmasked" with later learning. >> The Spanish overhearers' pronunciation is the case of unmasking. || The two populations also differ in potentially important ways. >> The adoptees were tested on what they knew, with no new Korean instruction. >> The overhearers were tested at the end of a second-year college Spanish course. That gave hidden knowledge a chance to surface. || So complete loss of exposure can make a first language seem to vanish, while traces may still remain. These and other differences make the two sets of results hard to compare directly.
33|L2 can appear to completely replace an L1 that is removed from the environment, but residual knowledge may be unmasked later. Several **differences between the two populations** could explain why overhearers kept traces and adoptees did not. >> Korean is **rarely heard** or encountered in France, but Spanish is common in Los Angeles. So overhearers may have kept hearing Spanish throughout their lives. >> A **heritage language** spoken by other family members differs from a language not spoken by anyone you know. Overhearers often heard Spanish from relatives, while adoptees lost all contact with Korean speakers. >> **Cognates** are common between Spanish and English but rare between Korean and French. Shared vocabulary could help overhearers relearn. >> A **shared writing system** is another difference. Spanish and English use the same alphabet, while Korean uses a different script from French. || This is a **general issue** with work on second language acquisition and bilingualism. >> Languages and learning situations differ in many, many ways that are hard, perhaps impossible, to control experimentally. >> Researchers cannot assign people to grow up with particular languages in particular settings. || So separating these factors would take groups that differ in only one way. Real language histories rarely provide that.
