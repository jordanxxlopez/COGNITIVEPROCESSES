2|**Aphasia** is a loss or impairment of language caused by brain damage. In the **1870s**, German neurologists including **Lichtheim** and **Wernicke** began making **more systematic observations of aphasia**. >> They ascribed specific losses of function to **“disconnections” between “centers.”** >> A **center** is a brain region that stores one kind of language representation. >> A **disconnection** is damage to the pathway linking two centers, so information can no longer travel between them. >> This was reasoning about aphasia before Chomsky. || **Lichtheim’s “House” model** arranges the centers and pathways in the shape of a house. >> **A** is the center for **sound images**, the stored sound patterns of words. >> **M** is the center for **movement images**, the stored motor plans for saying words. >> **B** is the center for **concepts**. >> The **acoustic pathway a** brings heard speech into A. The **motor pathway m** carries plans from M out to the speech muscles. >> Seven numbered sites mark different forms of aphasia. Each site is damage at a particular center or pathway. || Each damage site predicts its own pattern of loss. >> For a break between **A and B**, the word’s sound arrives but cannot reach its concept. So the person might lose the ability to **understand spoken words**. >> For a break between **A and M**, a heard word cannot pass directly to the plan for saying it. So the person might lose **verbatim repetition**. || So this kind of **“box and arrow” model** is foundational in **aphasiology**, the study of aphasia, and in modeling typical language function. >> The **interactive activation model** of word recognition has the same basic form, with levels of representation connected by pathways. >> This history comes from Eling and Whitaker (2009).
3|The **“Geschwind-Wernicke model”** links language functions to particular brain regions and the pathways between them. **Norman Geschwind** revived the box-and-arrow approach of Wernicke and Lichtheim in the **1970s**. >> **Broca’s area** sits toward the front of the left hemisphere, in the frontal lobe. >> **Wernicke’s area** sits farther back, in the temporal lobe. >> The **arcuate fasciculus** is a bundle of nerve fibers that connects Wernicke’s area with Broca’s area. || Two major grooves serve as landmarks. >> The **central sulcus**, also called the **fissure of Rolando**, separates the frontal lobe from the parietal lobe behind it. >> The **lateral sulcus**, also called the **fissure of Sylvius**, separates the temporal lobe from the frontal and parietal lobes above it. || In the century between Wernicke and Geschwind, the field of **linguistics evolved dramatically**. >> Linguists developed a theoretical model in which **syntax was independent** of other aspects of language. Syntax had **its own computational principles**. >> That opened the door to theorizing about **dissociations** and **double dissociations** of language functions in more sophisticated ways. || In a **dissociation**, one ability is impaired while another is spared. >> In a **double dissociation**, one patient loses the first ability and keeps the second. Another patient has the reverse pattern. >> A double dissociation is strong evidence that the two abilities depend on **separate systems**. >> So once syntax, word knowledge and meaning are treated as separate parts, a patient can be impaired in one part while the others work.
4|**Fromkin (1997)** describes the classic profiles of the two main aphasias. || For **Broca’s aphasia**, speech has **word-finding pauses**, **loss of grammatical morphemes**, and quite often **disturbed word order**. >> A **grammatical morpheme** is a small word or word part that marks a grammatical relationship, such as “the,” “is” or past-tense “-ed.” >> Comprehension of everyday conversation **gives the impression of being generally good**. But **controlled testing reveals considerable impairments**. || **Agrammatism** is difficulty producing or understanding grammatical structure. It is almost a synonym for Broca’s aphasia. >> Still, **some patients with lesions in Broca’s area are not agrammatic**. >> And **some agrammatic patients would not be classified neurologically as Broca’s aphasics**. >> So Broca’s aphasia names a pattern of behavior. That pattern does not always follow damage to Broca’s area. || For **Wernicke’s aphasia**, speech is **fluent** with **good intonation and pronunciation**. >> It has **many word substitutions**. Some are related in meaning to the intended word and some are not. >> It has **neologisms**, which are invented words, and **phonological errors**, which are errors in the sounds of words. >> These patients also have **comprehension difficulties**. >> Fluency describes how smoothly speech flows. It is separate from whether the speech carries a coherent message. || So Broca’s patients keep meaningful words but lose the small grammatical pieces that connect them. Wernicke’s patients keep the grammatical frame but fill it with wrong or invented words.
5|An attractive **dichotomy**, a two-way division, contrasts the two aphasias. || For **Wernicke’s aphasia**, speech is **“word salad”** or **“Jabberwocky,”** often with **neologisms**. **Syntax is roughly intact**. >> “Jabberwocky” is Lewis Carroll’s poem of grammatical sentences filled with nonsense words. >> Asked about his health, one patient said “I felt worse because I can no longer keep in mind from the mind of the minds to keep me from mind and up to the ear which can be to find among ourselves.” Kriendler and colleagues (1971) reported this answer. >> Asked about his poor vision, another patient said “My wires don’t hire right.” The sentence frame is normal, but the words do not express the problem with his eyes. >> Asked if he was a doctor, an aphasic physician said “Me? Yes, sir, I’m a male demaploze on my own. I still know my tubaboys what for I have that’s gone hell and some of them go.” **Demaploze** and **tubaboys** are neologisms. >> One patient called a **fork** “a need for a schedule” and a **spoon** “How many schemes on your throat.” || For **Broca’s aphasia**, speech is **“telegraphic.”** There is **difficulty with word order and grammatical particles**, with **relative sparing of important content words**. >> Asked what brought him to the hospital, one patient said “Yes … ah … Monday … ah … Dad and P.H. (the patient’s name) and Dad … hospital. Two … ah doctors … and ah … thirty minutes … and yes … ah … hospital. And, er Wednesday … nine o’clock and eh Thursday, ten o’clock … doctors. Two doctors … and ah … teeth. Yeah … fine.” Badecker and Caramazza (1985) reported this answer. >> Content words such as Dad, hospital, doctors and teeth carry pieces of the story. >> The grammatical words and endings that would connect them are largely missing. || So the two patterns look like mirror images. Wernicke’s patients have **fluent grammar with wrong words**. Broca’s patients have **meaningful words with little grammar**.
6|The dichotomy can be stated by what is damaged. For **Wernicke’s aphasia**, there are **problems with activation, or possibly inhibition, in the lexicon**. For **Broca’s aphasia**, there are **problems with grammar**. >> The **lexicon** is the mental store of words. >> **Activation** makes a candidate word available. >> **Inhibition** suppresses competing words that should not be chosen. So weak inhibition could let wrong words slip into speech. || This description is **fairly distant from how Broca described his original cases**. Paul Broca described **Leborgne**, known as **“Tan.”** >> Tan **could only produce one jargon syllable**, “tan.” >> He **seemed otherwise to be in possession of his senses**. >> He **seemed to understand what was being said**. || Broca’s description raises three questions. >> It is critical that Leborgne’s other senses were intact. Intact senses mean the loss was specific to speech, which makes it a **dissociation**. >> A “capacity for articulate speech” seems a more plausible function for a brain region than “criminality” or “philoprogenitiveness,” a phrenology term for love of one’s children. >> Examining Tan today would mean measuring his deficits at the **levels of description used in modern linguistics**, such as phonetic, phonological, lexical and syntactic. || So there is a confusion. **Broca’s actual patients would not necessarily be classified as Broca’s aphasics** today. >> They were arguably **unable to produce speech at all**, apparently because of **motor control problems**. >> They would be classified as having **apraxia of speech**, a disorder in planning and coordinating the movements needed to speak. >> Apraxia of speech can limit output severely without any loss of grammatical knowledge.
7|The dichotomy treats Wernicke’s aphasia as a lexicon problem and Broca’s aphasia as a grammar problem. It is interesting partly because it **aligns well with a sharp distinction between the grammar and the lexicon** in late 20th-century formal linguistics. >> The **grammar** is the system of rules for combining words into sentences. >> The **lexicon** is the store of individual words, which must be memorized. || The distinction starts with **Chomsky’s formulation of the computational problem of language learning**. >> We recognize a new item as a sentence **not because it matches some familiar item** in any simple way. >> We recognize it because **it is generated by the grammar** that each person has internalized. >> So a sentence never heard before can be understood, because the listener can work out how the grammar builds it. || **Chomsky (1955)** also gave **compelling demonstrations of the independence of syntax and semantics**. >> Neither “colorless green ideas sleep furiously” nor “furiously sleep ideas green colorless” has ever occurred in an English speaker’s past experience. >> For “colorless green ideas sleep furiously,” the meaning makes no sense, but the word order follows English grammar. So it is **grammatical**. >> For “furiously sleep ideas green colorless,” the same words come in an order the grammar does not allow. So it is **not grammatical**. >> Both sentences are equally unfamiliar and equally meaningless. So the difference between them comes from **grammar as a separate system**. || That is why the aphasia dichotomy lines up with this split. Wernicke’s aphasia would be a disorder of the lexicon, and Broca’s aphasia would be a disorder of the grammar.
8|The dichotomy also fits the **speech production model**, especially the difference between the **functional level** and the **positional level**. || The model has several levels of processing. >> At the **message level**, which is conceptual, the speaker is choosing a story angle and arousing concepts of referents and event structure. A **referent** is the person, object or event being talked about. >> At the **functional level**, the first linguistic level, the speaker is arousing **word meanings and semantic roles**. A **semantic role** is the part a participant plays in an event, such as the doer or the receiver. >> At the **positional level**, the second linguistic level, the speaker is getting **word forms in grammatically appropriate order**. >> The **phonological encoding level** supplies the sounds of the words. >> The **speech gesture level** handles **articulation**, the physical movements of speaking. || At the functional level, **lemmas have an arbitrary relationship to their referents**. >> A **lemma** is a word’s entry in the lexicon, linking its meaning to its grammatical properties. >> Arbitrary means nothing about the concept determines the word. There is no reason a dog must be called “dog.” >> This is the lexical side of language. || At the positional level, **syntactic trees and affixes are the product of abstract syntactic processes**. >> An **affix** is a word part added to a stem, such as “-ed” or “-ly.” >> This is the grammatical side of language, built by rules. || One objection is that **HOUSEBOAT** and **DOGHOUSE** have meanings that are clearly not arbitrary, because they are built from parts. >> The reply is **speech error evidence** that **derivational morphology** is done at the positional level. >> **Derivational morphology** builds new words from existing parts, such as adding “-ly” to make an adverb. >> In the error “easy enoughly,” the speaker meant “easily enough,” but “-ly” attached to the wrong word. >> The suffix moved as a separate piece. So a grammatical operation at the positional level added it.
9|Popular theories of **sentence processing** say **the syntactic structure of a sentence is computed independently of meaning** as we listen or read. >> Under these theories, an autonomous parser builds the structure first. Meaning is worked out afterward. || **Isolating these “parsing” mechanisms requires carefully constructing stimulus materials**. >> One such sentence is “The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite.” >> For the **preferred** structure, “with the dynamite” attaches to the **verb phrase**. So the dynamite is the instrument. >> For the **unpreferred** structure, it attaches to the **noun phrase** “the safe.” So the phrase tells which safe. >> The words are the same in both structures and only the attachment changes. So such sentences can test parsing without changing word meanings. || One possibility is that **Broca’s patients generally understand what is being said even though they have a core grammatical deficit**. >> In ordinary conversation, word meanings, familiar events and context all point toward the same message. >> A listener can use these cues to understand without fully computing the grammar. >> So a patient can succeed without parsing if meaning and plausibility point toward the right answer. || That is why testing a parsing deficit requires sentences whose grammatical relationships cannot be guessed from the most likely event. >> **Reversible sentences**, where either person could plausibly do the action, force the listener to use grammar.
10|**None of this theory was available to Broca in the 1860s**. For him, it was interesting enough to **dissociate “articulate speech” from comprehension**. >> Leborgne could produce only one syllable, yet he seemed to understand what was said to him. >> So speaking and understanding could come apart. That was the key finding at the time. || The modern question is different. The goal is to establish that there are patients whose **difficulty is more generally with syntax**. >> A **syntactic impairment** concerns the organization of grammatical relationships. >> Such an impairment should affect both producing and understanding sentences. || The way to do it is to **test comprehension**. >> A comprehension test lets the patient respond without speaking. So motor problems are removed from the test. >> If the patient still struggles to understand grammatical relationships, the deficit **goes beyond articulation**. || So Broca asked whether producing speech and understanding speech could be separated. Modern researchers ask whether the difficulty lies in grammar itself, which requires testing comprehension of specific structures.
11|It was interesting to test whether **Broca’s patients have comprehension deficits** because their speech alone cannot tell what is damaged. >> **Broca’s aphasia is almost always accompanied by some level of motor dysfunction**. >> So agrammatic speech might **merely reflect an attempt to communicate despite motor limitations**. >> If speaking takes great effort, a patient might drop the small grammatical words and keep the most informative content words. || **Hughlings Jackson** made a related point. **We are observing what the individual can do despite the impairment**. >> A brain injury that leads to agrammatic speech does not literally mean that **the grammar itself is injured**. >> Caramazza and Zurif (1976) cite a version of this idea from Lenneberg (1973). If anterior damage spares tacit knowledge of language, the telegraphic output could come from a neuromuscular problem. >> On that account, the strain of speaking is so great that the patient **speaks asyntactically in order to economize effort**. || **Chomsky’s distinction between competence and performance** also applies. >> **Competence** is a person’s underlying knowledge of language. >> **Performance** is the use of that knowledge in speaking or understanding. Memory limits, distraction or motor problems can disrupt it. >> When motor deficits obviously interfere with performance, **comprehension may provide a clearer window into competence**. || That is why **Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** tested comprehension in Broca’s patients. >> For the motor explanation, comprehension of grammatical structure should be normal. >> For the grammar explanation, comprehension should also suffer. >> Both explanations predict telegraphic speech, so only comprehension tests can tell them apart.
12|**Psycholinguistic tests** are needed to observe grammatical difficulties in comprehension. This is because **in conversation, there are lots of redundant cues to what a speaker intends**. >> **Redundant** cues are several different sources of information that all point to the same meaning. >> “Man bites dog” is the newspaper example of an unexpected event. || “The dog bit the man” offers **many ways to get the right meaning**. >> A listener can form the **most likely combination of DOG, BITE and MAN** into an event representation, **with or without syntax**. Dogs bite people much more often than people bite dogs. >> The sentence follows **canonical word order**, which is agent, then verb, then undergoer, or subject, then verb, then object. >> The **agent** is the doer of the action. The **undergoer** is the one the action happens to. In English, the first noun is usually the agent. >> A lot of comprehension also happens in a **discourse context**, the surrounding conversation, which clarifies a great deal. || Broca’s patients’ comprehension might depend on these **compensatory heuristics**. >> A **heuristic** is a mental shortcut. A compensatory one makes up for a weakness elsewhere. >> A patient might assume the most plausible event, or assume the first noun is the doer. >> Either shortcut gives the right answer for “The dog bit the man” without any grammatical analysis. || That is why the test sentences must defeat the shortcuts, as in “The dog that the man is biting is angry.” >> Here the **man** is doing the biting, even though the dog comes first and dogs are more likely biters. >> So only working out the grammatical relationships gives the right answer.
13|**Caramazza and Zurif** devised a list of exactly this kind of sentence, along with a set of **control sentences**. The materials have four groups of eight sentences each. || For **control sentences**, group A, the sentence is simple and in canonical order. >> Three of them are “The girl is kicking a green ball,” “The cat is chasing a blue bird” and “The boy is riding a new bicycle.” || For **center-embedded sentences with semantic constraints**, group B, meaning makes the roles obvious. >> A **center-embedded** sentence has a relative clause placed in the middle of the main clause. >> Three of them are “The apple that the boy is eating is red,” “The wagon that the horse is pulling is green” and “The balloon that the clown is holding is round.” >> Apples cannot eat boys, so meaning alone tells who did what. || For **reversible center-embedded sentences**, group C, either participant could plausibly do the action. >> Three of them are “The cow that the monkey is scaring is yellow,” “The horse that the bear is kicking is brown” and “The girl that the boy is tickling is happy.” >> Meaning gives no help, so only grammar reveals the roles. || For **improbable center-embedded sentences**, group D, the event goes against expectations. >> Three of them are “The bird that the worm is eating is blue,” “The man that the horse is riding is fat” and “The policeman that the robber is chasing is skinny.” >> Common sense points to the wrong answer. Worms do not usually eat birds, but here the worm is the eater. || In every embedded sentence, the first noun is acted on and the doer is inside the relative clause. So the structure stays the same across groups B, C and D while the help from meaning changes. >> A listener who relies on meaning should succeed on groups A and B and fail on groups C and D. >> A listener who parses normally should succeed on all four.
14|**Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** used a **sentence-picture matching task**. Participants heard a sentence and chose which picture matched its meaning. >> Accuracy reveals whether the listener understood who did what to whom, without requiring a spoken answer. >> Healthy listeners and patients with Broca’s, conduction and Wernicke’s aphasia heard all four sentence types. >> The types were control, semantically constrained, reversible and improbable sentences. || For **healthy controls**, accuracy was **more or less equally good on all stimulus types**, near the maximum score. || For **Wernicke’s patients**, accuracy was **poor overall**. >> Their scores were low even on the simple control sentences. >> This is probably a result of their **comprehension deficits**, which affect understanding of words as well as grammar. || For **Broca’s patients**, there was a particular difficulty keeping **“who did what to whom”** straight when **syntax conflicted with common sense** or with the probable semantic roles for nouns. >> Their scores were **high** on control and semantically constrained sentences, where meaning supplies the answer. >> Their scores **dropped** on reversible and improbable sentences, where only grammar can reveal the roles. || So this **specific comprehension deficit** was taken as evidence that **Broca’s aphasia impacts syntax specifically**. >> The deficit appeared in understanding as well as speaking, which places it in the grammar itself. >> The paper’s title is “Dissociation of algorithmic and heuristic processes.” >> Broca’s patients lose the **algorithmic** process of grammatical analysis. They keep the **heuristic** shortcuts based on meaning.
15|A more **specific theory of the “parts of language”** opens up **new ways of exploring structure-function relationships**. >> A **structure-function relationship** is a link between a brain structure and the function it supports. >> Before such theories, aphasias could only be described in broad terms. || Researchers no longer have to just divide aphasics into those with good and poor comprehension, or those who are more or less fluent. They can **isolate problems with particular aspects of language**. >> One patient might know word meanings well but assign the wrong roles in complex sentences. >> Another patient might produce a fluent grammatical frame filled with words that miss the intended message. >> A single fluency rating would hide the difference between these two patients. || The key division is **the grammar versus the lexicon**. >> **Grammar** concerns the rules that relate words to one another in a sentence. >> The **lexicon** concerns stored words and the ability to retrieve the right ones. || Each kind of deficit makes its own prediction. >> For a **grammar deficit**, there is trouble with reversible sentences but not with word meanings. >> For a **lexical deficit**, there is trouble with word meanings and naming but not with grammatical structure. || So controlled tasks can test whether a particular operation is impaired and relate that impairment to a particular brain region.
16|There are **problems with this dichotomy**. **Dick and colleagues** had participants listen to **four types of sentences** and **indicate which animal is doing the action**. >> Two pictures appeared on a computer screen, one of a dog and one of a cat. >> Participants pressed a button on a response box to choose the animal. >> The event was always a dog biting a cat, but the sentence structure changed. || Two sentence types put the doer first. >> For an **active** sentence, the wording is “The dog is biting the cat.” >> For a **subject cleft**, the wording is “It’s the dog that is biting the cat.” >> A **cleft** sentence splits a simple sentence with “It’s … that” to put focus on one part. In a subject cleft, the focused noun is the doer. >> For both types, choosing the first animal mentioned gives the right answer. || Two sentence types put the receiver of the action first. >> For an **object cleft**, the wording is “It’s the cat that the dog is biting.” The focused noun is the one being bitten. >> For a **passive**, the wording is “The cat is bitten by the dog.” The receiver becomes the grammatical subject. >> For both types, choosing the first animal mentioned gives the wrong answer. || So a listener who always picks the first animal succeeds on actives and subject clefts and fails on object clefts and passives. Only a listener who **parses the grammar** succeeds on all four.
17|When **aphasia patients and healthy aging controls** did this task, a pattern emerged **much like what Caramazza and Zurif observed**. Patients were grouped by aphasia type using the Western Aphasia Battery. || Each group had its own profile across active, subject cleft, object cleft and passive sentences. >> For **elderly controls**, accuracy was close to 100% on all four types. >> For **anomic** patients, whose main problem is word finding, accuracy was about 89% on actives and subject clefts. It dropped to about 69% on object clefts and about 73% on passives. >> For **conduction** aphasia patients, accuracy was about 75% on actives and subject clefts. It fell to about 42% on object clefts. >> For **Wernicke’s** patients, accuracy was low on every type, around 43% to 50%. || For **Broca’s aphasia patients**, there was **particular difficulty with the syntactically complex stimuli**, the object clefts and passives. >> They scored about 61% on actives and 65% on subject clefts. >> They dropped to about 42% on object clefts and about 54% on passives. >> These are **noncanonical** structures, meaning they depart from the usual doer-first order. || Their performance was **overall not so great**, however. >> Even on simple active sentences, Broca’s patients were far below the elderly controls. >> So the pattern combines a **general drop** in performance with an **extra cost** for complex structures. >> The groups differ in both overall accuracy and the size of the drop for complex sentences.
18|The same task was given to **healthy undergraduates** under **conditions that stress the language system** in different ways. >> The stress conditions included **speeding up the speech**, adding noise, and **asking people to remember lists of numbers as a secondary task**. >> **Compressed** speech is speech played faster than normal. >> A **low-pass filter** removes the higher frequencies from speech, which makes it sound muffled. >> **Auditory digits** and **visual digits** are number lists that participants had to remember while listening, which uses up memory resources. || For **single-stress conditions**, accuracy stayed high on actives and subject clefts. >> Object clefts suffered most. With noise, accuracy fell from about 97% on actives to about 86% on object clefts. >> Passives also dropped under noise, to about 91%. || For **dual-stress conditions**, which combine two stressors, the effects grew larger. >> With low-pass filtering plus compression, accuracy was about 91% on actives and 90% on subject clefts. >> It dropped to about 66% on object clefts and 68% on passives. || The general rule is that **object cleft and passive sentences are particularly vulnerable to all manipulations**. This is the same pattern seen in Broca’s aphasia. >> These students have no brain damage and no loss of grammatical knowledge. >> So stressing their processing system is enough to produce an aphasia-like profile. || That is why the comprehension deficits in Broca’s aphasia **may be a “performance” issue after all**. >> Complex structures may demand more processing resources, so any reduction in resources hurts them first. >> The patients may still have the grammatical knowledge, or competence, but lack the resources to use it efficiently.
19|**Saygin and colleagues (2004)** asked whether **Broca’s aphasics’ comprehension deficits are language-specific**. Patients had to **match an action to an object**. >> For the **non-linguistic** version, a drawing had a boy pantomiming an action, such as licking. >> Participants chose the matching object from pictures such as an ice cream cone, a cake, broccoli and a rooster. >> For the **linguistic** version, the action was given in words instead of by pantomime. || Controls were nearly perfect in both versions, and aphasic patients made more errors in both. >> For the non-linguistic version, controls scored **98.5%** and aphasic patients scored **93.4%**. >> For the linguistic version, controls scored **99.4%** and aphasic patients scored **87.5%**. >> So **Broca’s aphasics had difficulty with this task whether the stimuli were linguistic or not**. || Accuracy also depended on the type of wrong choice offered, called the **distractor**. >> For **unrelated** distractors, controls scored 99.8% and patients scored 93.3%. >> For **semantic** distractors, which are related in meaning to the correct object, controls scored 99.2% and patients dropped to **86.3%**. >> For **affordance-based** distractors, controls scored 97.7% and patients scored 91.6%. An **affordance** is an action an object allows, such as a different object that can also be licked. || The non-linguistic version removes language from the input but keeps meaning in the task. >> A pantomimed action has to be understood and linked to the right object, just as a verb is linked to its object. >> A purely grammatical deficit predicts good performance on the non-linguistic version. >> So difficulty on that version points to a **broader problem with action meanings**, shared with language.
20|**Performance in the non-verbal version of this task was significantly correlated with damage to Broca’s area**. >> Lesion maps mark the brain areas linked to deficits in **non-linguistic pantomime interpretation**. >> The maps cover three horizontal slices of the brain. Colored regions mark where damage was associated with poorer performance. >> The hottest colors, red and yellow, mark the strongest relationships. >> The strongest associations fall in the left frontal region in front of the **central sulcus**, which includes Broca’s area. || The task did not require producing or parsing language. >> Participants only had to interpret a pantomimed action and choose an object. >> They did not have to produce a sentence. >> They did not have to work out the grammar of a spoken instruction. || So damage to Broca’s area **affects more than syntax**. >> Patients with damage there can struggle to connect an action with its meaning even when no language is involved. >> Broca’s area supports action understanding as well as grammatical processing. >> That is why any account of Broca’s area has to explain both the grammatical comprehension findings and this nonlinguistic difficulty with actions.
21|**Saygin and colleagues** asked whether **Wernicke’s aphasics’ comprehension deficits are language-specific**. Patients had to **match environmental sounds to pictures**. >> For the **nonverbal** version, a speaker played an environmental sound, such as a cow mooing. Participants chose between pictures, such as a cow and a sheep. >> For the **verbal** version, the speaker played a spoken phrase, such as “cow mooing.” Participants chose between pictures, such as a cow and a violin. || The groups differed in overall accuracy. >> For **controls**, accuracy was 99.2% verbal and 98.1% nonverbal. >> For patients with **right hemisphere damage**, accuracy was 99.1% and 98.2%. >> For **anomic** patients, accuracy was 98.6% and 96.7%. >> For **Broca’s** patients, accuracy was 96.3% and 95.2%. >> For **Wernicke’s** patients, accuracy was **90.0% verbal** and **91.3% nonverbal**. So **Wernicke’s patients were particularly bad at this task**. || **No significant difference was observed between the verbal and nonverbal versions**. >> Wernicke’s patients struggled as much with an animal call as with a spoken phrase. >> So the deficit goes beyond understanding spoken words. >> Their difficulty concerns **linking sounds of any kind to their meanings**, whether the meaning arrives through language or through ordinary sounds.
22|**Deficits in both verbal and nonverbal sound-picture matching are associated with Wernicke’s area**. >> Lesion overlays mark where damage occurred in patients who were impaired on each version. >> **Eight** patients were impaired in the **nonverbal** version. >> **Ten** patients were impaired in the **verbal** version. >> The warmest colors, red and yellow, mark areas damaged in the largest number of patients. || The two groups look very similar. >> For both, the region of greatest overlap is the **posterior temporal area of the left hemisphere**, around Wernicke’s area. >> So the verbal and nonverbal impairments come from damage to the **same general region**. || The two versions differ in their input but share a demand. >> A spoken phrase conveys the meaning through words. >> An animal call conveys the meaning without language. >> Both tasks require connecting meaningful sounds to what they represent. || So damage around Wernicke’s area disrupts that connection in both cases. That is why Wernicke’s area appears to support **meaningful sound processing in general**, with language as one important case.
23|How language-specific the deficits in aphasia are is **still a controversy**. >> Some patients are especially vulnerable to complex grammatical structures. That suggests some aspects of language are handled in specialized ways. >> The same patients can also struggle with action meanings or environmental sounds in tasks with no language. That suggests language shares brain systems with other kinds of meaning. >> An explanation has to account for both kinds of finding. >> If aphasia deficits were purely linguistic, language would appear to have its own dedicated brain systems. || What is clear is **how the terms of the debate have evolved**. The field now relies on three kinds of tools. >> The first is **relatively sophisticated tests that tap specific aspects of language**, such as reversible and improbable sentences, cleft sentences and nonverbal matching tasks. >> The second is **quantitative analyses of behavior**, which measure accuracy across sentence types, patient groups and stress conditions. >> The third is **quantitative analyses of structure-function relationships**, which relate patterns of brain damage to performance across many patients. || So the field has come **a long way from the 19th century**. >> Wernicke and Lichtheim drew boxes and arrows from a small number of individual cases. >> Modern research can test which specific operations are impaired. It can map those impairments onto damaged tissue across large groups of patients.

25|**Voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping** formalizes **structure-function relationships** in aphasia using MRI data. It relates the location of brain damage to the severity of symptoms across many patients. >> The method comes from Bates, Wilson, Saygin, Dick, Sereno, Knight and Dronkers. >> A **voxel** is a small three-dimensional unit of an MRI brain image, like a 3D pixel. >> For each voxel, patients with damage there are compared with patients without damage there. >> If the damaged patients score much worse, that voxel is associated with the symptom. >> Colors from blue for weaker to red for stronger mark how strong the association is. || The **“symptoms”** are **defined very broadly**. >> For **fluency deficits**, the strongest associations are in the front part of the left hemisphere. >> For **comprehension deficits**, the strongest associations are farther back, in the posterior temporal region. || So data from a large number of patients is consistent with a **rough double dissociation**. >> **Posterior regions** are associated with **perception**, or comprehension. >> **Anterior regions** are associated with **production**, or fluency. >> Each patient contributes a damage pattern and a continuous score. So the method turns many individual cases into one statistical map linking brain location to symptoms.
