2|**Critical periods** and **sensitive or “optimal” periods** describe how the timing of experience affects learning. They differ in how strict the time limits are. || A **critical period** has a **discrete, “programmed” onset and offset**. >> The window opens and closes at fixed points in development. >> Change in response to the environment, which is learning, is **strictly limited to this period**. >> There is **no sensitivity to the environment before or after** the window, so experience outside it has no effect. || A **sensitive or “optimal” period** is a looser link between timing and learning. >> **Learning is possible throughout the life span**. >> Learning is **optimal during particular windows**, when it is easiest or works best. >> These windows can have a **gradual onset and offset**, and experience can itself change their timing. || The two ideas make different predictions for late learners. >> A critical period predicts **no learning** after the window, so any late improvement counts against it. >> A sensitive period predicts that late learning is **harder but still possible**, with a lasting advantage for early learners. || So the difference is between impossibility and reduced effectiveness. Even the classic critical period in vision fits the term sensitive period better, and the same question arises for language.
3|**Amblyopia** is **poor vision in one eye despite normal peripheral function**. **Peripheral function** is the working of the eye itself. So the eye can be healthy while vision through it is poor. The problem lies in how the brain handles input from that eye. || Three conditions can lead to amblyopia. >> In **strabismus**, the eyes are misaligned, so they point in different directions and send the brain two conflicting images. >> In a **cataract**, the lens of the eye is clouded, so light reaching the back of the eye is blurred or blocked. >> In **anisometropia**, the two eyes differ greatly in focusing power, so one eye's image is much blurrier than the other's. || These conditions cause **periods of deprivation from normal visual input**. That deprivation can result in **poor vision long after the conditions themselves have been remediated**, meaning corrected. >> Straightening the eyes, removing the cataract or correcting the focus restores the input from the eye. >> The brain may still fail to use that input well. || So a temporary problem early in life can leave a **lasting effect**. That is why the timing of the problem matters as much as the problem itself. >> A cataract removed early may leave little lasting damage. >> A cataract removed late may leave poor vision even after the lens is clear.
4|**Amblyopia** occurs because of **competitive dynamics between cortical inputs**. Both eyes send input to **visual cortex**. The two inputs **compete** to control the neurons there. >> When both eyes work normally, they share the cortical neurons evenly. >> Abnormal vision in one eye can lead to **“colonization”** of the cortical representation of that eye by the other eye. The stronger eye takes over neurons that would have served the weaker eye. || **Monocular deprivation** means blocking or degrading the input to one eye. In mice, it reveals how the takeover unfolds over time. >> Within **2 to 3 days**, there is **increased spine motility**. **Dendritic spines** are small projections where neurons receive connections, and they become unstable and move around. >> Within **3 to 4 days**, there is **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration**. Connections from the deprived eye disappear. Wallerian degeneration is the breakdown of nerve fibers cut off from their source. >> After **more than a week**, there is **axon sprouting and new spine birth**. The open eye's fibers branch out and form connections in the space the deprived eye lost. || When normal function is restored to the deprived eye, it **remains at a competitive disadvantage**. >> The other eye now holds the connections. >> Ordinary viewing with both eyes open keeps favoring the stronger eye. || That is why **normal cortical responses cannot be restored by normal experience**.
5|Treatments for amblyopia are **based on competition**. One of the most effective treatments involves **intermittently patching the “good” eye**. >> A patch on the stronger eye blocks its input for part of the day. >> The brain then has to rely on the weaker eye. || Patching **restores the competitive balance between the eyes**. It then permits the **brain's own plasticity** to restore normal function. **Plasticity** is the brain's ability to change its connections in response to experience. >> With the strong eye covered, the weak eye's input can win back cortical connections. >> The patch does not repair the eye itself. It changes the experience the brain receives, and plasticity does the rest. || The treatment is **most effective early in development**. It **may be ineffective by late childhood**, around 10 years. >> A young brain is highly plastic, so rebalancing the input leads to rapid reorganization. >> As the visual system matures, the same treatment helps less and less. || So the timing of treatment matters as much as the treatment itself.
6|**Levi and Polat (1996)** tested whether adults with amblyopia can still improve after the critical period. Adults with amblyopia are normally considered past the point where treatment works. Some of these patients were **in their 50s**. || The adults did **long-term, intensive training** on specific **psychophysical tasks** with the affected eye. A psychophysical task measures the relationship between a physical stimulus and what a person perceives. One such measure is the faintest contrast a person can detect. >> They practiced visual judgments with the amblyopic eye over many thousands of trials. >> Training was counted in **kilotrials**, and one kilotrial is a thousand trials. || Improvement was measured as a falling **threshold**. A threshold is the smallest stimulus a person can reliably detect, so a **lower threshold means better vision**. >> Over the first **19 kilotrials**, the threshold **dropped steadily**, and this change was highly significant. >> From about **20 to 35 kilotrials**, the threshold **leveled off** with little further change. >> Around **36 kilotrials**, the threshold **dropped again** to a new, lower level and stayed there. || Training improved both **task performance** and **general visual function**, so the benefits spread beyond the trained task. || So **plasticity is not abolished entirely**, even well into adulthood. The adult visual system keeps some **experience-dependent plasticity**. The right kind and amount of training can still produce change.
7|**Li, Ngo, Nguyen, Lam and Levi (2008)** tested video game play as training for **adult amblyopia**. Adults played an action video game with their amblyopic eye. The game was a first-person shooter set in a battle scene. || Playing video games **enhanced visual acuity and visual attention**. >> **Visual acuity** is sharpness of vision, the ability to see fine detail. Better acuity means clearer vision through the weak eye. >> **Visual attention** is the ability to pick out and focus on the important parts of a scene. Targets in a busy battle are one such part. || Action games demand quick visual decisions and constant attention. So they force the visual system to use the amblyopic eye's input. >> These adults were long past childhood visual development, so their gains came from the training itself. >> The gains came well after the age when patching usually works best. || So video game training adds to the evidence for **plasticity** in adult amblyopia. A demanding, engaging visual task can drive change in a mature visual system. That is why the closing of an early optimal window does not remove every chance of improvement.
8|**Amblyopia** is a classic example of a **sensitive period**. Three features of the early window support this. >> A **peripheral disturbance in early experience** can lead to **life-long disruption in visual function**. An early problem with the eye itself permanently affects vision. >> The long-term loss of function is the **sequela** of a temporary deficit during **experience-dependent organization of the brain**. A sequela is a lasting result of an earlier condition. The brain organized itself around abnormal input, and that organization stays. >> **Treatment is most effective during a limited developmental window**. Patching works best early and loses its power by late childhood. || However, new evidence suggests that the **right kind of experience can reverse deficits late into adulthood**. >> Intensive perceptual training improved vision in adults. >> Video game play also improved vision in adults. >> Late learning is harder and needs special conditions, but it does happen. || A strict critical period would rule out any late improvement. Amblyopia has a strong early window and some ability to change later. || That is why amblyopia fits a sensitive period better than a strict critical period.
9|Two lines of work relate the vision evidence to **language**. >> The first is **Lenneberg's early attempts** to synthesize **developmental neurology** with the notion of a **“language acquisition device”**. Developmental neurology studies how the nervous system develops. A language acquisition device is a proposed innate capacity for learning language. >> The second is **studies of early deprivation**. These use data from **neglected children** and from **Deaf children with limited input to sign**. || Lenneberg asked whether changes in the maturing brain could open and close a window for language. >> He linked language milestones to stages of brain development. >> If such a window exists, language learning depends on the state of the brain as well as on the input. || Deprivation studies look at people who first met an accessible language unusually late. >> Neglected children were cut off from ordinary language input. >> Some Deaf children have no access to sign language in early childhood. >> Comparing them with typical learners tests whether the age of first exposure affects the final outcome. || So the two lines answer two questions. Lenneberg's work asks whether the brain has a window for language. The deprivation cases ask what happens when language input arrives late.
10|In **Biological Foundations of Language**, **Lenneberg (1967)** tried to synthesize contemporary linguistics with what was known about brain development. Studies of language development were starting to find a fairly robust and **stage-like pattern**. || Data from Austrian, British and American children follow the same order between 8 and 36 months. >> **Words** come first, mostly between about 10 and 18 months. >> **Two-word phrases** come next, mostly between about 18 and 26 months. >> **Sentences of five words or more** come last, between about 28 and 36 months. || Lenneberg paired language milestones with motor milestones at each age. >> At **8 months**, infants stand holding on and pick up small objects. **Reduplicated** babbling, such as repeated syllables, becomes frequent. Its intonation patterns can signal emphasis and emotion. >> At **10 months**, infants creep efficiently and pull to standing. Vocalizations mix with sound play such as gurgling and bubble-blowing. They try to imitate sounds and begin to tell words apart. >> At **12 months**, infants walk with one hand held. Words such as **mama** or **dada** emerge. Infants show definite signs of understanding some words and simple commands. >> At **18 months**, children have a vocabulary of more than three but fewer than fifty words. Much babbling continues, and comprehension progresses rapidly. || **Maturational milestones** are developmental achievements tied to physical growth. Language milestones line up with motor milestones, so the two seem to share one biological timetable. || That is why language milestones were seen as consistent with the **unfolding of a biological program**. Lenneberg proposed that brain maturation sets the pace for both.
11|Data on typical brain development were **sparse** in Lenneberg's time. They were almost entirely based on **post-mortem analyses of tissue**, and post-mortem means after death. >> Researchers could not follow the same children's brains as they grew. >> They had to piece development together from the brains of children who had died at different ages. || **Neuron size** was measured as the cell body volume of neurons in the cerebral cortex. Cortical layers III, IV and V were measured separately. >> Neuron size grows rapidly in the **first two years** and then levels off. >> **No data were available between ages 2 and 18**. >> That gap overlaps much of the period of language acquisition. || Lenneberg observed abrupt changes in neuron size and **packing density** before age two. He proposed that these changes might be **permissive** for the beginning of language acquisition. >> Packing density is how closely neurons are crowded together in the tissue. >> Permissive means the changes make language acquisition possible without directly causing it. || So the **critical period seems to open after this initial period of rapid development**. The brain first goes through a burst of physical growth. Once that growth settles, the window for learning language opens. >> Lenneberg filled the gap in the data with a reasonable guess about how the brain matures. >> Later research would need to test that guess directly.
12|More extensive data came from studies of **recovery from stroke**. **Aphasia is surprisingly rare in children with brain lesions**. A **lesion** is an area of damaged brain tissue. >> In adults, damage to language areas often causes lasting aphasia. >> In children, similar damage often has much milder effects on language. || In extreme cases, a **hemispherectomy** was performed. This surgery removes or disconnects one entire **cerebral hemisphere**, typically to relieve seizures in people with epilepsy. >> **Children** whose left hemisphere is removed still seem to **develop typical language abilities**. >> **Adults do not recover language** after this procedure. || Lenneberg summarized hemispherectomy outcomes from **Basser (1962)** by the age when the lesion was acquired. >> For lesions **before the teens** with **left** removal, **49** had speech not affected or improved. Only **3** had permanent aphasia, and all 3 had aphasia before the operation. >> For lesions before the teens with **right** removal, **38** had speech not affected or improved. Another 5 had permanent aphasia, and all 5 had aphasia before the operation. >> For one lesion **during puberty** with left removal, there was **slow improvement**. Some aphasia remained to the end of life, 27 months after surgery. >> For **adults** with **left** removal, **none** had speech unaffected or improved, and **6 had permanent aphasia**. >> For adults with right removal, 25 had speech unaffected or improved, and none had permanent aphasia. || These findings, among other things, led Lenneberg to hypothesize what closes the critical period. He proposed that a key factor was the development of **adult-like cerebral dominance**. Cerebral dominance is the specialization of one hemisphere, usually the left, for language. >> In young children, language is not yet fixed in the left hemisphere, so the right hemisphere can take over. >> Once dominance is established in adulthood, the right hemisphere can no longer take over. That is why removing the left hemisphere then leaves permanent aphasia.
13|Systematic studies of children with **perinatal strokes** complicate the hemispherectomy findings. A perinatal stroke happens between the **prenatal period and 28 days after birth**. || Outcomes for these children are **clearly much better than for adults with similar injuries**. The children still show some effects of the injury. >> **Early language deficits** appear in **both right-hemisphere and left-hemisphere patients**. >> In adults, language problems mainly follow left-hemisphere damage. >> In young children, damage to either side causes early language delays. >> **Some deficits are worse for children with right-hemisphere damage**, which is the opposite of the adult pattern. || Language outcomes are generally in the **low-normal range**. >> That suggests a **persistent delay** rather than a catching up. >> The children recover far better than adults. >> They often do not reach the level of children without injury. || So early plasticity is **real but limited**. A young brain can reorganize to support language. The reorganized system may still work less well than the original would have.
14|**Curtiss and colleagues (1974)** studied the **effects of early deprivation** through the case of **Genie**. >> Genie was born in **April 1957**. >> When researchers first met her, she was **13 years and 7 months old**. >> She was so thin that she looked six or seven. || Her early life involved extreme isolation and abuse. >> **From the age of 20 months**, she was confined to a small room under increasing physical restraint. >> Most of the time she was harnessed to an infant's potty chair. Otherwise she was confined in a homemade sleeping bag in an infant's crib covered with wire mesh. >> She received minimal care from a mother who was rapidly losing her sight. She was fed only infant food. >> Her father **physically punished her if she made any sounds**. >> Her father and older brother did not speak to her, but at times they barked at her like dogs. >> The home had no television or radio, and her contact with other people was extremely limited. || Genie was hospitalized for malnutrition. At that point she **could not stand up straight or chew food** and was **not toilet trained**. She **did not speak, cry or produce any vocal sounds**. || So her case tells what happens when first-language learning starts in adolescence. Her lack of language came together with severe physical, social and emotional deprivation. That is why the effect of missing language input **cannot be separated** from her other hardships.
15|Genie's language was observed for **four years**, and she **learned quite a bit** in that time. >> She came to **comprehend hundreds of words**. >> She came to comprehend some aspects of **morphology**, and she was **particularly good with plurals**. Morphology is the system of meaningful word parts, such as the plural ending “-s”. >> She came to **comprehend some prepositions**, which are words for spatial relationships between objects. || A test of prepositions used **buttons and plastic boxes** of different colors and sizes. Genie had to move the buttons and boxes according to spoken instructions. >> A sample item was **“Put the red box behind the blue box.”** >> The test covered **under, over, in, on, behind, in front of, in back of** and **next to**. || Between October 1972 and August 1973, she gave **more correct than incorrect responses for every preposition**. >> **In** had 37 correct and 4 incorrect. **On** had 36 correct and 9 incorrect. >> **Over** had 11 correct and 2 incorrect. **Under** had 17 correct and 7 incorrect. >> **In front of** had 20 correct and 6 incorrect. **In back of** had 7 correct and 3 incorrect. >> **Next to** had 7 correct and 1 incorrect. **Behind** had 12 correct and 5 incorrect. || So **some language can be acquired late**. This learning took place after puberty, long after the window Lenneberg proposed. Her learning was uneven across different parts of language.
16|Genie's **production lagged severely behind her comprehension**. >> **Some lag is typical of development**, because all children understand more than they can say. >> Genie had also been **punished for making sounds** as a child, which could limit how much she spoke. >> So her speech revealed less than she actually knew. || Her **grammatical abilities developed much more slowly than her vocabulary**. A test of **conjunctions** placed **five familiar objects in a row** in front of her. She was asked to point to one or more of them. >> The items included **“Show me the fork and pencil”**, **“Show me the crayon or the knife”** and **“Show me either the spoon or the crayon”**. >> For **and**, the right response is to point to every named object. >> For **or**, the right response is to point to just one, which depends on understanding a logical relationship. || Her responses changed over the testing, from October 1971 to December 1973. >> In **early responses**, she treated **or** sentences the same as **and** sentences, so she pointed to both objects. >> In **later responses**, she told and from or apart, but she still did not understand **either/or**. >> **And** sentences produced **100 correct**, 3 incorrect and 0 unanalyzable responses. >> **Or** sentences produced only **7 correct**, **51 incorrect** and 6 unanalyzable responses. || So Genie understood and almost perfectly but failed or almost entirely, despite knowing hundreds of words. That is why her case fits the idea that **grammar depends more on early exposure than vocabulary does**.
17|Genie's case leaves **lots of questions** open. >> One question is whether Genie was **learning disabled**. She may have had difficulties from birth that would have affected her language even without deprivation. >> Another question is what **residual impacts neglect and abuse** had on her social development. Severe trauma could affect learning in many ways beyond the missing language input. || **After four years, funding to study her was pulled**. >> So nobody can know what she might have learned after a longer time. >> The evidence covers only what she learned within those four years. || Nonetheless, the **disparity between her vocabulary development and her grammatical development** is widely interpreted in one way. It is taken as suggesting that **grammar learning in a first language is specifically impaired after a sensitive period**. >> She learned hundreds of words but made little progress with grammar. >> On this interpretation, vocabulary can be learned at any age, while grammar depends on early exposure. || Disability, abuse and late exposure could each have affected her grammar. One case cannot separate them. So her pattern stays suggestive, and it still became an influential piece of evidence for a sensitive period in first-language learning.
18|**Mayberry, Lock and Kazmi (2002)** studied deaf people who learned a first language late. Many deaf children **do not get sign language input** but have an **otherwise typical upbringing**. >> Unlike Genie, these children grow up in ordinary, caring homes. >> Their only unusual experience is a lack of accessible language early in life. So they are a cleaner test of first-language timing. || Two groups of children learned **American Sign Language (ASL)** late. >> For children aged **9 to 15** learning a language for the **first time**, ASL was **difficult to learn**. >> For a comparison group who **went deaf after first learning a spoken language**, ASL learning went **much better**. >> Both groups learned ASL late, but only one group had an earlier language. >> **These differences persist into adulthood**. || Two tasks measured the adult outcome. >> The first task was **recall of complex ASL sentences**. Deaf adults with **no early language** scored about **36%**. Deaf adults with an **early spoken language** scored about **78%**. >> The second task was judging whether **complex English sentences were grammatically correct**, where chance is 50%. Deaf adults with **no early language** scored about **52%**, close to chance. Deaf adults with ASL in infancy scored about **80%**. Hearing adults with an early spoken language other than English also scored about 80%. || So the advantage comes from **having any language early**, whatever its type. Early ASL helped later English learning as much as an early spoken language did. That is why early accessible language, signed or spoken, is the foundation for learning languages later.
19|The evidence bears on whether a first language (**L1**) has a **“critical period”** or a **“sensitive period”**. Three points come out of it. || **Recovery from stroke**, and certainly from **hemispherectomy**, looks **very different in childhood and adulthood**. >> Children can develop language after damage that would leave an adult with permanent aphasia. >> So age strongly affects the brain's ability to reorganize for language. || **Deprivation of input does not appear to extend the critical period**. >> One might expect a child with no language input to keep the window open until input arrives. >> Instead, Genie and the deaf adults who learned sign late have lasting difficulty, especially with grammar. >> So the window seems to follow a maturational schedule, whether or not language has been experienced. || All of these data come from **highly unusual cases with lots of other things going on**. >> Stroke, brain surgery, abuse and neglect each bring complications beyond the timing of language input. >> There is also a **lack of animal models**, because animals do not learn human language. >> **Gene knockout experiments**, which disable specific genes to test their role, cannot be done with people. || That is why it is **difficult to reach firm conclusions**. The evidence supports age effects in first-language learning, but the exact nature of the window stays uncertain.
