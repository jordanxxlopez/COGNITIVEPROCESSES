2|**Speech perception** is the process of recovering a linguistic message from the sound of speech. Listeners take an acoustic signal that seems like a mess and retrieve a message as clear as “I can see you.” >> In the signal for “I can see you,” the energy of each sound runs into the next. Nothing in the signal cleanly separates letters or even words. >> Real-time MRI of the **vocal tract** from USC SPAN reveals why. The tongue, lips and other articulators move without pause. The movement for one sound overlaps the movement for the next, so their acoustic effects overlap as well. || Listeners also extract **the same message from very different physical stimuli**. Voices and vocal tract sizes differ, so one word has different acoustic properties from each speaker. Listeners still hear the same word. >> Across **76 speakers**, each of ten vowels covers a broad range of first and second formant values. >> Many of those ranges **overlap**. So one pair of formant values can be one vowel for one speaker and a different vowel for another speaker. || That is why recognizing speech means finding a **stable linguistic identity** inside enormous physical variation.
3|A working premise is that listeners must **turn a highly variable, analog signal into something more abstract**. The language system can then operate on that abstract form. >> An **analog signal** varies continuously, so frequency, loudness and timing can each shift by tiny amounts. >> A continuous signal has no built-in markers of where one speech sound stops and the next starts. >> Many small changes leave the speech sound the same, so two physically different productions can be the same sound. || The abstract form is a **symbolic representation**. It identifies a language unit, such as a particular consonant or vowel, and sets the fine physical detail aside. >> The unit is the **phoneme**, a sound category that distinguishes words in a language. >> /b/ in “bat” and /p/ in “pat” are different phonemes, because swapping them changes the word. >> Many slightly different acoustic versions of /b/ all count as one phoneme. || **Categorical perception** is thought to reflect this **“translation” from physical to symbolic**. Listeners are less sensitive to differences inside a category. They are more sensitive to equally large differences that cross a category boundary. >> A change stands out when it alters the identity the listener gives the sound. >> A change of the same physical size can pass almost unnoticed when both sounds remain the same phoneme. >> So categorical perception is one account of the translation, and other accounts exist.
4|The **vocal tract** is the passage sound travels through in speech. It runs from the larynx through the throat and mouth to the lips. Its shape changes as the tongue, jaw and lips move. || Following **Ladefoged (1996)**, the shape of the vocal tract is related in systematic ways to **which resonant frequencies get amplified**. >> A **resonant frequency** is one that a space naturally strengthens. A tube of a given shape boosts some frequencies and weakens others. >> Changing the shape of the vocal tract changes which frequencies get the biggest boost. || **Formants** are the resonant frequencies the vocal tract emphasizes. Every vowel has its own combination of formants. >> **For heed, hid, head and had**, the tongue moves from high and front toward low. Heed has its second formant far above its first. The two formants draw closer through hid, head and had. >> **For hod, hawed, hood and who’d**, the tongue moves back and the lips round more. The amplified frequencies gather at the low end, so the first and second formants sit close together. >> A vowel is defined by the **relationship among its resonances**. That relationship stays similar across speakers even when the exact frequencies differ. || A **formant transition** is a change in a formant’s frequency over time. >> As the mouth moves from a consonant into a vowel, the resonances glide from one set of values to another. >> The steady part of a vowel gives information about the vowel. The moving part gives information about how the neighboring sound is made. || So changing the shape of the vocal tract changes the formants. The pattern of formants is what tells one vowel from another.
5|A **spectrogram** maps acoustic energy over time and frequency. Each vowel has several bands of strong energy, and these bands are its **formants**. >> The recordings are Rob Hagiwara’s English vowel samples. || The words **bead, bid, bade, bed, bad, bod, baud, bode, “bood” and booed** all begin with /b/ and end with /d/. The consonants are the same, so the differences between the words come mainly from the vowels. >> **For bead**, the first formant is very low and the second formant is very high, above 2,000 Hz. The two formants are far apart. >> **Through bid, bade, bed and bad**, the first formant rises and the second formant falls. The two formants move closer together. >> **For bode, “bood” and booed**, the back rounded vowels, the second formant drops low, near 1,000 Hz or below. It sits near the first formant. || **F1** is the lowest formant and **F2** is the next one up. Both run through the whole vowel at the same time. >> Bead pairs a **low F1 with a high F2**. Booed pairs a **low F1 with a low F2**. >> So the pattern of the two lowest formants gives most of what tells vowels apart.
6|**Source-filter synthesis** builds speech sounds from two parts. The **source** is the sound energy the voice produces. The **filter** is how the vocal tract shapes that energy. >> Separating the two parts makes it possible to change one while the other stays fixed. >> The work is done in **Praat**, free speech analysis software from praat.org. Praat displays recordings, measures formants and resynthesizes sounds. || The recording is a speaker saying **/u/**, the vowel in “booed.” >> A **waveform** records changes in air pressure over time, so it captures timing and loudness. >> A **spectrogram** breaks the same sound into its frequency components, so it captures the resonance pattern. || This /u/ has its **first formant at about 300 Hz**. Its **second formant is very low, around 900 Hz**. >> Both resonances run together through the whole vowel. >> A low F1 and a low F2 together fit the back rounded quality of /u/. || If changing only the filter changes the vowel, the vowel’s identity must lie in the filter. The filter is the vowel’s pattern of formants. That is why these measured formants are the starting point for building new filters.
7|**Praat can find formants automatically**. It follows each resonance through a recording and marks its frequency moment by moment. In this recording of /u/ it identifies **five formants**. >> Several resonances run at the same time, and each one is a frequency component of the same sound. >> Automatic tracking measures the resonance pattern without anyone estimating frequencies by eye. || **Only the first three formants are usually important for phonemic processing**. >> The numbers give the order of the resonances from low to high. F1 is the lowest, F2 is next and F3 is third. >> F1, F2 and F3 give most of the information that separates phonemes. The higher formants depend more on the individual voice. || The synthesis changes only **the first two formants**. In this /u/ they sit near 300 Hz and 900 Hz. >> These two formants hold most of the vowel information, so changing them is enough to turn one vowel into another. >> F3 and the higher formants stay fixed. So any change listeners hear comes from F1 or F2 alone.
8|The **source** can be **extracted** from the recorded /u/. Praat builds a model of the filter from the measured formant frequencies. That model is used to strip the filter’s effect from the recording. >> What remains is a **more-or-less “unfiltered” version of the voice**. It is the sound energy the vocal folds supplied. >> It is only more or less unfiltered, because the separation is an estimate. || The extracted source **sounds like a buzz**. >> Its energy is spread across many frequencies, without the strong low formants of /u/. >> The pitch survives, because pitch comes from vocal fold vibration. >> The vowel quality disappears, because vowel quality comes from the filter. || The separation is **a little bit like doing a multiple regression analysis**. >> In multiple regression, one predictor’s contribution is estimated and removed so the rest can be examined. >> Here the filter’s contribution is estimated and removed, and what is left is treated as the source. || So with the source isolated, new filters can be applied to it. Keeping one source while swapping filters makes the effect of the resonance pattern testable.
9|A **filter** specifies which frequencies get more emphasis and which get less, so every filter is a resonance pattern. Three different filters can be applied to the one source. >> **The first filter** is just the first four formants from the original recording of /u/. Applying it should bring back the original vowel. >> **The second filter** raises **the first formant a little**. The other formants stay roughly where they were. >> **The third filter** raises **the second formant a lot**. F1 and the higher formants stay roughly in place. || Each filter changes one part of the resonance pattern instead of shifting every frequency together. >> In the original filter, F1 and F2 are both low, which fits /u/. >> Raising F1 moves only the lowest resonance up. >> Raising F2 moves only the second resonance up, by a much larger amount. || The **sound source stays the same** for all three filters. No new speaker or recording is needed. So any change in vowel quality comes from the filter. >> Comparing the outputs isolates what a change in the lowest resonance does and what a change in the second resonance does.
10|**Multiplying the source by the filters gives new vowels**. The output sound is the source shaped by the filter. >> The source provides the raw sound energy. >> The filter decides how much of that energy stays strong in each frequency region. >> Each filter boosts a different set of frequencies, so each combination is a different vowel. || One source and three filters give three results. >> **With the original filter**, F1 and F2 stay low, so the result keeps the quality of /u/. >> **With F1 raised a little**, the lowest resonance rises, so the vowel changes. >> **With F2 raised a lot**, the second resonance rises far, so the vowel changes in another way. The back rounded /u/ moves toward a more front vowel. || **Source-filter synthesis** separates two contributions that ordinary speech combines. The voice provides the sound, and the vocal tract resonances shape its quality. >> All three outputs share the same buzz, pitch and timing, yet they sound like different vowels. >> So a researcher can change vowel quality by swapping only the filter. No speaker has to record each version.
11|The vowel a speaker intends only **partly** determines formant values. Three other things also shape them. >> **Anatomical features** differ between speakers of the same language. Vocal tracts vary in length and shape, and a longer tract gives lower resonances overall. >> **Different rates of speech** matter, because fast speech gives the articulators less time to reach their targets. >> **Context** matters, such as the phones produced before and after a vowel. The tongue and lips are already heading toward the next sound or leaving the previous one. A **phone** is one particular spoken realization of a speech sound. || Across 76 speakers, each of ten vowels covers a wide range of formant values, and the ranges overlap. >> The same F1 and F2 values can be different vowels for different speakers. >> So no single pair of frequencies defines a vowel for everyone. || Formant values nonetheless give a pretty good description of **vowel space**, especially for **a specific talker speaking carefully**. Vowel space is the layout of a language’s vowels by their formant frequencies. >> Within one careful talker, the vowels separate far more cleanly than across many talkers. >> A **steady-state formant** is the resonance in the stable middle of a vowel, as opposed to the transitions at its edges. >> So, all else being equal, **reasonable approximations of vowels** can be produced **by manipulating steady-state formants**.
12|The **pattern playback synthesizer** turns a painted spectrographic pattern back into sound. Researchers paint bands on a transparent sheet wherever they want acoustic energy in time and frequency. The machine then plays the pattern aloud. >> Changing the painting changes the sound. >> So researchers could build controlled, speech-like sounds. They did not have to rely on natural recordings, which vary in many uncontrolled ways. || The machine works by shining light through the painted pattern. >> A light source shines through a **cylindrical lens** onto a spinning **tone wheel**. The rings of the tone wheel modulate the light at many frequencies at once. >> A lens and a **mirror** send that light onto the painted **spectrogram**. The painted areas pass along the matching frequencies. >> A **light collector** and a **photocell** turn the light into an electrical signal. >> An **amplifier** and a **loudspeaker** turn that signal into sound. || A researcher can alter a single formant band or transition and hear the result right away. >> Every other part of the pattern stays exactly the same. So any change in what listeners hear comes from the one painted change. >> That is why series of patterns differing in tiny steps revealed how formant transitions signal consonants.
13|**Stop consonants are distinguished by formant transitions**. The stop consonants **/b/, /d/ and /g/** are made by briefly closing the vocal tract and then opening it. >> The audible part of a /b/, /d/ or /g/ happens **while the jaw is opening**. >> During that opening, the vocal tract changes shape quickly. >> The changing shape makes the formants glide from the consonant’s position toward the vowel’s position. So the consonant’s identity lies in how the formants move. || **Liberman and colleagues (1957)** used the **pattern playback synthesizer** to create speech-like sounds that replicated these formant transitions. Each painted pattern had two formants. >> **The lower formant** was nearly the same in every stimulus. >> **The upper formant** started at a different frequency in each stimulus and then settled at the same steady vowel level. >> In the early stimuli it started low and **rose** into the vowel. In the middle stimuli it was nearly **flat**. In the later stimuli it started high and **fell** into the vowel. || The fourteen patterns kept the vowel the same and changed only where the upper transition began. >> A rising transition was heard as **/b/**. A roughly flat transition was heard as **/d/**. A falling transition was heard as **/g/**. >> So listeners’ responses could be tied directly to the transition, because only the transition differed.
14|A **speech continuum** is a series of sounds that change step by step along one controlled acoustic property. This continuum **keeps F1 constant** and **varies the F2 onset frequency linearly** from stimulus 1 to stimulus 13. >> The **F2 onset frequency** is the frequency at which the second formant begins its transition. >> Keeping F1 constant removes one possible difference between stimuli. >> Varying the F2 onset linearly means it changes by the same step every time. So **adjacent stimuli differ by the same amount across the entire continuum**. || The F2 transition shifts gradually along the series. >> **For the early stimuli**, F2 starts well below the vowel frequency, so the transition rises. >> **For the middle stimuli**, the onset is higher, so the rise shrinks until the transition is nearly flat. >> **For the later stimuli**, F2 starts above the vowel frequency, so the transition falls. || Every step is physically equal, so the continuum is a fair test of whether perception follows the acoustics or the categories. >> Some neighboring pairs fall inside one consonant category, and other pairs straddle a boundary. >> A purely acoustic listener would hear every step as the same size. >> If perception is categorical, steps within a category will sound alike and steps across a boundary will sound different. >> So any advantage for boundary pairs comes from the boundary itself.
15|An **ideal observer model** predicts how a listener would respond if perception worked according to one particular account. This simple version assumes that perception is **non-categorical**. >> Non-categorical perception tracks the acoustic signal gradually, with no sharp category boundaries. >> The model is a baseline. Real data can be compared against it to see whether perception is more categorical than a gradual account allows. || The first prediction is that **categorization gets “blurry” as you move away from category prototypes**. A **prototype** is the clearest example of a category. >> The endpoints of the continuum are /b/ and /g/, with /d/ in the middle. >> Between these points, **stimulus ambiguity should scale with the distance from the prototype**. >> A stimulus farther from the /b/ prototype sounds less like /b/ and more like /d/. >> **Stimulus 2** would be labeled **75% /b/ and 25% /d/**. It sits a quarter of the way from the /b/ prototype toward the /d/ prototype. || So under this account, **labeling** changes smoothly. >> Responses for one category fade out in a straight slope while responses for the neighboring category fade in. >> The /b/ responses reach 0 at stimulus 6, where the /d/ responses peak at 1. The /d/ responses fall back to 0 at stimulus 10, where the /g/ responses reach 1. >> Two neighboring labels are equally likely halfway between their prototypes. No label changes in a sudden step.
16|The same simple **ideal observer** can assume that perception is **linear**. Linear perception means equal acoustic differences are perceived as equally large. This leads to a second prediction, that **discrimination performance should be independent of labeling**. >> **Labeling** asks a listener to identify a sound as /b/, /d/ or /g/. >> **Discrimination** asks only whether two sounds are the **same or different**. >> These are separate tasks, even when they use the same continuum. || Labeling **has to have a “peaked” function**. >> There are three categories, and listeners are choosing between labels. >> So each category’s responses rise toward its prototype and fall away from it. >> These peaks come from the structure of the labeling task itself. || Discrimination works differently under the linear account. Pairs are compared two steps apart, from **1 and 3** through **9 and 11**. Every pair has the same physical separation. >> If perception of differences in F2 onset were constant over the whole continuum, discrimination accuracy would also be constant. >> So accuracy would stay level for every pair, at about 0.8, even where labeling changes. >> A listener can be unsure which label to choose and still hear that two sounds differ. || So the two possible outcomes mean different things. A **flat** pattern would mean category boundaries play no role in hearing differences. A **peaked** pattern would mean the boundaries make some differences easier to hear.
17|**Liberman and colleagues (1957)** measured actual labeling and discrimination along the /b/, /d/ and /g/ continuum. Their results departed sharply from the non-categorical, linear predictions, and the pattern is called **categorical perception**. >> Listeners labeled each stimulus. >> Listeners also judged pairs of stimuli that were **one step**, **two steps** or **three steps** apart. || Labeling changed **sharply** from one consonant to the next. >> **Stimuli 1 through about 3** were labeled /b/ close to 100% of the time. >> **Around stimuli 4 and 5**, /b/ responses dropped steeply and /d/ responses climbed to nearly 100%. /d/ stayed dominant through about stimulus 8. >> **Around stimuli 9 and 10**, /d/ responses dropped steeply and /g/ responses rose to near 100%. >> These switches are far more abrupt than the gradual slopes the ideal observer predicted. The narrow region where the label flips is the **category boundary**. || **Listeners were best at discriminating sounds near the category boundaries**. >> Accuracy peaked at the /b/ to /d/ boundary and again at the /d/ to /g/ boundary. >> Pairs inside one category were discriminated poorly, close to **chance**, which is the level expected from guessing. >> Larger steps raised accuracy overall, but the peaks stayed at the boundaries. || Liberman and colleagues also predicted discrimination from the labeling data. They assumed listeners can tell two sounds apart only when they give them different labels. >> The predictions captured much of the real pattern. >> Real accuracy was often a little higher than predicted, especially within categories. >> So listeners keep some sensitivity to acoustic differences beyond the category labels alone.

19|Data from students in prior years tested **categorical perception** along a **beach to peach continuum**. The two words differ in their first consonant, /b/ or /p/. >> In the **identification** task, each listener labeled every stimulus from 1 to 9. >> In the **discrimination** task, each listener judged pairs two steps apart, from stimuli 1 and 3 through stimuli 7 and 9. || **For the first listener**, identification stays on one word for the early stimuli. It then **switches to the other word** around the middle of the continuum. >> Responses are mixed only in a small region near the switch. The acoustic change is gradual, but the label changes within a narrow region. >> Discrimination is best for the pair of stimuli 4 and 6 and the pair of stimuli 5 and 7. These pairs **straddle the identification boundary**. >> Pairs well inside one category are hard to tell apart, even though their physical separation is the same. >> The pair of stimuli 6 and 8 falls in between, because stimulus 6 itself received mixed labels. || **For the second listener**, the word label **switches later** along the continuum. Discrimination is best near that later boundary. || So different people can place their category boundaries at different points. For each listener, the best discrimination falls close to that person’s **own identification boundary**.
20|The data from prior years support a few general points. The first is that **not everyone has exactly the same category boundary**. >> A sound near the beach to peach boundary for one listener can fall squarely inside a category for another listener. >> A pair that straddles one person’s boundary may stay inside one category for someone else. >> So when individual boundaries differ, averaging across the group can flatten the boundary peak. || **In general, discrimination is best for stimuli perceived as being in different categories**. >> Listeners may miss a physical difference between two versions of the same phoneme. >> A difference of the same size is far easier to hear when the two sounds are heard as different words. || The discrimination experiment included **only “different” trials**, which made the lab doable in a short amount of time. Every pair held two physically different stimuli. >> The correct answer on every trial was **“different,”** if the listener was able to discriminate the two sounds. >> Answering “same” meant missing a real difference. >> Accuracy is the share of pairs heard as different. Someone who always said “different” would score perfectly. >> So low accuracy on within-category pairs means listeners often failed to hear differences that were really there.
21|More results from prior years compare a **bear to pear continuum** with the **beach to peach continuum**. >> **For bear to pear**, identification switches fairly clearly from one word to the other. Discrimination jumps between 0 and 1 from one pair to the next. It includes a miss on a pair that crosses the boundary. >> **For another listener’s beach to peach data**, the responses fluctuate even more. Identification zigzags instead of switching once, and discrimination bounces between high and low values. || The one iron-clad law of experimental research is that **stuff happens**. Responses to a carefully built continuum can still bounce around. >> Identification records which word a listener chooses. Discrimination records whether a difference is detected. These two behaviors can vary in different ways across trials and people. >> An isolated rise or drop in discrimination reflects noise in a few responses. || The experiment was not the best in two ways, and it could be done differently. >> Only “different” trials were used. So nothing measured how often listeners said “different” when the sounds were identical. >> The short session gave each listener **few trials per pair**. So a single response could move accuracy a great deal. >> So a better version would include trials with identical sounds and more trials for each pair. >> The real test is whether better discrimination lines up with the place where identification changes, across the whole continuum.
22|It is not obvious **how categorical perception would help**, or what the advantage is of being worse at hearing some differences between sounds. >> Categorical perception makes listeners worse at hearing differences within a category. >> That can seem like a weakness, because the listener is throwing away acoustic detail. || The advantage is **stable word recognition**. >> Different productions of the same phoneme are always physically different, because of speakers, speaking rates and neighboring sounds. >> If every small acoustic change were heard as a new identity, everyday versions of one word would sound like unrelated sounds. >> Reduced sensitivity within a category lets many acoustic versions count as **one phoneme**. || Differences **between categories** stay easy to hear, so the distinctions that change words are kept. >> Variation inside a category is tolerated, because it leaves the word unchanged. >> So ignoring small within-category differences frees the listener to focus on the differences that change meaning.

24|**Categorical perception in infants** can be tested even though infants cannot name sounds. Infants react to novelty, so researchers watch for a behavior that changes when an infant notices something new. || The **conditioned head turn procedure** comes from Janet Werker’s infant studies lab. >> An infant learns to turn toward a sound when the sound changes. >> A correct turn earns an interesting visual reward, such as a moving toy. >> After the infant learns this link, a head turn following a change means the infant detected the change. || The infant results on categorical perception come from **high-amplitude sucking**. >> The infant sucks on a pacifier attached to a recording device, and strong sucks make a sound play. >> As the same sound repeats, the infant grows used to it and sucking slows. Growing used to a repeated sound is **habituation**. >> If a new sound makes sucking **increase again**, the infant has noticed the change. || Both methods make perception testable before an infant can talk. >> The experimenter controls exactly which sound difference is presented. >> So a head turn or renewed sucking reveals sensitivity to that difference without any labeling. This works even in infants who are months from saying words.
25|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** tested categorical perception in **4-month-old infants** using **high-amplitude sucking**. The sounds varied in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**. VOT is the delay between the release of a consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration. >> Adults hear a short VOT as a voiced consonant such as /b/. Adults hear a longer VOT as a voiceless consonant such as /p/. >> With a VOT of +100 milliseconds, voicing begins much later after the release than with a VOT of +10 milliseconds. || Infants heard one synthetic syllable over and over until their sucking slowed down. Then the sound either changed or stayed the same. >> **In condition 20D**, the VOT changed from **+20 to +40 milliseconds**. This 20-millisecond change **crossed** the adult boundary between two consonant categories. >> **In condition 20S**, the VOT also changed by **20 milliseconds**, from −20 to 0 or from +60 to +80. Both sounds came from the **same** adult category. >> **In condition 0**, the control group kept hearing the same sound. || In every group, sucking rose at first and then dropped as the sound became familiar. >> **In 20D**, sucking **recovered** sharply after the change. >> **In 20S**, sucking kept falling with little recovery. >> **In 0**, sucking kept declining steadily. || The two changed conditions were matched at 20 milliseconds, so the difference comes from crossing the boundary. >> Infants noticed a change that adults would hear as a new consonant. They did not notice an equally large change inside one category. >> So even at four months, infants perceive voicing **categorically**.
26|**Perceptual narrowing** is the process in which experience tunes perception to the sounds of the surrounding language. Along the way, sensitivity to some distinctions from other languages declines. || **Werker and Tees (1984)** tested how age and experience affect infants’ ability to discriminate two **non-English contrasts**. >> The **Hindi contrast** is a distinction between two “ta”-like sounds that English does not use. >> The **Salish contrast** is a distinction between “ki” and “qi” in Nthlakampx, a Salish language. English does not use it either. >> The measure was the percentage of infants who reached a **discrimination criterion** at each age. || The English-learning infants did worse as they got older. >> **At 6 to 8 months**, about 95% met criterion for Hindi and about 80% for Salish. >> **At 8 to 10 months**, about 67% met criterion for Hindi and about 52% for Salish. >> **At 10 to 12 months**, about 20% met criterion for Hindi and about 10% for Salish. >> **Hindi-learning and Salish-learning infants** met criterion close to 100% of the time for the contrast from their own language. || These results are described as **perceptual reorganization during the first year of life**. >> Young infants can detect these nonnative contrasts. >> With experience of English, English-learning infants become much less likely to discriminate them by about their first birthday. >> Infants who hear the contrast in their own language keep it, so the decline depends on language experience. >> So contrasts the language uses are kept, and contrasts it lacks become hard to hear.
27|The earlier findings feed **a very compelling narrative** about speech perception. The narrative makes three linked claims. || **The first claim** is that speech perception depends on **categorical perception abilities that are a special property of the language system**. >> On this claim, the mechanism that sorts sounds into categories is specialized for language. || **The second claim** is that these abilities are **present very early in development**. >> The infant sucking and head-turn experiments suggest the abilities are in place long before children talk. || **The third claim** is that children **start off as “citizens of the world,”** able to hear **all** contrasts. They then **rapidly tune their perceptual systems to their specific language**. >> As “citizens of the world,” infants could learn any human language and are ready to hear the distinctions of every one. >> Tuning means the contrasts of the surrounding language stay available while contrasts from other languages fade, as in the Werker and Tees results. || So the narrative covers both the **starting state** and **developmental change**. >> It proposes broad, language-specific abilities at birth. >> It treats development mostly as narrowing from that broad start. >> Each early result seems to fit. Infants discriminate categorically before talking. They hear contrasts from many languages. They lose nonnative contrasts with experience.
28|The compelling narrative meets **some issues**. Three of them concern **categorical perception**, and one concerns **infant speech perception**. || Categorical perception is **not limited to language**. >> It also appears with nonspeech sounds. So the claim that categorical perception is special to language is weakened. || Categorical perception is **not limited to humans**. >> Nonhuman animals also perceive speech sounds categorically. So the claim that categorical perception is special to humans is weakened. || Categorical perception is **task-dependent**. >> Results look more or less categorical depending on what the listener is asked to do. >> Choosing between labels makes responses more categorical than a task that measures fine acoustic differences. || Infant speech perception is **not actually “universal.”** >> Some contrasts are harder for infants to detect than others from early on. >> So development includes initial differences in sensitivity as well as changes driven by experience. || That is why these issues weaken the strong claim that every speech distinction starts out equally available in a mechanism devoted only to language.
29|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** tested categorical perception with **non-speech sounds**. The sounds varied in **rise time**, which is how quickly a sound reaches full loudness after it begins. >> A sound with a **rapid rise time** sounds **plucked**, like a plucked string. >> A sound with a **slow rise time** sounds **bowed**, like a bowed string. >> Pluck and bow are labels for two kinds of sound onset. They are not phonemes. || The stimuli included **sawtooth waves**, **sine waves** and **speech syllables**. Each was made with short and long rise times, such as 10 and 70 milliseconds. >> **With a 10-millisecond rise**, the sound reaches full strength almost at once. >> **With a 70-millisecond rise**, the sound builds up gradually. || For the sawtooth waves, listeners labeled the sounds and discriminated pairs that differed by 10 milliseconds. >> Short rise times were labeled pluck close to 100% of the time. Long rise times were labeled bow close to 100% of the time. >> The label switched steeply around 30 to 50 milliseconds, in the middle of the continuum. >> Discrimination stayed around 55 to 60% for most pairs, only a little above chance. >> Discrimination **peaked near 75%** for the pairs around 30 to 50 milliseconds, right where the labels switched. || So the link between a label boundary and better discrimination appears for a sound distinction that has nothing to do with language. The physical continuum is gradual, yet the label changes over a narrow region and discrimination improves there.
30|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** tested whether **infants** show categorical perception of **non-speech sounds**. They used **high-amplitude sucking** to measure whether a change in sound was detected. >> Infants heard one sound repeatedly until their sucking declined. >> The sound was then shifted, and the change was compared across three conditions. || The three conditions were **no change**, a change **within categories** and a change **between categories**. In every condition, sucking declined before the shift as the sound became familiar. >> **After no change**, sucking stayed low. >> **After a within-category change**, sucking also stayed low. >> **After a between-category change**, sucking jumped back up. It then declined again as the new sound became familiar. || So infants responded more strongly to a non-speech change that crossed a category boundary than to a change within a category. >> This mirrors the pattern **Eimas and colleagues (1971)** found for speech. >> Infants cannot be using knowledge of words or phonemes when they respond to plucked and bowed sounds. >> So their categorical response must come from more general auditory processing. The same processing could also underlie their categorical response to speech.
31|Categorical perception of speech sounds has also been found in **chinchillas**. The evidence comes from **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and the earlier work of **Kuhl and Miller (1975)**. || The animals were trained with **operant conditioning**, a form of learning in which consequences change how often a behavior occurs. >> Each chinchilla learned that either the **0-millisecond lag** stimulus or the **80-millisecond lag** stimulus was an **SD** for food. >> **SD** stands for **discriminative stimulus**, which is a signal that a response will be rewarded. >> The lag is **voice onset time**. Humans hear a 0-millisecond lag as /ga/ and an 80-millisecond lag as /ka/. || After training, the chinchillas were tested with the VOTs in between, along the **/ga/ to /ka/ continuum**. Human listeners were tested on the same sounds. >> Short VOTs were labeled /ga/ almost every time. Long VOTs were almost never labeled /ga/. >> The switch happened over a fairly narrow range of VOTs, mostly between about 30 and 70 milliseconds. >> The chinchillas’ labeling had the same steep, categorical shape as the humans’ labeling. || The study also varied the **burst** and the **aspiration noise**. The burst is the brief noise at the release of a stop consonant. Aspiration is the breathy noise that follows a voiceless release. >> Making the burst and aspiration louder shifted the boundary toward shorter VOTs, made the switch steeper, or both. >> This happened for one chinchilla and for one human listener, so both species responded to these sound properties in a similar way. || So animals without human language can sort a speech continuum into categories. That argues against the view that categorical perception is special to human language.
32|Some contrasts are **less salient than others** for infants. A **salient** contrast is one that is easy to notice. >> The contrasts tested here differ in **place of articulation**, which is where in the mouth a sound is made. >> **In [ma]**, the nasal is made with the lips. >> **In [na]**, the nasal is made with the tongue near the ridge behind the teeth. >> **In [ŋa]**, the nasal is made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate. [ŋ] is the sound at the end of “sing.” || Researchers compared infants’ **looking time** on **same** trials and **change** trials. On same trials the syllable stayed the same. On change trials it switched to the other syllable. Longer looking on change trials means the infant noticed the change. || **For English-hearing infants at 4 to 5 months**, the result depended on the contrast. >> For the native **[ma] and [na]** contrast, they looked about 3.3 seconds on same trials and about 5.4 seconds on change trials. So they detected the change. >> For the nonnative **[na] and [ŋa]** contrast, they looked about 4.5 seconds on same trials and about 4.2 seconds on change trials. So they did not detect the change. >> English never uses [ŋ] at the start of a syllable. || **For Filipino-learning infants**, [na] and [ŋa] is a contrast in their own language. >> At **6 to 8 months**, they looked about 3.8 seconds on both kinds of trials, so they did not yet detect the change. >> By **10 to 12 months**, looking rose from about 3.3 seconds on same trials to about 4.8 seconds on change trials. || So some contrasts start out hard for infants to hear, even when they belong to the native language. Experience with the language **strengthens** sensitivity to such contrasts over the first year. That is why development includes gains as well as losses.
