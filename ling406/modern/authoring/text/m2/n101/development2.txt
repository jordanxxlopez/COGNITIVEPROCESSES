2|**Speech has no physical boundaries between words, yet listeners find the words anyway.** The job of finding them is **word segmentation** (working out where one word stops and the next one starts in continuous speech). >> In “I hope it’s in my desk,” the sound energy of each word flows straight into the next word. >> No silent gap separates the words. || The pauses in the speech stream come from **closures** (moments when the vocal tract is fully blocked, as for /p/, /t/, /d/ and /k/). >> Very little sound escapes during a closure, so a brief silence appears. >> Closures are **as likely to happen within words as between words**. >> In “hope” and “it’s,” the silences fall inside words or at their edges, so they do not match word boundaries. >> Treating every silence as a boundary would split some words and merge others. || We are still **really good at finding words** in this “sea of sounds.” >> Adults hear separate words with no effort and can lean on the words they already know. >> The **harder question** is **how we learn to do this**. >> Infants have to discover the words in the stream before they can learn what the words mean.
3|**Several cues point to word boundaries, and no single cue works for every word.** >> Some words **appear in isolation**, as in “Hello,” “No,” “Please,” “Go” and “Stop.” >> Not all words are heard alone, so this cue covers only part of the vocabulary. || **Phonotactics** (the patterns for which sound sequences a language allows) is a second cue. It works because **some sequences of phonemes are very unlikely to occur within words**. >> In “wordboundary,” /db/ almost never occurs inside an English word, so it suggests a break between word and boundary. >> In “speechcode,” /tʃk/ suggests a break between speech and code. >> In “movement,” /vm/ might suggest a break, but movement is one word. || **Stress** (emphasis that makes a syllable louder, longer or higher in pitch) is a third cue. It works because **in English most words are stressed on the first syllable**. >> **Jusczyk and colleagues (1999)** familiarized **7.5-month-old infants** with one word and then played passages. >> The kingdom passage repeats “kingdom,” as in “Your kingdom is in a faraway place.” >> The device passage repeats “device,” as in “Your device can do a lot.” >> Infants familiarized with “kingdom” (first-syllable stress) **showed a preference for the kingdom passage**. >> Infants familiarized with “device” (second-syllable stress) **did not show the same kind of preference**. >> An infant who starts a word at each stressed syllable finds kingdom but cuts device in the wrong place. >> Stress **may help signal the beginnings of words**, but not in every language or for every English word.
4|**Syllables inside a word predict each other much more strongly than syllables across two words.** This is the cue from **transitional probabilities**, and it is found through **statistical learning** (picking up patterns in how often things occur together). >> Consider “pretty baby.” >> In English words, the syllable **/ti/** is very strongly predicted by the syllable **/prɪ/**. >> After “pre,” the next syllable is very often “ty,” because pretty is a common word. >> Many different words can follow “ty,” so “ba” is poorly predicted. || The comparison can be written with frequencies of occurrence. >> The frequency of “pretty” divided by the frequency of “pre” is the chance that “ty” follows “pre.” >> The frequency of “tyba” divided by the frequency of “ty” is the chance that “ba” follows “ty.” >> The first value is **much greater** than the second. || A learner who counts how often each syllable follows each other syllable can find the joints in speech. >> Pairs inside a word recur together again and again. >> Pairs across a word boundary recur much less reliably.
5|**Syllable B is more likely to follow syllable A within a word than between words, but only as a matter of probability.** That likelihood is the **transitional probability** from A to B. >> Inside a word, the same syllables always come in the same order, so the probability is high. >> At a word boundary, almost any word can come next, so the probability is lower. >> A drop in transitional probability is a good guess for a word boundary. || The cue is **probabilistic**, so it works most of the time and fails some of the time. >> “What’s that in the road a/head?” can be heard as “a head” or as “ahead.” >> The sounds are identical, so the meaning of the whole sentence decides the boundary. >> “I scream you scream we all scream for ice cream” plays on “I scream” and “ice cream” having almost the same sounds. >> One sequence of sounds can be split into words two ways. || Transitional probabilities work alongside stress, phonotactics and words heard alone. >> When the cues agree, segmentation is easy. >> When the cues clash or are missing, listeners fall back on context.
6|**Saffran and colleagues (1996) removed every cue to word boundaries except transitional probability.** They wanted to find out whether **transitional probabilities** alone can be used to identify words, so the stimuli were **tightly controlled**. >> The speech was **monotone** (no changes in pitch, duration or amplitude), so stress could not mark word beginnings. Amplitude is loudness. >> The syllables were **simple CV syllables** (one consonant followed by one vowel), so there were no phonotactic cues. >> The syllables were **presented continuously** (no pauses), so silence could not mark boundaries. || They created an **artificial language** from a set of **three-syllable “words,”** such as tokibu, gikoba and gopila. >> The words were joined in random order into one long stream. >> The only clue to where a word began and ended was the transitional probability between syllables. >> Infants who find the words under these conditions must be using transitional probabilities alone.
7|**The artificial stream has no breaks, so the only signal of the words is the order of the syllables.** >> The stream runs like “tokibugikobagopilatipolutokibu” and “gopilatipolutokibugikobagopila” and “gikobatokibugopilatipolugikoba.” >> The infant hears exactly this, with no spaces and no pauses. >> **Four words** are buried in it, which are tokibu, gikoba, gopila and tipolu. >> Each word recurs many times in random order. || The syllables follow **regular patterns** even with no marked boundaries. >> Inside **tokibu**, “to” is always followed by “ki.” >> Inside tokibu, “ki” is always followed by “bu.” >> After “bu,” the stream may go on with “gi,” “go” or “ti,” depending on the next word. || Some syllable pairs are **fully predictable** and others are not. >> A learner who tracks which syllables follow which can discover this. >> The words come out of the statistics of the sequence.
8|**Each word keeps the same inner order every time, while the words next to it keep changing.** >> Marking each word in its own color makes the four words visible to a reader. >> Tokibu is red, gikoba is green, gopila is pink and tipolu is purple. >> The infant gets no such marking and hears one unbroken run of syllables. || The inner order is fixed and the neighbors vary. >> “To-ki-bu” always comes in that order. >> Tokibu may be followed by gikoba, gopila or tipolu. >> Transitional probabilities are **high inside each word**. >> Transitional probabilities are **lower across word edges**. || The word edges are exactly where **transitional probability falls**. >> The signal itself contains no spaces. >> By noticing where predictability drops, the learner recovers where one word ends and the next begins.
9|**Words were tested against part-words heard equally often, so only transitional probability could tell them apart.** >> After a long stream of syllables, infants are trivially more familiar with sequences they heard than with ones they did not. >> A preference for heard sequences could come from familiarity alone. || **Two additional details** rule that out. >> **Half of the words were repeated more frequently** than the other half. >> The familiarity test was against **part-words** (three-syllable sequences that span a word boundary) that were **matched for frequency of occurrence** with real words. >> A part-word joins the end of one word to the start of the next. || Take the stream “tokibu gikoba gopila gikoba tokibu gopila tokibu gikoba gikoba tokibu.” >> **GOPILA** and **BU-GIKO** both appear **twice**. >> The probability of GI given BU is **2 out of 4**, because BU occurs four times and GI follows it twice. >> The transitional probabilities inside GOPILA are both **2 out of 2**, because PI always follows GO and LA always follows PI. >> Frequency is equal, so any difference between gopila and bu-giko must come from predictability.
10|**Infants looked longer toward the part-words, so they had learned the statistics and not only the frequencies.** This longer looking is a **novelty effect**. >> Words got about 6.8 seconds of looking. >> Part-words got about 7.6 seconds of looking. >> The difference was statistically significant. || The longer looking is a **novelty preference** (looking longer at whatever seems new). >> The words had grown familiar during listening, so they were less interesting. >> The part-words felt less familiar, even though infants had heard them just as often. || The pattern suggests that infants **had learned the statistical regularities of the speech stream**. >> They were **not merely picking up on simple frequency of occurrence**, because frequency was equal for words and part-words. >> Only transitional probabilities separated the two. >> After a brief exposure, infants pulled units out of a continuous stream using only how predictable the syllables were.
11|**Finding a word in speech does not tell the child what the word means.** The next problem is **identifying word referents**. A **referent** is whatever a word refers to. >> Children can now “find” the words. >> They still have to learn what the words mean. >> A child may hear “cat” while looking at a cat. >> Recognizing the sound pattern “cat” is one achievement. >> Knowing that it names the furry animal is a separate achievement. || **Segmentation** delivers a recurring chunk of sound with no information about meaning. >> Any scene holds many objects, parts, properties and actions. >> The learner needs more evidence to tell which one the speaker has in mind. || Learning a word means **linking its form to the speaker’s intended meaning**. >> The child gathers that evidence from the situation in which the word is used.
12|**One experience with a new word fits many possible meanings, so the child cannot tell which one is right.** This is the **inductive problem of early word learning**. **Induction** is reasoning from particular examples to a general rule, and here the rule is a word’s meaning. >> A child hears a new word while watching a cat. >> It might name a category at some level (**CAT**, **ANIMAL** or **PET**). >> It might name this particular cat (**FELIX**). >> It might name a property (**FURRY** or **ALIVE**). >> It might name an action (**WALKING**). >> It might name a part (**WHISKERS** or **LEG**). || All the candidate meanings together make up the **hypothesis space**. >> Each candidate fits what the child sees. >> Nothing in one scene singles out the meaning the speaker intends. || This is a form of **Quine’s “gavagai” problem**. >> A linguist studying an unknown language hears a speaker say “gavagai” as a rabbit runs by. >> “Gavagai” might mean rabbit, undetached rabbit parts, a stage of a rabbit or something else. >> Children meet this problem with every new word and still learn words fast. >> Joint attention, cues to the speaker’s intention and default strategies cut down the possibilities.
13|**Joint attention ties a new word to the object the speaker is attending to.** **Joint attention** means sharing an attentional focus with another person. >> Two people look at the same object. >> Each knows that the other is attending to it too. || **Gaze following** (looking where someone else is looking) **emerges around 9 months of age**. >> An adult looks over at an object. >> The infant follows the adult’s eyes to it. || Gaze following **signals an understanding of reference**. >> The infant grasps that the adult’s attention is aimed at something specific. >> If the adult then says a word, the shared object becomes the most likely referent. >> Joint attention shrinks the hypothesis space (the set of possible meanings) for the word.
14|**Eighteen-month-olds learned a new word from an engaged adult but not from a voice behind a screen.** **Baldwin and colleagues** ran this study, and it supports the claim that **joint attention optimizes word learning**. >> Two groups of infants heard a novel word while playing with an object. >> In the **joint attention group**, an adult interacting with the baby labeled the object the baby was playing with. >> In the **no joint attention group**, a voice from behind a screen labeled the object the baby was playing with. || The two conditions matched closely. >> In both, the word came while the infant was focused on the same object. >> They differed only in whether a visible person was engaged with the infant and the object. || The 18-month-olds **learned the novel word in the joint attention group** and **not in the no joint attention group**. >> Hearing a word while seeing an object was not enough. >> Infants needed evidence that a speaker meant the word to refer to that object. >> The hidden voice gave the same word at the same moment, so the difference came from the visible, engaged speaker.
15|**Children narrow the meaning of a new word by using cues to what the speaker intends, such as where the speaker looks.** These are **intentional cues** (signals of what the speaker means to refer to), and they narrow the **hypothesis space** for new words. >> **Eye gaze direction** is one intentional cue. >> A speaker who looks toward one toy marks that toy as the object of attention. || **Baldwin** taught new words to infants of 16 to 17 months and of 18 to 19 months in two conditions. >> In **follow-in labeling**, the adult looked at the toy the infant was focused on while saying the new word. >> In **discrepant labeling**, the adult looked at a toy in a bucket, a different toy, while saying the new word. || Discrepant labeling pulls the infant’s focus apart from the speaker’s focus. >> Infants who attach a new word to whatever they are looking at would link it to their own toy. >> Infants who follow the speaker’s gaze would link it to the toy in the bucket. >> Comparing the conditions reveals whether infants treat the speaker’s gaze as evidence of meaning.
16|**A child can follow a speaker’s gaze to find which of several objects a new word names.** >> A speaker who looks at one object while saying a new word ties the word to that object. >> The child follows the speaker’s eyes to the **intended referent** (the thing the speaker means). || The speaker’s focus can be **different from the child’s own focus**. >> Following it means using information about someone else’s attention. >> The child can attach a new word to an object it was not looking at when the word was spoken. || **Gaze direction** narrows the possible meanings even though the word’s sound gives no clue. >> The child combines the new word with evidence of what the speaker intends. >> Following gaze rests on understanding that a speaker’s eyes point to what the speaker means. >> The mapping is far more specific than linking the word to everything in view.
17|**Infants use a speaker’s facial expression to work out which object a new word names.** **Akhtar and colleagues** found that infants **used adults’ facial expressions to identify the intended referent of a novel word**. >> The adult’s emotional reaction to each object signaled whether it was the one meant. >> Infants linked the new word to the object that the adult’s expression picked out. || These are intentional cues from the speaker, and they come in two kinds. >> **Affective cues** are emotional expressions. An expression can reveal whether an object is the one the speaker was searching for. >> **Behavioral cues** are the speaker’s actions. Reaching for something can reveal what the speaker wants. || The speaker’s reaction can single out one object among several. >> A satisfied expression signals that the speaker found the desired object. >> A disappointed expression signals that the object is not the one meant. >> Word learning involves grasping the goal behind the interaction.
18|**A speaker’s emotional reaction to each object tells the learner which object the new word is for.** The reaction works as an **intentional cue** (a signal of what the speaker means to refer to). >> Satisfaction or excitement can tie one object to what the speaker was trying to find or do. >> The learner uses that tie to interpret a new label. || **Intention is conveyed through more than gaze direction**. >> Facial expressions also reveal what a speaker means. >> Actions also reveal what a speaker means. >> A word may be said near several objects that are not all equally likely referents. || The helpful information lies in how the adult responds to each object. >> Connecting the label to that response shrinks the word’s **hypothesis space** (the set of meanings it might have). >> Infants who use emotional reactions are following the **speaker’s goals**. >> The word attaches to the object that meets the goal. >> Objects that disappoint the speaker drop out.
19|**Children assume by default that a new word names a whole object.** This is the **whole object assumption**, one of the **word learning strategies**. >> Word learning strategies are **default strategies that children bring to the word learning situation**. >> A **default** is a starting assumption that holds until evidence says otherwise. >> The strategies **function to narrow the hypothesis space** (the set of possible meanings) for new words. >> They give children a place to start when other cues are weak. || The whole object assumption favors the object over everything else about it. >> The word is not expected to name a part of the object. >> The word is not expected to name its color or texture. >> The word is not expected to name something it is doing. >> A child who hears a new word while looking at a cat favors “cat” over “whiskers,” “furry” or “walking.” || The assumption rules out many unlikely meanings at once and fits how adults usually name objects for children. >> Later evidence can override it. >> Children do eventually learn words for parts and properties.
20|**Children assume by default that an object has only one name, so a new word goes to an object without a name.** This is **mutual exclusivity**, from **Markman (1989)**. >> As a default, **objects can have only one name**. >> The implication is that **new words map to nameless objects**. >> It **emerges during the second half of the second year** (roughly 18 to 24 months). || Suppose a child knows “dog” but has no word for a parrot, and both animals are in view. >> The child hears a new word. >> The dog already has a name, so the child takes the word to mean the parrot. >> No gaze or emotional cue is needed. || Mutual exclusivity combines with the whole object assumption. >> The whole object assumption says a new word names an object. >> Mutual exclusivity says it names an object that has no name yet. >> Later learning can override the default when an object turns out to have two names.
21|**The words a child already knows decide which object gets a new word.** With **mutual exclusivity** (the default that an object has only one name), the child gives an unfamiliar word to an object with no known name. >> An object the child can already name is an unlikely target for a new word. >> The choice comes from comparing the objects in view. || **Prior vocabulary** determines which objects count as already named. >> Two children in the same scene can guess differently if they know different words. >> The strategy builds directly on earlier learning. || Vocabulary growth feeds itself. >> A child who knows many names can rule out many objects. >> The unnamed objects left over become the likeliest referents for new words. >> Every word the child knows helps narrow the meaning of the next.
22|**A short utterance can come from several different processes, so length does not reveal how the child built it.** In production, it is difficult to tell what is going on when children produce only one, two or three words at a time. >> **Menn** distinguishes among four kinds of early utterance. >> **“Just a bunch of relevant words”** is words that fit the situation, with no grammar linking them. >> **Formulas** are phrases memorized as whole units, without the child knowing they have separate parts. >> **Word combinations** are separate words joined productively into new combinations. >> **Stable constructions** are consistent patterns for building utterances, and they are **not necessarily adult-like**. || A child might say “Up!” or “Pick you up!” or “Mommy up!” >> “Pick you up!” sounds more advanced but may be a formula copied whole from adult speech. >> “Mommy up!” is shorter but may be a real combination of two separate words. || A **full description of the context** in which an utterance is produced is **necessary but often not sufficient** to distinguish among these. >> Context reveals what the child intends and how the utterance is used across situations. >> Even with complete context, the source of an utterance can remain uncertain.
23|**Children who speak only one word at a time can already use transitive word order to understand sentences.** So **comprehension** runs ahead of **production**. >> The **one-word stage** is when a child speaks only in single words. >> A **transitive** sentence has a verb with a subject and an object. >> English transitive order puts the actor first and the one acted on after the verb. || **Gertner and Fisher** describe this with the **structure-mapping account** of early **syntactic bootstrapping** (using sentence structure to help learn meaning). >> The input sentence is “The boy is tickling the girl!” >> The child builds a **partial sentence representation**, which is tickle with boy as the first noun and girl as the second noun. >> The observed scene is a boy tickling a girl. >> The child builds **conceptual representations** of possible events from the scene. >> The events are giggle (girl as agent), tickle (boy as agent, girl as patient), play (boy as agent) and play (girl as agent). >> An **agent** performs an action, and a **patient** is affected by it. >> A **one-to-one mapping between nouns and participant roles** lines up the two nouns with the two participants in the tickling event. || A child who says only single words can use noun order to tell who did what. >> Matching nouns to participants also helps the child figure out what the verb means.
24|**Two-year-olds understand a transitive sentence with a made-up verb, so they have learned the structure itself.** This is a **generalization test**. If children have really learned the **transitive structure**, they will generalize it to new verbs. >> A child who only memorized particular sentences is lost with an unfamiliar verb. >> A child who knows the structure can use word order to interpret any verb. || The sentence is “The duck is gorping the bunny,” and the child must select which of two novel actions it could depict. >> “Gorp” is invented, so only noun order can help. >> The first noun is the doer and the second noun is the one acted on. || **Gertner and colleagues (2006)** tested two-year-olds. >> Children first heard “Hey look! The duck’s gonna gorp the bunny!” >> Two scenes appeared side by side while children heard “The duck is gorping the bunny! See?” >> In one scene, a person dressed as a duck pushed a person dressed as a bunny in a wagon. >> In the other scene, the bunny acted on the duck. >> After a short break they heard “The duck gorped the bunny! Find gorping!” and the scenes appeared again. >> Children **looked significantly longer at the duck acting on the rabbit** than at the reverse. || At this age, children typically have a **mean length of utterance (MLU)** of **around 2**. MLU is the average number of morphemes (meaningful words or word parts) in each utterance. >> Their three-morpheme utterances might be like “Duck gorp bunny.” >> Their own speech is short, yet they understand the full transitive structure.
