2|**A critical period strictly limits learning to one window, while a sensitive period makes learning best during a window.** Both describe how the timing of experience affects learning. >> A **critical period** has a **discrete, “programmed” onset and offset** (the window opens and closes at fixed points in development). >> In a critical period, learning (change in response to the environment) is **strictly limited to this period**. >> In a critical period, there is **no sensitivity to the environment before or after**. || A **sensitive or “optimal” period** is a looser link between timing and learning. >> In a sensitive period, **learning is possible throughout the life span**. >> In a sensitive period, learning is **optimal during particular windows**. >> These windows can have a **gradual onset and offset**, which experience can itself affect. || The two make different predictions for late learners. >> A critical period predicts no late learning, so any late improvement counts against it. >> A sensitive period predicts late learning that is harder but still possible, with a lasting early advantage. >> Even the classic critical period in vision fits the term sensitive period better. The same question arises for language.
3|**Amblyopia is poor vision in one eye even though the eye itself works normally.** The full definition is poor vision in one eye despite normal **peripheral function** (the working of the eye itself). The problem lies in how the brain handles that eye's input. >> **Strabismus** (misaligned eyes) sends the brain two conflicting images. >> A **cataract** (clouding of the lens) blurs or blocks the light reaching the back of the eye. >> **Anisometropia** (a large difference in focusing power between the eyes) makes one eye's image much blurrier. || These conditions cause **periods of deprivation from normal visual input**. >> The deprivation can result in **poor vision long after the conditions have been remediated** (corrected). >> Correcting the eye restores its input. >> The brain may still fail to use that input well. || A temporary early problem can leave a **lasting effect**, so the timing of the problem matters as much as the problem itself. >> A cataract removed early may leave little lasting damage. >> A cataract removed late may leave poor vision even after the lens is clear.
4|**Amblyopia happens because the two eyes compete for neurons in visual cortex, and the weaker eye loses.** Amblyopia occurs because of **competitive dynamics between cortical inputs**. >> When both eyes work normally, they share the cortical neurons evenly. >> Abnormal vision in one eye can lead to **“colonization”** (the other eye takes over that eye's cortical representation). || **Monocular deprivation** (blocking or degrading input to one eye) in mice reveals the steps. >> Within **2 to 3 days**, there is **increased spine motility**. **Dendritic spines** (small projections where neurons receive connections) become unstable and move around. >> Within **3 to 4 days**, there is **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration** (breakdown of nerve fibers cut off from their source). Connections from the deprived eye disappear. >> After **more than a week**, there is **axon sprouting and new spine birth**. The open eye's fibers form connections in the space the deprived eye lost. || A restored eye **remains at a competitive disadvantage**. >> The other eye now holds the connections. >> Ordinary viewing with both eyes keeps favoring the stronger eye. >> So **normal cortical responses cannot be restored by normal experience**.
5|**Patching the good eye treats amblyopia because it gives the weaker eye a chance to win back cortex.** Treatments for amblyopia are **based on competition**. >> One of the most effective treatments is **intermittently patching the “good” eye**. >> The patch blocks the stronger eye's input for part of the day. >> The brain then has to rely on the weaker eye. || Patching **restores the competitive balance between the eyes**. >> The balance permits the brain's own **plasticity** (its ability to change connections in response to experience) to restore normal function. >> The weak eye's input can win back cortical connections. >> The patch does not repair the eye itself. It changes the experience the brain receives. || Timing decides how well patching works. >> The treatment is **most effective early in development**, because a young brain is highly plastic. >> The treatment **may be ineffective by late childhood (10 years)**, because it helps less and less as the visual system matures.
6|**Adults with amblyopia can still improve with intensive training, so plasticity is not abolished entirely after the critical period.** **Levi and Polat (1996)** tested adults, and some patients were **in their 50s**. >> Adults did **long-term, intensive training** on specific **psychophysical tasks** with the affected eye. These tasks measure the relationship between a physical stimulus and what a person perceives. >> Practice ran over many **kilotrials** (thousands of trials). >> Improvement was measured as a falling **threshold** (the smallest stimulus a person can reliably detect). A lower threshold means better vision. || The threshold fell in steps across about 50 kilotrials. >> Over the first **19 kilotrials**, the threshold **dropped steadily**, a highly significant change. >> From about **20 to 35 kilotrials**, the threshold **leveled off**. >> Around **36 kilotrials**, the threshold **dropped again** and stayed at the new lower level. || Training improved **both task performance and general visual function**. >> The benefits spread beyond the trained task. >> The adult visual system keeps some **experience-dependent plasticity**. >> The right kind and amount of training can still produce change.
7|**Playing action video games improved visual acuity and visual attention in adults with amblyopia.** **Li, Ngo, Nguyen, Lam and Levi (2008)** ran the study. >> Adults played an action video game with their amblyopic eye. >> The game was a first-person shooter set in a battle scene. >> **Visual acuity** (sharpness of vision, the ability to see fine detail) improved. >> **Visual attention** (the ability to pick out and focus on the relevant parts of a scene) improved. || Action games demand fast visual judgments and constant attention. >> Those demands push the visual system to use the amblyopic eye's input. >> The adults were long past childhood visual development, so the gains came from the training itself. >> The gains came well after the age when patching usually works best. || The study adds to the evidence for adult **plasticity** in amblyopia. >> A demanding, engaging visual task can drive change in a mature visual system. >> An early optimal window can close without removing every chance of improvement.
8|**Amblyopia is a classic sensitive period, because early deprivation has lasting effects yet adults can still improve.** Three features mark the early window. >> A **peripheral disturbance in early experience** can lead to **life-long disruption in visual function**. >> The long-term loss is the **sequela** (lasting result) of a temporary deficit during **experience-dependent organization of the brain**. >> The brain organized itself around abnormal input, and that organization stays. >> **Treatment is most effective during a limited developmental window**. Patching works best early. || New evidence suggests the **right kind of experience can reverse deficits late into adulthood**. >> Intensive perceptual training improved adults' vision. >> Video game play improved adults' vision. >> Late learning is harder and needs special conditions, but it happens. || The pattern fits a **sensitive period** better than a strict critical period. >> A strict critical period would rule out any late improvement. >> A sensitive period has a strong early window with some ability to change later.
9|**Two lines of work relate the vision findings to language, Lenneberg's account of brain development and studies of early deprivation.** Each line takes up a different question. >> **Lenneberg** tried to synthesize **developmental neurology** (the study of how the nervous system develops) with a **“language acquisition device”** (a proposed innate capacity for learning language). >> **Studies of early deprivation** use data from **neglected children** and **Deaf children with limited input to sign**. || Lenneberg asked whether the maturing brain opens and closes a window for language. >> He linked language milestones to brain development. >> If a window exists, language learning depends on the state of the brain as well as the input. || Deprivation studies ask what happens when language input arrives late. >> Neglected children were cut off from ordinary language input. >> Some Deaf children have no access to sign language in early childhood. >> Comparing them with typical learners tests whether the age of first exposure affects the final outcome.
10|**Lenneberg (1967) argued that language develops in stages on a biological timetable, alongside motor development.** In **Biological Foundations of Language**, he tried to synthesize contemporary linguistics with what was known about brain development. >> Studies were finding a fairly robust and **stage-like pattern** of language development. >> Austrian, British and American children follow the same order between 8 and 36 months. >> **Words** come first, mostly between about 10 and 18 months. >> **Two-word phrases** come next, mostly between about 18 and 26 months. >> **Sentences of five words or more** come last, between about 28 and 36 months. || Language milestones pair with motor milestones at each age. >> At **8 months**, infants stand holding on and pick up small objects. **Reduplicated** babbling (repeated syllables) becomes frequent, with intonation that can signal emphasis and emotion. >> At **10 months**, infants creep and pull to standing. Vocalizations mix with sound play. Infants try to imitate sounds and begin to tell words apart. >> At **12 months**, infants walk with one hand held. Words such as **mama** or **dada** emerge, and some words and simple commands are understood. >> At **18 months**, vocabulary is more than three but fewer than fifty words, and comprehension progresses rapidly. || **Maturational milestones** (developmental achievements tied to physical growth) were seen as consistent with the **unfolding of a biological program**. >> Language and motor milestones line up, which suggests a common timetable. >> Lenneberg proposed that brain maturation sets the pace for both.
11|**Lenneberg proposed that the critical period for language opens after the rapid brain growth of the first two years.** His data on typical brain development were sparse. >> The data were almost entirely based on **post-mortem** (after death) analyses of tissue. >> Researchers could not follow the same children's brains as they grew. >> They pieced development together from children who had died at different ages. || **Neuron size** (cell body volume of neurons in cortical layers III, IV and V) grows rapidly in the first two years, then levels off. >> **No data were available between ages 2 and 18**. >> That gap overlaps much of the period of language acquisition. || Lenneberg observed abrupt changes in neuron size and **packing density** (how closely neurons are crowded together) before age two. >> These changes might be **permissive** (making language acquisition possible without directly causing it) for the beginning of language acquisition. >> The brain first goes through a burst of growth, and the window opens once that growth settles. >> Lenneberg filled the data gap with a reasonable guess that later research would need to test.
12|**Children can develop language after losing the left hemisphere and adults cannot, so Lenneberg tied the closing of the critical period to cerebral dominance.** More extensive data came from studies of recovery from stroke. >> **Aphasia is surprisingly rare in children with brain lesions** (areas of damaged brain tissue). >> In extreme cases, surgeons performed a **hemispherectomy** (removing or disconnecting one whole cerebral hemisphere), typically to relieve seizures in epilepsy. >> **Children** whose left hemisphere is removed still seem to **develop typical language abilities**. >> **Adults do not recover language** after this procedure. || Lenneberg's summary of outcomes from **Basser (1962)** sorts cases by age at lesion. >> Before the teens, left removal left **49** with speech not affected or improved and **3** with permanent aphasia. >> Before the teens, right removal left **38** with speech not affected or improved and **5** with permanent aphasia. >> All of those children with permanent aphasia already had aphasia before the operation. >> During puberty, one left removal had slow improvement, with some aphasia remaining to the end of life 27 months after surgery. >> In adults, left removal left **none** with speech unaffected or improved and **6 with permanent aphasia**. >> In adults, right removal left **25** with speech unaffected or improved and none with permanent aphasia. || Lenneberg hypothesized that a key factor in closing the critical period is adult-like **cerebral dominance** (specialization of one hemisphere, usually the left, for language). >> In young children, language is not yet fixed in the left hemisphere, so the right can take over. >> In adults, dominance is established, so the right can no longer take over.
13|**Children with perinatal strokes do much better than adults with similar injuries, but they do not fully catch up.** A **perinatal stroke** happens between the prenatal period and 28 days after birth. >> Systematic studies find outcomes **clearly much better than for adults with similar injuries**. >> The children still have some effects of the injury. || **Early language deficits** appear in **both right-hemisphere and left-hemisphere patients**. >> In adults, language problems mainly follow left-hemisphere damage. >> In young children, damage to either side causes early language delays. >> **Some deficits are worse with right-hemisphere damage**, the opposite of the adult pattern. || Language outcomes are generally in the **low-normal range**. >> That range suggests a **persistent delay** rather than a catching up. >> Recovery is strong but incomplete. >> Early plasticity is real but limited. A young brain can reorganize for language, yet the reorganized system may work less well than the original would have.
14|**Genie was isolated and abused from 20 months of age and had no speech when researchers met her at 13 years and 7 months.** **Curtiss and colleagues (1974)** studied her as a case of the **effects of early deprivation**. >> Genie was born in **April 1957**. >> When researchers first met her, she was so thin that she looked six or seven. || Her early life involved extreme isolation and abuse. >> From **20 months**, she was confined to a small room under increasing physical restraint. >> She was mostly harnessed to an infant's potty chair. Otherwise she was in a homemade sleeping bag in a crib covered with wire mesh. >> Her mother, who was rapidly losing her sight, gave minimal care, and Genie was fed only infant food. >> Her father **physically punished her if she made any sounds**. >> Her father and older brother did not speak to her but at times barked at her like dogs. >> The home had no television or radio, and contact with other people was extremely limited. || She was hospitalized for malnutrition. >> She **could not stand up straight or chew food**. >> She was **not toilet trained**. >> She **did not speak, cry or produce any vocal sounds**. >> Her lack of language came with severe physical, social and emotional deprivation, so the two **cannot be separated**. >> Her case still reveals what happens when first-language learning starts in adolescence.
15|**In four years of observation, Genie learned to understand hundreds of words, some morphology and some prepositions.** She learned quite a bit. >> She came to **comprehend hundreds of words**. >> She came to comprehend some aspects of **morphology** (the system of meaningful word parts, such as the plural ending “-s”). She was **particularly good with plurals**. >> She came to **comprehend some prepositions** (words for spatial relationships between objects). || A preposition test used **buttons and plastic boxes** of different colors and sizes. >> Genie moved the buttons and boxes according to spoken instructions. >> A sample item was **“Put the red box behind the blue box.”** >> The test covered **under, over, in, on, behind, in front of, in back of** and **next to**. || From October 1972 to August 1973, **every preposition had more correct than incorrect responses**. >> In had 37 correct and 4 incorrect. On had 36 correct and 9 incorrect. >> Over had 11 correct and 2 incorrect. Under had 17 correct and 7 incorrect. >> In front of had 20 correct and 6 incorrect. In back of had 7 correct and 3 incorrect. >> Next to had 7 correct and 1 incorrect. Behind had 12 correct and 5 incorrect. || **Some language can be acquired late**, after puberty and long after the window Lenneberg proposed. >> The learning was uneven across different parts of language.
16|**Genie's speech lagged far behind her understanding, and her grammar developed much more slowly than her vocabulary.** Her **production** (what she said) lagged severely behind her **comprehension** (what she understood). >> **Some lag is typical of development**. All children understand more than they can say. >> Genie was also **punished for making sounds** as a child, which could limit how much she spoke. >> Her speech therefore revealed less than she actually knew. || A test of **conjunctions** placed **five familiar objects in a row** and asked her to point to one or more. >> Items included **“Show me the fork and pencil”**, **“Show me the crayon or the knife”** and **“Show me either the spoon or the crayon”**. >> **And** requires pointing to every named object. >> **Or** requires pointing to one named object, which depends on understanding a logical relationship. || From October 1971 to December 1973, she passed and but failed or. >> **Early responses** treated or sentences like and sentences, so she pointed to both objects. >> **Later responses** distinguished and from or, but she still did not understand **either/or**. >> And sentences had **100 correct**, 3 incorrect and 0 unanalyzable. >> Or sentences had **7 correct**, **51 incorrect** and 6 unanalyzable. >> Strong vocabulary with weak grammar fits the idea that **grammar depends more on early exposure than vocabulary does**.
17|**Genie's gap between vocabulary and grammar is widely interpreted as suggesting that first-language grammar learning is impaired after a sensitive period.** The full claim is that grammar learning in a first language is **specifically impaired after a sensitive period**. >> She learned hundreds of words but made little progress with grammar. >> On this interpretation, vocabulary can be learned at any age, while grammar depends on early exposure. || Her case leaves **lots of questions**. >> Was Genie **learning disabled**? She may have had difficulties from birth that would have affected her language anyway. >> What were the **residual impacts of neglect and abuse** on her social development? Severe trauma could affect learning in many ways beyond missing language input. >> One case cannot separate disability, abuse and late exposure. || **After four years, funding to study her was pulled**. >> Nobody can know what she might have learned after a longer time. >> The evidence covers only those four years. >> Her pattern still became an influential piece of evidence for a sensitive period in first-language learning.
18|**Deaf people who had any early language, signed or spoken, learn a later language much better than those with none.** **Mayberry, Lock and Kazmi (2002)** tested this with deaf people. >> Many deaf children **do not get sign language input** but have an **otherwise typical upbringing**. >> Unlike Genie, they grow up in ordinary, caring homes, so they are a cleaner test of first-language timing. || Two groups learned **American Sign Language (ASL)** late. >> Children aged **9 to 15** learning ASL as their **first language** had **difficulty**. >> A comparison group who **went deaf after first learning a spoken language** did **much better**. >> Both groups learned ASL late, but only one had an earlier language. >> **These differences persist into adulthood**. || Two adult tasks measured the difference. >> In **recall of complex ASL sentences**, adults with no early language scored about **36%**. Adults with an early spoken language scored about **78%**. >> In **judging whether complex English sentences were grammatically correct**, chance is 50%. >> Deaf adults with no early language scored about **52%**, close to chance. >> Deaf adults with ASL in infancy scored about **80%**. >> Hearing adults with an early spoken language other than English scored about 80%. >> Early ASL helped later English as much as an early spoken language did.
19|**First-language learning clearly depends on age, but the evidence cannot settle whether the window is a critical or a sensitive period.** Three points bear on the question for a first language (**L1**). >> **Recovery from stroke**, and certainly from **hemispherectomy**, looks **very different in childhood and adulthood**. >> Children can develop language after damage that would leave an adult with permanent aphasia. || **Deprivation of input does not appear to extend the critical period**. >> One might expect a window to stay open until language input arrives. >> Genie and the deaf adults who learned sign late have lasting difficulty, especially with grammar. >> The window seems to follow a maturational schedule, whether or not language has been experienced. || The data come from **highly unusual cases with lots of other things going on**, so it is **difficult to reach firm conclusions**. >> Stroke, brain surgery, abuse and neglect each bring complications beyond the timing of input. >> There is a **lack of animal models**, because animals do not learn human language. >> **Gene knockout experiments** (disabling specific genes to test their role) cannot be done with people.
