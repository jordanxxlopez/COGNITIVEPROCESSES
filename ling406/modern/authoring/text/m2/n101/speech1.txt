2|**Speech perception recovers one stable message from a signal that is messy and that differs from speaker to speaker.** **Speech perception** (recovering a linguistic message from the sound of speech) turns a mess into a message as clear as “I can see you.” >> In the signal for “I can see you,” the energy of each sound runs into the next. >> Nothing in the signal cleanly separates letters or words. >> Real-time MRI of the **vocal tract** from USC SPAN gives the reason. The articulators move without pause, so the movements for neighboring sounds overlap and their acoustic effects overlap. || Listeners also get **the same message from very different physical stimuli**. >> Voices and vocal tract sizes differ, so one word has different acoustic properties from each speaker. >> Across **76 speakers**, each of ten vowels covers a broad range of first and second formant values. >> The ranges **overlap**, so one pair of formant values can be different vowels for different speakers. >> Recognizing speech means finding a **stable linguistic identity** inside enormous physical variation.
3|**Listeners must turn a variable, analog signal into abstract symbols, and categorical perception is thought to reflect that translation.** The premise is that the language system can only operate on something more abstract than the signal. >> An **analog signal** (one that varies continuously) can shift in frequency, loudness and timing by tiny amounts. >> It has no built-in markers of where one speech sound stops and the next starts. >> Many small changes leave the speech sound the same. || The abstract form is a **symbolic representation** (one that identifies a language unit and sets its fine physical detail aside). >> The unit is the **phoneme** (a sound category that distinguishes words in a language). >> /b/ in “bat” and /p/ in “pat” are different phonemes, because swapping them changes the word. >> Many slightly different acoustic versions of /b/ count as one phoneme. || **Categorical perception** is thought to reflect this **“translation” from physical to symbolic**. >> Listeners are less sensitive to differences inside a category. >> Listeners are more sensitive to equally large differences that cross a category boundary. >> Categorical perception is one account of the translation, and other accounts exist.
4|**The shape of the vocal tract decides which resonant frequencies get amplified, and those amplified frequencies are the formants.** This relation is systematic, following **Ladefoged (1996)**. >> The **vocal tract** (the passage from the larynx through the throat and mouth to the lips) changes shape as the tongue, jaw and lips move. >> A **resonant frequency** (a frequency that a space naturally strengthens) depends on the shape of the tube. >> **Formants** are the resonant frequencies the vocal tract emphasizes, and every vowel has its own combination. || The vowels differ in how their formants are arranged. >> Across **heed, hid, head and had**, the tongue moves from high and front toward low. The second formant starts far above the first, and the two draw closer. >> Across **hod, hawed, hood and who’d**, the tongue moves back and the lips round more. The first and second formants sit close together at the low end. >> A vowel is defined by the **relationship among its resonances**, which stays similar across speakers. || A **formant transition** (a change in a formant’s frequency over time) happens as the mouth moves from a consonant into a vowel. >> The steady part of a vowel gives information about the vowel. >> The moving part gives information about how the neighboring sound is made.
5|**On a spectrogram, vowels differ mainly in how far apart their two lowest formants are.** A **spectrogram** (a map of acoustic energy over time and frequency) has bands of strong energy for each vowel, and these bands are its **formants**. >> The words **bead, bid, bade, bed, bad, bod, baud, bode, “bood” and booed** come from Rob Hagiwara’s English vowel samples. >> All ten begin with /b/ and end with /d/, so the differences come mainly from the vowels. >> **F1** is the lowest formant and **F2** is the next one up. Both run through the whole vowel at once. || The spacing of F1 and F2 changes across the words. >> In **bead**, F1 is very low and F2 is very high, above 2,000 Hz, so they are far apart. >> Through **bid, bade, bed and bad**, F1 rises and F2 falls, so they move closer together. >> In **bode, “bood” and booed**, the back rounded vowels, F2 drops near 1,000 Hz or below and sits near F1. >> Bead pairs a low F1 with a high F2. Booed pairs a low F1 with a low F2. >> The pattern of these two lowest formants gives most of what tells vowels apart.
6|**Source-filter synthesis treats a speech sound as a source shaped by a filter, and this /u/ has low first and second formants.** **Source-filter synthesis** builds speech sounds from those two parts. >> The **source** is the sound energy the voice produces. >> The **filter** is how the vocal tract shapes that energy. >> Separating them makes it possible to change one while the other stays fixed. >> The work is done in **Praat** (free speech analysis software from praat.org), which displays recordings, measures formants and resynthesizes sounds. || The recording is a speaker saying **/u/**, the vowel in “booed.” >> A **waveform** (a record of air pressure changes over time) captures timing and loudness. >> A **spectrogram** breaks the same sound into its frequency components, so it captures the resonance pattern. >> The **first formant is at about 300 Hz**. >> The **second formant is very low, around 900 Hz**. >> A low F1 and a low F2 fit the back rounded quality of /u/. || If changing only the filter changes the vowel, the vowel’s identity must lie in the filter. >> The measured formants are the starting point for building new filters.
7|**Praat finds five formants in the /u/, but only the first two are changed in this synthesis.** **Praat can find formants automatically**, by following each resonance through the recording and marking its frequency moment by moment. >> It identifies **five formants** in this recording. >> Each one is a frequency component of the same sound, and they run at the same time. >> No one has to estimate the frequencies by eye. || **Only the first three formants are usually important for phonemic processing**. >> F1 is the lowest resonance, F2 is the next and F3 is the third. >> F1, F2 and F3 give most of the information that separates phonemes. >> Higher formants depend more on the individual voice. || The example deals with only **the first two formants**, near 300 Hz and 900 Hz in this /u/. >> Changing these two is enough to turn one vowel into another, because they hold most of the vowel information. >> F3 and the higher formants stay fixed, so any change listeners hear comes from F1 or F2 alone.
8|**Removing the filter from the recorded /u/ leaves the source, which sounds like a buzz.** The **source** is **extracted** using a model of the filter built from the measured formant frequencies. >> Praat uses the model to strip the filter’s effect from the recording. >> What remains is a **more-or-less “unfiltered” version of the voice**, the sound energy the vocal folds supplied. >> It is only more or less unfiltered, because the separation is an estimate. || The extracted source **sounds like a buzz**. >> Its energy is spread across many frequencies, without the strong low formants of /u/. >> Pitch survives, because pitch comes from vocal fold vibration. >> Vowel quality disappears, because vowel quality comes from the filter. || The separation is **a little bit like doing a multiple regression analysis**. >> In multiple regression, one predictor’s contribution is estimated and removed so the rest can be examined. >> Here the filter’s contribution is estimated and removed, and the rest is treated as the source. >> New filters can then be applied to the one source, so the effect of the resonance pattern can be tested.
9|**Three filters are applied to one source, and each filter changes only one part of the resonance pattern.** A **filter** (a specification of which frequencies get more emphasis and which get less) is a resonance pattern. >> The **first filter** is just the first four formants from the original recording, so it should bring back the original /u/. >> The **second filter** raises **the first formant a little** and leaves the other formants roughly where they were. >> The **third filter** raises **the second formant a lot** and leaves F1 and the higher formants roughly in place. || The changes are separate, not a shift of every frequency together. >> In the original filter, F1 and F2 are both low, which fits /u/. >> Raising F1 moves only the lowest resonance up. >> Raising F2 moves only the second resonance up, by a much larger amount. || The **sound source stays the same** for all three filters. >> No new speaker or recording is needed. >> Any change in vowel quality comes from the filter. >> Comparing the outputs isolates what each resonance change does.
10|**Combining one source with three different filters gives three different vowels.** **Multiplying the source by the filters gives new vowels**, because the output is the source shaped by the filter. >> The source provides the raw sound energy. >> The filter decides how much of that energy stays strong in each frequency region. || Each filter gives its own result. >> With the **original filter**, F1 and F2 stay low and the result keeps the quality of /u/. >> With **F1 raised a little**, the lowest resonance rises and the vowel changes. >> With **F2 raised a lot**, the second resonance rises far and the vowel changes another way. The back rounded /u/ moves toward a more front vowel. || **Source-filter synthesis** separates two contributions that ordinary speech combines. >> The voice provides the sound. >> The vocal tract resonances shape its quality. >> All three outputs share the same buzz, pitch and timing, yet they sound like different vowels. >> A researcher can change vowel quality by swapping only the filter, without asking a speaker to record each version.
11|**Formant values vary with speaker, speech rate and context, yet they still describe vowel space well for one careful talker.** The vowel a speaker intends only **partly** determines the formant values. >> **Anatomical features** differ between speakers of the same language. A longer vocal tract gives lower resonances overall. >> **Different rates of speech** matter, because fast speech gives the articulators less time to reach their targets. >> **Context** matters, because the tongue and lips are already heading toward the next sound or leaving the previous one. >> Context includes the **phones** (particular spoken realizations of speech sounds) produced before and after a vowel. || Across 76 speakers, each of ten vowels covers a wide range of formant values, and the ranges overlap. >> The same F1 and F2 values can be different vowels for different speakers. >> No single pair of frequencies defines a vowel for everyone. || Formant values nonetheless describe **vowel space** (the layout of a language’s vowels by their formant frequencies) pretty well, especially for **a specific talker speaking carefully**. >> Within one careful talker, the vowels separate far more cleanly than across many talkers. >> All else being equal, **reasonable approximations of vowels** can be produced by manipulating **steady-state formants** (the resonances in the stable middle of a vowel, not the transitions at its edges).
12|**The pattern playback synthesizer turns a painted spectrogram back into sound, so researchers can control exactly what listeners hear.** Researchers paint bands on a transparent sheet wherever they want acoustic energy in time and frequency, and the **pattern playback synthesizer** plays the pattern aloud. >> Changing the painting changes the sound. >> Researchers could build controlled, speech-like sounds without relying on natural recordings that vary in many uncontrolled ways. || The machine works by shining light through the painted pattern. >> A light source shines through a **cylindrical lens** onto a spinning **tone wheel**, whose rings modulate the light at many frequencies at once. >> A lens and a **mirror** send that light onto the painted **spectrogram**, and the painted areas pass along the matching frequencies. >> A **light collector** and a **photocell** turn the light into an electrical signal. >> An **amplifier** and a **loudspeaker** turn that signal into sound. || A researcher can alter one formant band or one transition and hear the result right away. >> Every other part of the pattern stays the same. >> Any change in what listeners hear comes from the one painted change. >> Series of patterns differing in tiny steps revealed how formant transitions signal consonants.
13|**Stop consonants are told apart by their formant transitions, and Liberman and colleagues (1957) copied those transitions with painted patterns.** The stops **/b/, /d/ and /g/** are made by briefly closing the vocal tract and then opening it. >> The audible part of a /b/, /d/ or /g/ happens **while the jaw is opening**. >> The vocal tract changes shape quickly during the opening. >> The formants glide from the consonant’s position toward the vowel’s position, so the consonant’s identity lies in how the formants move. || **Liberman and colleagues (1957)** used the **pattern playback synthesizer** to create speech-like sounds that replicated these **formant transitions** (changes in formant frequency over time). >> Each pattern had two formants. >> The **lower formant** was nearly the same in every stimulus. >> The **upper formant** started at a different frequency in each stimulus and then settled at the same steady vowel level. >> The fourteen patterns kept the vowel the same and changed only where the upper transition began. || The direction of the upper transition decided the consonant. >> A **rising** transition was heard as /b/. >> A roughly **flat** transition was heard as /d/. >> A **falling** transition was heard as /g/. >> Only the transition differed, so listeners’ responses could be tied directly to it.
14|**The speech continuum changes F2 onset in equal steps, so every neighboring pair of stimuli differs by the same physical amount.** A **speech continuum** (a series of sounds that change step by step along one controlled acoustic property) is built here from 13 stimuli. >> **F1 is kept constant**, which removes one possible difference between stimuli. >> The **F2 onset frequency** (the frequency at which the second formant begins its transition) is **varied linearly** from stimulus 1 to stimulus 13. >> Linearly means the onset changes by the same step every time. >> So **adjacent stimuli differ by the same amount across the entire continuum**. || The F2 transition shifts gradually along the series. >> Early stimuli start F2 well below the vowel frequency, so the transition rises. >> Middle stimuli start F2 higher, so the rise shrinks until the transition is nearly flat. >> Later stimuli start F2 above the vowel frequency, so the transition falls. || Equal steps make the continuum a fair test of whether perception follows the acoustics or the categories. >> Some neighboring pairs fall inside one consonant category, and others straddle a boundary. >> A purely acoustic listener would hear every step as the same size. >> A categorical listener would hear steps within a category as alike and steps across a boundary as different. >> Any advantage for boundary pairs would come from the boundary itself.
15|**If perception were non-categorical, labeling would shift gradually from /b/ to /d/ to /g/ along the continuum.** An **ideal observer model** (a prediction of how a listener would respond under one particular account) is used as a baseline. >> This simple version assumes perception is **non-categorical** (it tracks the acoustic signal gradually, with no sharp category boundaries). >> Real data can be compared against the baseline to see whether perception is more categorical than that. || The first prediction is that **categorization gets “blurry” as you move away from category prototypes**. >> A **prototype** is the clearest example of a category. >> The endpoints of the continuum are /b/ and /g/, with /d/ in the middle. >> Between these points, **stimulus ambiguity should scale with the distance from the prototype**. >> **Stimulus 2** would be **75% /b/ and 25% /d/**, because it sits a quarter of the way from the /b/ prototype toward the /d/ prototype. || Predicted **labeling** changes smoothly. >> Responses for one category fade out in a straight slope while responses for the neighboring category fade in. >> Two neighboring labels are equally likely halfway between their prototypes. >> No label changes in a sudden step.
16|**If perception were linear, discrimination accuracy would be the same for every pair, whatever the labeling looks like.** A **linear** ideal observer perceives equal acoustic differences as equally large. It predicts that **discrimination performance should be independent of labeling**. >> **Labeling** asks a listener to identify a sound as /b/, /d/ or /g/. >> **Discrimination** asks only whether two sounds are the **same or different**. >> The two tasks are separate, even when they use the same continuum. || Labeling **has to have a “peaked” function**. >> There are three categories, and listeners are choosing between labels. >> Each category’s responses rise toward its prototype and fall away from it. >> The peaks come from the structure of the labeling task itself. || Discrimination is predicted to be flat. >> Pairs are two steps apart, from **1 and 3** through **9 and 11**, so every pair has the same physical separation. >> If perception of differences in F2 onset were constant over the whole continuum, discrimination accuracy would also be constant, at about 0.8. >> A **flat** result would mean category boundaries play no role in hearing differences. >> A **peaked** result would mean the boundaries make some differences easier to hear.
17|**Liberman and colleagues (1957) found sharp labeling boundaries and the best discrimination right at those boundaries.** This pattern is **categorical perception**, and it departed sharply from the non-categorical, linear predictions. >> Listeners labeled each stimulus on the /b/, /d/ and /g/ continuum. >> Listeners also judged pairs that were **one step**, **two steps** or **three steps** apart. || Labeling switched abruptly. >> Stimuli 1 through about 3 were labeled /b/ close to 100% of the time. >> Around stimuli 4 and 5, responses switched almost completely to /d/, which stayed dominant through about stimulus 8. >> Around stimuli 9 and 10, responses switched just as quickly to /g/. >> The narrow region where the label flips is the **category boundary**. || **Listeners were best at discriminating sounds near the category boundaries**. >> Accuracy peaked at the /b/ to /d/ boundary and again at the /d/ to /g/ boundary. >> Pairs inside one category were discriminated poorly, close to **chance** (the level expected from guessing). >> Larger steps raised accuracy overall, but the peaks stayed at the boundaries. || Liberman and colleagues also predicted discrimination from the labeling data. >> They assumed listeners can tell two sounds apart only when they give them different labels. >> The predictions captured much of the real pattern. >> Real accuracy was often a little higher than predicted, especially within categories, so listeners keep some sensitivity beyond the labels.

19|**Each listener discriminates best near the point where that listener’s own label switches from one word to the other.** Data from students in prior years tested **categorical perception** with a **beach to peach continuum**, where the words differ in the first consonant, /b/ or /p/. >> In **identification**, each listener labeled every stimulus from 1 to 9. >> In **discrimination**, each listener judged pairs two steps apart, from stimuli 1 and 3 through stimuli 7 and 9. || The first listener switches around the middle of the continuum. >> Identification stays on one word for the early stimuli and then **switches to the other word**. >> Responses are mixed only in a small region near the switch. >> Discrimination is best for stimuli 4 and 6 and for stimuli 5 and 7, the pairs that straddle the boundary. >> Pairs well inside one category are hard to tell apart, even with the same physical separation. >> Stimuli 6 and 8 fall in between, because stimulus 6 itself received mixed labels. || The second listener **switches later** along the continuum. >> Discrimination is best near that later boundary. >> Different people can place their category boundaries at different points.
20|**Listeners differ in where their boundary falls, but discrimination is generally best for sounds heard as different categories.** **Not everyone has exactly the same category boundary**. >> A sound near the beach to peach boundary for one listener can fall squarely inside a category for another. >> A pair that straddles one person’s boundary may stay inside one category for someone else. >> Averaging across the group can flatten the boundary peak when individual boundaries differ. || **In general, discrimination is best for stimuli perceived as being in different categories**. >> Listeners may miss a physical difference between two versions of the same phoneme. >> The same size of difference is far easier to hear when the two sounds are heard as different words. || The discrimination experiment included **only “different” trials**, so the lab could be done in a short amount of time. >> Every pair held two physically different stimuli. >> The correct answer on every trial was **“different,”** if the listener could discriminate the sounds. >> Answering “same” meant missing a real difference. >> Accuracy is the share of pairs heard as different, and someone who always said “different” would score perfectly. >> Low accuracy on within-category pairs means listeners often failed to hear real differences.
21|**Real data are noisy, and this experiment had too few trials and no trials with identical sounds.** The one iron-clad law of experimental research is that **stuff happens**. >> Responses to a carefully built continuum can still bounce around. >> An isolated rise or drop in discrimination reflects noise in a few responses. >> Identification (which word is chosen) and discrimination (whether a difference is detected) can vary in different ways across trials and people. || More results from prior years compare a **bear to pear continuum** with the **beach to peach continuum**. >> For bear to pear, identification switches fairly clearly from one word to the other. >> Bear to pear discrimination jumps between 0 and 1 from pair to pair, including a miss on a pair that crosses the boundary. >> Another listener’s beach to peach data fluctuate even more. Identification zigzags instead of switching once, and discrimination bounces between high and low values. || The experiment could be done differently in two ways. >> Only “different” trials were used, so nothing measured how often listeners said “different” when the sounds were identical. >> The short session gave each listener **few trials per pair**, so a single response could move accuracy a great deal. >> A better version would add trials with identical sounds and more trials for each pair. >> The real test is whether better discrimination lines up with where identification changes, across the whole continuum.
22|**Being worse at hearing differences inside a category helps, because it keeps word recognition stable.** >> Categorical perception makes listeners worse at hearing differences within a category. >> That can seem like throwing away useful acoustic detail. || The advantage is **stable word recognition**. >> Every production of a phoneme differs physically from every other, because of speakers, speaking rates and neighboring sounds. >> If each small acoustic change were heard as a new identity, everyday versions of one word would sound like unrelated sounds. >> Reduced sensitivity within a category lets many acoustic versions count as **one phoneme**. || Differences **between categories** stay easy to hear. >> The distinctions that change words are kept. >> Variation inside a category is tolerated, because it leaves the word unchanged. >> Ignoring small within-category differences frees the listener to focus on the differences that change meaning.

24|**Infants cannot name sounds, so researchers test their speech perception with behaviors that change when the infant notices something new.** **Categorical perception in infants** can be tested this way because infants react to novelty. >> The experimenter controls exactly which sound difference is presented. >> The infant’s response reveals sensitivity to that difference without any labeling. || The **conditioned head turn procedure** comes from Janet Werker’s infant studies lab. >> An infant learns to turn toward a sound when the sound changes. >> A correct turn earns an interesting visual reward, such as a moving toy. >> After this link is learned, a head turn following a change means the infant detected the change. || The infant results on categorical perception come from **high-amplitude sucking**. >> The infant sucks on a pacifier attached to a recording device, and strong sucks make a sound play. >> As the same sound repeats, sucking slows through **habituation** (growing used to a repeated sound). >> If a new sound makes sucking **increase again**, the infant has noticed the change. >> Both methods work in infants who are months from saying words.
25|**Eimas and colleagues (1971) found that 4-month-old infants notice a voicing change only when it crosses the adult category boundary.** They used **high-amplitude sucking** with sounds that varied in **voice onset time**, or **VOT** (the delay between the release of a consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration). >> Adults hear a short VOT as a voiced consonant such as /b/. >> Adults hear a longer VOT as a voiceless consonant such as /p/. || Infants heard one synthetic syllable over and over until their sucking slowed, and then the sound changed or stayed the same. >> In condition **20D**, the VOT changed from **+20 to +40 milliseconds**, which **crossed** the adult boundary between two consonant categories. >> In condition **20S**, the VOT also changed by **20 milliseconds**, from −20 to 0 or from +60 to +80, and both sounds came from the **same** adult category. >> In condition **0**, the control group kept hearing the same sound. || Sucking rose at first and then dropped in every group as the sound became familiar. >> In 20D, sucking **recovered** sharply after the change. >> In 20S, sucking kept falling with little recovery. >> In 0, sucking kept declining steadily. >> Both changes were 20 milliseconds, so the difference comes from crossing the boundary. >> Even at four months, infants perceive voicing **categorically**.
26|**English-learning infants lose the ability to discriminate non-English contrasts during the first year, and this is perceptual narrowing.** **Perceptual narrowing** (experience tuning perception to the sounds of the surrounding language) brings a decline in sensitivity to some distinctions from other languages. >> **Werker and Tees (1984)** tested how age and experience affect infants’ ability to discriminate two **non-English contrasts**. >> The **Hindi contrast** is a distinction between two “ta”-like sounds that English does not use. >> The **Salish contrast** is a distinction between “ki” and “qi” in Nthlakampx, a Salish language, which English also does not use. >> The measure was the percentage of infants reaching a **discrimination criterion** at each age. || English-learning infants succeeded less often at each older age. >> At **6 to 8 months**, about 95% met criterion for Hindi and about 80% for Salish. >> At **8 to 10 months**, about 67% met criterion for Hindi and about 52% for Salish. >> At **10 to 12 months**, about 20% met criterion for Hindi and about 10% for Salish. >> **Hindi-learning and Salish-learning infants** met criterion close to 100% of the time for their own language’s contrast. || The results are described as **perceptual reorganization during the first year of life**. >> Young infants can detect these nonnative contrasts. >> Infants who hear a contrast in their own language keep it, so the decline depends on language experience. >> Contrasts the language lacks become hard to hear by about the first birthday.
27|**The compelling narrative says categorical perception is special to language, present early, and tuned from a universal start.** The earlier findings feed **a very compelling narrative** with three linked claims. >> First, speech perception depends on **categorical perception abilities that are a special property of the language system**. The mechanism that sorts sounds into categories is specialized for language. >> Second, these abilities are **present very early in development**. The infant sucking and head-turn experiments suggest they exist long before children talk. >> Third, children **start off as “citizens of the world,”** able to hear **all** contrasts, and then **rapidly tune their perceptual systems to their specific language**. || The third claim has two parts. >> “Citizens of the world” means infants could learn any human language and are ready to hear the distinctions of every one. >> Tuning means the contrasts of the surrounding language stay available while contrasts from other languages fade, as in the Werker and Tees results. || The narrative covers both the **starting state** and **developmental change**. >> It proposes broad, language-specific abilities at birth. >> It treats development mostly as narrowing from that broad start. >> Each early result seems to fit, since infants discriminate categorically before talking, hear contrasts from many languages and lose nonnative contrasts with experience.
28|**Four findings weaken the compelling narrative that categorical perception is a special language ability with a universal start.** The narrative meets **some issues**, three about **categorical perception** and one about **infant speech perception**. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to language**. It also appears with nonspeech sounds, so it does not look special to language. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to humans**. Nonhuman animals also perceive speech sounds categorically, so it does not look special to humans. >> Categorical perception is **task-dependent**. Results look more or less categorical depending on the task. >> Choosing between labels makes responses more categorical than a task that measures fine acoustic differences. || Infant speech perception is **not actually “universal.”** >> Some contrasts are harder for infants to detect than others from early on. >> Development includes initial differences in sensitivity as well as changes driven by experience. >> These issues weaken the strong claim that every speech distinction starts out equally available in a mechanism devoted only to language.
29|**Cutting and Rosner (1974) found categorical perception for plucked and bowed sounds, which are not speech.** They tested **non-speech sounds** that varied in **rise time** (how quickly a sound reaches full loudness after it begins). >> A **rapid rise time** sounds **plucked**, like a plucked string. >> A **slow rise time** sounds **bowed**, like a bowed string. >> Pluck and bow are labels for two kinds of sound onset, and they are not phonemes. || The stimuli included **sawtooth waves**, **sine waves** and **speech syllables**, with short and long rise times such as 10 and 70 milliseconds. >> With a 10-millisecond rise, the sound reaches full strength almost at once. >> With a 70-millisecond rise, the sound builds up gradually. || For the sawtooth waves, labeling switched in a narrow region and discrimination peaked there. >> Short rise times were labeled pluck and long rise times were labeled bow, each close to 100% of the time. >> The label switched steeply around 30 to 50 milliseconds. >> Discrimination of pairs 10 milliseconds apart stayed around 55 to 60% for most pairs, a little above chance. >> Discrimination **peaked near 75%** around 30 to 50 milliseconds, right where the labels switched. >> The link between a label boundary and better discrimination holds for a sound distinction that has nothing to do with language.
30|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977) found that infants also perceive non-speech sounds categorically.** They tested **infants** on **non-speech sounds** with **high-amplitude sucking**, which measures whether a change in sound is detected. >> Infants heard one sound repeatedly until their sucking declined. >> The sound was then shifted in one of three ways. || The three conditions were **no change**, a change **within categories** and a change **between categories**. >> Before the shift, sucking declined in every condition as the sound became familiar. >> After no change, sucking stayed low. >> After a within-category change, sucking also stayed low. >> After a between-category change, sucking jumped back up and then declined again as the new sound became familiar. || Infants responded more strongly to a change that crossed a category boundary than to a change within a category. >> This mirrors the pattern **Eimas and colleagues (1971)** found for speech. >> Infants cannot be using knowledge of words or phonemes when they respond to plucked and bowed sounds. >> Their categorical response must come from more general auditory processing. >> That same processing could also underlie their categorical response to speech.
31|**Chinchillas trained on two speech sounds label the sounds in between as steeply as humans do.** The evidence comes from **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and the earlier work of **Kuhl and Miller (1975)**. >> The animals were trained with **operant conditioning** (learning in which consequences change how often a behavior occurs). >> Each chinchilla learned that either the **0-millisecond lag** stimulus or the **80-millisecond lag** stimulus was an **SD** for food. >> **SD** stands for **discriminative stimulus** (a signal that a response will be rewarded). >> The lag is **voice onset time**. Humans hear a 0-millisecond lag as /ga/ and an 80-millisecond lag as /ka/. || After training, chinchillas and human listeners were tested on the VOTs in between, along the **/ga/ to /ka/ continuum**. >> Short VOTs were labeled /ga/ almost every time. >> Long VOTs were almost never labeled /ga/. >> The switch happened over a narrow range, mostly between about 30 and 70 milliseconds, for both species. || The study also varied the **burst** (the brief noise at the release of a stop consonant) and the **aspiration noise** (the breathy noise that follows a voiceless release). >> Making them louder shifted the boundary toward shorter VOTs, made the switch steeper, or both. >> This happened for one chinchilla and one human listener, so both species responded similarly. >> Animals without human language can sort a speech continuum into categories, which argues against categorical perception being special to human language.
32|**Some contrasts are hard for infants to hear at first, even native ones, and experience makes them easier.** Some contrasts are **less salient** (less easy to notice) than others for infants. >> The contrasts tested differ in **place of articulation** (where in the mouth a sound is made). >> In **[ma]**, the nasal is made with the lips. >> In **[na]**, the nasal is made with the tongue near the ridge behind the teeth. >> In **[ŋa]**, the nasal is made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate, as at the end of “sing.” || Researchers compared **looking time** on **same** trials (the syllable stayed the same) and **change** trials (the syllable switched). Longer looking on change trials means the infant noticed the change. >> English-hearing infants at **4 to 5 months** looked longer on change trials for the native [ma] and [na] contrast, about 5.4 seconds against about 3.3 seconds. >> The same infants looked about equally for the nonnative [na] and [ŋa] contrast, about 4.2 seconds against about 4.5 seconds. English never uses [ŋ] at the start of a syllable. >> **Filipino-learning infants** hear [na] and [ŋa] as a contrast in their own language. >> At **6 to 8 months**, they looked about 3.8 seconds on both kinds of trials, so they did not yet detect the change. >> By **10 to 12 months**, they looked about 4.8 seconds on change trials and about 3.3 seconds on same trials. || Experience with the language **strengthens** sensitivity to such contrasts over the first year. >> Development includes gains in sensitivity as well as losses.
