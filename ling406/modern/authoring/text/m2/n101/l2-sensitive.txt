2|**Lenneberg (1967) tied the sensitive period to the brain becoming specialized for language on one side.** L2 learning serves this research as a **model system** (a common, easily studied situation used to answer questions about a process that is hard to study directly). >> His theory says the sensitive period has to do with the development of **cerebral dominance** (one hemisphere, usually the left, becoming specialized for language). >> The same idea is called developmental change in **language laterality** (how much language relies on one side of the brain instead of both). >> The window for learning language closes as language becomes fixed in the left hemisphere. || Two kinds of evidence motivated the theory. >> Brain development roughly aligned with language milestones. Cortical neurons grow fast for about two years and then level off, with no measurements from age 2 to 18. >> Lenneberg linked the end of fast brain growth to the start of language learning. >> A review of **resilience after brain injury or surgery** gave the second kind. Children who lost the left hemisphere often developed language. Adults did not recover it. || The theory concerns **maturational state** (the brain's stage of physical development). >> A young, less specialized brain can shift language to other tissue. >> A mature, specialized brain has fewer options, so language learning becomes harder.
3|**Second-language learning lets researchers study the timing of the sensitive period in large groups of ordinary learners.** Most of what is known about first-language (L1) sensitive periods comes from **idiosyncratic** (particular to one individual) cases or relatively rare phenomena. >> One source is children recovering from surgery that removed a hemisphere. >> Another source is Genie, who was isolated from language until age 13. >> Each case carries unusual circumstances such as injury, illness or abuse, so the cause of the outcome is hard to identify. || In contrast, many people learn second languages at different times in their lives, often with somewhat **limited success**. >> L2 learners are common, so large groups can be studied. >> L2 learners start at every age from early childhood to late adulthood. >> Their final success can be measured. || The **limits of L2 success** are used to reason about the **timing of the sensitive or critical period**. >> If later starters reliably end at lower levels, the age where the decline appears could mark when the period closes. >> The first language complicates this. Every L2 learner already knows a language, and it shapes how the new one is learned.
4|**Johnson and Newport (1989) found that people who arrived in the United States younger ended up with better English grammar.** **Lenneberg (1967)** hypothesized that language could be acquired only within a **critical period** (from early infancy until puberty). Johnson and Newport asked whether that also holds for a second language. >> In its basic form, the critical period hypothesis only has consequences for first-language acquisition. >> If it extends to a second language, young children should learn an L2 better than adults and reach higher final proficiency. >> Their paper is about the influence of **maturational state** on the acquisition of English as a second language. || They compared the English proficiency of **46 native Korean or Chinese speakers**. >> Age on arrival in the United States ranged from 3 to 39. >> Time lived in the United States ranged from 3 to 26 years. >> A **grammaticality judgment task** tested a wide variety of structures of English grammar. || Earlier arrivals had a **clear and strong advantage**. >> Up to puberty, test performance was **linearly related to age of arrival**. Each extra year meant a lower score. >> After puberty, performance was **low but highly variable** and unrelated to age of arrival. >> The effect was not an accident of experience with English, motivation, self-consciousness or American identification. >> The effect appeared on every grammatical structure tested, although later learners mastered some structures far better than others. || The authors concluded that a critical period for language acquisition **extends its effects to second-language acquisition**.
5|**Each example set on the grammaticality judgment test pairs a correct sentence with an incorrect one to test one rule of English.** **Johnson and Newport (1989)** devised this **GJT**. Participants listened to recorded sentences and gave a **grammaticality judgment** (a decision about whether a sentence follows the rules of the language) for each one. >> The test was **untimed**, so participants could take as long as they needed. >> (1a) “The farmer bought two pigs at the market” and (1b) “The farmer bought two pig at the market” test **plural marking** on nouns. >> (2a) “The little boy is speaking to a policeman” and (2b) “The little boy is speak to a policeman” test the **progressive -ing** form of the verb. >> (6a) “Tom is reading a book in the bathtub” and (6b) “Tom is reading book in the bathtub” test whether a needed **determiner** is present, here the article “a.” >> (7a) “The boys are going to the zoo this Saturday” and (7b) “A boys are going to the zoo this Saturday” test whether determiner and noun agree in number. >> (8a) “Larry went home after the party” and (8b) “Larry went the home after the party” test a spot where English does not allow an article. >> (23a) “When will Sam fix his car?” and (23b) “When Sam will fix his car?” test **wh-question** word order, where the helping verb comes before the subject. >> (24a) “What do they sell at the corner store?” and (24b) “What they sell at the corner store?” test the need for the helping verb “do” in a question.
6|**Age of arrival predicts grammar scores strongly for people who arrived as children and hardly at all for people who arrived as adults.** Each participant's score is items correct out of 276, set against **age of arrival** (AOA). >> The participants are split at puberty, the end of the critical period in Lenneberg's proposal. >> A **correlation** (r, a measure from −1 to +1 of how closely two variables are related) near −1 means one variable falls steadily as the other rises. A value near 0 means little relationship. || **Early arrivals**, aged 3 to 15, had a correlation of **−0.87**. >> Arrivals at about 3 to 7 scored near the maximum, around 270. >> Scores fell to around 250 near age 10 and to around 210 to 230 near age 15. >> Age of arrival predicts much of the score in this group. || **Late arrivals**, aged 17 to 39, had a correlation of only **−0.16**. >> Scores were lower overall and widely scattered, from about 160 to 250. >> Some scored around 250, while others of similar age scored below 180. >> Age of arrival predicts little about who does well in this group. || The pattern is a **strong age effect in childhood** and **lower, highly variable performance in adulthood**.
7|**The youngest arrivals score like native speakers, later child arrivals score lower, and adult arrivals are scattered.** These are the three features of the **Johnson and Newport (1989)** data. >> There is a **strong relationship between AOA** (age of arrival) **and accuracy**. Earlier arrival goes with higher scores. >> **Early learners**, up to about age 8, are often **near-perfect** (native-like, close to native English speakers). >> **Late arrivals** have a **nearly random distribution of accuracy** (age of arrival barely predicts the score). || The first two features are about people who arrived as children. >> The youngest arrivals reach native-like grammar. >> Arrivals between about 8 and 15 reach progressively lower levels. >> So the decline starts well before puberty. || “Nearly random” has a specific meaning. >> It means age of arrival and score are only weakly related among late arrivals. >> Late arrivals still scored well above chance. >> Scores varied greatly from person to person. Two adults who arrived at the same age could end up far apart.
8|**Johnson and Newport (1989) argue that both halves of their data fit a sensitive period for L2 learning that closes.** They call it a **“critical period.”** >> First, **ultimate attainment** (the final level a learner reaches) depends on AOA. >> Scores drop steadily with later arrival in childhood, which is consistent with a **loss of language-learning ability over development**. >> The timing is not entirely consistent with Lenneberg's hypothesis. He put the end at puberty, but the decline here starts around age 7 or 8. >> Lenneberg was working with a large gap between early childhood and adolescence. He had no brain measurements between ages 2 and 18, so his timing was a rough estimate. || Second, attainment in late acquisition is **all over the place**. >> Adults who arrived at the same age reached very different levels. >> After the sensitive period closes, attainment depends on **idiosyncratic learning abilities** (individual differences in things such as memory, motivation or analytical skill), not the usual language acquisition processes. >> Children share one natural acquisition process, so they reach similar levels at similar ages. >> Adults no longer have that process, so they rely on abilities that differ greatly between people.
9|**Johnson and Newport's data fit the maturational state hypothesis, because learners with a normal first language still did worse when they started later.** There are **two versions of the “critical period” hypothesis**. Both say that early in life humans have a superior capacity for acquiring languages. >> The **exercise hypothesis** says the capacity disappears or declines with maturation if it is not exercised during this time. >> It also says that if the capacity is exercised, further language-learning abilities remain intact throughout life. >> It predicts that an adult with a normal L1 learns an L2 as well as a child. >> The **maturational state hypothesis** says the capacity disappears or declines with maturation, whether or not it has been used. >> It predicts that even an adult with a normal L1 learns an L2 less well. || Every participant had a normal first language, yet later starters learned the L2 less well. >> That fits the maturational state hypothesis, the idea named in the paper's title. || This changes how the **L1 critical period data** are interpreted. >> In cases like Genie, the capacity was never exercised. >> Both hypotheses predict poor learning for such cases, so those cases cannot separate them. >> Only second-language data can, because only L2 learners exercised the capacity before learning another language.
10|**An untimed grammaticality judgment task may measure explicit knowledge about rules instead of the implicit knowledge used in fluent language.** The task is **very different from fluent language use**, which is a methodological issue of test design. >> In fluent use, people speak and understand quickly without thinking about rules. >> In the task, people consider each sentence and decide whether it is correct. >> The worry is **“metalinguistic”** or **“explicit” knowledge** (conscious knowledge about language, such as knowing that English plurals add “-s”). || **Ellis (2005)** contrasts **implicit** and **explicit knowledge** on several characteristics. >> In awareness, implicit is **intuitive** awareness of linguistic norms and explicit is **conscious** awareness. >> In type of knowledge, implicit is **procedural** (knowing how) and explicit is **declarative** (knowing facts that can be stated). >> In systematicity, implicit is variable but systematic and explicit is often anomalous and inconsistent. >> In accessibility, implicit uses **automatic processing** and explicit uses **controlled processing** (effortful). >> In use of L2 knowledge, implicit serves **fluent performance** and explicit serves a speaker who is planning and having difficulty. >> In self-report, implicit is **nonverbalizable** (people cannot explain it) and explicit is **verbalizable**. >> In learnability, implicit is potentially only learnable **within the critical period** and explicit is learnable at **any age**. || A consciously applied taught rule and automatic knowledge give the same score, so the test may miss what the critical period is supposed to affect.
11|**The untimed GJT may measure explicitly learned “metalinguistic” knowledge better than it measures grammar acquisition.** The reason is its task demands. **Ellis (2005)** compared the design features of five tests. >> **Imitation** and **oral narrative** rely on feel, have time pressure, focus on meaning and require no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **timed GJT** relies on feel, has time pressure, focuses on form and requires no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **untimed GJT** relies on rules, has no time pressure, focuses on form and does allow metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **metalanguage test** (people state rules) relies on rules, has no time pressure, focuses on form and requires metalinguistic knowledge. || Ellis related each test to starting age and to years of formal instruction, and only two relationships were significant. >> Starting age was related to the **timed GJT**, with a correlation of **−0.23**. >> Years of formal instruction were related to the **untimed GJT with ungrammatical items**, with a correlation of **0.27**. >> The other tests had only weak relationships with either measure. || Consistent with this, untimed GJT performance is correlated with **years of formal education in L2**. >> Classroom instruction teaches explicit rules, which help on the untimed test. >> Starting age matters for the timed test, which limits the use of explicit rules. >> So an age effect on an untimed GJT may partly reflect explicit, taught knowledge instead of natural grammar acquisition.
12|**If the two languages were more similar, learners could get more help from transfer, so the age pattern might come out differently.** **Transfer** (using first-language knowledge in learning a second language) is another methodological and theoretical issue. >> Languages that are **typologically more similar** (similar in structure, such as word order and word forms) can benefit more from transfer. >> Cognates and loan words give learners familiar words to build on, such as French **auto**, **rivière** and **bicyclette**, and Japanese **korokke** and **monoreru**. || **Johnson and Newport (1989)** tested people with English as L2 and **Chinese or Korean as L1**. >> Chinese and Korean are very different from English in grammar and vocabulary. >> Those learners got little help from transfer. || A learner whose L1 is close to English can reuse much of what they already know. >> Their attainment might relate to age differently. >> If so, the age pattern in Johnson and Newport's data depends on the particular languages as well as on age.
13|**Late learners find some English rules much harder than others, partly following the order children learn them and partly following transfer.** **Johnson and Newport (1989)** compared errors on **12 types of English rules**. The groups were native speakers and arrivals at ages 3 to 7, 8 to 10, 11 to 15, 17 to 24 and 25 to 39. >> The rule types are determiners, plurals, **verb subcategorization** (which kinds of words or phrases can follow a verb), past tense, pronouns, particles and auxiliaries. The rest are third-person singular, wh-questions, yes/no questions, word order and progressive -ing. >> Native speakers and the youngest arrivals make very few errors on any rule. >> The latest arrivals make the most errors on **determiners**, around 65 to 68%, and **plurals**, around 45 to 55%. >> Errors on **word order** and **progressive -ing** are under about 10% for every group. || Some of this looks like the **order in which children learn language**. >> Progressive -ing and word order are easiest, and they are among the first structures children master. || Some could be **transfer**. >> **Chinese has no determiners**, so Chinese speakers have no L1 category for “a” and “the” and find English articles especially hard. >> Children learn questions very late, but questions are among the **easiest structures for late learners**. >> Adults may use general cognitive skills or L1 knowledge for question forms. >> Difficulty depends on the structure itself and on what the learner's first language provides.
14|**Spanish speakers learning English had almost the reverse age pattern, so the age effect depends on the language pairing as well as on age.** **Birdsong and Molis (2001)** repeated the Johnson and Newport study with Spanish speakers. >> For **Chinese and Korean speakers**, early arrivals (aged 3 to 15) had a correlation of **−0.87** and late arrivals (aged 17 to 39) had **−0.16**. >> For **Spanish speakers**, early arrivals (aged 3 to 16) had a correlation of only **−0.23** and late arrivals (aged 17 to 44) had **−0.69**. >> Spanish-speaking early arrivals scored near the maximum regardless of age, with only a slight decline around 16. >> Spanish-speaking late arrivals fell from around 260 near age 18 to around 160 after 40. || The consequence concerns **maturational constraints** (limits on learning caused by the brain's development). >> A fixed maturational schedule should give the same pattern for every first language. >> Instead, the pattern changed with the learners' first language. >> Spanish is much closer to English than Chinese or Korean, so **transfer** may help Spanish speakers reach native-like grammar even when they arrive late in childhood.
15|**Older learners reach lower L2 attainment overall, but how much lower depends on factors that are hard to separate.** These findings may not even count as “conclusions.” || The size of the age effect depends on three things. >> It depends on **what constructions are tested**. Determiners are much harder for late learners than word order. >> It depends on **how they are tested**. Timed and untimed tests can differ because the untimed test allows explicit knowledge. >> It depends on **language pairings**. Spanish speakers and Chinese or Korean speakers have different age patterns when learning English. || Three pairs are **hard to dissociate** (hard to separate). >> It is hard to separate **age of acquisition** from the **amount and quality of experience**. Younger arrivals usually have more years of exposure and more immersion, such as schooling in the language. >> It is hard to separate **transfer effects** from **L2 acquisition**. L2 performance reflects what was learned and what carries over from the L1. >> It is hard to separate **performance** (how well knowledge is used on a particular task) from **competence** (underlying knowledge of the language). A test score measures performance, so it may not reveal competence directly.
