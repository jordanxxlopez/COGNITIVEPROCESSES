2|**In the 1870s, German neurologists explained specific losses of language as disconnections between centers in the brain.** **Lichtheim** and **Wernicke** began making more systematic observations of **aphasia** (a loss or impairment of language caused by brain damage). >> A **center** is a brain region that stores one kind of language representation. >> A **disconnection** is damage to the pathway between two centers, so information cannot travel between them. >> This was reasoning about aphasia before Chomsky. || **Lichtheim’s “House” model** draws the centers and pathways in the shape of a house. >> **A** holds **sound images** (the stored sound patterns of words). >> **M** holds **movement images** (the stored motor plans for saying words). >> **B** holds **concepts**. >> Pathway **a** is acoustic and brings heard speech into A. Pathway **m** is motor and carries plans from M to the speech muscles. >> Seven numbered sites mark different forms of aphasia, each tied to damage at a particular center or pathway. || Each damage site predicts its own loss. >> A break between **A and B** might cause loss of **understanding spoken words**, because the word’s sound cannot reach its concept. >> A break between **A and M** might cause loss of **verbatim repetition**, because a heard word cannot pass directly to the plan for saying it. || This **“box and arrow” model** is foundational in **aphasiology** (the study of aphasia) and in modeling typical language function. >> The **interactive activation model** of word recognition has the same form, with levels of representation connected by pathways. >> The history comes from Eling and Whitaker (2009).
3|**Geschwind revived the box-and-arrow approach in the 1970s, after linguistics had come to treat syntax as its own system.** The **“Geschwind-Wernicke model”** links language functions to particular brain regions and the pathways between them. >> **Broca’s area** is toward the front of the left hemisphere, in the frontal lobe. >> **Wernicke’s area** is farther back, in the temporal lobe. >> The **arcuate fasciculus** (a bundle of nerve fibers) connects Wernicke’s area with Broca’s area. >> The **central sulcus**, or **fissure of Rolando**, separates the frontal lobe from the parietal lobe behind it. >> The **lateral sulcus**, or **fissure of Sylvius**, separates the temporal lobe from the frontal and parietal lobes above it. || In the century between Wernicke and Geschwind, the field of **linguistics evolved dramatically**. >> Linguists developed a theoretical model in which **syntax was independent** of other aspects of language and had **its own computational principles**. >> That opened the door to theorizing about **(double) dissociations** of language functions in more sophisticated ways. || The two terms name patterns across abilities. >> In a **dissociation**, one ability is impaired while another is spared. >> In a **double dissociation**, one patient loses the first ability and keeps the second, and another patient has the reverse. >> A double dissociation is strong evidence that the two abilities depend on **separate systems**. >> With syntax, word knowledge and meaning treated as separate parts, a patient can be impaired in one part while the others work.
4|**Broca’s aphasia damages the grammatical side of speech, and Wernicke’s aphasia leaves speech fluent but full of wrong words.** These are the classic profiles described by **Fromkin (1997)**. || **Broca’s aphasia** has three marks in speech. >> There are **word-finding pauses**. >> There is **loss of grammatical morphemes** (small words or word parts that mark a grammatical relationship, such as “the,” “is” or past-tense “-ed”). >> Quite often there is **disturbed word order**. >> Comprehension of everyday conversation gives the impression of being generally good, but **controlled testing reveals considerable impairments**. || **Agrammatism** (difficulty producing or understanding grammatical structure) is almost a synonym for Broca’s aphasia. >> Some patients with lesions in Broca’s area are **not agrammatic**. >> Some agrammatic patients would **not be classified neurologically as Broca’s aphasics**. >> So the label names a pattern of behavior that does not always follow damage to Broca’s area. || **Wernicke’s aphasia** has its own marks. >> Speech is **fluent**, with good intonation and pronunciation. >> There are **many word substitutions**, some related in meaning to the intended word and some not. >> There are **neologisms** (invented words) and **phonological errors** (errors in the sounds of words). >> There are **comprehension difficulties**. >> Fluency is how smoothly speech flows, which is separate from whether it carries a coherent message. || Broca’s patients keep meaningful words but lose the small grammatical pieces that connect them. Wernicke’s patients keep the grammatical frame but fill it with wrong or invented words.
5|**Wernicke’s aphasia sounds like fluent word salad with syntax roughly intact, and Broca’s aphasia sounds telegraphic.** This **dichotomy** (a two-way division) is attractive. || **Wernicke’s aphasia** is **“word salad”** or **“Jabberwocky,”** often with **neologisms**. >> Asked about his health, one patient said “I felt worse because I can no longer keep in mind from the mind of the minds to keep me from mind and up to the ear which can be to find among ourselves.” Kriendler and colleagues (1971) reported it. >> Asked about his poor vision, another said “My wires don’t hire right.” >> Asked if he was a doctor, an aphasic physician said “Me? Yes, sir, I’m a male demaploze on my own. I still know my tubaboys what for I have that’s gone hell and some of them go.” **Demaploze** and **tubaboys** are neologisms. >> One patient called a fork “a need for a schedule” and a spoon “How many schemes on your throat.” || **Broca’s aphasia** is **“telegraphic” speech**, with difficulty with **word order and grammatical particles** and relative sparing of important **content words**. >> Asked what brought him to the hospital, one patient said “Yes … ah … Monday … ah … Dad and P.H. (the patient’s name) and Dad … hospital. Two … ah doctors … and ah … thirty minutes … and yes … ah … hospital. And, er Wednesday … nine o’clock and eh Thursday, ten o’clock … doctors. Two doctors … and ah … teeth. Yeah … fine.” Badecker and Caramazza (1985) reported it. >> Content words carry the story and the connecting grammatical words are missing.
6|**Broca’s own patients would probably be classified today as having apraxia of speech and not Broca’s aphasia.** The dichotomy says Wernicke’s aphasia is **problems with activation, or possibly inhibition, in the lexicon** and Broca’s aphasia is **problems with grammar**. >> The **lexicon** is the mental store of words. >> **Activation** makes a candidate word available. >> **Inhibition** suppresses competing words, so weak inhibition could let wrong words slip into speech. || That is **fairly distant from how Broca described his original cases**. He described **Leborgne**, known as **“Tan.”** >> Tan could produce only **one jargon syllable**, “tan.” >> He seemed otherwise to be in possession of his senses. >> He seemed to **understand what was being said**. || Broca’s description raises three questions. >> Intact senses matter because they mean the loss was specific to speech, which makes it a **dissociation**. >> A “capacity for articulate speech” seems a more plausible function for a brain region than “criminality” or “philoprogenitiveness” (a phrenology term for love of one’s children). >> Examining Tan today would mean measuring his deficits at the phonetic, phonological, lexical and syntactic levels of description. || This is confusing. >> Broca’s actual patients were arguably **unable to produce speech at all**, apparently because of **motor control problems**. >> They would be classified as having **apraxia of speech** (a disorder in planning and coordinating the movements needed to speak). >> Apraxia of speech can limit output severely without any loss of grammatical knowledge.
7|**The aphasia dichotomy is attractive because formal linguistics also separates the grammar from the lexicon.** Late 20th-century formal linguistics drew a **sharp distinction** between the two. >> The **grammar** is the system of rules for combining words into sentences. >> The **lexicon** is the store of individual words, which must be memorized. >> Wernicke’s aphasia would be a disorder of the lexicon. >> Broca’s aphasia would be a disorder of the grammar. || The distinction starts with **Chomsky’s formulation of the computational problem of language learning**. >> We do not recognize a new sentence because it matches some familiar item in any simple way. >> We recognize it because it is **generated by the grammar** each person has internalized. >> A sentence never heard before can be understood because the listener can work out how the grammar builds it. || **Chomsky (1955)** also gave compelling demonstrations of the **independence of syntax and semantics**. >> Neither “colorless green ideas sleep furiously” nor “furiously sleep ideas green colorless” has occurred in an English speaker’s past experience. >> “colorless green ideas sleep furiously” makes no sense, but it is **grammatical** because its word order follows English grammar. >> “furiously sleep ideas green colorless” is **not grammatical** because the same words come in an order the grammar does not allow. >> Both are equally unfamiliar and equally meaningless, so the difference comes from **grammar as a separate system**.
8|**In the speech production model, the lexical side is at the functional level and the grammatical side is at the positional level.** The dichotomy fits this difference. || The model has five levels. >> The **message level** is conceptual. The speaker chooses a story angle and arouses concepts of **referents** (the people, objects or events being talked about) and event structure. >> The **functional level** is the first linguistic level. It arouses word meanings and **semantic roles** (the parts participants play in an event, such as doer or receiver). >> The **positional level** is the second linguistic level. It gets word forms in grammatically appropriate order. >> The **phonological encoding level** supplies the sounds of the words. >> The **speech gesture level** handles **articulation** (the physical movements of speaking). || The two key levels work differently. >> At the functional level, **lemmas** (lexicon entries linking a word’s meaning to its grammatical properties) have an **arbitrary** relationship to their referents. There is no reason a dog must be called “dog.” >> At the positional level, syntactic trees and **affixes** are the product of **abstract syntactic processes**. Affixes are word parts added to a stem, such as “-ed” or “-ly.” || One objection is that **HOUSEBOAT** and **DOGHOUSE** have meanings that are clearly not arbitrary, because they are built from parts. >> Speech errors are evidence that **derivational morphology** (building new words from existing parts) is done at the positional level. >> In “easy enoughly,” the speaker meant “easily enough,” but “-ly” attached to the wrong word. >> The suffix moved as a separate piece, so a grammatical operation added it.
9|**Broca’s patients may generally understand what is said even though they have a core grammatical deficit.** This is possible because conversation gives cues other than grammar. >> In ordinary conversation, word meanings, familiar events and context all point toward the same message. >> A listener can use these cues without fully computing the grammar. >> So a patient can succeed without parsing when meaning and plausibility point toward the right answer. || Popular theories of **sentence processing** say syntactic structure is computed **independently of meaning** as we listen or read. >> An autonomous **parser** builds the structure first. >> Meaning is worked out afterward. || Isolating these **“parsing” mechanisms** requires **carefully constructed stimulus materials**. >> The test sentence is “The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite.” >> In the **preferred** structure, “with the dynamite” attaches to the **verb phrase**, so the dynamite is the instrument. >> In the **unpreferred** structure, it attaches to the **noun phrase** “the safe,” so it tells which safe. >> The words are the same and only the attachment changes, so parsing is tested without changing word meanings. || A real test of a parsing deficit has one requirement. >> The grammatical relationships cannot be guessable from the most likely event. >> **Reversible sentences**, where either person could plausibly do the action, force the listener to use grammar.
10|**To establish that a patient’s difficulty is more generally with syntax, researchers test comprehension.** A comprehension test works for two reasons. >> The patient can respond without speaking, so motor problems are removed from the test. >> If the patient still struggles to understand grammatical relationships, the deficit **goes beyond articulation**. || **None of this theory was available to Broca in the 1860s.** >> For him, it was interesting enough to **dissociate “articulate speech” from comprehension**. >> Leborgne could produce only one syllable, yet he seemed to understand what was said to him. >> Speaking and understanding coming apart was the key finding at the time. || The modern question is different. >> A **syntactic impairment** concerns the organization of grammatical relationships. >> Such an impairment should affect **both producing and understanding** sentences. >> So the question of whether the difficulty lies in grammar itself requires testing comprehension of specific structures.
11|**Comprehension deficits in Broca’s patients matter because agrammatic speech alone could be only a motor problem.** Three ideas lead to that worry. >> **Broca’s aphasia is almost always accompanied by some level of motor dysfunction.** >> Agrammatic speech might merely reflect **an attempt to communicate despite motor limitations**. A patient who finds speaking effortful might drop small grammatical words and keep informative content words. >> **Hughlings Jackson** said we are observing **what the individual can do despite the impairment**. Agrammatic speech after injury does not literally mean the grammar itself is injured. >> Caramazza and Zurif (1976) cite Lenneberg (1973) for a version of this. The strain of speaking is so great that the patient **speaks asyntactically to economize effort**. || **Chomsky’s distinction between competence and performance** applies. >> **Competence** is a person’s underlying knowledge of language. >> **Performance** is the use of that knowledge, which memory limits, distraction or motor problems can disrupt. >> When motor deficits obviously interfere with performance, **comprehension may provide a clearer window into competence**. || **Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** tested comprehension for this reason. >> If Broca’s aphasia is a motor problem, comprehension of grammatical structure should be normal. >> If it is damage to grammar, comprehension should also suffer. >> Both explanations predict telegraphic speech, so only comprehension can tell them apart.
12|**Psycholinguistic tests are needed because ordinary sentences can be understood correctly without any grammar.** In conversation, there are lots of **redundant cues** (several sources of information that all point to the same meaning) to what a speaker intends. >> “Man bites dog” is the newspaper example of an unexpected event. || “The dog bit the man” gives many ways to get the right meaning. >> The listener can form the **most likely combination of DOG, BITE and MAN** into an event representation, with or without syntax. Dogs bite people far more often than the reverse. >> The sentence has **canonical word order**, which is agent, verb, undergoer, or subject, verb, object. >> The **agent** is the doer and the **undergoer** is the one the action happens to. In English the first noun is usually the agent. >> The **discourse context** (the surrounding conversation) clarifies a lot. || Broca’s patients’ comprehension may depend on these **compensatory heuristics** (mental shortcuts that make up for a weakness elsewhere). >> A patient might assume the most plausible event. >> A patient might assume the first noun is the doer. >> Either shortcut gets “The dog bit the man” right with no grammatical analysis. || A better test sentence is “The dog that the man is biting is angry.” >> The **man** is the biter, although the dog comes first and dogs are likelier biters. >> Only grammatical analysis gives the right answer.
13|**Caramazza and Zurif wrote four groups of sentences that differ in how much meaning helps the listener.** Each group has eight sentences. >> **A, control sentences**, are simple and in canonical order. Three are “The girl is kicking a green ball,” “The cat is chasing a blue bird” and “The boy is riding a new bicycle.” >> **B, center-embedded sentences with semantic constraints**, make the roles obvious from meaning. Three are “The apple that the boy is eating is red,” “The wagon that the horse is pulling is green” and “The balloon that the clown is holding is round.” >> **C, reversible center-embedded sentences**, let either participant do the action. Three are “The cow that the monkey is scaring is yellow,” “The horse that the bear is kicking is brown” and “The girl that the boy is tickling is happy.” >> **D, improbable center-embedded sentences**, describe events against expectations. Three are “The bird that the worm is eating is blue,” “The man that the horse is riding is fat” and “The policeman that the robber is chasing is skinny.” || A **center-embedded** sentence has a relative clause in the middle of the main clause. >> In every embedded sentence, the first noun is acted on and the doer is inside the relative clause. >> In B, apples cannot eat boys, so meaning alone gives the roles. >> In C, only grammar gives the roles. >> In D, common sense points to the wrong answer. || A listener who relies on meaning should pass A and B and fail C and D. >> A listener who parses normally should pass all four.
14|**Broca’s patients failed only when grammar was the sole guide to who did what to whom.** **Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** used a **sentence-picture matching task** (hearing a sentence and choosing which picture fits it). >> Accuracy tells whether the listener understood who did what to whom, with no spoken answer required. >> Healthy listeners and patients with Broca’s, conduction and Wernicke’s aphasia heard all four sentence types. || Each group had its own pattern. >> **Healthy controls** were more or less **equally good** at all stimulus types, near the maximum. >> **Wernicke’s patients** were **poor overall**, even on simple control sentences. This is probably a result of their comprehension deficits, which affect words as well as grammar. >> **Broca’s patients** scored **high** on control and semantically constrained sentences, where meaning supplies the answer. >> **Broca’s patients** scored **low** on reversible and improbable sentences, where syntax conflicted with common sense or probable semantic roles. || This **specific comprehension deficit** was taken as evidence that **Broca’s aphasia impacts syntax specifically**. >> The deficit appeared in understanding as well as speaking, which places it in the grammar itself. >> The paper’s title is “Dissociation of algorithmic and heuristic processes.” >> Broca’s patients lose the **algorithmic** process (grammatical analysis). >> They keep the **heuristic** shortcuts (based on meaning).
15|**A specific theory of the parts of language lets researchers isolate grammar problems from lexicon problems.** This opens new ways of exploring **structure-function relationships** (links between a brain structure and the function it supports). >> Before such theories, aphasias could only be described in broad terms. >> The old divisions were good or poor comprehension and more or less fluent speech. >> The new approach isolates problems with **particular aspects of language**. || Two patients make the difference concrete. >> One patient might know word meanings well but assign the wrong roles in complex sentences. >> Another patient might produce a fluent grammatical frame filled with words that miss the intended message. >> A single fluency rating would hide the difference between them. || The key division is **the grammar versus the lexicon**. >> **Grammar** concerns the rules that relate words to one another in a sentence. >> The **lexicon** concerns stored words and the ability to retrieve the right ones. >> A **grammar deficit** predicts trouble with reversible sentences but not with word meanings. >> A **lexical deficit** predicts trouble with word meanings and naming but not with grammatical structure. >> Controlled tasks can test whether one operation is impaired and relate it to a particular brain region.
16|**Dick and colleagues described one event with four sentence types, and only two of them put the doer first.** This study begins the **problems with the dichotomy**. >> Participants listened to a sentence and had to **indicate which animal is doing the action**. >> A picture of a dog and a picture of a cat appeared on a computer screen. >> Participants pressed a button on a response box to choose the animal. >> The event was always a dog biting a cat. || Two types put the **doer first**. >> **Active** is “The dog is biting the cat.” >> **Subject cleft** is “It’s the dog that is biting the cat.” >> A **cleft** splits a simple sentence with “It’s … that” to put focus on one part. In a subject cleft the focused noun is the doer. >> Choosing the first animal mentioned gives the **right** answer. || Two types put the **receiver first**. >> **Object cleft** is “It’s the cat that the dog is biting.” The focused noun is the one being bitten. >> **Passive** is “The cat is bitten by the dog.” The receiver becomes the grammatical subject. >> Choosing the first animal mentioned gives the **wrong** answer. || The types separate two kinds of listener. >> A listener who always picks the first animal passes actives and subject clefts and fails object clefts and passives. >> A listener who parses the grammar passes all four.
17|**Broca’s patients had particular difficulty with object clefts and passives, but their performance was not great overall.** Aphasia patients and healthy aging controls did the task, and the pattern was **much like what Caramazza and Zurif observed**. Patients were grouped with the Western Aphasia Battery. >> **Elderly controls** scored close to 100% on all four types. >> **Anomic** patients (main problem is word finding) scored about 89% on actives and subject clefts, about 69% on object clefts and about 73% on passives. >> **Conduction** patients scored about 75% on actives and subject clefts and about 42% on object clefts. >> **Broca’s** patients scored about 61% on actives, 65% on subject clefts, 42% on object clefts and 54% on passives. >> **Wernicke’s** patients scored low on every type, around 43% to 50%. || Object clefts and passives are the **syntactically complex** stimuli. >> They are **noncanonical** (they depart from the usual doer-first order). || Two things are true of the Broca’s group at once. >> Even on simple actives, they were far below the elderly controls. >> They paid an extra cost on complex structures. >> So the pattern is a **general drop** plus an **extra cost** for complex structures. >> The groups differ in both overall accuracy and the size of the drop.
18|**Healthy undergraduates under stress make the same errors on object clefts and passives that Broca’s patients make.** The same task was run under **conditions that stress the language system**. >> One stressor was **compressed** speech (speech played faster than normal). >> One was noise. >> One was a **low-pass filter** (removes the higher frequencies, so speech sounds muffled). >> Two were **auditory digits** and **visual digits** (number lists to remember while listening, a secondary task that uses up memory resources). || The cost fell on the complex sentences. >> With a **single stressor**, accuracy stayed high on actives and subject clefts. >> With noise, accuracy fell from about 97% on actives to about 86% on object clefts and about 91% on passives. >> With **two stressors**, the effects grew larger. >> With low-pass filtering plus compression, accuracy was about 91% on actives and 90% on subject clefts. >> It was about 66% on object clefts and 68% on passives. || **Object cleft and passive sentences are particularly vulnerable to all manipulations.** >> These students have no brain damage and no loss of grammatical knowledge. >> Stress alone produced an aphasia-like profile. >> So the comprehension deficits in Broca’s aphasia **may be a “performance” issue after all**. >> Complex structures may demand more processing resources, so any reduction hurts them first. >> The patients may keep their grammatical knowledge, or competence, but lack the resources to use it efficiently.
19|**Broca’s aphasics had difficulty matching an action to an object whether the stimuli were linguistic or not.** **Saygin and colleagues (2004)** used this task to ask whether Broca’s aphasics’ comprehension deficits are **language-specific**. >> In the **non-linguistic** version, a drawing had a boy pantomiming an action, such as licking. >> Participants chose the matching object from pictures such as an ice cream cone, a cake, broccoli and a rooster. >> In the **linguistic** version, the action was given in words. || Aphasic patients made more errors than controls in both versions. >> Non-linguistic accuracy was **98.5%** for controls and **93.4%** for patients. >> Linguistic accuracy was **99.4%** for controls and **87.5%** for patients. || Accuracy also depended on the **distractor** (the type of wrong choice offered). >> With **unrelated** distractors, controls scored 99.8% and patients 93.3%. >> With **semantic** distractors (related in meaning to the correct object), controls scored 99.2% and patients 86.3%. >> With **affordance-based** distractors, controls scored 97.7% and patients 91.6%. An **affordance** is an action an object allows, such as a different object that can also be licked. || The non-linguistic version removes language but keeps meaning. >> A pantomimed action must be understood and linked to the right object, just as a verb is linked to its object. >> A purely grammatical deficit predicts good performance on it. >> Difficulty on it points to a **broader problem with action meanings**, shared with language.
20|**Performance in the non-verbal version of this task was significantly correlated with damage to Broca’s area.** So damage there **affects more than syntax**. >> Lesion maps mark the areas linked to deficits in **non-linguistic pantomime interpretation**. >> The maps cover three horizontal slices of the brain. >> Colored regions mark where damage went with poorer performance, and red and yellow mark the strongest relationships. >> The strongest associations are in the left frontal region in front of the **central sulcus**, which includes Broca’s area. || The task did not require language. >> Participants only interpreted a pantomimed action and chose an object. >> They did not produce a sentence. >> They did not work out the grammar of a spoken instruction. || Two things follow about Broca’s area. >> Patients with damage there can struggle to connect an action with its meaning even when no language is involved. >> Broca’s area supports **action understanding** as well as grammatical processing. >> Any account of Broca’s area has to explain both the grammatical comprehension findings and this nonlinguistic difficulty with actions.
21|**Wernicke’s patients were particularly bad at matching sounds to pictures, and equally bad with words and with environmental sounds.** **Saygin and colleagues** used this task to ask whether Wernicke’s aphasics’ comprehension deficits are **language-specific**. >> In the **nonverbal** version, a speaker played an environmental sound, such as a cow mooing. Participants chose between pictures such as a cow and a sheep. >> In the **verbal** version, the speaker played a spoken phrase, such as “cow mooing.” Participants chose between pictures such as a cow and a violin. || The groups differed in overall accuracy. >> **Controls** scored 99.2% verbal and 98.1% nonverbal. >> **Right hemisphere damage** patients scored 99.1% and 98.2%. >> **Anomic** patients scored 98.6% and 96.7%. >> **Broca’s** patients scored 96.3% and 95.2%. >> **Wernicke’s** patients scored **90.0%** verbal and **91.3%** nonverbal. || **No significant difference** was observed between the verbal and nonverbal versions. >> Wernicke’s patients struggled as much with an animal call as with a spoken phrase. >> So the deficit goes beyond understanding spoken words. >> The difficulty concerns **linking sounds of any kind to their meanings**, through language or through ordinary sounds.
22|**Deficits in both verbal and nonverbal sound-picture matching are associated with Wernicke’s area.** Lesion overlays mark where damage occurred in the patients impaired on each version. >> **Eight** patients were impaired in the **nonverbal** version. >> **Ten** patients were impaired in the **verbal** version. >> Red and yellow mark areas damaged in the largest number of patients. >> In both groups, the greatest overlap is in the **posterior temporal area of the left hemisphere**, around Wernicke’s area. >> So both impairments come from damage to the **same general region**. || The two versions differ in input and share one demand. >> A spoken phrase conveys the meaning through words. >> An animal call conveys the meaning without language. >> Both require connecting meaningful sounds to what they represent. >> Damage around Wernicke’s area disrupts that connection in both cases. || Wernicke’s area appears to support **meaningful sound processing in general**, with language as one important case.
23|**How language-specific the deficits in aphasia are is still a controversy.** Two kinds of finding pull in different directions. >> Some patients are especially vulnerable to complex grammatical structures. This suggests some aspects of language are handled in **specialized** ways. >> The same patients can struggle with action meanings or environmental sounds with no language involved. This suggests language **shares brain systems** with other kinds of meaning. >> An explanation has to account for both. >> If the deficits were purely linguistic, language would appear to have its own dedicated brain systems. || What is clear is **how the terms of the debate have evolved**. >> There are **relatively sophisticated tests** that tap specific aspects of language, such as reversible and improbable sentences, cleft sentences and nonverbal matching tasks. >> There are **quantitative analyses of behavior**, measuring accuracy across sentence types, patient groups and stress conditions. >> There are **quantitative analyses of structure-function relationships**, relating patterns of brain damage to performance across many patients. || The field has come **a long way from the 19th century**. >> Wernicke and Lichtheim drew boxes and arrows from a small number of individual cases. >> Modern research tests which specific operations are impaired and maps them onto damaged tissue across large groups of patients.

25|**Across many patients, damage toward the front goes with fluency deficits and damage toward the back goes with comprehension deficits.** **Voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping** produced this result by formalizing **structure-function relationships** with MRI data. >> The method comes from Bates, Wilson, Saygin, Dick, Sereno, Knight and Dronkers. >> A **voxel** is a small three-dimensional unit of an MRI brain image, like a 3D pixel. >> At each voxel, patients with damage there are compared with patients without damage there. >> If the damaged patients score much worse, that voxel is associated with the symptom. >> Colors run from blue for a weaker association to red for a stronger one. || The **“symptoms”** are **defined very broadly**. >> **Fluency deficits** are tied most strongly to the front part of the left hemisphere. >> **Comprehension deficits** are tied most strongly to the posterior temporal region, farther back. || The data are consistent with a **rough double dissociation**. >> **Posterior regions** are associated with **perception**, or comprehension. >> **Anterior regions** are associated with **production**, or fluency. >> Each patient contributes a damage pattern and a continuous score, so many individual cases become one statistical map.
