2|**Klatt (1979) describes speech perception as a chain of stages that ends in word recognition.** It is a very old and very general model, and each stage transforms the output of the stage before. >> The **speech waveform** enters the **peripheral auditory system**, which does a **spectral analysis** (the **cochlea** splits sound into its frequency components). >> **Acoustic property detectors** respond to particular properties of the sound. >> **Phonetic feature detectors** estimate the probability of features such as voicing at each moment. >> **Segmental analysis** combines features into a sequence of speech segments. >> **Lexical search** compares that sequence with stored words and yields **lexical hypotheses** (candidate words). || **Categorical perception** fits this model. Between the **analog signal** from the cochlea and the **activation of phonemes**, **information is lost**. >> Many physically different sounds become the same phoneme. >> This **loss is helpful**, because it lets listeners make contact with higher levels of representation. >> The model assumes that to identify words, listeners **first identify their constituent phonemes**. || Two problems challenge this simple view. >> In **cascaded processing**, partial information moves on before the earlier decision is finished. >> In **feedback**, a higher level such as words sends influence back down to a lower level such as phonemes.
3|**Equal changes in voice onset time are heard as different only when they cross the beach and peach boundary.** The beach and peach continuum is a familiar **voicing continuum**, with /b/ in beach and /p/ in peach. >> The two consonants differ mainly in **voice onset time**, or **VOT** (the delay between releasing the lips and the start of vocal fold vibration). >> VOT gets shorter in equal steps from “peach” to “beach”. >> A long delay sounds like peach. A short delay sounds like beach. || In **identification**, the labels switch sharply. >> Stimuli 1 through 5 get one label every time. >> Stimulus 6 gets mixed labels, near 0.33. >> Stimuli 7 through 9 get the other label every time. >> The **category boundary** is the narrow region where the label switches. || In **discrimination**, accuracy follows the boundary. >> Pairs inside one category are almost never heard as different. >> Pairs 4 and 6 and 5 and 7 straddle the boundary and are heard as different every time. >> Pair 6 and 8 is intermediate, near 0.33. Pair 7 and 9 falls back to 0. >> A step across the boundary changes the word. A step inside a category does not.
4|**In a threshold model, categorical perception arises at phoneme identification because activity passes on only after a threshold is crossed.** This is one answer to how categorical perception happens. >> A **threshold** is a set level of activity that must be reached before anything is sent onward. >> Activity below the threshold has no effect on the next level. || Take the voicing evidence for a sound between /b/ and /p/. >> At the **feature level**, voicing evidence swings between a **+voiced** threshold and a **−voiced** threshold. >> At the **phoneme level**, /b/ and /p/ stay silent while the evidence wavers. >> Once +voiced evidence crosses its threshold, **/b/ switches on all at once**. || An ambiguous stimulus either crosses a threshold or does not. >> The word level receives a finished /b/ or a finished /p/. >> The earlier uncertainty stays behind at the feature level. >> Two different sounds that both end up as /b/ are identical at word recognition. >> This **all-or-none switch** produces categorical perception at the phoneme level.
5|**In a cascaded model, activity flows forward continuously, so categorical perception might happen later or be an artifact of the task.** Each level responds to whatever activity is present at the moment, while decisions are still forming. >> Feature activity and phoneme activity rise and fall together. >> More **+voiced** evidence means more **/b/** activation. >> Evidence moving toward **−voiced** shifts activation toward **/p/**. >> Mixed evidence gives /b/ and /p/ each some activation, in proportion to the evidence. || Graded phoneme activation passes graded activation to words. >> A sound near the boundary **partly activates both beach and peach**. >> Two sounds both labeled /b/ can differ in how much they supported /p/. || Categorical perception might be an **artifact of the task structure** (a result created by how something is measured, not by the process under study). >> An identification task forces a click on beach or peach, even if both words were partly active. >> The answer looks categorical because the response choices are categorical.
6|**Making furniture is a metaphor for thresholded activity, where each step needs the finished product of the step before.** Each operation turns one specific input into one specific output. >> The **lumber mill** turns **trees** into **lumber**. >> The **furniture factory** turns **lumber** into **unassembled furniture**. >> The **IKEA customer** turns **unassembled furniture** into finished **furniture**. || Each step waits for a completed product. >> Trees become lumber before any furniture is made. >> Lumber becomes parts before any assembly begins. >> No part of one stage's work moves on until the whole job is done. || **Thresholded processing** works the same way. >> A higher level waits until the lower level supplies the representation it needs. >> Information moves forward **only as a completed product**.
7|**In a thresholded system, each layer can only take input that the previous layer has transformed completely.** The factory must wait for the shipment of lumber before manufacturing can start. >> Many computer programs work this way. >> Each stage hands on only its finished output. >> A lower stage can keep working while the higher stage sits idle. || In speech, a strict threshold account makes **word processing depend on an identified phoneme**. >> A partly resolved acoustic signal stays at the lower level until it is identified as a phoneme. >> The word level receives only the finished segment. || This design decides **what information is lost**. >> Different sounds that become the same phoneme lose their differences before word recognition. >> This helps listeners treat varied sounds alike. >> The cost is that later stages never learn how uncertain the earlier stages were.
8|**A food web is a metaphor for cascaded processing, because its levels continuously interact.** The desert food web links desert plants, insects, kangaroo rats, small lizards, large lizards, scorpions, snakes, desert foxes and hawks by who eats whom. >> A drought that reduces plants **immediately** affects the health of kangaroo rats. >> The change in kangaroo rats then affects the snakes. >> No population waits for the level below to reach a finished state. || **Cascaded processing** has the same continuous dependence. >> Changing feature evidence changes phoneme activity as it happens. >> Changing phoneme activity changes support for words. || The food web also has clear **feedback loops**. >> More hawks change the populations of snakes, lizards and rats. Those changes then affect the hawks’ own food supply. >> In recognition, **feedback** is influence from a higher level back down to a lower one. >> Cascading sends evidence forward. Feedback lets a higher level change an earlier representation.
9|**The deciding question is whether phoneme identification for word recognition is thresholded like the lumber mill or cascaded like the food web.** >> In a **thresholded account**, word recognition starts only after the phoneme decision is finished. >> In a **cascaded account**, a word can gain support while the phoneme is still uncertain. || The accounts differ for two sounds that are both labeled **/b/** but differ in how much they resemble **/p/**. >> In a thresholded system, both become the same finished /b/, so pear should **not compete** with bear. >> In a cascaded system, the more ambiguous sound passes on more /p/ support, so pear should **compete with bear for a while**. || Both accounts can predict the same final word. >> They differ only before the final choice. >> The deciding test measures **temporary support for a competing word** during recognition.
10|**McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003) used eye-tracking to look for gradient effects of within-category VOT on lexical access.** >> **Lexical access** is retrieving a word from memory during recognition. >> **Gradient** means changing by degrees. >> **Within-category VOT** is the differences in voice onset time among sounds all heard as the same phoneme. || Listeners identified sounds from a continuum between /b/ and /p/ in a **“visual world” display**. >> **Eye-tracking** recorded where they looked among the pictures as they heard speech. >> Each display had a **target** such as pear and a **competitor** such as bear. >> It also had two unrelated **fillers**, a lamp and a ship. >> Target and competitor differ only in their first consonant. || Eye movements are fast and continuous during word recognition. >> Listeners look at objects as they think about them, often before they know they have decided. >> A listener can click bear after a quick look at the pear. >> The number and timing of such looks measure how active the competitor was.
11|**The mouse clicks gave a typical step-like identification function, so the final choices look fully categorical.** Participants clicked whichever of the pictures was most consistent with what they heard, such as a “bear” or a “pear”. >> Each trial had one sound from the VOT continuum and the four pictures. >> The click records the word the listener finally settled on. || The proportion of /p/ responses changed sharply. >> From 0 to about 15 milliseconds of VOT, /p/ responses stay near 0. >> Between about 15 and 20 milliseconds, they jump up steeply. >> From about 20 to 40 milliseconds, they stay near 1. >> The **category boundary** is at **17.5 milliseconds**, plus or minus 0.83 milliseconds. || Taken alone, this sharp switch would seem to support the threshold model. >> A click records only the word chosen at the end. >> Eye movements record which other words got support before that choice. >> A sharp identification function can exist together with gradual differences in competition.
12|**Eye movements reveal that ambiguity is gradient, with more looks to the competitor close to the boundary.** Gradient means the uncertainty varies by degree. >> The analysis holds the final response constant, comparing only bear clicks or only pear clicks. >> So differences in looks cannot come from listeners changing their answer. || Response bear, looks to the **pear**, at VOTs of 0, 5, 10 and 15 milliseconds. >> Looks rise after the word begins and peak around 400 to 600 milliseconds. Then they decline as bear wins. >> The 15 millisecond VOT, nearest the boundary, gets more and longer looks to the pear than 0 milliseconds. || Response pear, looks to the **bear**, at VOTs of 20, 25, 30, 35 and 40 milliseconds. >> The 20 millisecond VOT, just past the boundary, gets the most looks to the bear. >> The 40 millisecond VOT, the clearest /p/, gets the fewest. || VOT mainly affects the **duration of activation** (how long the competitor stays active). >> Two sounds that both end as bear still produce different amounts of looking at the pear. >> The system keeps track of how ambiguous each sound was, which a threshold model would throw away.
13|**Competitor looks increase as VOT approaches the category boundary from either side.** The effect is clearest across the whole VOT range, with the **category boundary** in the middle. >> On the bear side, listeners clicked bear, and the measure is looks to the pear. >> On the pear side, listeners clicked pear, and the measure is looks to the bear. || Looks were measured in two time windows. >> The **early** window is 300 to 1100 milliseconds after the word begins. >> The **late** window is 1100 to 1900 milliseconds after the word begins. || Both windows show the same pattern. >> On the bear side, late looks to the pear rise from about 0.02 at 0 milliseconds to about 0.045 near 15 milliseconds. >> On the pear side, late looks to the bear are about 0.04 near 20 milliseconds and fall to about 0.01 by 40 milliseconds. >> Early looks are higher overall but follow the same pattern. >> The rise is gradual, not sudden. || Uncertainty about the first sound affects looking throughout recognition. >> A sound can support the winner while still supporting its rival. >> The closer the sound is to the boundary, the stronger the rival support.
14|**The eye-movement result fits activation that flows in a cascaded way from features to phonemes.** Changing features affect phonemes, and changing phonemes affect words, while recognition is still going on. >> Even stimuli identified as /b/ **100% of the time** vary in how strongly the **“voiced” feature** is activated. >> A clear /b/ activates voicing strongly. A /b/ near the boundary activates it weakly. || /b/ and /p/ share most of their features. >> Both are **bilabial** (the two lips form the closure). >> Both are **stops** (airflow is fully blocked and then released). >> A stimulus that is obviously **+bilabial** and **+stop** supports both equally. >> Only voicing separates them, so weak voicing creates **momentary ambiguity between /b/ and /p/**. >> That ambiguity shows up in the **eye movements** while the participant decides where to click. || The **TRACE** model represents this cascade with three layers. >> **Feature layer**, which takes a **pseudo-spectral input representation** (a simplified version of the sound’s frequency content over time). Its detectors are POW, VOC, DIFF, ACU, GRD, VOI and BUR, for power, vocalic, diffuseness, acuteness, gradualness of onset, voicing and burst. >> **Phoneme layer**, with units such as /b/, /p/, /r/ and /t/ repeated at every point in time. >> **Word layer**, with word units also repeated across time. >> Activity passes upward, and units within a layer compete through **inhibition**.
15|**Andruski and colleagues (1994) found that partial activation can reach all the way to the lexical level, even from nonwords.** They found **semantic priming** (a faster response to a word after hearing a word related in meaning) with **nonwords**. >> The nonword “beace” primes “war”, because beace is peace with a voiced first consonant. >> The nonword “ging” primes “queen”, because ging is king with a voiced first consonant. >> In **word to word** items, the altered prime is a real word, as when “bill” primes “drug” because bill is an altered form of pill. || Participants did a **lexical decision task** (deciding as fast as possible whether a target is a real word). >> Primes were **unaltered**, had VOT reduced by **one third**, had it reduced by **two thirds**, or were **unrelated**. >> Unrelated primes gave the slowest responses, near 950 to 970 milliseconds. >> Unaltered primes gave much faster responses, around 810 to 860 milliseconds. >> With one third removed, responses stayed fast, around 825 to 855 milliseconds. >> With two thirds removed, word to nonword items stayed near 820 milliseconds, but word to word items slowed to about 890 milliseconds. >> In word to word items, the altered prime also partly activates a competing real word, which reduces the benefit. || **Lexical representations can be activated even by incomplete or imperfect input**. >> Beace matches peace closely enough to partly activate it. >> That activation spreads to meaning and speeds the response to war, which fits **cascaded processing**.
16|**A thresholded model cannot explain why looks to the other member of the minimal pair rise gradually toward the category boundary.** This eye-tracking result is one piece of evidence for **cascaded processing**. >> A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in one sound, such as bear and pear. >> Competitor looks climb steadily as VOT nears the boundary. >> This holds even when the final response is the same, so it comes from graded activation within one category. || In a thresholded model, graded feature activity never reaches the phoneme level. >> Feature activation swings between +voiced and −voiced. >> Phoneme activation stays flat until /b/ suddenly switches on. >> Every token in a category sends the **same finished phoneme** to the word level. >> So word-level competition would be the same for every /b/. || In a cascaded model, graded information does reach the word level. >> More support for the competing phoneme passes on more support for its word. >> That explains the gradual rise in competitor looks directly.
17|**TRACE also includes feedback from the lexical layer to the phoneme layer.** Activity runs up from phonemes to words and back down from words to phonemes. >> In **lexical feedback**, an active word sends support back to its own phonemes. >> An uncertain sound can partly activate a word. >> That word then strengthens the reading of the sound that fits it. || Influence runs in both directions. >> Acoustic evidence changes which words are active. >> Active words change the balance between possible phonemes while the decision is still forming. >> This helps listeners recover from noisy or ambiguous input. || **Feedback is different from cascading**. >> Cascading passes incomplete evidence forward. >> Feedback sends influence from words back down to phonemes. >> An interactive model can have both. >> Experiments where word knowledge changes how sounds are heard, such as the **Ganong effect**, motivate adding feedback.
18|**Ganong (1980) tested whether knowing which sequences are real words changes how an ambiguous sound is heard.** The finding is the **Ganong effect**. “Gisses and kifts for Valentine’s day” plays on it by swapping the voicing of the first consonants in “kisses and gifts”. || He made **word and nonword pairs** such as “corpse/gorpse”. >> The /k/ end gives corpse, a real word. >> The /g/ end gives gorpse, a nonword. >> The continua spanned the phoneme boundary in **5 millisecond steps** of VOT. || The design pairs **uncertain phonetic evidence** with a **difference in lexical status**. >> The rest of the word, “orpse”, never changes. >> It tells the listener which beginning would complete a real word. >> If word knowledge matters, an ambiguous first consonant should more often be heard as the sound that makes a word. >> The key test is the **ambiguous middle** of the continuum, where word knowledge has the most room to act.
19|**A late correction account predicts a reporting bias toward words that is the same size at every step of the continuum.** In this account, **categorical perception functions as usual**, but there is a **bias to report having heard a word**. >> The listener first categorizes the sound without word knowledge. >> At the response stage, the listener leans toward the option that forms a word. || Three kinds of continua are compared. >> In a **word-nonword** continuum, the voiced end forms a word. >> In a **nonword-word** continuum, the voiceless end forms a word. >> In a **neutral** continuum, neither end has a lexical advantage. || A bias added after perception pushes every step toward the word. >> Word-nonword responses stay near 0.25 voiced at the long VOT end instead of falling to 0. >> Nonword-word responses start near 0.75 voiced at the short VOT end instead of 1. >> So even clear sounds are sometimes reported as the word. || The **lexical effect** (the difference in voiced responses between the two lexical conditions) is a **flat line** near 0.25. >> Perception and word knowledge stay separate. >> Word knowledge only nudges the reported answer.
20|**If word knowledge shifts the category boundary, the lexical effect should peak at the items closest to the boundary.** In this account, word knowledge changes where the listener switches from one phoneme category to the other. >> Near an ambiguous onset, the reading that forms a real word gets extra support. >> So categorization itself changes, not only the reported answer. || The conditions differ only in the middle of the continuum. >> At both ends, the sound is clear, so voiced responses start near 1 and end near 0 in every condition. >> Near the boundary, word-nonword crosses later and nonword-word crosses earlier. || The **lexical effect** forms a **peak**. >> It is near 0 at the endpoints. >> It rises to about 0.35 for items closest to the boundary. >> Those are the stimuli for which lexical information can help resolve ambiguity about the initial phoneme. >> At the endpoints, strong acoustic evidence already settles the consonant. || A **flat effect** points to a reporting bias. A **peaked effect** points to a boundary shift.
21|**Ganong’s results are strikingly consistent with the second model, the boundary shift.** >> At the short and long VOT ends, the word-nonword and nonword-word conditions give nearly the same answers. >> Near the boundary, the two conditions separate the most. >> The **lexical effect** rises from about 0.1 at the endpoints to a peak near **0.3** at the boundary. || The peak rules out a simple reporting bias. >> Word knowledge matters most where acoustic evidence is weakest. >> At the boundary, the word that fits tips the balance. >> At the endpoints, the acoustics decide alone. || Ganong raised several possible explanations, focused on **guessing strategies**. >> A guessing strategy would let listeners use word knowledge only when unsure of the sound. || Later models emphasize the potential role of **feedback** in these shifts. >> In **interactive models**, an active word sends support back to its own phonemes. >> The whole word can then shape how an ambiguous part of it is heard during recognition.
22|**A TRACE simulation of the Ganong effect shows word and phoneme activations changing as an ambiguous start unfolds into the word plug.** The input is a sound **ambiguous between /b/ and /p/**, then /l/, the vowel /ʌ/ and /g/. >> Activations are recorded in four frames, one as each new sound arrives. || The first frame has only the ambiguous sound. >> /b/ and /p/ are both weakly active at about the same level, near 0.15 to 0.2. >> Word activation is still very low. || The second and third frames add /l/ and the vowel. >> Partial /b/ evidence supports words beginning with bl. Partial /p/ evidence supports words beginning with pl. >> **Plug**, **blood** and **blush** all gain activation, because all fit the beginning. >> /p/ starts to pull ahead of /b/. || The fourth frame adds the final **/g/**. >> /g/ fits plug but not blood or blush. >> **Plug** becomes the most active word, at about 0.45, and the alternatives fall away. >> **/p/ ends up more active than /b/**.
23|**TRACE explains word effects on phoneme boundaries through top-down feedback from the lexical level to the phoneme level.** **Top-down processing** (higher-level knowledge influencing lower-level analysis). || Activity accumulates for **“plug”** as the input unfolds. >> The biggest gain comes when the **final /g/** arrives. >> The /g/ is highly inconsistent with “blood” and “blush”, which were also active in Frame 3. >> Blood and blush fit the early input, so they were plausible for a while. >> Once /g/ arrives, their endings do not match, so they lose support. || The lexical level then sends **feedback to the phoneme level**. >> Feedback gives **greater activation to the phoneme consistent with a word**. >> Plug is the winning word, so /p/ gets extra support. >> The ambiguous first sound comes to be heard as /p/. >> Information at the end of a word can change how an earlier sound is heard.
24|**TRACE is a version of the Interactive Activation model for written word recognition, and many principles are the same.** That model is from **McClelland and Rumelhart (1981)**. >> **Feature** units connect to **letter** units, and letter units connect to **word** units such as WEAR, WORK, WORD and WEAK. >> Compatible units at different levels support each other. >> Competing units in the same level inhibit each other. >> Active words send support back down to their letters. || A reader sees **W, O and R** clearly, while ink blots cover part of the last letter. >> The visible part of the last letter fits both **R** and **K**. >> W, O and R activate WORK and WORD. >> Only WORK fits the last letter, so WORK becomes the strongest word. >> WORK sends support back to K, so the letter is **perceived as K**, just as plug supports /p/. || A key issue in speech is that **the stimuli unfold over time**. >> A written word is all there at once. A spoken word arrives one sound at a time. >> TRACE repeats feature, phoneme and word units at every point in time. >> A candidate can fit the beginning, gain activation and then weaken when the ending conflicts.
25|**Categorical perception still holds, but spoken word recognition is more interactive than a lumber mill.** **Categorical perception** (given the same physical difference, stimuli from the same category are harder to tell apart than stimuli from different categories). >> Equal acoustic steps have different effects depending on whether they cross a boundary. >> Nothing about cascading or feedback undermines that finding. || The **lexical-phonetic interface** adds two findings. >> **Ambiguity about phoneme identity does have an impact on word recognition**. Sounds with the same label still differ in how strongly they activate a competing word. >> **Word-level information seems to have an impact on phoneme categorization**. In the Ganong effect, word knowledge shifts the boundary, which TRACE explains with lexical feedback. || So the word recognition system **cannot just sit there waiting** for the phoneme system to finish its job. >> Partial information passes forward while uncertainty is still being resolved. >> Active words send support back down. >> Spoken word recognition works more like the food web. >> Labels can be categorical while the processing behind them is graded.

27|**Spoken word recognition is also very fast, and candidate words become active while the word is still unfolding.** **Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** used a visual world eye-tracking task. >> Participants saw a grid of pictures and geometric shapes and followed spoken instructions to move objects. || The pictures were chosen for their sound overlap with **beaker**. >> **Beaker** is the **referent** (the target). >> **Beetle** is a **cohort competitor** (shares the beginning). >> **Speaker** is a **rhyme competitor** (shares the ending). >> **Carriage** is **unrelated** (shares no sounds). || The measure is the probability of fixating each item over the first 1,000 milliseconds. The spoken word ends a little before 400 milliseconds on average. >> Looks to beaker and beetle rise together from around 200 milliseconds, because both fit the beginning. >> Looks to beetle peak near 0.2 around 500 milliseconds and fall once the distinguishing sounds arrive. >> Looks to beaker pass 0.8 by about 800 milliseconds and approach 1 by 1,000 milliseconds. >> Looks to speaker rise later and stay small, near 0.1. >> Looks to carriage stay near zero. >> Words that stop matching the input drop out.
28|**A TRACE simulation predicts the eye movements moment by moment, so the looks reveal processing before the final response.** Real fixation probability is compared with TRACE’s predicted fixation probability over 1,000 milliseconds. >> For the referent, real and predicted looks both rise steadily from about 300 milliseconds and approach 1 by 1,000 milliseconds. >> For the cohort competitor, both rise early, peak near 0.17 to 0.2 around 500 milliseconds, and then fall. >> For the rhyme competitor, both rise later and stay small, near 0.1. >> The predictions closely match the data. || The **granularity** of the simulation is fine (it predicts activity moment by moment, not only the final choice). >> **Eye movements give an idea of the changes in “activity”** for different competitors over time. >> So researchers can **see cognitive processing before a final response is issued**. || This parallels the **graded effects of category goodness** in categorical perception. >> Better and worse examples of a category produce different amounts of competitor activity. >> This happens even when the final label is the same.
