2|**“The horse raced past the barn fell” is grammatical, but its opening words lead readers to build the wrong structure.** It usually feels broken on first reading. >> Most readers first take **raced** as the main verb. >> Then “the horse raced past the barn” seems to be a complete event in which the horse did the racing. >> When **fell** arrives, that structure has no place for it, so the sentence seems to fall apart. || The sentence describes **two events**. >> Someone raced the horse past the barn. >> The horse then fell. >> The intended meaning is “The horse that was raced past the barn fell”. >> Here “raced past the barn” tells which horse is meant, and **fell** is the main verb. || The sentence can be **disambiguated** by putting back the missing words. >> Adding “that was” makes the modifying relationship explicit, so raced can no longer be the main verb. >> Understanding a sentence depends on assigning relationships among words, not only on knowing what each word means. || It could fairly be called **the sentence that launched a thousand experiments**. >> Researchers have used it again and again to study how readers build structure as words arrive.
3|**A garden path sentence leads the reader into one interpretation early, and later words reveal that reading to be wrong.** “The horse raced past the barn fell” is the classic case of the **“garden path” effect**. >> The name comes from the expression “to lead someone down the garden path”, meaning to mislead. >> Raced looks like the main verb. >> Fell then conflicts with the structure already built. >> The reader must go back and reinterpret “raced past the barn” as “that was raced past the barn”. || The horse sentence is almost as famous as “Colorless green ideas sleep furiously”. >> “Colorless green ideas sleep furiously” is grammatical but meaningless. >> The horse sentence is grammatical and meaningful but very hard to process. || **Grammaticality and processing difficulty are different things**. >> A sentence can have an acceptable structure and still be very hard to follow as it unfolds. >> Readers build structure word by word and choose one analysis before the sentence is complete. >> If they waited until the end, they would never be led astray. || A dinosaur comic jokes that nobody would ever say the horse sentence. >> The reply is that farmers could say it if their horses kept falling over. >> The joke works only because the sentence really is grammatical.
4|**A parser has to build a syntactic tree for each sentence while working with limited memory and limited time.** Syntactic explanations of the garden path effect start from this **parser** (a mental process that builds a tree for the sentences we hear or read). >> A **syntactic tree** records how words group into phrases and how the phrases relate. >> **Limited memory** means the parser can keep only a limited number of structures available at once. >> **Time constraints** mean words arrive quickly, so the parser cannot wait for the end of the sentence. || The correct tree for the horse sentence is large. >> The subject is a big **NP** (noun phrase) containing “the horse” plus a reduced clause meaning “that was raced past the barn”. >> “That” and “was” are silent, and the clause has an empty subject position. >> Inside the clause, raced takes the **PP** (prepositional phrase) “past the barn”. >> The main **VP** (verb phrase) contains only fell. || The failed parse is small. >> “The horse” is an ordinary NP. >> The main VP is “raced past the barn”, with raced as the main verb. >> Fell has nowhere to attach, so the parse breaks down.
5|**Minimal attachment is a strategy in which the parser initially prefers the smaller tree, the one with fewer nodes.** A **node** is a point in a syntactic tree that stands for a grammatical grouping, such as an NP, a VP or a sentence. >> The grammatical version of the horse sentence is **much more complex syntactically** than the failed parse. >> It has **many more nodes**, for the embedded clause and for the silent “that” and “was”. >> The failed parse makes “raced past the barn” the main VP, which takes far fewer nodes. || **“Minimal attachment”** saves space. >> Each new word is attached to the existing structure with the fewest possible new nodes. >> Fewer nodes means less structure to build and hold in memory at that moment. || In the horse sentence, minimal attachment favors **raced as the main verb**. >> That analysis needs the fewest nodes. >> The reading where “raced past the barn” modifies horse needs an embedded relative clause, so the parser passes it over. >> When fell arrives, it demands the larger structure, so the reader has to rebuild the tree.
6|**Late closure is a bias to keep the current clause open as long as possible, so new words join the phrase being built.** It is another parsing strategy in the same framework as minimal attachment. >> Keeping a clause “open” means adding incoming words to it instead of closing it and starting a new structure. || **Frazier (1987)** gives two structures for “The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite”. >> The phrase “with the dynamite” wants to associate with the big verb phrase starting with “blew open”. >> The **preferred** structure attaches the PP to the VP. This is **verb-phrase attachment**, and the dynamite is the **instrument** used to blow open the safe. >> The **unpreferred** structure attaches the PP to the NP “the safe”. This is **noun-phrase attachment**, and the phrase describes which safe was blown open, the one that had the dynamite. || Both attachments are grammatical, but they mean different things. >> Late closure keeps the verb phrase open, so the incoming phrase joins it. >> Minimal attachment also prefers VP attachment, because it needs fewer nodes than an extra NP around “the safe with the dynamite”. >> Here the preferred parse is also the sensible one, so readers reach the right meaning easily.
7|**People read more slowly when the sensible meaning of a sentence requires the unpreferred parse.** Late closure is at work in “The burglar blew open the safe with the jewels”. >> Jewels are an implausible tool for blowing open a safe. >> The sensible meaning is that the burglar blew open **the safe that contained the jewels**. >> That meaning needs “with the jewels” attached to the noun phrase “the safe”, the unpreferred NP attachment. || Careful tests of **reading time** and **eye movements** find the slowdown. >> Reading time measures how long readers spend on each part of a sentence. >> Eye movement recordings track where readers look and when they jump back to reread. >> The extra time appears at the point where the parse has to change. || Readers **momentarily entertain the idea** that the jewels were used to blow open the safe, and then **retrace their steps**. >> The parser first attaches the phrase to the verb phrase, as it did for the dynamite. >> The meaning of jewels conflicts with the instrument reading. >> The parser moves the attachment to the noun phrase, and this repair takes measurable time.
8|**The syntactic account assumes a parser that works only on abstract syntactic structure and ignores ambiguity until it becomes a problem.** It parallels the **“lumber mill”** example from speech perception. >> In a lumber mill, each stage works on its own material and hands a finished product to the next stage. >> The parser builds structure from grammatical categories alone, without consulting meaning or context. >> Meaning is worked out afterward from the finished structure. || The parser has **its own operating characteristics**. >> It wants the simplest tree possible, which is **minimal attachment**. >> It gets there partly by leaving clauses open as long as possible, which is **late closure**. >> Both limit how much structure must be built and held in memory at once. || Ignoring ambiguity is **very efficient**. >> The parser commits to one likely structure and builds only that one. >> Most of the time the first choice is right, so no extra effort is needed. >> When a later word does not fit, the structure must be revised, and that revision causes garden path difficulty.
9|**Sentences with reduced relative clauses are syntactically “difficult”, yet readers often parse them with no trouble.** This is a problem for a purely syntactic explanation. A **reduced relative clause** is a relative clause with “that was” or “that were” left out, as in “the horse raced past the barn fell”. >> Trueswell, Tanenhaus and Garnsey (1994) wrote “a noun phrase FOLLOWED by a MORPHOLOGICALLY AMBIGUOUS VERB … will be temporarily ambiguous”. >> Britt, Perfetti, Garrod and Rayner (1992) wrote “Referential information PROVIDED by the discourse is of no help”. >> Frazier and Rayner (1987) wrote “Recent research REPORTED by FERREIRA AND CLIFTON (1986) has demonstrated that syntactic processing is quite independent and that the initial syntactic analysis ASSIGNED to a sentence is little influenced by the semantic information ALREADY ANALYZED”. >> Altmann and Steedman (1988) wrote “The pattern of results OBTAINED with global reading times is reflected by differences LOCATED on the critical disambiguating phrase”. || Each verb in capitals introduces a reduced relative, as in the horse sentence. >> **But these are not impossibly difficult sentences**. >> In “recent research reported by Ferreira and Clifton”, reported describes research that someone reported. || **MacDonald, Perlmutter and Seidenberg (1994)** argue that **semantic factors** and **statistical regularities** interact with “parsing” from very early on. >> Semantic factors concern meaning. >> Statistical regularities are patterns in how often words appear in particular structures. >> Particular words and their familiar uses can support a reduced relative reading from the start.
10|**Many of the reduced relative examples come from papers that argue reduced relatives are hard to parse.** That makes the collection of examples **wonderfully passive-aggressive**. >> Those papers use the difficulty of parsing reduced relatives as evidence for an **autonomous parser** (a parser that builds syntactic structure without help from meaning or context). >> Yet the authors wrote reduced relatives in their own articles, and readers understood them easily. || Each capitalized verb modifies the noun before it. >> In “a noun phrase FOLLOWED by a morphologically ambiguous verb”, followed modifies noun phrase. >> In “the initial syntactic analysis ASSIGNED to a sentence”, assigned modifies analysis. >> In “differences LOCATED on the critical disambiguating phrase”, located modifies differences. >> Ferreira and Clifton (1986) wrote “A person PRESENTED with the evidence EXAMINED by the lawyer may interpret the evidence as a theme, not an agent, because examined requires an animate agent”. Presented modifies person, and examined modifies evidence. || The cited examples were **not the only reduced relatives** in those articles. >> The rate was about **one per journal page** in this sample of articles. >> Reduced relatives are common in ordinary scientific writing. >> In a supportive context, a formally complex structure is easy to understand.
11|**Meaning, word statistics and world knowledge interact with the syntax of a sentence from very early on.** This is the argument for why “Recent research reported by…” is easy to read. >> **Semantics** helps, because research is **inanimate** (not a living thing) and unlikely to be the subject of a verb like report. >> **Lexical statistics** (learned patterns of how often particular words appear in particular structures) help, because “reported” occurs frequently in reduced relatives. >> Lexical statistics and **“world knowledge”** help together, because “reported” occurs very frequently in reduced relatives in scientific papers, especially when following the word “research”. >> These and many other factors help decide which structure is chosen in the first place. || Two sentences with identical grammar differ in difficulty. >> “The burglar arrested in the parking lot was charged today” is easy. Burglars are likely to be arrested, so arrested is read as a reduced relative describing the burglar. >> “The policeman arrested in the parking lot was charged today” is harder. Policemen are likely to do the arresting, so arrested is first taken as the main verb, and “was charged” causes a garden path. >> The difference comes from what we know about burglars and policemen.
12|**Listeners briefly treat the empty towel as the place to put the apple, as minimal attachment predicts.** **Spivey and colleagues (1995)** tested how local **“world knowledge”** can be, using the visual scene in front of the listener. >> People saw a pencil, a box, an empty towel and an apple on a towel. >> They heard “Put the apple on the towel in the box” and carried it out while their eye movements were recorded. >> The unambiguous comparison instruction was “Put the apple that’s on the towel in the box”. || With **minimal attachment**, “the towel” would temporarily seem like the end of the clause “put the apple on the towel”. >> In that reading, “on the towel” names the **destination**. >> In the intended reading, “on the towel” tells **which apple** to move, and “in the box” is the destination. || People **do look at the empty towel** while planning their movements, as though considering it as a target. >> With the ambiguous instruction, looks go from the apple (near 1,000 milliseconds) to the empty towel and then to the box (near 2,000 milliseconds). >> With the unambiguous instruction, looks go from the apple straight to the box. >> The listener recognizes every word correctly but briefly takes the location phrase as the destination.
13|**With two apples in the display, listeners take “on the towel” as telling which apple, right from the start.** This display is the **two-referent context**. >> One apple sits on a towel, and the other sits elsewhere. >> The empty towel and the box are still present. >> Now “the apple” alone does not say which apple to move, so “on the towel” becomes useful. || Eye movements change in this context. >> Participants usually look at the apple on the towel and then at the box. >> Looks to the empty towel are much less common. >> The **sequence and timing of eye movements**, relative to the nouns in the speech, **did not differ** between ambiguous and unambiguous instructions. || The ambiguous instruction is processed just like the explicit one with “that’s on the towel”. >> The listener needs to know which apple, so a phrase that picks one out is expected. >> The destination reading never takes hold. >> The same sentence gets a different grammatical analysis because the visual context differs.
14|**People look at the empty towel much less when the display has two apples.** With two apples it becomes **felicitous** (appropriate and natural in the situation) to give more information about which apple is meant. >> With one apple, there is no need to say which apple, so “on the towel” is easily taken as a destination. >> With two apples, “on the towel” has a clear job, which is to identify the right apple. || The measure is the proportion of trials with eye movements to the incorrect destination, the empty towel. >> In the one-referent context, ambiguous instructions led to such looks on about **55%** of trials. >> In the one-referent context, unambiguous instructions led to almost none. >> In the two-referent context, ambiguous instructions led to such looks on only about **17%** of trials. >> In the two-referent context, unambiguous instructions led to such looks on roughly 12% of trials. || The words of the instruction were identical in both contexts, so the visual context alone changed how often listeners were misled. >> This is an effect of **referential context** (the set of objects a phrase could refer to). >> Referential context influences which grammatical relationship listeners assign while the sentence is still being heard.
15|**The same sentence is parsed differently depending on the scene, and this happens very early, which purely syntactic accounts cannot explain.** The Spivey results are **a big problem** for those accounts. >> Different parses of the same sentence are favored under different **semantic** and **pragmatic** conditions. >> Semantic conditions concern meaning. >> Pragmatic conditions concern how language is used in a situation, such as whether a speaker needs to specify which object is meant. || The words never change, and only the scene changes. >> With one apple, the ambiguous sentence led to looks at the empty towel on about 55% of trials. >> With two apples, it led to such looks on only about 17% of trials. || The difference appears in **eye movements during the sentence**, before the action is carried out. >> The eye movements capture the parse while it is being built. >> If context only corrected the parse afterward, listeners in both contexts should first look at the empty towel. >> Those looks drop with two apples, so context shaped the **first parse**. >> A parser that always starts with the same structure does not predict that.
16|**Syntactic ambiguity bends the path of the hand toward the wrong destination, not only the path of the eyes.** **Farmer and colleagues (2007)** tracked computer **mouse movements** while participants carried out spoken instructions in a version of the apple and towel display. >> The actual execution of **arm movements** can be influenced by syntactic ambiguity and its interaction with the **visually defined context**. >> Mouse tracking records the whole path of the hand, so a competing interpretation can pull the movement toward another location. || The paths for ambiguous and unambiguous instructions were compared in each context. >> In the one-referent context, the ambiguous path **curved toward the empty towel** on the way from the apple to the box. >> The two paths separated most in the middle of the movement, and the hand still ended at the correct place. >> In the two-referent context, the two paths were nearly identical. >> The second apple makes the location phrase useful for picking out an apple, so it is not taken as a destination. || The **Euclidean distance** (straight-line distance) between the two paths was measured at each moment. >> In the one-referent context, it peaked at about 200 pixels around 40% of the way through the movement. >> In the two-referent context, it peaked lower and earlier, at about 140 pixels around 30%. >> Comprehension and action planning interact while both are underway.

18|**After “the”, the parser expects a noun to follow, so one word already limits what can come next.** **The** is a **determiner** (a word that introduces a noun phrase). >> “The” does not name anything by itself. >> It limits which kinds of words can come next. >> An adjective or other modifier may come before the noun, as in “the hot pizza”. || The expectation is about a **grammatical category**, not a particular word. >> Many different nouns could follow, such as pizza, dog or book. >> The parser predicts the form of the phrase while its meaning is still open. >> Words that fit the expectation are processed smoothly. >> Words that break the expectation cause difficulty.
19|**“Pizza” completes the noun phrase begun by “the”, so the parser now expects a verb.** **Pizza** is a **noun** and the **head of the noun phrase** (the central word the rest of the phrase is organized around). >> “The” combines with “pizza” to form “the pizza”. >> “The pizza” can now serve as the subject of the sentence. >> The parser expects a verb or verb phrase telling what the pizza did or what happened to it. || Recognizing the word makes two kinds of information available. >> Its **category**, noun, tells the parser how it can combine with other words. >> Its **meaning**, a kind of food, sets up expectations about plausible events, such as being eaten or baked. >> Grammatical expectations and meaning-based expectations both guide how the next words are processed.
20|**“Was” leaves several structures possible, so the parser has to wait for the next words to know its role.** **Was** is a past-tense form of **be**, and it can act as an **auxiliary** (a helping verb that combines with another verb to mark tense or voice). >> In “the pizza was eaten”, was helps form a **passive** with the participle eaten. >> In “the pizza was in the oven”, was links the subject to a phrase giving a location. || The sentence could now continue in several ways. >> It could say where the pizza was, and a location phrase would follow with a preposition. >> It could say what was done to the pizza, and a passive would follow with a participle such as eaten. >> It could say what the pizza was like, and an adjective would follow. >> The parser keeps the developing structure available while more words arrive.
21|**“In” starts a prepositional phrase, so the parser now expects a noun phrase that names a location.** **In** is a **preposition** (a word that expresses a relationship such as location or containment). >> “In” does not name the location by itself. >> It sets up the expectation of a noun phrase that supplies the location, as in “in the oven” or “in the box”. || After “the pizza was”, the word “in” narrows the possible structures. >> The sentence is heading toward a statement about where the pizza was. >> The expectation is grammatical, so the next word must be able to start a noun phrase. >> Words like “the”, “an” or a noun would fit. >> Understanding the phrase requires combining the preposition with that noun phrase.
22|**After “in the”, the parser strongly expects a noun, and that expectation is about word category.** This second **the** begins a new **noun phrase** inside the prepositional phrase “in the …”. >> “The” does not supply the location by itself. >> A noun is still needed, possibly with modifiers before it, as in “in the hot oven”. || The expectation concerns a **word category**, not just a sensible topic. >> Whatever comes next must work grammatically as part of a noun phrase. >> Knowing the sentence is about pizza makes words like oven or box likely. >> The grammatical requirement holds regardless of meaning. >> A word that cannot be part of a noun phrase will clash with the expectation.
23|**“Eaten” is a verb form arriving where a noun is required, so it breaks the structure itself.** **Eaten** is the **past participle** of eat. >> It cannot complete the noun phrase that “in the” has started. >> “In the eaten” is a **word-category violation** (a word of the wrong grammatical category in a position that demands a different one). || A category error differs from a strange but grammatical meaning. >> “The pizza was in the ocean” is odd in meaning but grammatically fine, because ocean is a noun. >> “The pizza was in the eaten” cannot be fixed by imagining an unusual situation, because the problem is the grammatical type of the word. || The contrast makes such sentences useful for studying the brain. >> Brain responses to category errors and to meaning errors can be compared. >> The comparison can reveal whether grammar and meaning are processed differently.
24|**The ELAN is an early brain response over the left front of the scalp to a word of the wrong category.** **ELAN** stands for **early left anterior negativity**. It is an **ERP** (event-related potential, electrical brain activity recorded at the scalp, averaged over many trials and timed to an event). It is elicited by **word class violations**. >> A **word class** is a grammatical category such as noun, verb or preposition. || Classic ELAN stimuli are word-category violations. >> “The pizza was in the eaten” has a participle where a noun is expected. >> “The scientist criticized Max’s of proof the theorem” has the preposition **of** where a noun is expected after “Max’s”. || The ELAN has three features. >> It is **early**, about **100 to 200 milliseconds** after the critical word. >> It is **left and anterior**, strongest over the left front of the scalp, which raises the question of whether it is specific to language. >> It occurs **specifically for word category violations**, such as a noun when a verb is expected, or a preposition in place of a noun. || Incorrect sentences produce the early negative response at left frontal sites, and correct sentences do not. >> The response is so fast that it was taken as evidence for a syntactic check of word categories before meaning is processed.
25|**With written words, the early response to a word-category error comes from visual cortex.** **Dikker, Rabagliati, Farmer and Pylkkänen (2010)** reported this in “Early occipital sensitivity to syntactic category is based on form typicality”. **Visual cortex** is the region at the back of the brain, in the **occipital** lobe, that processes what we see. >> They compared “The beautiful princess …” with “The beautifully princess …”. >> Princess is a **bimorphemic noun** (a noun ending in an identifiable noun suffix). >> Beautiful is an adjective, which correctly predicts a noun. >> Beautifully is an adverb, which predicts a verb or an adjective, so princess is unexpected. >> The visual response was **larger** for the unexpected noun at about 120 to 130 milliseconds. || The finding suggests that a prediction can **feed back down to the earliest parts of the visual system**. >> When we expect a verb, the prediction covers visual patterns typical of the way verbs are written. >> The very early response to the error might reflect a **mismatch** between that prediction and what finally appears on the screen. || Does this only work for items that are **morphologically marked** (containing a part, such as a suffix, that signals the category) as nouns or verbs? >> The other possibility concerns **open-class** words (content words that keep gaining members) and **closed-class** words (a small fixed set of function words). >> The effect appears for nouns with a noun-marking morpheme and for nouns with a typical noun form. >> It does not appear for neutral nouns like “infant” that lack strong cues.
26|**The same word form can be a noun in one sentence and a verb in another, and words differ in how noun-like or verb-like they sound.** >> In “Chris and Ben are glad that the bird perches seem easy to install”, perches is a **noun** (the bars that birds sit on), and seem is the verb. >> In “Chris and Ben are glad that the bird perches comfortably in the cage”, perches is a **verb** (what the bird does). >> In “The teacher told the principal that the student needs were not being met”, needs is a **noun** (the student’s requirements). >> In “The teacher told the principal that the student needs to be more focused”, needs is a **verb**. >> The **continuation** after the ambiguous word settles its grammatical role. || **Phonological form typicality** is how much a word’s sound pattern resembles the sound patterns of typical nouns or typical verbs. >> A corpus analysis placed **3,158 words** by their mean distance in phonological feature space from all nouns and from all verbs. >> A small distance means the word sounds similar to that category. >> Nouns tend to sound closer to other nouns, and verbs closer to other verbs. >> The groups are **noun-like nouns**, **verb-like nouns**, **verb-like verbs** and **noun-like verbs**. >> A word can sound like one category while actually belonging to the other.
27|**“Perches” is noun-like and “needs” is verb-like, so each word has one use that matches its form and one that clashes.** These are tendencies in **form typicality**, learned statistical tendencies in how words sound. Both words can still appear in either role. >> In “the bird perches seem easy to install”, perches is a noun, which **matches** its noun-like form. >> In “the bird perches comfortably in the cage”, perches must be a verb, which **goes against** its noun-like form. >> In “the student needs to be more focused”, needs is a verb, which **matches** its verb-like form. >> In “the student needs were not being met”, needs must be a noun, which **goes against** its verb-like form. || The four sentences set up a test of form typicality. >> The same word form is compared in a matching use and a mismatching use. >> The comparison separates what a word’s form suggests from what the grammar of the sentence requires. >> If form typicality guides parsing, the mismatching uses should be harder to process. >> All four sentences are grammatical.
28|**Readers slow down when the sentence requires the category that does not match how the word sounds.** The measure is reading time at **disambiguation** minus reading time at the **point of ambiguity**, adjusted for word length. >> The point of ambiguity is the ambiguous word itself, such as perches or needs. >> Disambiguation is the point where the continuation makes the intended category clear, such as “seem” or “comfortably”. >> A larger increase signals more difficulty. || **Noun-like homonyms** such as perches were harder to resolve as verbs. >> Noun-resolved sentences added only about **50 milliseconds**. >> Verb-resolved sentences added about **120 milliseconds**. || **Verb-like homonyms** such as needs were harder to resolve as nouns. >> Noun-resolved sentences added about **110 milliseconds**. >> Verb-resolved sentences added almost nothing, under 10 milliseconds. || The ambiguous word stays the same in each pair, and only the continuation changes. >> The extra cost appears when the continuation demands the category that does not match the form. >> Parsing uses learned statistics about how word forms sound, along with the structure the sentence finally requires.
29|**The early brain response to an unexpected noun appears only when the word itself carries a physical cue that it is a noun.** The **ELAN effect** for nouns where a verb is expected is limited to cases with **syntactic** or **statistical form-based** information **physically available**. >> Syntactic information means **morphemes** (the smallest meaningful parts of a word, such as a suffix) that indicate noun-ness. >> Statistical form-based information means **“nouniness”** (how much a word’s form resembles typical nouns, even without a noun suffix). || Three kinds of nouns were compared on the **M100** (a brain response peaking about 100 milliseconds after a word appears). >> **Bimorphemic nouns** were tested with “The beautiful princess …” and “The beautifully princess …”. Princess has a noun-marking suffix, and the unexpected version gave a significantly larger response. >> **Typical nouns** were tested with “The tasteless soda …” and “The tastelessly soda …”. Soda has a statistically noun-like form, and the unexpected version again gave a significantly larger response. >> **Neutral nouns** were tested with “The cute infant …” and “The cutely infant …”. Infant has neither cue, and the two versions did not differ. || **No effect** was observed for neutral nouns like “infant” that lack strong cues. >> An abstract category mismatch alone is not enough to produce this earliest visual response. >> An expected category generates predictions about what the next word will look like. >> The response appears only when the arriving word carries physical evidence against the prediction.
30|**Four sentences sum up sentence processing, and each one carries one idea.** “Colorless green ideas sleep furiously” carries the first idea, that **syntax is independent of semantics** to some extent. >> In the limit, a sentence can be grammatically correct but meaningless. >> Words can fill a grammatical structure without describing a sensible event. || “The horse raced past the barn fell” carries the second idea, that syntactic structure is often highly **ambiguous** (actually **vague**), but we do not notice except where things fail. >> Reading times for “preferred” and “unpreferred” structures suggest that syntactic processes **take shortcuts to save memory**. >> They do not activate every possible parse of a sentence. >> When the shortcut picks the wrong structure, the reader is led down the garden path. || “Put the apple on the towel in the box” carries the third idea, that **context, pragmatics and other factors** interact with syntactic processing from very early on. >> With two apples in view, listeners take “on the towel” as a description of the apple, not a destination. || “The bird perches were comfortable” carries the fourth idea, that the very earliest syntactic effects observable in the brain are driven in part by **form typicality**. >> They are not necessarily driven by **morphosyntactic** structures (grammatical structure signaled by word parts and word order). >> They seem to be related to **predictions about the physical forms of words**. >> Perches has a noun-like form, so its use as a noun here matches the expectation its form creates.
