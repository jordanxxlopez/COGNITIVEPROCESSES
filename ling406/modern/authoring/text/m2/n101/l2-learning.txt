2|**Adults learn a second language faster at first, but children more often end up native-like.** Babies are overrated as learners, so **sensitive periods** for a second language (**L2**) should not be taken too far. >> A typical child by 18 months uses at least 20 words. >> That child also understands "in and out" and "off and on" and points to body parts when asked. >> That child answers "Where's teddy?" or "What's that?" with words or gestures and gives teddy a pretend drink. >> That child makes at least four consonant sounds, such as b, n, d, g, w and h. That child enjoys being read to and points to pictures with one finger. >> An adult who knew only about 20 words after 18 months of instruction would be a very poor learner. || **Adults initially learn more quickly** than children, for two main reasons. >> **Transfer from L1** (using what they know from the first language) gives them a head start. >> **Better general cognitive skills** (memory, attention and reasoning) help too. || Adults do worse on **ultimate attainment** (the final level of skill a learner reaches). >> Adults rarely become native-like. >> That may also be due in part to transfer. >> Early speed and final success are separate measures, and children and adults rank differently on each.
3|**Many toddler language milestones are general conceptual abilities that adult learners already have.** So later learners mainly learn how the new language expresses old ideas. >> Understanding "in and out" and "off and on" rests on **spatial relations between objects**. >> **Pretend play**, such as giving teddy a drink, rests on **event representations** (mental models of who does what in an event). >> A toddler builds these ideas and the words at the same time. || Later learners of a second language have much more of this in place. >> An adult learning Spanish already has the concepts behind "in" and "on". >> The adult can focus on how these concepts are represented in the **target language** (the language being learned). || Later learners also already know another language, which affects how the second language is learned. >> The first language offers ready-made ways to express concepts. >> Some fit the second language and help. >> Others clash with it and cause errors.
4|**Transfer means using first-language habits for a second language, and it can help or hurt.** >> **Transfer** (the use of native language, or L1, processes and representations for a second language, or L2) covers sounds, sentences and words. >> The learner arrives with a complete working language system. || Transfer can have positive and negative impacts on learning and performance. >> **Positive transfer** happens when an L1 pattern matches the L2, so the learner gets a head start. >> **Negative transfer** happens when an L1 pattern conflicts with the L2, so the learner makes errors. || A cartoon gives an example of negative transfer in grammar. >> A man in a pub asks "Do you not be happy with me as the translator of the books of you?" >> The words are English. >> The word order and phrasing come from another language, carried over by word-by-word translation.
5|**English speakers hear two different Hindi consonants as one English sound, so transfer makes the Hindi contrast hard to learn.** >> **Categorical perception** leads to **acquired similarity** (sounds in one category come to sound more alike with experience). || **Minimal pairs in Hindi** (two words that differ in only one sound) reveal the problem. >> Two words sound like "tap" to English ears. One means hot, and one means a kind of beat. >> Two words sound like "dab". One means pressure, and one means a kind of fruit. >> Two words sound like "ari". One means saw (the tool), and one means crooked. >> Each pair has a **dental** consonant (tongue against the teeth) and a **retroflex** consonant (tongue curled back toward the roof of the mouth). || Native English speakers use their **alveolar** category (tongue on the ridge behind the teeth, as in /t/ or /d/) for both. >> English has only the alveolar category. >> Both Hindi sounds fall into it and seem identical. >> So the learner must separate sounds the first language taught them to treat as one.
6|**English speakers struggle with Chinese classifiers because English grammar taught them to ignore the cues classifiers depend on.** >> **Classifiers** are a somewhat unusual grammatical feature across languages. >> A **classifier** (a word between a number or demonstrative and a noun) depends on the kind of thing the noun names. >> The order is demonstrative pronoun, nominal classifier, noun, as in **yí gè rén** ("one person"). || Each Chinese classifier goes with a kind of noun. >> **Gè** is the general classifier. >> **Bǎ** goes with objects that have a handle. >> **Běn** goes with books and similar objects. >> **Kuài** goes with objects divided into pieces. >> **Liàng** goes with wheeled vehicles. >> **Tái** goes with devices and gadgets. >> **Xiē** marks an indeterminate amount, or the plural of pronouns and demonstratives. >> **Zhāng** goes with flat objects. >> **Zhī** goes with long, thin objects. || English does not care about noun meaning here. >> Saying "a chair", "that dog", "the car" or "one book" needs no attention to the noun's meaning. >> In Chinese, different nouns require different classifiers. >> So learners must notice shape and function again.
7|**Grammatical gender causes transfer problems because English speakers have no habit of tracking it, yet it controls agreement.** >> **Grammatical gender** (a system sorting nouns into classes such as masculine and feminine) is different from social or cultural gender. >> Nothing is "male" about a book or "female" about liberty, yet French has le livre and la liberté. >> Other masculine nouns are le cheval (the horse), le chien (the dog) and le bruit (the noise). >> Other feminine nouns are la colombe (the dove), la chemise (the shirt) and la maison (the house). || Gender matters for **agreement** (related words must match the noun). >> Pronouns, adjectives and some verb forms must be coordinated with noun gender. || French has synonyms with different genders. >> **Auto** and **voiture** both mean car. >> **Rivière** and **fleuve** both mean river. >> **Vélo** and **bicyclette** both mean bicycle. >> In each pair one is masculine and one is feminine, so gender is learned word by word. || Word endings give clues. >> Masculine endings include -age (le fromage), -r (le professeur), -t (le chat) and -isme (le capitalisme). >> Feminine endings include -ie (la boulangerie), -ion (la nation), -ité or -tié (la fraternité), -ance (la balance), and -nne, -mme or -lle (la fille, l’indienne).
8|**Transfer is not all bad, because similar words across languages make vocabulary easier to learn.** >> French **auto** resembles English "auto". >> French **rivière** resembles English "river". >> French **bicyclette** resembles English "bicycle". >> Japanese **コロッケ (korokke)** means croquette. >> Japanese **モノレール (monoreru)** means monorail. || The similarity has two sources. >> **Shared etymology** (a shared word history) gives **cognates**, such as rivière and river. >> More recent **borrowing** gives **loan words** (words taken from another language), such as korokke and monoreru. >> Loan words get their sounds adapted, as Japanese did with these European words. || Both give **positive transfer** for vocabulary. >> The learner already knows a similar form with a similar meaning. >> That familiar anchor makes the new word easier to store and recall.
9|**Not all similar-sounding words are cognates, and the pull to link them can cause embarrassing errors.** >> A **false friend** (a word that sounds like a word in another language but means something else) misleads learners. >> The bias to read L2 words as related to similar L1 words can be very strong. || "Arnold está embarazado" does not mean Arnold is embarrassed. >> Spanish **embarazado** means **pregnant**. >> An English speaker going by sound gets it badly wrong. || The link forms before the learner checks the meaning. >> When meanings match, the link speeds learning, as with cognates. >> When meanings differ, the link causes errors. >> A cognate and a false friend differ only in whether the meanings match.
10|**De Groot and Keijzer (2000) tested in the lab whether cognate status helps people learn new second-language words.** >> They used **paired-associate learning** (studying pairs, then recalling one member when given the other) with made-up words. >> Participants were Dutch speakers, so Dutch stood in for L1 and the made-up words for an L2. || The made-up words varied in two ways. >> **Similarity** to their Dutch partners made some cognate-like and some not. >> **Concreteness** made some concrete (something seen or touched) and some abstract (an idea). || Crossing the two gave four kinds of pairs. >> Gitaar and gettar ("guitar") is concrete and cognate. >> Tijger and duunze ("tiger") is concrete and non-cognate. >> Domein and domaan ("domain") is abstract and cognate. >> Herstel and groop ("repair") is abstract and non-cognate. || Participants learned the pairs. >> They were simply shown each Dutch word with its made-up partner. >> They were tested on **retention** a week later.
11|**Cognates were learned faster and remembered better, so cognate status helps from the very first stage of second-language word learning.** De Groot and Keijzer (2000) tested recall in three training sessions, then in session 4 after a week without training. >> Concrete cognates went from 69.7% to 93.4% recall by session 3. >> Concrete non-cognates went from 43.7% to 92.0%. >> Abstract cognates went from 42.7% to 86.0%. >> Abstract non-cognates went from 19.0% to 72.7%. || After the week, the kinds of words separated again. >> Concrete cognates dropped to 78.4%. >> Concrete non-cognates dropped to 69.0%. >> Abstract cognates dropped to 68.4%. >> Abstract non-cognates fell sharply to 36.0%. >> Responses were faster for cognates in every session. || **Concreteness** also had a large effect. >> The two factors add together, so abstract non-cognates were hardest. >> **Context availability** (concrete words are easier to link with a supporting context) is one explanation.
12|**The experiment is convincing because it tested productive vocabulary, not just receptive vocabulary.** >> **Receptive** testing gives the made-up word and asks for the Dutch word. >> **Expressive** testing, which De Groot and Keijzer call **productive** testing, gives the Dutch word and asks for the made-up word. || A receptive test alone would not be very impressive. >> A cognate-like word gives away its Dutch partner. >> Gitaar, tijger and domein could be guessed without Dutch training, since they resemble guitar, tiger and domain. || The concrete cognates are very close to the Dutch words. >> Broer and breur, brood and brodde, gitaar and gettar, jongen and jange, lepel and lippel. >> Lichaam and lechem, naald and nadel, paraplu and parredu, rivier and riffer, schaar and skare. >> Stier and stire, trein and trinen, varken and vark, viool and vialo, vrouw and vrauk. || Productive testing removes the shortcut. >> The learner must recall the exact new form, such as gettar. >> A cognate advantage there means real learning.
13|**Cognates and concrete words were forgotten less over the week in both test directions, so participants truly learned the new forms.** >> Recall was measured right after learning (session 3) and a week later (session 4). >> The drop over the week is the measure that matters. || In productive testing, learners produced the new word. >> Concrete words fell from 92.7% to 73.7%, a 19.0 point drop. >> Abstract words fell from 79.3% to 52.2%, a 27.1 point drop. >> Cognates fell from 89.7% to 73.3%, a 16.4 point drop. >> Non-cognates fell from 82.3% to 52.5%, a 29.8 point drop. || In receptive testing, learners gave the Dutch word. >> Concrete words fell from 97.7% to 88.9%, an 8.8 point drop. >> Abstract words fell from 91.5% to 64.9%, a 26.6 point drop. >> Cognates fell from 98.5% to 90.7%, a 7.8 point drop. >> Non-cognates fell from 90.7% to 63.0%, a 27.7 point drop. || Receptive scores were higher, because recognizing is easier than producing. >> The cognate advantage held in productive testing too. >> Similarity to a known word helped build a lasting memory.
14|**Researchers ask whether similar first-language words always become active while a person processes a second language.** There have been many, many studies of this. >> An L2 word might also activate L1 words that sound like it. >> That could happen automatically, even when only the L2 is being used. >> If it does, the two languages are never fully separate in the mind. || **Marian and Spivey (2003)** gave one clear example with a **visual world experiment**. In this kind of experiment, people hear spoken instructions while viewing objects, and their eye movements are recorded. >> Looks toward an object whose L1 name sounds like the spoken L2 word would mean the L1 word was active. >> Eye movements reveal this without asking for a report. >> The person still chooses the correct object.
15|**Eye movements reveal which words compete during recognition, even when the final choice is correct.** The same method was used earlier to study categorical perception. >> **McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)** studied gradient effects of within-category **VOT** (voice onset time) on **lexical access** (finding a word in memory). >> Participants saw a lamp, a ship, a bear and a pear. >> They heard a word from a continuum between "bear" and "pear". >> Bear and pear differ only in the first consonant, and the lamp and ship are fillers. || Looks to a competitor reveal what participants are considering. >> A participant can click the bear after glancing at the pear. >> Those glances mean "pear" was active. || Competition between BEAR and PEAR depended on **distance from the category boundary**. >> Sounds near the /b/ and /p/ boundary drew more looks to the competitor. >> Sounds far from it drew fewer. >> The same method can test competition across two languages.
16|**Allopenna and colleagues (1994) found the cohort effect, where words sharing a target's beginning compete early in recognition.** >> A **cohort** is the set of words that share the same beginning sounds. >> Participants saw a grid of pictures and shapes and followed spoken instructions. >> The display had a beaker (target), a beetle (same beginning), a speaker (rhyme) and a carriage (unrelated). || Looks were tracked over the first 1,000 milliseconds after the word began. >> Looks to the beaker and beetle rose together early. >> Looks to the beetle peaked near 0.2 around 500 milliseconds, then fell once the words diverged. >> Looks to the beaker kept rising toward 1 by about 1,000 milliseconds. >> Looks to the speaker rose later and peaked near 0.1, because its overlap comes at the end. >> Looks to the carriage stayed near zero. || Words compete as soon as they match the incoming sounds. >> They drop out when the input stops matching. >> Eye movements track this moment by moment.
17|**Marian and Spivey tested whether a Russian word competes with an English target when the two words only begin with the same sounds.** >> A **cross-language competitor** is an object whose name in the other language begins like the target. >> They hypothesized that if L1 words are always activated, Russian speakers hearing English "speaker" should look at matches. >> English **speaker** sounds like Russian **спички (spichki)**, meaning matches. || Each English target had an English and a Russian competitor. >> Speaker had spear and spichki (matches). >> Boot had book and buben (tambourine). >> Shark had shovel and sharik (balloon). >> Chair had chess set and cherepaha (turtle). >> Marker had marbles and marka (stamp). >> Barbed wire had bark and barhat (velvet). >> Plug had plum and platye (dress). >> Gun had gum and gaika (nut). >> Lock had lobster and lak dlea nogtei (nail polish). || For Russian targets the roles were reversed. >> Spichki had English spear and Russian spitsi (knitting needles). >> Marka had English marker and Russian marlya (cheese cloth). >> Platye had English plum and Russian plashch (raincoat). || The tie is only sound, since speakers and matches share no meaning. >> So looks to the matches measure activation of spichki.
18|**While listening in their second language, bilinguals looked at objects with similar first-language names, so the first language stays active.** Russian and English bilinguals followed instructions in English. >> The **between-language competitor** had a similar Russian name. >> The **within-language competitor** had a similar English name. || The measure was the percent of trials with a look to each object. >> Between-language competitor alone drew looks on 18% of trials. >> Within-language competitor alone drew looks on 18% of trials. >> With both present, the between-language one drew 13% and the within-language one 19%. >> A control filler with no competitor drew only 7%. || With one competitor, both kinds drew looks to the same extent. >> The Russian name pulled the eyes as strongly as an English one. || With both present, the within-language competitor drew more looks. >> The between-language competitor still drew about twice as many as the control. >> Bilinguals still chose correctly, but L1 words competed along the way.
19|**While listening in their first language, Russian speakers did not reliably activate English words, so the effect runs one way.** The same bilinguals followed instructions in Russian. >> The between-language competitor now had a similar English name. || The measure was again the percent of trials with a look. >> Between-language competitor alone drew 8%. >> Within-language competitor alone drew 14%. >> With both present, the between-language one drew 8% and the within-language one 13%. >> The control filler drew 5%. || The small difference between the between-language and control conditions is **not statistically significant** (within chance variation). >> So English words do not seem to be activated in the Russian task. >> The within-language competitor still produced a cohort effect in Russian. || The result is **asymmetric**. >> Russian words became active during English tasks. >> English words did not become reliably active during Russian tasks. >> This fits the idea that the first language is more firmly established and more easily activated.
20|**In the Revised Hierarchical Model, activation spreads more easily from second-language words to first-language words, because learning emphasizes that direction.** Kroll and Stewart built the model on **spreading activation** (activating one representation partly activates those linked to it). >> The model has an L1 vocabulary, an L2 vocabulary and a store of **concepts** (meanings). >> **Lexical links** join L1 and L2 words directly. >> **Conceptual links** join each vocabulary to the concepts. || The L1 lexicon is assumed to be larger. >> L1 words are strongly connected to relevant concepts. >> L2 words are more weakly connected to concepts. || The L2 lexicon has strong forward connections to the L1 lexicon. >> The link from L2 to L1 is strong. >> The link from L1 to L2 is weak. || Learning builds this pattern. >> Learners usually attach each new L2 word to its L1 translation. >> The direct link from L2 words to concepts stays weaker, at least early on.
21|**The Revised Hierarchical Model explains why Russian competitors drew looks in the English task but English competitors did not in the Russian task.** Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994) proposed the model. || In the L2 task with the target "speaker", activation spreads in steps. >> The concept for "matches" partially activates the Russian word spichki, since L1 words are tightly linked to concepts. >> The sounds of speaker also partly activate spichki, since the two begin alike. >> This activity feeds back to the concept for "matches". >> Conceptual activation for "matches" leads to eye movements toward the L1 competitor. || Speaker and spichki share only **sound overlap**. >> Speakers and matches share no meaning. >> The boost to spichki strengthens the concept of matches and pulls the eyes there. || In the L1 task, the conceptual links with L2 might be too weak to create this interference reliably. >> An English competitor would need a strong link from its English word to its concept. >> L2 words have only weak conceptual links. >> So the effect appears in one direction only.
22|**The model predicts that balanced bilinguals are not two monolinguals in one, because both languages compete all the time.** >> A **balanced bilingual** (someone about equally skilled in both languages) has similarly strong conceptual links for both languages. >> This prediction is counter-intuitive. >> One might expect each language to run separately. || Strong links in both languages keep both vocabularies active. >> Each concept strongly activates its word in both languages. >> **Competitive dynamics** (words of the two languages competing for selection) are engaged all the time. >> **Inhibition** (suppressing the word from the language not in use) is engaged all the time too. || The prediction led to a lot of work on bilingual advantages in non-linguistic **cognitive control** tasks. >> Cognitive control is the ability to manage attention and suppress irrelevant responses. >> Constant practice choosing one language might improve attention control in general.
23|**Stopping use of the first language after learning a second separates the effect of early learning from the effect of continued use.** >> The usual sequence is to begin learning L1. >> Then the person learns L2. >> The person continues learning and using L1, so both stay in use. || In the other sequence, L1 use stops after L2 arrives. >> The person begins L1 and later learns L2. >> The person does not continue using L1. >> L2 takes over. || The comparison has two possible outcomes. >> If early learning leaves a permanent foundation, traces of L1 should remain without use. >> If continued use is needed, L1 might fade or disappear.
24|**Pallier and colleagues tested Korean adoptees in France to see whether a second language can replace the first.** The authors were Pallier, Dehaene, Poline, LeBihan, Argenti, Dupoux and Mehler, using brain imaging of language plasticity in adopted adults. >> They tested adults aged 20 to 32. >> These adults were adopted into French homes from Korean orphanages between ages 3 and 8. >> They learned Korean as young children, then heard only French at home. >> They stopped using Korean entirely, so they are a natural case of L1 use stopping. || The test looked for **residual knowledge** (any remaining trace) of Korean. >> Monolingual French speakers served as the comparison group. >> If adoptees beat them on Korean tasks, some Korean survived. >> Any survival would reveal what early learning leaves behind.
25|**Korean has three kinds of stop at each place in the mouth, which makes a good test of retained Korean.** >> A **stop** is a consonant made by fully blocking airflow and releasing it, such as /p/, /t/ or /k/. >> English and French have two kinds at each place, such as /b/ and /p/. >> Korean has **aspirated** (strong puff of air), **plain** (lighter release) and **tense** (stiffened throat) stops. || The UCLA Phonetics Archive has three series. >> The **bilabial** series (lips) is pʰul ("grass"), pul ("fire") and p’ul ("horn"). >> The **dental** series (tongue at teeth) is tʰal ("mask"), tal ("moon") and t’al ("daughter"). >> The **velar** series (back of tongue) is kʰo ("nose"), kok ("music") and k’ok ("exactly"). || Each series shares a place and differs only in stop type. >> French speakers tend to hear the three sounds as two. >> Korean speakers hear three. >> Adoptees who kept Korean sound knowledge should hear the differences better than French speakers.
26|**Ventureyra and colleagues found that Korean adoptees heard Korean stop contrasts no better than French speakers who had never learned Korean.** >> Listeners heard pairs of sounds and judged whether they were different. >> Adoptees, French native speakers and Korean native speakers were compared. || On the Korean contrasts, only Korean speakers usually said "different". >> Plain and tense p and k drew about 96% from Koreans, 8% from adoptees and 3% from French speakers. >> Plain and aspirated p and k drew about 90%, 19% and 26%. >> Tense and aspirated p and k drew about 95%, 23% and 20%. >> Plain and tense s drew about 79%, 26% and 23%. || Two controls checked that everyone could do the task. >> A vowel contrast drew "different" on about 97 to 99% for all groups. >> Identical pairs drew "different" only about 3 to 13% of the time. || So the difficulty was specific to the Korean stops. >> Early Korean experience left no measurable advantage.
27|**Pallier and colleagues looked for any remaining trace of Korean in the adoptees with unusual tasks, and found none.** In a more extensive study, adoptees were no better than monolingual French speakers on three tasks. >> These are not standard psycholinguistic tasks. >> They were designed to catch even weak knowledge. || Distinguishing Korean from largely unrelated languages tested familiarity with its sound. >> Both groups rated Korean sentences around 4 on a 1 to 7 scale. >> Japanese got around 4 to 5, Polish about 2, and Swedish and Wolof about 2.5 to 3. >> Adoptees did not pick out Korean better. || Identifying the correct Korean translation of French words tested vocabulary. >> Adoptees scored about 55% and French speakers about 50%, both near chance. || Deciding whether a short excerpt was repeated from a sentence just heard tested sound sequences. >> Both groups scored about 95% on French. >> Both scored about 80% on Korean, Japanese and Polish. >> Adoptees had no Korean advantage.
28|**Brain imaging found that the adoptees' brains treated Korean as a foreign language, just as French speakers' brains did.** >> Listening to French activated the **classical language network** in both groups. >> That network includes **superior temporal** regions (upper temporal lobe, speech sounds). >> It also includes **inferior frontal** regions (lower frontal lobe, including Broca's area). || Pallier and colleagues subtracted activity for another language from activity for French. >> They computed French minus Polish, French minus Japanese and French minus Korean. >> Each was computed for the French group and the Korean group (adoptees). >> French always produced more language-network activity. || French minus Korean could have differed between groups if residual Korean processing remained. >> Adoptees still processing Korean would show a smaller difference. >> No such difference appeared. >> The imaging agrees with the behavioral tests.
29|**An unused first language could lose its brain connections to the second language through competition.** L2 and L1 interactions can be seen as **competitive dynamics**. >> Imagine L1 as a set of connections within and between brain regions. >> Use builds and maintains these connections. || **Monocular deprivation** (depriving one eye of input) works this way in mice. >> After 2 to 3 days, the deprived eye's connections show **increased spine motility**. >> After 3 to 4 days, there is **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration** (breakdown of cut-off nerve fibers). >> After more than a week, the other eye shows **axon sprouting and new spine birth** and takes over the space. || If L1 connections are never activated again, L2 could take them over. >> L2 is used constantly, so its connections strengthen. >> L1 is unused, so its connections weaken. >> L2 replaces L1 by winning the same neural resources.
30|**A second language can apparently replace the first, but traces of the first might still be salvaged.** >> The adoptees showed no behavioral or brain sign of their childhood Korean. >> As far as these tests could tell, French had replaced Korean. || The open question is whether traces of L1 are left behind. >> **Salvaging** a trace means recovering or using a leftover effect of the earlier language. >> A trace might not show on a test of current ability. >> It could still make relearning easier. || The two kinds of test ask different questions. >> A recognition test asks what the person can do now. >> A relearning test asks whether new learning benefits from something left over. >> Relearning is the measure that could reveal a trace.
31|**Students who overheard Spanish as children later pronounced it much more like native speakers, but their grammar was no better.** Terry Au and colleagues at UCLA (Au, Knightly, Jun and Oh, "Overhearing a language during childhood") studied undergraduates who overheard Spanish often but never spoke it. >> All started classroom Spanish around age 14. >> All were tested at the end of second-year college Spanish. >> They were compared with native speakers and typical late learners. || Values are given as natives, overhearers and late learners. >> **Voice onset time** (delay before voicing starts) for word-initial voiceless stops was 19.3, 19.6 and 36.2 milliseconds. >> Word-medial voiceless stops were 22.4, 23.4 and 31.2. >> **Lenition** (softening of voiced stops between vowels) was 43%, 24% and 1% word-initially. >> It was 72%, 63% and 23% word-medially. >> Accent ratings out of 5 were 4.3, 3.5 and 2.2 for voiceless stops, and 4.4, 3.3 and 2.7 for voiced. >> Childhood slang production was 79%, 17% and 3%, and comprehension was 88%, 22% and 3%. || Grammar showed little advantage. >> Gender agreement was 93%, 70% and 68%. >> Number agreement was 92%, 88% and 85%. >> Grammaticality judgment accuracy was 94%, 64% and 63%. >> Reaction times were 1,042, 2,475 and 2,209 milliseconds.
32|**Removing a first language can make it seem fully replaced, yet some knowledge may survive and resurface with later learning.** >> The adoptee and overhearer data are taken together. >> If L1 is removed from the environment entirely, L2 can appear to completely replace it. >> The adoptees showed no Korean on tests of current ability. || The replacement is not necessarily complete. >> **Residual knowledge** (a leftover trace) may be "unmasked" with later learning. >> The overhearers' pronunciation is the example. || The two groups also differ in important ways. >> The adoptees had no new Korean instruction. >> The overhearers had studied Spanish through a second-year college course, giving hidden knowledge a chance to surface.
33|**The adoptees and overhearers differ in many ways besides early exposure, so their results are hard to compare.** L2 can appear to replace L1, but residual knowledge may be unmasked later. >> Korean is rarely heard in France, but Spanish is common in Los Angeles. >> A **heritage language** (spoken by family members) differs from a language nobody you know speaks. >> **Cognates** are common between Spanish and English but rare between Korean and French. >> A **shared writing system** links Spanish and English, while Korean uses a different script from French. || These differences could explain the different outcomes. >> Overhearers may have kept hearing Spanish, often from relatives. >> Adoptees lost all contact with Korean speakers. || This is a general issue for research on second language acquisition and bilingualism. >> Languages and learning situations differ in many, many ways. >> Those ways are hard, perhaps impossible, to control experimentally. >> Researchers cannot assign people to grow up with particular languages.
