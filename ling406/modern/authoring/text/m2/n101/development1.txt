2|**Nativists say children acquire grammar because they are born with knowledge of language, not because of reward and punishment.** This position grew out of Chomsky's critique of Skinner. The debate concerns **nature and nurture in child language** (how much of language is built in and how much comes from experience). >> **Skinner** was a behaviorist who explained behavior, including language, with general learning mechanisms such as reward and punishment. >> **Chomsky** argued that these mechanisms could not explain how children acquire grammar. || For Chomsky, the important feature of language is its **generativity** (the ability to produce and understand an unlimited number of new sentences). >> Acquiring a language means **identifying the abstract grammar** that produces the sentences of the language. || General learning mechanisms were **not up to this task**. >> Grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are **not "similar"** in any straightforward way. >> Acquisition does not look like **operant conditioning** (shaping behavior with rewards and punishments). Children are not reliably rewarded for grammatical sentences, so the **contingencies do not hold**. || Nativist approaches assert two things instead. >> Children have innate knowledge of what language is like, called **Universal Grammar**. >> Children use **language-specific acquisition processes** (learning mechanisms built only for language). || Chomsky argued for this position by writing, "The fact that all normal children acquire essentially comparable grammars of great complexity with remarkable rapidity suggests that human beings are somehow specially designed to do this, with data-handling or 'hypothesis-formulating' ability of unknown character and complexity." >> Every typical child reaches a complex grammar quickly, despite very different experiences.
3|**Categorical perception first looked like something only humans do, and only for speech.** **Categorical perception** (hearing differences between speech sounds in terms of categories) was treated by early theories as language-specific and species-specific. >> **Language-specific** means it was thought to occur only for language. >> **Species-specific** means it was thought to occur only in humans. >> At the time, only humans were known to show it, and only for speech sounds. || The classic evidence is a synthetic consonant continuum of fourteen stimuli. >> The upper formant transitions change in equal steps. The lower formant stays the same. >> **Labeling** switches sharply from **/b/** to **/d/** around stimuli 4 and 5. >> Labeling switches from /d/ to **/g/** around stimuli 9 and 10. >> **Discrimination** accuracy peaks near those boundaries and falls inside each category, for one-step, two-step and three-step pairs. || Similarity between stimuli is defined in **linguistic, rather than physical, terms**. >> Two sounds in the same category can sound almost identical. >> Two sounds on opposite sides of a boundary can sound clearly different. >> The acoustic difference can be the same size in both cases. >> These patterns were taken as signs of a specialized speech system.
4|**Infants cannot say what they hear, so researchers measure head turns and sucking instead.** Both behaviors change when an infant notices something new. >> Neither method asks the infant to name or produce a sound. >> The experimenter controls exactly which acoustic difference is presented. || The **conditioned head turn procedure** (training an infant to turn toward a sound when it changes) is used in Janet Werker's infant studies lab. >> The infant sits on a parent's lap. >> An experimenter keeps the infant's attention forward with toys. >> A correct head turn after a change earns a visual reward, such as a lighted, moving toy. >> After the infant learns this link, a head turn means the infant **heard the change**. || **High-amplitude sucking** relies on the infant's natural interest in something new. >> The infant sucks on a pacifier connected to a pressure recorder. >> Strong sucks make a sound play. >> As one sound repeats, sucking slows because of **habituation** (getting used to a repeated sound). >> If a new sound makes sucking **increase again**, the infant **noticed the change**. || Both methods test whether infants hear the same distinctions that adults hear. >> The head turn relies on a learned link between a sound change and a reward. >> Sucking relies on a natural response to something new.
5|**Four-month-old infants notice a change in voice onset time only when it crosses an adult category boundary.** **Eimas and colleagues (1971)** found this with high-amplitude sucking. >> The sounds varied in **voice onset time**, or VOT (the delay between the release of a stop consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration). >> With a VOT of +100 milliseconds, voicing starts much later after the release than with +10 milliseconds. >> Infants heard one syllable repeatedly until sucking declined. Then the sound changed or stayed the same. || Three conditions were compared. >> **20D** had sounds 20 milliseconds apart, +20 versus +40, from **different** adult phonemic categories. >> **20S** had sounds 20 milliseconds apart, −20 versus 0 or +60 versus +80, from the **same** category. >> **0** was the **control**, with the same sound throughout. || Sucking rose above baseline and then declined before the change in every condition. >> In 20D, sucking **jumped back up**, to around 50 responses per minute. >> In 20S, sucking showed **little recovery** and kept falling. >> In the control, sucking kept declining, to about 15 responses per minute. || The 20D and 20S changes were the same acoustic size. >> So the difference comes from **crossing the category boundary**. >> The control establishes what repetition alone does.
6|**Categorical perception at four months seemed like evidence for innateness because it appears so early.** Eimas's result was a big deal for that reason. >> Categorical perception was considered the first step in turning speech into a **symbolic format** (one that represents a sound as a category, such as /b/). >> Later linguistic processes could then use that format. >> Finding it in infants suggested the starting point for language processing was already in place. || Four-month-olds are **really little**. >> Gross motor milestones (large body movements) at that age include manipulating objects with two hands. >> They also include holding the head and chest up while prone (lying face down). >> Color vision and general **visual acuity** (sharpness of vision) are still "in progress." >> They are just starting to produce **vowel-like sounds** instead of creaky whines. || This is very, very early for an apparently **language-specific** ability to emerge. >> Infants categorize speech sounds before they can produce them. >> Their other abilities are still immature at that age. >> So the ability seemed built in, not learned through long experience.
7|**English-learning infants can hear non-English contrasts at six months and mostly lose them by twelve months.** This decline is **perceptual narrowing** (losing sensitivity to sound distinctions the child's language does not use). >> At around six months, English-learning infants discriminate speech contrasts they have never heard before. >> As development goes on, the ability to perceive these contrasts declines. || **Werker and Tees (1984)** tested two non-English contrasts. >> The **Hindi contrast** is between two "ta"-like sounds. >> The **Salish contrast** is between "ki" and "qi" from Nthlakampx, a Salish language. >> The measure was the percentage of infants reaching a **discrimination criterion**. || The percentage dropped with age. >> At 6 to 8 months, about **95%** reached it for Hindi and about **80%** for Salish. >> At 8 to 10 months, about 67% reached it for Hindi and about 52% for Salish. >> At 10 to 12 months, about **20%** reached it for Hindi and about **10%** for Salish. >> Hindi-learning and Salish-learning infants reached it close to 100% of the time. || This is **perceptual reorganization** during the first year of life. >> Infants who hear a contrast keep it, so the decline depends on language experience. >> Narrowing is related to categorical perception because native categories form. Sounds the language groups together become harder to tell apart. >> A nonnative distinction inside one native category is then heard as a **within-category difference**.
8|**The compelling narrative says infants are born able to hear every contrast, and learning only removes the ones they do not need.** It combines the infant findings into one account. >> Speech perception depends on **categorical perception abilities** that are a special property of the language system. >> These abilities are present very early in development. || Children start off as **"citizens of the world"** (able to hear all contrasts). >> This means they have innate, **language-general** knowledge about speech (knowledge that applies to the speech sounds of every language). >> A newborn is ready to learn any human language. >> Children then **rapidly tune** their perceptual systems to the specific language in their environment. || The role of learning is to **select** among the many possible innately specified phonetic contrasts. >> Learning narrows the **inventory** (the full set of phonetic contrasts the child can perceive). >> Learning removes the contrasts the native language does not use. >> Learning keeps the contrasts that are meaningful in the native language. >> The native language decides which innate categories survive.
9|**The citizens-of-the-world view has four problems, three about categorical perception and one about infants.** >> Categorical perception is **not limited to language**. It also occurs for nonspeech sounds. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to humans**. Animals without language also perceive speech sounds categorically. >> Categorical perception is **task-dependent** (results look more or less categorical depending on the task). The task could be naming a sound or judging whether two sounds differ. >> Infant speech perception is **not actually "universal."** || Infants do not start out able to hear every contrast equally well. >> Some contrasts are hard for young infants to detect. >> This is true even for contrasts in their own language. || Each problem targets a different part of the narrative. >> Nonspeech and animal findings question whether categorical perception is special to language. >> Task effects question how categorical perception really is. >> Uneven infant sensitivity questions whether all contrasts are innately available. >> A full account needs learning that **builds categories**, not only learning that removes them.
10|**Listeners perceive non-speech sounds categorically too, so categorical perception does not require a speech mechanism.** **Cutting and Rosner (1974)** varied **rise time** (how quickly a sound grows to full loudness after it starts). >> A **short rise time** sounds like a **plucked** string. >> A **long rise time** sounds like a **bowed** string. || The stimuli were sawtooth waves, speech syllables and sine waves. >> Each was made with a 10-millisecond and a 70-millisecond rise time. >> A 10-millisecond rise reaches full size almost immediately. >> A 70-millisecond rise grows gradually. || The sawtooth results were categorical. >> Pluck responses were near 100% at short rise times. >> Bow responses were near 100% at long rise times. >> The labels switched steeply between about **30 and 50 milliseconds**. >> Discrimination of pairs 10 milliseconds apart was near 55 to 60% for most pairs. >> Discrimination **peaked near 75%** for pairs around 30 to 50 milliseconds, where the labels switch. || Pluck and bow are labels for kinds of sound onset, not speech categories. >> A label boundary and a discrimination peak appear together without phonemes. >> Categorical perception can arise in **general auditory processing**.
11|**Infants also perceive non-speech sounds categorically, so their early categorical perception is a general auditory ability.** **Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** tested this with high-amplitude sucking. >> Infants heard one sound repeatedly until sucking declined. >> Then the sound was changed. || Three conditions were compared, **no change**, a change **within categories** and a change **between categories**. >> Before the change, sucking fell from near 20 per minute to about 12 in all three. >> After no change, sucking stayed near 12. >> After a within-category change, sucking stayed near 12. >> After a between-category change, sucking **jumped back up** to about 18 and then declined again. || Infants responded more to a change that crossed a category boundary. >> Eimas and colleagues found the same pattern for speech. >> Adults show categorical labeling and discrimination for plucked and bowed sounds. >> Infants show categorical discrimination for nonspeech changes. >> Neither group needs language knowledge to show the pattern.
12|**Chinchillas sort speech sounds into categories the way humans do, so categorical perception of speech is not limited to humans.** The evidence is from **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and earlier from **Kuhl and Miller (1975)**. >> The animals were trained with **operant conditioning** (a behavior becomes more likely when a reward follows it). >> Each learned that the 0-millisecond lag or the 80-millisecond lag stimulus was an **SD** for food. >> SD means **discriminative stimulus** (a signal that a response will be rewarded). >> The lag is the **voice onset time**. Humans hear a 0-millisecond lag as /ga/ and an 80-millisecond lag as /ka/. || After training, the animals heard **intermediate VOTs** along the /ga/-/ka/ continuum. >> Human listeners heard the same sounds. >> At short VOTs, the proportion labeled /ga/ stayed near 1. >> At long VOTs, the proportion labeled /ga/ fell to near 0. >> The fall was steep, mostly between about 30 and 70 milliseconds. >> Chinchilla labeling had the **same steep, categorical shape** as human labeling. || Increasing the intensity of the **burst** and **aspiration** noise changed labeling. >> The burst is the brief noise at a stop release. Aspiration is the breathy noise after a voiceless release. >> The boundary shifted to a **shorter VOT**, the switch became steeper, or both. >> This happened for one chinchilla and one human listener. >> Both species responded to these acoustic properties in a similar way.
13|**Four-month-olds do not readily hear the /na/-/ŋa/ contrast, so some contrasts are less salient than others.** **Narayan and colleagues (2010)** compared it with /ma/-/na/, which is relatively **salient** (easy to notice). >> Both contrasts use **nasal consonants** in the **onset** (the start of a syllable). >> The consonants differ in **place of articulation** (where in the mouth the sound is made). >> /m/ is made with the lips. >> /n/ is made with the tongue behind the teeth. >> /ŋ/, the final sound in "sing," is made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate. || Looking time was compared on **same** trials (the syllable did not change) and **change** trials (the syllable switched). >> Longer looking on change trials means the infant noticed the change. >> English-hearing infants at 4 to 5 months were tested. >> For native **[ma]-[na]**, they looked about 3.3 seconds on same trials and about 5.4 seconds on change trials. >> For nonnative **[na]-[ŋa]**, they looked about 4.5 seconds on same trials and about 4.2 seconds on change trials. || The /na/-/ŋa/ contrast is not readily perceived by four-month-olds, **independent of language experience**. >> The difference lies in the sounds themselves, not in experience. >> Some contrasts are hard to hear from the beginning. >> So not every contrast starts out equally available.
14|**Hearing the /na/-/ŋa/ contrast in Filipino makes infants better at it, so not all contrasts are available innately.** **Narayan and colleagues (2010)** found the change between 6 and 10 months. >> In Filipino, [na] and [ŋa] are different syllables. >> Filipino-learning infants hear the contrast regularly. || Exposure in a Filipino language environment **increases the salience** of the contrast. >> At 6 to 8 months, Filipino-learning infants looked about 3.8 seconds on both same and change trials. >> At 10 to 12 months, they looked about 3.3 seconds on same trials and about 4.8 seconds on change trials. >> English-hearing infants at 4 to 5 months did not respond to this contrast. >> Those English-hearing infants did respond to [ma]-[na]. || Exposure to contrasts in the environment is **necessary for acquisition** in some cases. >> The contrast is weak at the start, even for infants whose language uses it. >> Experience improves sensitivity over the first year. >> Learning can strengthen a contrast as well as preserve it. >> Development builds sensitivity to hard contrasts and does not only remove unused ones.
15|**Emergentist approaches say language is learned by general associative learning that picks up statistical patterns in the input.** This is the alternative to the nativist approach. >> **Associative learning** (learning that two things go together because they repeatedly occur together) is the mechanism. >> **Statistical regularities** (patterns in how often things occur and which things occur together) are what it is sensitive to. >> Language structure emerges from learning these patterns in rich input. || Classical conditioning is the simplest case. >> There are three neurons, one for the **bell**, one that detects **food** and one for **salivation**. >> The food detector already connects strongly to salivation, so food produces salivation. >> The bell repeatedly occurs with food. >> The **synapse** (a connection between neurons) from the bell neuron to the salivation neuron is **strengthened**. >> After enough pairings, the bell alone produces salivation. || The same mechanism can extend to **pattern recognition** (identifying a recurring structure, such as a speech sound, across many varied examples). >> Strengthening could link acoustic features that tend to occur together. >> Repeated co-occurrence could build categories that were never specified in advance. >> In conditioning, one event predicts another. In pattern recognition, many features predict a category. >> Infants would learn categories by tracking which sounds occur together and how often.
16|**We may learn each vowel by tracking the distribution of its sounds, with the prototype at the center.** When **Kuhl and others** talk about learning the statistics of distributions in the environment, the American vowels are what they mean. >> A **distribution** describes how often each value occurs across many examples. >> The **vowel space** is determined by the first and second **formants**. || Each vowel occupies a region of the vowel space. >> Each production of a vowel has its own first and second formant values. >> Many productions of one vowel cluster together. >> The regions of different vowels **overlap** heavily. >> One pair of formant values can belong to more than one vowel, so speech categorization is difficult. >> Listeners must learn where each vowel is centered and how far it spreads. || Maybe we learn vowels by **extracting the statistics** of these distributions. >> Over many exposures, the listener tracks where each vowel's productions cluster. >> The categories reflect the shape of the input. || A **"prototypical" /a/** is the **mean** (the average value) or perhaps the **mode** (the most frequent value) of all the /a/ sounds we hear. >> The prototype comes from experience. >> It can differ across languages and listeners.
17|**Infants who heard a two-peaked distribution of sounds treated two test sounds as different, and infants who heard one peak did not.** **Maye and colleagues (2002)** had infants **listen passively** (with no task or reward) to a **[da]-[ta] continuum**. >> The continuum had eight stimuli that differed in **voice onset time**. >> The study asked whether infants really learn categories from distributions. || Two groups heard different distributions. >> The **bimodal** group heard stimuli 2 and 7 most often, 16 times each, and the middle rarely. Two peaks are evidence for **two categories**. >> The **unimodal** group heard stimuli 4 and 5 most often and the ends rarely. One peak is evidence for a **single category**. || At test, infants heard **stimuli 3 and 6**. >> In **alternating** trials, the two stimuli switched back and forth. >> In **non-alternating** trials, one stimulus repeated. >> If 3 and 6 sound different to infants, the two kinds of trials should hold attention differently. || Only the **bimodal** infants were sensitive to the difference between trial types. >> Bimodal six-month-olds looked 5.66 seconds on alternating and 6.41 seconds on non-alternating trials. >> Bimodal eight-month-olds looked 5.45 and 6.15 seconds. >> Unimodal six-month-olds looked 4.85 and 4.53 seconds. >> Unimodal eight-month-olds looked 4.98 and 5.20 seconds. >> The unimodal infants acted as if stimuli 3 and 6 were **members of the same category**. >> A few minutes of exposure changed how infants grouped the sounds.
18|**Infants learn speech sounds better from live, responsive people than from recordings.** **Social interaction** is important for learning. >> Nine-month-old American infants were exposed to **Mandarin Chinese** in twelve 25-minute sessions. >> The **live** group had a Mandarin speaker playing and reading with them in person. >> The **auditory or audiovisual** groups heard or watched Mandarin on audio or television. >> A control group had English exposure. >> All were tested on a Mandarin contrast with the **head-turn technique**. || Only live exposure produced learning. >> Live-exposure infants scored about **65% correct**. English controls scored about 56%. >> TV and audio infants scored about the same as controls, close to chance. || The second experiment manipulated **social responses** to eight-month-olds' vocalizations. >> In the **contingent** condition, mothers responded right after the infant vocalized. >> In the **yoked-control** condition, mothers responded on a schedule copied from another infant, so responses were **noncontingent**. >> Familiarization lasted 30 minutes, then baseline, social response and **extinction** (responses stopped) lasted 10 minutes each. || Contingent feedback **increased the quantity and complexity** of vocalizations. >> At baseline, both groups produced few syllables. >> During social response, the contingent group reached about **0.32** and the noncontingent group stayed near 0.05. >> During extinction, the contingent group was about 0.2 and the noncontingent group about 0.1. || Contingent social interaction seems to work like a **reinforcing stimulus** (something that makes a behavior more likely when it follows it). >> One idea is that humans find social interaction innately reinforcing. >> Another idea is that we are innately good at using **social cues** to learn from other people.
19|**Statistical learning is guided by constraints, and constraints means at least three different things.** **Statistical learning with constraints** is learning from patterns in the input that is guided and limited. >> The first constraint is on which stimulus properties can be **contrastive** (able to distinguish words, such as voicing in /b/ versus /p/). >> **Speaker** identity and **speech rate** change the sound but not the word, so they are not contrastive. >> A learner tracking every pattern would form categories for different voices and speeds. >> Learning has to focus on the properties that matter for meaning. || The second constraint is **based on earlier learning**. >> What is already acquired shapes what is easy to learn next. >> Once native categories are in place, distinctions that cut across them are harder to notice and learn. >> This kind is central to **sensitive periods** (times when earlier experience limits later learning). || The third constraint is the **social and interactive context** for learning. >> Sounds are learned more effectively in live, responsive interaction than from a recording. >> Contingent responses from caregivers shape infants' vocal behavior. >> Together the constraints explain why learning is fast and focuses on the distinctions that matter.
