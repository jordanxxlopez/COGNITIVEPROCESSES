2|**Aphasia** is a loss or impairment of language caused by brain damage. In the **1870s**, a number of German neurologists, including **Lichtheim** and **Wernicke**, began making **more systematic observations of aphasia**. They ascribed specific losses of function to **“disconnections” between “centers,”** and this was reasoning about aphasia before Chomsky. >> A **center** is a brain region that stores one kind of language representation. A disconnection is damage to the pathway that links two centers, so information can no longer travel between them. || **Lichtheim's “House” model** arranges the centers and pathways in the shape of a house. **A** is the center for **sound images**, which are the stored sound patterns of words, and **M** is the center for **movement images**, which are the stored motor plans for saying words. **B** is the center for **concepts**, and it sits at the peak of the roof. >> The **acoustic pathway a** brings heard speech into A, and the **motor pathway m** carries plans from M out to the speech muscles. >> Seven numbered sites mark different forms of aphasia, and each one is tied to damage at a particular center or pathway. || Each damage site predicts its own pattern of loss. A loss of the ability to **understand spoken words** might be due to a disconnection between **A and B**, because the sound of the word arrives but cannot reach its concept. A loss of the ability to do **verbatim repetition** might be due to a disconnection between **A and M**, because the heard word cannot pass directly to the plan for saying it. >> This kind of **“box and arrow” model** is foundational in **aphasiology**, the study of aphasia, and in modeling typical language function. The **interactive activation model** of word recognition has the same basic form, with levels of representation joined by pathways. >> This history comes from Eling and Whitaker (2009).
3|The **“Geschwind-Wernicke model”** of brain and language ties language functions to particular brain regions and the pathways that join them. **Norman Geschwind revived** the box-and-arrow approach of Wernicke and Lichtheim in the **1970s**. >> **Broca's area** sits toward the front of the left hemisphere, in the frontal lobe, and **Wernicke's area** sits farther back, in the temporal lobe. The **arcuate fasciculus** is a bundle of nerve fibers that connects Wernicke's area with Broca's area. >> Two major grooves serve as landmarks. The **central sulcus**, also called the **fissure of Rolando**, separates the frontal lobe from the parietal lobe behind it. The **lateral sulcus**, also called the **fissure of Sylvius**, separates the temporal lobe from the frontal and parietal lobes above it. || In the century between Wernicke and Geschwind, **the field of linguistics evolved dramatically**. Linguists developed a theoretical model in which **syntax was independent of other aspects of language** and had **its own computational principles**. That model opened the door to theorizing about **dissociations** and **double dissociations** of language functions in more sophisticated ways. >> In a **dissociation**, one ability is impaired while another is spared. In a **double dissociation**, one patient has the first ability impaired and the second spared, while another patient has the reverse pattern. >> A double dissociation is strong evidence that the two abilities depend on separate systems. Once syntax, word knowledge and meaning are treated as separate parts of language, a patient can be described as impaired in one part while the others work well.
4|**Fromkin (1997)** describes the classic profiles of the two main aphasias. The speech of **Broca's aphasia** patients has **word-finding pauses**, **loss of grammatical morphemes**, and quite often **disturbed word order**. Their understanding of everyday conversation **gives the impression of being generally good**, but **controlled testing reveals considerable impairments**. >> A **grammatical morpheme** is a small word or word part that marks a grammatical relationship, such as “the,” “is” or the past-tense ending “-ed.” || **Agrammatism** means difficulty producing or understanding grammatical structure, and the term is almost a synonym for Broca's aphasia. Even so, **some patients with lesions in Broca's area are not agrammatic**, and **some agrammatic patients would not be classified neurologically as Broca's aphasics**. So the behavior and the damaged region overlap heavily but not perfectly. >> Broca's aphasia names a pattern of behavior, and that pattern does not always follow damage to the area with the same name. || Patients with **Wernicke's aphasia** produce **fluent speech** with **good intonation and pronunciation**. Their speech contains **many word substitutions**, some related in meaning to the intended word and some unrelated. They also produce **neologisms**, which are invented words, and **phonological errors**, which are errors in the sounds of words, and they have **comprehension difficulties**. >> Fluency describes how smoothly speech is delivered, so it is separate from whether the words carry a coherent message. >> Broca's patients keep meaningful words but lose the small grammatical pieces that connect them, while Wernicke's patients keep the grammatical frame but fill it with wrong or invented words.
5|An attractive **dichotomy**, meaning a two-way division, sets the two aphasias against each other. **Wernicke's aphasia** is described as **“word salad”** or **“Jabberwocky,”** often with **neologisms**, while **syntax is roughly intact**. “Jabberwocky” is Lewis Carroll's poem of grammatical sentences filled with nonsense words. >> One Wernicke's patient, asked about his health, replied “I felt worse because I can no longer keep in mind from the mind of the minds to keep me from mind and up to the ear which can be to find among ourselves,” which is an example from Kriendler and colleagues (1971). The sentence flows smoothly but never forms a clear message. >> Another patient said “My wires don’t hire right” when he was asked about his poor vision. >> An aphasic physician who was asked if he was a doctor said “I’m a male demaploze on my own” and “I still know my tubaboys what for I have that’s gone hell and some of them go,” and **demaploze** and **tubaboys** are neologisms. >> One patient described a fork as “a need for a schedule” and a spoon as “How many schemes on your throat.” || **Broca's aphasia** is described as **“telegraphic” speech**, with **difficulty with word order and grammatical particles** and **relative sparing of important content words**. One Broca's patient from **Badecker and Caramazza (1985)** was asked what brought him to the hospital. His answer included “Two … ah doctors … and ah … thirty minutes … and yes … ah … hospital” and “Two doctors … and ah … teeth,” with long pauses throughout. >> Content words such as Dad, hospital, doctors and teeth carry pieces of the story. The grammatical words and endings that would connect them into sentences are largely missing, and the speech is full of pauses. || The two patterns look like mirror images. Wernicke's patients have fluent grammar with empty or wrong words, while Broca's patients have meaningful words with little grammar.
6|The dichotomy can also be stated in terms of what is damaged. On this account, **Wernicke's aphasia** reflects **problems with activation, or possibly inhibition, in the lexicon**, and **Broca's aphasia** reflects **problems with grammar**. >> The **lexicon** is the mental store of words. **Activation** makes a candidate word available, and **inhibition** suppresses competing words that should not be chosen, so weak inhibition could let wrong words slip into speech. || This description is **fairly distant from how Broca described his original cases**. Paul Broca's presentation of **Leborgne**, known as **“Tan,”** says that Tan **could only produce one jargon syllable**, which was “tan.” He **seemed otherwise to be in possession of his senses**, and he in fact **seemed to understand what was being said**. >> **Why is it critical that Leborgne had his other senses intact?** Intact senses mean that the loss was specific to speech, so it counts as a dissociation. >> A “capacity for articulate speech” also seems a more plausible function for a brain region than “criminality” or “philoprogenitiveness,” which is a phrenology term for love of one's children. >> If Tan could be examined today, his deficits would be specified at the **levels of description used in modern linguistics**, which are phonetic, phonological, lexical and syntactic. || The result is confusing, because **Broca's actual patients would not necessarily be classified as Broca's aphasics** today. They were arguably **unable to produce speech at all**, apparently because of **motor control issues**. They would be classified as suffering from **apraxia of speech**, which is a disorder in planning and coordinating the movements needed to speak. >> Apraxia of speech can limit output severely without any loss of grammatical knowledge.
7|The dichotomy treats Wernicke's aphasia as a problem in the lexicon and Broca's aphasia as a problem with grammar. It is interesting partly because it **aligns well with a sharp distinction that late 20th-century formal linguistics drew between the grammar and the lexicon**. >> The **grammar** is the system of rules for combining words into sentences. The **lexicon** is the store of individual words, which must be memorized. || The distinction starts with **Chomsky's formulation of the computational problem of language learning**. A person recognizes a new item as a sentence **not because it matches some familiar item in any simple way**, but because **it is generated by the grammar** that each person has internalized. >> A sentence never heard before can still be understood, because the listener can work out how the grammar builds it. || Chomsky also gave **compelling demonstrations of the independence of syntax and semantics**. In **Chomsky (1955)**, neither “colorless green ideas sleep furiously” nor “furiously sleep ideas green colorless” has ever occurred in an English speaker's past experience, and yet **the first is grammatical while the second is not**. >> The first sentence has no sensible meaning, but its word order follows English grammar. The second uses the same words in an order the grammar does not allow. >> The two sentences are equally unfamiliar and equally meaningless, so the difference between them must come from grammar as its own system. >> The aphasia dichotomy maps onto this split, with Wernicke's aphasia as a disorder of the lexicon and Broca's aphasia as a disorder of the grammar.
8|The dichotomy also fits the **speech production model**, and in particular the difference between the **functional level** and the **positional level**. At the functional level, which is the first linguistic level, the speaker is **arousing word meanings and semantic roles**. At the positional level, which is the second linguistic level, the speaker is **getting word forms in grammatically appropriate order**. >> A **semantic role** is the part a participant plays in an event, such as the doer or the receiver. >> Before these two levels comes the conceptual **message level**, where the speaker is choosing a story angle and arousing concepts of referents and event structure. After them, the **phonological encoding level** supplies the sounds of the words, and the **speech gesture level** handles **articulation**, the physical movements of speaking. || At the functional level, **lemmas have an arbitrary relationship to their referents**. A **lemma** is a word's entry in the lexicon, which links its meaning to its grammatical properties, and a referent is the person, object or event being talked about. Arbitrary means that nothing about the concept determines the word, so there is no reason a dog must be called “dog.” At the positional level, in contrast, **syntactic trees and affixes are the product of abstract syntactic processes**. >> An **affix** is a word part added to a stem, such as “-ed” or “-ly.” The positional level is the grammatical side of language, which is built by rules. || One objection is that words like **HOUSEBOAT** and **DOGHOUSE** have meanings that are clearly not arbitrary, because they are built from parts. The reply is that **speech error evidence** places **derivational morphology** at the positional level. >> **Derivational morphology** builds new words from existing parts, such as adding “-ly” to make an adverb. >> In the error **“easy enoughly,”** the speaker meant “easily enough,” but the “-ly” suffix attached to the wrong word. The suffix moved as a separate piece, so a grammatical operation at the positional level must have added it.
9|Popular theories of **sentence processing** hold that **the syntactic structure of a sentence is computed independently of meaning** as people listen or read. Under these theories, an autonomous parser builds the structure first, and the meaning is worked out afterward. **Isolating these “parsing” mechanisms** requires carefully constructing stimulus materials. || The sentence “The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite” has two possible structures. The **preferred** structure attaches “with the dynamite” to the **verb phrase**, so the dynamite is the instrument. The **unpreferred** structure attaches it to the **noun phrase** “the safe,” so the phrase tells which safe. >> The words are the same in both structures and only the attachment changes, so a test built on such sentences can target parsing without changing word meanings. || **One possibility** is that **Broca's patients generally understand what is being said even though they have a core grammatical deficit**. In ordinary conversation, word meanings, familiar events and context all point toward the same message. A listener can use these cues to understand without fully computing the grammar. >> Testing a specific parsing deficit requires sentences where the grammatical relationships cannot be recovered by guessing the most likely event. >> Reversible sentences, where either person could plausibly do the action, force the listener to use grammar.
10|**None of this theory was available to Broca back in the 1860s**. For him, it was interesting enough to **dissociate “articulate speech” from comprehension**. Leborgne could produce only one syllable, yet he appeared to understand what was said to him. >> Showing that speaking and understanding can come apart was the key finding at the time. || The modern question is different from the one Broca asked. Suppose the goal is to find out whether **some patients have a difficulty that is more generally with syntax**. **How would we do it?** The way to do it is to **test comprehension**. >> A **syntactic impairment** concerns the organization of grammatical relationships, so it should affect both producing and understanding sentences. >> A comprehension test lets the patient respond without speaking, so motor problems cannot explain a poor score. If the patient still struggles to understand grammatical relationships, the deficit goes beyond articulation. || Broca's original question was simpler than the one modern researchers ask. He wanted to separate producing speech from understanding speech, and Leborgne, who could understand but could not speak, separated the two. Modern researchers ask whether a patient's difficulty lies in grammar itself, and that question requires testing comprehension of specific structures.
11|**Why was it interesting to show that Broca's patients have comprehension deficits?** The first reason is that **Broca's aphasia is almost always accompanied by some level of motor dysfunction**. Agrammatic speech might **merely reflect an attempt to communicate despite motor limitations**. If speaking takes great effort, a patient might drop the small grammatical words and keep only the most informative content words. >> **Hughlings Jackson** made a related point, which is that **we are observing what the individual can do despite the impairment**. A brain injury that leads to agrammatic speech does not literally mean that **the grammar itself is injured**. >> Caramazza and Zurif (1976) cite a version of this idea from Lenneberg (1973). If anterior damage spares the patient's tacit knowledge of language, the telegraphic output can be attributed to a neuromuscular problem, because **the strain of speaking is so great that the patient speaks asyntactically in order to economize effort**. || **Chomsky's distinction between competence and performance** also applies. **Competence** is a person's underlying knowledge of language. **Performance** is the actual use of that knowledge in speaking or understanding, and it can be disrupted by memory limits, distraction or motor problems. So **when motor deficits obviously interfere with performance, comprehension may provide a clearer window into competence**. || **Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** tested comprehension in Broca's patients for this reason. Speech production cannot tell the motor account and the grammar account apart, because both predict telegraphic speech. >> If these patients understand grammar normally, their agrammatic speech is a motor performance problem. If they also fail to understand grammatical relationships, the deficit reaches the grammar itself.
12|**“Man bites dog”** is the newspaper example of an unexpected event. **Why do we need psycholinguistic tests to observe grammatical difficulties in comprehension?** The reason is that **in conversation, there are lots of redundant cues to what a speaker intends**. >> **Redundant** cues are several different sources of information that all point to the same meaning. || In “The dog bit the man,” there are **many ways to get the right meaning**. A listener can form the **most likely combination of DOG, BITE and MAN into an event representation**, **with or without syntax**, because dogs bite people much more often than people bite dogs. The sentence also follows **canonical word order**, which is **agent, then verb, then undergoer**, or **subject, then verb, then object**. >> The **agent** is the doer of the action, and the **undergoer** is the one the action happens to. In English, the first noun is usually the agent. >> A lot of comprehension also takes place in a **discourse context**, the surrounding conversation, which clarifies a great deal. || Broca's patients' comprehension might depend on these **compensatory heuristics**. A **heuristic** is a mental shortcut, and a compensatory one makes up for a weakness elsewhere. A patient might assume the most plausible event, or assume that the first noun is the doer, and either shortcut gives the right answer for “The dog bit the man” without any grammatical analysis. >> To test grammar directly, the sentences have to defeat the shortcuts, as “The dog that the man is biting is angry” does. Here the **man** is doing the biting, even though the dog comes first and dogs are more likely biters, so only grammatical analysis gives the right answer.
13|**Caramazza and Zurif** devised **a list of just this kind of sentence, along with a set of control sentences**. The materials come in four groups of eight sentences each. **Control sentences** are simple sentences in canonical order, such as “The girl is kicking a green ball,” “The cat is chasing a blue bird” and “The boy is riding a new bicycle,” where the doer comes first and the event is ordinary. || **Center-embedded sentences with semantic constraints** contain one clause inside another, but meaning makes the roles obvious. A **center-embedded** sentence has a relative clause placed in the middle of the main clause. Examples are “The apple that the boy is eating is red,” “The wagon that the horse is pulling is green” and “The balloon that the clown is holding is round,” and apples cannot eat boys, so meaning alone reveals who does what. || **Reversible center-embedded sentences** use two participants who could each plausibly do the action. Examples are “The cow that the monkey is scaring is yellow,” “The horse that the bear is kicking is brown” and “The girl that the boy is tickling is happy,” where meaning gives no help and only grammar reveals the roles. **Improbable center-embedded sentences** describe events that go against expectations. Examples are “The bird that the worm is eating is blue,” “The man that the horse is riding is fat” and “The policeman that the robber is chasing is skinny,” where common sense points to the wrong answer. >> Worms do not usually eat birds, but in the improbable sentence the worm is the eater. || In every embedded sentence, the first noun is the one acted upon, and the doer is inside the relative clause. The grammatical structure stays the same across the three embedded groups, while the help or interference from meaning changes. >> A listener who relies on meaning should succeed on the control and semantically constrained sentences and fail on the reversible and improbable ones. A listener who parses normally should succeed on all four.
14|**Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** used a **sentence-picture matching task**, in which participants heard a sentence and chose which of the pictures matched its meaning. Accuracy reveals whether the listener understood who did what to whom, and no spoken answer is required. They compared healthy listeners with patients who had Broca's, conduction and Wernicke's aphasia on the four sentence types. >> The four types were control sentences, semantically constrained sentences, reversible sentences and improbable sentences. || **Healthy controls are more or less equally good at all stimulus types**, and they stay near the maximum score on each one. **Wernicke's patients are poor overall**, with low scores even on the simple control sentences. >> The poor scores of Wernicke's patients are probably a result of their **general comprehension deficits**, which affect understanding of words as well as grammar. || **Broca's patients** have a **particular difficulty keeping “who did what to whom” straight** when **syntax conflicts with common sense** or with the probable semantic roles for the nouns. Their scores are high on control sentences and on semantically constrained sentences, because meaning supplies the answer there. Their scores drop on reversible and improbable sentences, because only grammar can reveal the roles there. || This **specific comprehension deficit** was taken as evidence that **Broca's aphasia impacts syntax specifically**. The deficit appeared in understanding as well as in speaking, so it lies in the grammar itself. >> The title of the paper is “Dissociation of algorithmic and heuristic processes,” which states the idea. Broca's patients lose the **algorithmic** process of grammatical analysis but keep the **heuristic** shortcuts based on meaning.
15|A more **specific theory of the “parts of language”** opens up **new ways of exploring structure-function relationships**. A **structure-function relationship** is a link between a brain structure and the function it supports. Before such theories, aphasias could only be described in broad terms. || **Rather than just breaking up aphasics into those with good and poor comprehension, or those who are more or less fluent**, researchers can **isolate problems with particular aspects of language**. One patient might know word meanings well but assign the wrong roles in complex sentences. Another might produce a fluent grammatical frame filled with words that fail to carry the intended message. >> A single fluency rating would hide the difference between these two patients. || The key division is **the grammar versus the lexicon**. **Grammar** concerns the rules that relate words to one another in a sentence, and the **lexicon** concerns stored words and the ability to retrieve the right ones. This division gives specific predictions, so controlled tasks can test whether a particular operation is impaired and relate that impairment to a particular brain region. >> A grammar deficit predicts trouble with reversible sentences but not with word meanings. >> A lexical deficit predicts trouble with word meanings and naming but not with grammatical structure.
16|There are **problems with this dichotomy**. **Dick and colleagues** had participants listen to **four types of sentences** and **indicate which animal was doing the action**. Pictures of a dog and a cat appeared on a computer screen, and participants pressed a button on a response box to choose the animal. >> The event is always a dog biting a cat, but the sentence structure changes. || Two sentence types put the doer first. The **active** type is “The dog is biting the cat,” and the **subject cleft** type is “It’s the dog that is biting the cat,” so for both types a shortcut of choosing the first animal mentioned gives the right answer. >> A **cleft** sentence splits a simple sentence with “It’s” and “that” to put focus on one part. In a subject cleft, the focused noun is the subject, which is the doer. || Two sentence types put the receiver of the action first. The **object cleft** type is “It’s the cat that the dog is biting,” where the focused noun is the object, the one being bitten. The **passive** type is “The cat is bitten by the dog,” where the receiver becomes the grammatical subject. >> For these two types the first-animal shortcut gives the wrong answer, so choosing the dog correctly requires grammatical analysis. >> A listener who always picks the first animal succeeds on the first two types and fails on the last two, while a listener who parses the grammar succeeds on all four.
17|When **aphasia patients and healthy aging controls** do this task, **a pattern emerges much like what Caramazza and Zurif observed**. Patients were grouped by type of aphasia with the Western Aphasia Battery, and the percentage of correct responses was measured for active, subject cleft, object cleft and passive sentences. >> **Elderly controls** score close to 100% on all four sentence types. >> **Wernicke's** patients score low on every sentence type, around 43% to 50%, which is near chance. || **Broca's aphasia patients have particular difficulty with the syntactically complex stimuli**, which are the object clefts and passives. They score about 61% on actives and 65% on subject clefts, and then they drop to about 42% on object clefts and about 54% on passives. These are **noncanonical** structures, meaning they depart from the usual doer-first order. >> **Their performance is overall not so great**, because even on simple active sentences they are far below the elderly controls. The pattern combines a general drop in performance with an extra cost for complex structures. || The other groups differ in both overall accuracy and the size of the drop for complex sentences. **Anomic** patients, whose main problem is word finding, score about 89% on actives and subject clefts, about 69% on object clefts and about 73% on passives. **Conduction** aphasia patients score about 75% on actives and subject clefts and fall to about 42% on object clefts. >> Each group's profile provides a clue to the nature of its deficit.
18|The same task was given to **healthy undergraduates** under **conditions that stress the language system** in different ways. The stress conditions were things like **speeding up the speech**, adding noise, or **asking people to remember lists of numbers as a secondary task**. >> **Compressed** speech is speech played faster than normal. A **low-pass filter** removes the higher frequencies from speech, so it sounds muffled. >> **Auditory digits** and **visual digits** are number lists that participants had to remember while listening, which uses up memory resources. || With a single stressor, accuracy stays near 99% in the normal condition and stays high for actives and subject clefts in every condition. Object clefts suffer most, and with noise accuracy falls from about 97% on actives to about 86% on object clefts. Passives also drop under noise, to about 91%. >> When two stressors are combined, the effects grow larger. With low-pass filtering plus compression, accuracy is about 91% on actives and 90% on subject clefts, but it drops to about 66% on object clefts and 68% on passives. >> Compression plus visual digits lowers object clefts to about 90% while leaving actives near 98%. || **Object cleft and passive sentences are particularly vulnerable to all the manipulations**, which is the same pattern seen in Broca's aphasia. These students have no brain damage and no loss of grammatical knowledge, so stressing their processing system is enough to produce an aphasia-like profile. This suggests that **the comprehension deficits seen in Broca's aphasia patients may be a “performance” issue after all**. >> Complex structures may demand more processing resources, so any reduction in resources hurts them first. The patients may still have the grammatical knowledge, or competence, but lack the resources to use it efficiently.
19|**Are Broca's aphasics' comprehension deficits language-specific?** **Saygin and colleagues (2004)** asked patients to **match an action to an object**. In the **non-linguistic** version, a drawing has a boy pantomiming an action, such as licking, and participants choose the matching object from pictures such as an ice cream cone, a cake, broccoli and a rooster. In the **linguistic** version, the action was given in words instead of by pantomime. || Controls were nearly perfect in both versions, and aphasic patients made more errors in both. In the non-linguistic version, controls scored **98.5%** and aphasic patients scored **93.4%**. In the linguistic version, controls scored **99.4%** and aphasic patients scored **87.5%**. So **Broca's aphasics had difficulty with this task whether the stimuli were linguistic or not**. >> Accuracy also depended on the type of wrong choice offered, which is called the **distractor**. With **unrelated** distractors, controls scored 99.8% and aphasic patients scored 93.3%. >> With **semantic** distractors, which are related in meaning to the correct object, controls scored 99.2% and aphasic patients dropped to **86.3%**. >> With **affordance-based** distractors, controls scored 97.7% and aphasic patients scored 91.6%. An **affordance** is an action an object allows, so an affordance-based distractor is a different object that can also be licked. || The nonverbal version removes language from the input but keeps meaning in the task. A pantomimed action has to be understood and linked to the right object, just as a verb has to be linked to its object in a sentence. >> A purely grammatical deficit predicts good performance on the nonverbal version. The patients' difficulty on that version points to a broader problem with action meanings, which is shared with language.
20|**Performance in the non-verbal version of this task was significantly correlated with damage to Broca's area**. Lesion maps link brain areas to deficits in **non-linguistic pantomime interpretation**. The strongest associations fall in the left frontal region in front of the **central sulcus**, and that region includes Broca's area. >> The maps use three horizontal slices of the brain, and colored regions mark where damage was associated with poorer performance. The hottest colors, red and yellow, mark the strongest relationships. || The task only asked participants to interpret a pantomimed action and choose an object. They did not have to produce a sentence, and they did not have to work out the grammar of a spoken instruction. >> Patients whose damage included Broca's area had the most trouble interpreting pantomimed actions. || Damage to Broca's area therefore affects **more than syntax**. Patients with damage there can struggle to connect an action with its meaning even when no language is involved. Broca's area supports **action understanding** as well as grammatical processing. >> Any account of Broca's area has to explain both the grammatical comprehension findings and this nonlinguistic difficulty with actions.
21|**Are Wernicke's aphasics' comprehension deficits language-specific?** **Saygin and colleagues** asked patients to **match environmental sounds to pictures**. In the **nonverbal** version, a speaker plays an environmental sound, such as a cow mooing, and participants choose between pictures such as a cow and a sheep. In the **verbal** version, the speaker plays a spoken phrase, such as “cow mooing,” and participants choose between pictures such as a cow and a violin. || The groups differed in overall accuracy. **Controls** scored 99.2% verbal and 98.1% nonverbal, patients with **right hemisphere damage** scored 99.1% and 98.2%, and **anomic** patients scored 98.6% and 96.7%. **Broca's** patients scored 96.3% verbal and 95.2% nonverbal. **Wernicke's patients were particularly bad at this task**, scoring **90.0% verbal** and **91.3% nonverbal**. >> Controls, right hemisphere patients and anomic patients performed equally well, Broca's patients performed worse, and Wernicke's patients performed worst. || **No significant difference was observed between the verbal and nonverbal versions of the task**. Wernicke's patients struggled as much with the sound of a cow mooing as with the spoken phrase. So the deficit is not specific to words. >> A spoken phrase tests verbal comprehension, and the environmental sound tests the same comprehension without words. >> Their difficulty concerns linking sounds of any kind to their meanings, whether the meaning arrives through language or through ordinary sounds.
22|**Deficits in both verbal and nonverbal sound-picture matching are associated with Wernicke's area**. Lesion overlays mark where damage occurred in patients who were impaired on each version. There were **8 patients impaired in the nonverbal version** and **10 patients impaired in the verbal version**. >> The warmest colors, red and yellow, mark areas damaged in the largest number of patients. || The two sets of overlays look very similar. In both, the region of greatest overlap falls in the **posterior temporal area of the left hemisphere**, around Wernicke's area. So the verbal and nonverbal impairments come from damage to the same general region. || The two versions differ in their input, but they share a common demand. A spoken phrase conveys the meaning through words, while an animal call conveys it without language. Both tasks require connecting meaningful sounds to what they represent, and damage around Wernicke's area disrupts that connection in both cases. >> Wernicke's area appears to support **meaningful sound processing in general**, with language as one important case.
23|**So, how language-specific are the deficits in aphasia?** This is **still a controversy**. Some patients are especially vulnerable to complex grammatical structures, which suggests that some aspects of language are handled in specialized ways. The same patients can also struggle with action meanings or environmental sounds in tasks that involve no language, which suggests that language shares brain systems with other kinds of meaning. >> An account of aphasia has to cover both kinds of finding. >> If aphasia deficits were purely linguistic, language would appear to have its own dedicated brain systems. || What should be clear is **how the terms of the debate have evolved**. The field now uses **relatively sophisticated tests to tap specific aspects of language**, such as reversible and improbable sentences, cleft sentences and nonverbal matching tasks. It uses **quantitative analyses of behavior**, which measure accuracy across sentence types, patient groups and stress conditions. It also uses **quantitative analyses of structure-function relationships**, which relate patterns of brain damage to performance across many patients. || The field has come **quite a long way from the 19th century**. Wernicke and Lichtheim drew boxes and arrows from a small number of individual cases. >> Modern research can test which specific operations are impaired and map those impairments onto damaged tissue across large groups of patients.

25|**Structure-function relationships** in aphasia data can be **formalized** with MRI data. **Voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping**, from **Bates, Wilson, Saygin, Dick, Sereno, Knight and Dronkers**, relates the location of brain damage to the severity of symptoms across many patients. >> A **voxel** is a small three-dimensional unit of an MRI brain image. For each voxel, patients with damage there are compared with patients without damage there. >> If the damaged patients score much worse, that voxel is associated with the symptom. || The **“symptoms”** here are **defined very broadly**. One set of maps marks regions that are likely to be damaged in individuals with **fluency deficits**, and the strongest associations fall in the front part of the left hemisphere. The other set marks regions associated with **comprehension deficits**, and the strongest associations fall farther back, in the posterior temporal region. >> Colors from blue for weaker to red for stronger mark how strong each association is. || So data from a large number of patients is consistent with a **rough double dissociation**. **Posterior regions** are associated with **perception**, or comprehension, whereas **anterior regions** are associated with **production**, or fluency. >> Each patient contributes a damage pattern and a continuous score, so the method turns many individual cases into a single statistical map. That map gives a quantitative link between brain location and symptoms.
