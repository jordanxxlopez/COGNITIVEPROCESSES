2|**Klatt (1979)** described a very old and very general model of speech perception, in which recognition runs through a **series of stages**. Each stage transforms the output of the stage before it. The **speech waveform** enters the **peripheral auditory system**, where the **cochlea** in the inner ear carries out a **spectral analysis** that splits the sound into its frequency components. >> **Acoustic property detectors** respond to particular properties of the sound, and **phonetic feature detectors** use their outputs to estimate the probability of features such as voicing at each moment. >> **Segmental analysis** combines the features into a sequence of speech segments called a **segmental feature matrix**. **Lexical search** then compares that sequence with stored words and produces **lexical hypotheses**, which are candidate words that might match. || **Categorical perception** fits this model. Somewhere between the **analog signal** that comes in through the cochlea and the **activation of phonemes** in segmental analysis, **information is lost**, so many physically different sounds become the same phoneme. This **loss of information is helpful**, because a shared phoneme sequence can make contact with higher levels of representation and be matched against stored words. >> The assumption that follows is that **in order to identify words, we need to first identify their constituent phonemes**. || There are **two problems** for this simple view. The first is **cascaded processing**, in which partial information moves on to the next level before the earlier decision is complete. The second is **feedback**, in which a higher level such as words sends influence back down to a lower level such as phonemes.
3|The **beach-peach continuum** is a familiar **voicing continuum**. The two words differ in their first consonant, which is /b/ in beach and /p/ in peach. These consonants differ mainly in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**, which is the delay between the release of the lips and the start of vocal fold vibration. >> The continuum runs from “peach” to “beach” as VOT gets shorter in equal steps. A **long delay** before voicing sounds like **peach**, and a **short delay** sounds like **beach**. || One listener's **identification** results have a **sharp, step-like shape**. Stimuli 1 through 5 are labeled as one word every time, and stimuli 7 through 9 are labeled as the other word every time. Only stimulus 6 gets mixed labels, with a proportion near 0.33. >> The **category boundary** is the narrow region where the preferred label switches. || The same listener's **discrimination** results follow the boundary. Pairs inside one category are almost never heard as different, so accuracy is near 0 for them. Accuracy jumps to 1 for the pair 4 and 6 and for the pair 5 and 7, because those pairs **straddle the boundary**. >> The pair 6 and 8 is in between, near 0.33, and accuracy falls back to 0 for the pair 7 and 9. >> Equal changes in VOT are much **easier to detect across the boundary**, where they change the word, than inside one category, where the word stays the same.
4|**How does categorical perception happen?** One answer is a **threshold model**, in which **activity is only passed forward from one level to the next when an activity threshold is crossed**. A **threshold** is a set level of activity that must be reached before anything is sent onward. Activity below the threshold can rise and fall without any effect on the next level. >> In this kind of model, **categorical perception arises at phoneme identification**. || Consider the voicing evidence for a sound between /b/ and /p/. At the **feature level**, the evidence swings up and down over time between a **+voiced** threshold and a **-voiced** threshold. At the **phoneme level**, nothing happens while the evidence wavers below threshold, so both /b/ and /p/ stay silent. Only when the evidence finally crosses the +voiced threshold does **/b/** switch on, **all at once**. || An ambiguous sound that gets mixed responses, such as the one near the middle of the beach-peach continuum, either crosses a threshold or does not. The word level receives a finished /b/ or a finished /p/, and the history of uncertainty stays behind at the feature level. >> Two acoustically different sounds that both end up as /b/ become **identical** by the time they reach word recognition, because the differences that existed before the threshold are discarded. >> This **all-or-none switch** would produce categorical perception directly at the phoneme level.
5|A **cascaded model** gives a different answer to how categorical perception happens. In a cascaded model, **activity is passed forward continuously over time from one level to the next**. Each level responds to whatever activity exists at the moment, while the decision is still forming. >> Feature activity and phoneme activity rise and fall together. As evidence for +voiced grows, **/b/** activation grows, and as the evidence shifts toward -voiced, activation moves toward **/p/**. || In this kind of model, **categorical perception might happen later on, or might even be an artifact of the task structure**. An **artifact** is a result created by the way something is measured and not by the process under study. An identification task forces a listener to click beach or peach, even if both words were partly active. The answer looks categorical because the **response choices are categorical**. >> Two sounds that both end up labeled /b/ can still differ in how much they supported /p/ along the way. || The cascaded model lets **uncertainty flow forward**. When the voicing evidence is mixed, /b/ and /p/ each get activation in proportion to their evidence. Graded phoneme activation passes graded activation to words, so a sound near the boundary **partly activates both beach and peach**. >> The processing before the final choice keeps fine-grained information about how ambiguous the sound was.
6|**Making furniture** is a metaphor for **thresholded activity**. Each operation takes one specific input and turns it into one specific output. A **lumber mill** takes **trees** as input and produces **lumber** as output. >> A **furniture factory** takes **lumber** as input and produces **unassembled furniture** as output. >> An **IKEA customer** takes **unassembled furniture** as input and produces finished **furniture** as output. || Each step needs a **finished product** in a form it can use. Trees must become lumber before any furniture is made, and lumber must become furniture parts before any assembly begins. No part of the work at one step passes forward until the whole job is done. || **Thresholded processing** depends on its inputs in the same way. A higher level **waits** until the lower level delivers the required representation, so information moves forward only as a completed product. >> The property the furniture chain captures is that each stage waits for a finished product from the stage before it.
7|The furniture metaphor says that each **“layer”** of the system **can only take a specific input that has been transformed completely by the previous layer**. Manufacturing has to wait for the shipment of lumber before it can start. **Many computer programs** function this way, because each step finishes before the next one starts. >> Each stage does its own transformation and hands on only its finished output. A lower stage can keep working while the higher stage sits idle and waits. || In speech recognition, a strict threshold account makes **word processing depend on an identified phoneme**. A partly resolved acoustic signal stays at the lower level until it is identified as a phoneme. The word level receives only the **finished segment**. || This design also decides **what information is lost**. When different sounds are converted into the same phoneme, their earlier differences never reach word recognition. That is useful for treating varied sounds alike, but it has a cost. >> The cost is that later stages never learn **how uncertain** the earlier stages were, because that uncertainty was never passed forward.
8|A **food web** is a metaphor for **cascaded processing**, because its **“levels” continuously interact with one another**. A desert food web links **desert plants**, **insects**, **kangaroo rats**, **small lizards**, **large lizards**, **scorpions**, **snakes**, **desert foxes** and **hawks** by who eats whom. A drought that reduces the number of plants **immediately** affects the health of kangaroo rats. That change then affects the snakes that eat the kangaroo rats, and the effect continues up the web. >> No population waits for the level below it to reach a finished state before it is affected. || **Cascaded processing** has the same continuous dependence. Shifting feature evidence changes phoneme activity as it happens, and shifting phoneme activity changes the support for words. Each level reacts to **partial information** as it arrives from the level below. || The food web also has clear **feedback loops**. **What impact does adding more hawks have on populations of snakes, lizards and rats?** More hawks reduce the populations they eat, and those changes in turn affect the hawks' own food supply. >> In a recognition model, **feedback** is influence that travels from a higher level back down to a lower level. >> Cascading and feedback are different ideas, because cascading sends developing evidence **forward** and feedback lets higher-level activity change an **earlier** representation.
9|**Is phoneme identification for word recognition more like thresholded processing or cascaded processing?** Thresholded processing is the lumber mill, and cascaded processing is the food web. The eye-tracking evidence from McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003) favors **cascaded processing**, but first the two accounts have to be told apart. In a **thresholded account**, word recognition starts only once a phoneme decision is complete. In a **cascaded account**, a word can gather support while the phoneme is still uncertain. || The two accounts differ in how much developing acoustic evidence reaches candidate words. Two sounds can both be labeled **/b/** and still differ in how much they resemble **/p/**. A thresholded system turns both into the same finished /b/, so **pear should not compete with bear**. A cascaded system passes along more /p/ support for the more ambiguous sound, so **pear should compete with bear for a while**. >> Both accounts can predict the same final word, because they differ only in what happens before the final choice. >> The deciding test therefore measures **temporary support for a competing word** during recognition.
10|**McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)** asked whether phoneme identification for word recognition is thresholded or cascaded. Their study looked for **gradient effects of within-category VOT on lexical access**. **Lexical access** is retrieving a word from memory during recognition. >> **Gradient** means changing by degrees. **Within-category VOT** means differences in voice onset time among sounds that are all heard as the same phoneme. || Listeners identified sounds from a **/b/-/p/ continuum** in a **“visual world” display** while **eye-tracking** recorded where they looked among the pictures. Each display had a **target** such as **pear**, a **competitor** such as **bear**, and two unrelated **fillers**, which were a **lamp** and a **ship**. The target and the competitor differ only in their first consonant. || Eye movements suit this question because they are **fast and continuous** during word recognition. Listeners look at objects as they think about them, often before they know they have decided. A listener may end up choosing bear after a quick look at the pear. >> A look at the pear while hearing a /b/-like sound means that pear was being considered. >> How often and when such looks happen measures **how active the competitor was** before the final click.
11|In the study by **McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)**, participants used the **mouse to select** whichever of the pictures was most consistent with what they heard, such as a **“bear”** or a **“pear.”** Each trial presented one sound from the VOT continuum along with the four pictures. The click records which word the listener finally settled on. || The clicks produced a **typical, step-like identification function**. For VOTs from 0 to about 15 milliseconds, /p/ responses stayed near 0, so listeners almost always clicked bear. From about 20 milliseconds onward, /p/ responses stayed near 1, so listeners almost always clicked pear. >> The switch happened abruptly at a **category boundary of 17.5 milliseconds**, plus or minus 0.83 milliseconds. || Judged by the clicks alone, perception looks **fully categorical**. A sound well inside the /b/ category and a sound just short of the boundary both produce bear nearly every time. On its own, this sharp shift would seem to support the **threshold model**. >> A click records only the word chosen at the end of recognition, while eye movements record which other words received support before that choice. >> A sharp identification function can therefore exist alongside **gradual differences in competition** among sounds with the same final label.
12|**Eye movements reveal that ambiguity is gradient**, because there are **more looks to the competitor close to the boundary**. Gradient ambiguity means that uncertainty varies by degree and does not switch all at once. The analysis holds the final response constant, so it compares only trials where the listener clicked bear, or only trials where the listener clicked pear. >> Every trial in a comparison ended with the same click, so differences in competitor looks cannot come from listeners changing their answer. || On trials where listeners clicked **bear**, they still looked at the **pear** for a while. The VOTs on these trials were 0, 5, 10 and 15 milliseconds. Looks to the pear rose after the word began, peaked around 400 to 600 milliseconds, and then declined as bear won. >> Sounds closer to the boundary, such as 15 milliseconds, drew **more looks** to the pear, and the looks **lasted longer**, than the sound at 0 milliseconds. || On trials where listeners clicked **pear**, the pattern was the mirror image. The VOTs on these trials were 20, 25, 30, 35 and 40 milliseconds. The sound at 20 milliseconds, just past the boundary, drew the most looks to the bear, and the clearest /p/ at 40 milliseconds drew the fewest. || The effect of VOT is mainly on the **duration of activation**, meaning how long the competitor stays active. Two sounds that both end up as bear can still produce different amounts of looking at the pear. >> The recognition system keeps track of how ambiguous each sound was, which is information a **threshold model would throw away**.
13|The gradient effect is clearer when looks to the competitor are compared across the **whole VOT range**, from 0 to 40 milliseconds. On one side of the **category boundary**, listeners clicked bear, and the measure is how often they **looked at the pear**. On the other side, listeners clicked pear, and the measure is how often they **looked at the bear**. >> Looks were measured in two time windows. The **early** window covers **300 to 1100 milliseconds** after the word begins, and the **late** window covers **1100 to 1900 milliseconds**. || In both windows, competitor looks **increase as VOT approaches the boundary from either side**. On the bear side, late looks to the pear rise from about 0.02 at 0 milliseconds to about 0.045 near 15 milliseconds. On the pear side, late looks to the bear are highest near 20 milliseconds, at around 0.04, and fall to about 0.01 by 40 milliseconds. >> Early looks are higher overall, but they follow the same pattern of peaking near the boundary. >> The rise is **gradual** and not sudden on both sides. || The effect appears in both windows, so uncertainty about the first sound shapes looking **throughout recognition** and not only in a brief first moment. A sound can support the eventual winner while still supporting its rival. The closer the sound is to the boundary, the stronger that rival support is.
14|The eye-movement finding is consistent with the idea that **activation flows in a cascaded way from features to phonemes**. Changing features affect phonemes, and changing phonemes affect words, while recognition is still under way. **Even stimuli that are identified as /b/ 100% of the time can vary in how strongly and unambiguously the “voiced” feature is activated**. >> A clear /b/ activates voicing strongly, while a /b/ near the boundary activates it only weakly. || **/b/ and /p/** share most of their features. Both are **bilabial**, which means the two lips make the closure, and both are **stops**, which means the airflow is fully blocked and then released. A stimulus that is obviously **+bilabial** and **+stop** supports /b/ and /p/ equally. Only voicing tells them apart, so weak voicing evidence creates **momentary ambiguity between /b/ and /p/**. >> That ambiguity is reflected in the **eye movements** while the participant decides where to click. || The **TRACE** model captures this cascade with three layers. The **feature layer** takes a **pseudo-spectral input representation**, which is a simplified version of the frequency content of the sound over time. The **phoneme layer** has units for phonemes such as /b/, /p/, /r/ and /t/, and the **word layer** has units for words. >> The feature detectors are POW, VOC, DIFF, ACU, GRD, VOI and BUR, which stand for power, vocalic, diffuseness, acuteness, gradualness of onset, voicing and burst. >> The phoneme units and word units are repeated at every point in time. Activity passes up from features to phonemes and from phonemes to words, and units within a layer compete through **inhibition**.
15|There is evidence that **partial activation can reach all the way to the lexical level**, which is the level of stored words and their meanings. **Andruski and colleagues (1994)** found that **semantic priming** can happen even with **nonwords**. Semantic priming is the speed-up in recognizing a word after hearing a word related in meaning. >> **“Beace”** primes **“war,”** because beace is peace with a voiced first consonant and peace is related in meaning to war. **“Ging”** primes **“queen,”** because ging is king with a voiced first consonant. >> In the **word-to-word** items, changing the voicing of the first consonant of one real word produces another real word, as in **“pet”** becoming **“bet.”** One such item is **“bill”** priming **“drug,”** because bill is an altered form of pill. || Participants did a **lexical decision task**, in which they decide as quickly as possible whether a target is a real word. The primes were **unaltered**, had their VOT reduced by **one third**, had it reduced by **two thirds**, or were **unrelated** to the target. Faster responses than after an unrelated prime indicate priming. || Every related prime **sped up responses** compared with unrelated primes. Unrelated primes gave the slowest responses, near 950 to 970 milliseconds, and unaltered primes gave responses around 810 to 860 milliseconds. With one third of the VOT removed, responses stayed fast, around 825 to 855 milliseconds. >> With two thirds removed, word-to-nonword items still stayed fast, near 820 milliseconds, while word-to-word items slowed to about 890 milliseconds. >> The word-to-word items slowed because the altered prime also partly activates a competing real word, such as bill competing with pill, which reduces the benefit. || This result means that **lexical representations can be activated even by incomplete or imperfect input**. Beace never becomes a real word, but it matches peace closely enough to partly activate it. That activation spreads to meaning and speeds recognition of war, which fits **cascaded processing**.
16|The bear and pear eye-tracking result is one piece of evidence for **cascaded processing** in speech perception. A thresholded model would have a hard time explaining why **the number of looks to the other member of the minimal pair increases gradually as you approach the category boundary**. A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in a single sound, such as bear and pear. >> Looks to the competitor climb steadily as VOT nears the boundary. This happens even when the final answer is the same, so it reflects **graded activation within one category**. || In a thresholded model, graded feature activity **never reaches the phoneme level**. Feature evidence can swing between +voiced and -voiced, but the phoneme level stays silent until /b/ is chosen all at once. The phoneme level therefore cannot know how close a sound came to the boundary. >> Every token placed in a category sends the **same finished phoneme** to the word level. Word-level competition would then be identical for every /b/, so nothing could produce gradually changing competitor looks. || A **cascaded account** explains the effect directly. More support for the competing phoneme passes on more support for its word, so sounds nearer the boundary draw more looks to the competitor.
17|The **TRACE** model also includes **feedback from the lexical layer to the phoneme layer**. Activity runs up from phonemes to words and also back down from words to phonemes. **Lexical feedback** means that an active word sends support back to its own phonemes. >> An uncertain sound can partly activate a word, and that word can then strengthen the reading of the sound that fits it. || Influence therefore travels **in both directions**. Acoustic evidence changes which words are active, and active words shift the balance between possible phonemes while the phoneme decision is still forming. This helps listeners cope with noisy or ambiguous input. || **Feedback is different from cascading**. Cascading sends incomplete evidence **forward**, from features to phonemes and from phonemes to words. Feedback sends influence from words **back down** to phonemes. >> An interactive model can have both cascading and feedback. >> The reason to add feedback comes from experiments in which **word knowledge changes how sounds are heard**, such as the **Ganong effect**.
18|**The Ganong effect** comes from **Ganong (1980)**, who looked at how **phoneme identification is affected by word knowledge**. He tested whether knowing which sound sequences are real words changes how an ambiguous sound is heard. The phrase “Gisses and kifts for Valentine’s day” plays on the effect, because it swaps the voicing of the first consonants in “kisses and gifts.” || Ganong came up with **word/nonword pairs** such as **“corpse/gorpse,”** with a real word at one end of the continuum and a nonword at the other. Hearing /k/ gives the familiar word corpse, and hearing /g/ gives gorpse, which is not a word. He produced continua that spanned the relevant phoneme boundary in **5 millisecond steps** of VOT. >> The rest of the word, “orpse,” is the same at every step, and it tells the listener which beginning would complete a real word. || The design pairs **uncertain phonetic evidence** with a **difference in lexical status**. If word knowledge affects identification, an ambiguous first consonant should more often be heard as the **sound that makes a word**. >> The crucial test is in the **ambiguous middle** of the continuum, because that is where word knowledge has the most room to act.
19|One prediction for the experiment of **Ganong (1980)** comes from a **late “correction” process**. In this account, **categorical perception functions as usual, but there is a bias to report having heard a word**. The listener first categorizes the sound with no help from word knowledge. At the response stage, the listener leans toward the option that forms a real word. || This account predicts a **general reporting bias**. Three kinds of continua are compared, measured by the proportion of voiced responses from short VOT to long VOT. In **word-nonword** continua the voiced end forms a word, in **nonword-word** continua the voiceless end forms a word, and in **neutral** continua neither end has a lexical advantage. >> A bias added after perception should push responses toward the word by the **same amount everywhere**. >> The word-nonword responses would be shifted up even at the long VOT end, staying near 0.25 and not falling to 0. The nonword-word responses would be shifted down at the short VOT end, starting near 0.75 and not at 1. || The **lexical effect** is the difference in voiced responses between the two lexical conditions at each step. Under late correction, the lexical effect is a **flat line near 0.25** across the whole continuum. >> The bias favors the word equally at clear steps and at ambiguous steps, because perception and word knowledge stay separate and word knowledge only nudges the reported answer.
20|A second possibility is that **word knowledge shifts the categorical boundary**. In this account, word knowledge changes where the listener switches from one phoneme category to the other. Near an ambiguous onset, the interpretation that forms a real word gets extra support. >> The effect changes **categorization itself**, and not only the reported answer. || This account predicts that the lexical conditions differ **only in the middle** of the continuum. The word-nonword, neutral and nonword-word responses all start near 1 at short VOT and end near 0 at long VOT, because the sound is clear at both ends. Near the boundary, the word-nonword condition crosses later and the nonword-word condition crosses earlier. || The **lexical effect** should therefore form a **peak**. It is near 0 at the endpoints, rises to about 0.35 for the items closest to the boundary, and falls again. The effect is **strongest for the items closest to the category boundary**, because those are the stimuli for which lexical information can help resolve some ambiguity about the initial phoneme. >> At the endpoints, strong acoustic evidence already settles the consonant, so there is little room for word knowledge to help. >> A **flat** lexical effect points to a reporting bias, while a **peaked** lexical effect points to a shift in the boundary itself.
21|The results of **Ganong (1980)** are strikingly consistent with the **second model**, which is the **boundary-shift** account. The proportion of voiced responses in the word-nonword and nonword-word conditions starts near 1 at short VOT and falls toward 0 at long VOT. The two conditions separate most in the middle, near the boundary. >> The **lexical effect** rises from about 0.1 at the endpoints to a peak near **0.3** at the boundary and then falls back. || A peak at the boundary **rules out a simple reporting bias**, because word knowledge matters most where the acoustic evidence is weakest. At the boundary, the word that fits the context tips the balance. At the endpoints, the acoustic evidence decides the answer alone. || Ganong raised a number of possibilities for how this could happen, and he focused on different kinds of **guessing strategies**. A guessing strategy would let listeners use word knowledge only when they were unsure of the sound. Later models would emphasize the potential role of **feedback** in these shifts. >> In **interactive models**, an active word sends support back to its own phonemes. >> The whole-word representation can then shape how an ambiguous part of the word is interpreted during recognition.
22|A **simulation of the Ganong effect in the TRACE model** tracks **phoneme-level and word-level activations** at four moments as the input unfolds. The input begins with a sound that is **ambiguous between /b/ and /p/**. It is followed by **/l/**, the vowel **/ʌ/** and **/g/**, which together make the word **plug**. >> In the **first frame**, only the ambiguous first sound has arrived. **/b/** and **/p/** are both weakly active at about the same level, near 0.15 to 0.2, and word activation is still very low. || In the **second and third frames**, /l/ and the vowel arrive. Partial /b/ evidence supports words beginning with bl, and partial /p/ evidence supports words beginning with pl. Several candidates gain activation together, including **plug**, **blood** and **blush**, because all of them fit the beginning heard so far. >> Phoneme activity for /p/ starts to pull ahead of /b/. || In the **fourth frame**, the final **/g/** arrives. The /g/ fits plug but does not fit the endings of blood or blush. **Plug** becomes the most active word, at about 0.45, while the alternatives fall away. >> **/p/ ends up more active than /b/** at the phoneme level.
23|As the input unfolds over time, **activity accumulates for “plug,”** especially when the **final /g/** arrives. The /g/ is highly inconsistent with **“blood”** and **“blush,”** which were also activated in the third frame. Blood and blush matched the early part of the input, so they were plausible candidates for a while. >> Once /g/ arrives, blood and blush lose support because their endings do not fit. || The TRACE model therefore explains **word effects on phoneme category boundaries** in terms of **top-down processing**. Top-down processing means that higher-level knowledge influences lower-level analysis. **Activity from the lexical level produces feedback to the phoneme level**, and the feedback gives **greater activation to the phoneme consistent with a word**. >> Plug is the winning word, so /p/ receives extra support from it. || The ambiguous beginning becomes clearer because /p/ belongs to the word that the whole sequence supports. Information that arrives at the **end of a word** can reach back and shape how an **earlier sound** is heard. >> This happens through interactions during recognition, and the ambiguous first sound comes to be heard as /p/.
24|The **TRACE** model is a version of the **Interactive Activation model** for **written word recognition**, from **McClelland and Rumelhart (1981)**. Many of the principles are the same in both models. In the Interactive Activation network, **feature** units connect to **letter** units, and letter units connect to **word** units such as **WEAR, WORK, WORD** and **WEAK**. >> Compatible units at different levels support each other, and competing units within a level inhibit each other. >> Active words send support back down to their own letters. || Suppose a reader sees **W, O** and **R** clearly, while ink blots hide part of the final letter. The visible parts of that letter fit both **R** and **K**. W, O and R activate words such as WORK and WORD, but only WORK fits the visible features of the final letter, so WORK becomes the strongest word. >> WORK sends support back to **K**, so the ambiguous letter is perceived as K. This is the same way plug supports /p/ in speech. || A key issue in speech perception is that **the stimuli unfold over time**. A written word is all there at once, while a spoken word arrives one sound after another. TRACE handles this by repeating feature, phoneme and word units at **every point in time**. >> A candidate word can match the beginning, gain activation and then fade when the ending conflicts.
25|**Categorical perception** means that perception is shaped by **category boundaries**. **Given the same physical difference, it is more difficult to distinguish stimuli from the same category than from different categories**. Nothing about cascading or feedback undermines that basic finding. >> Equal acoustic steps have unequal perceptual effects, depending on whether they cross a boundary. || Two findings add to it at the **lexical-phonetic interface**, where words and sounds meet. First, **ambiguity about phoneme identity does have an impact on word recognition**. Sounds with the same label differ in how strongly they activate a competing word, so labels can be categorical while the processing behind them is graded. Second, **word-level information seems to have an impact on phoneme categorization**. >> In the Ganong effect, word knowledge moves the category boundary, and in TRACE that happens through lexical feedback to the phoneme level. || It therefore **cannot be the case that the word recognition system just sits there waiting for the phoneme recognition system to finish doing its job**, like a lumber mill. **Spoken word recognition is more interactive** than that, and it works more like the food web. >> Partial information moves forward and active words send support back down, all while uncertainty is still being resolved.

27|**Spoken word recognition is also very fast**. **Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** used a visual world eye-tracking task to measure how quickly candidate words become active. Participants saw a grid of pictures and geometric shapes and followed spoken instructions to move objects. >> The display included a **beaker**, which was the **referent** or target, along with a **beetle**, a **speaker** and a **carriage**. || The pictures were chosen for their sound overlap with the word **beaker**. **Beetle** is a **cohort competitor** because it shares the beginning of the word. **Speaker** is a **rhyme competitor** because it shares the ending of the word. **Carriage** is **unrelated** and shares no sounds with beaker. || The measure was the probability of fixating each item over the first 1,000 milliseconds after the target word began. The spoken word ended a little before 400 milliseconds on average. Looks to the beaker and the beetle **rose together early**, starting around 200 milliseconds, because both fit the first sounds. >> Looks to the beetle peaked near 0.2 around 500 milliseconds and then fell once the sounds that separate the two words arrived. >> Looks to the beaker kept climbing, passing 0.8 by about 800 milliseconds and approaching 1 by 1,000 milliseconds. >> Looks to the speaker rose later and stayed smaller, peaking near 0.1, and looks to the carriage stayed near zero. || Looks to the target started rising **before the word had finished**, so recognition begins while the word is still unfolding. Words that match the input at a given moment become active, and they drop out as soon as they stop matching.
28|The eye-tracking data from the beaker task can be compared with a **TRACE simulation** of the same task. The word activations of the model over time were converted into predicted fixation probabilities and compared with the real fixations over 1,000 milliseconds after the target began. The TRACE predictions **closely match** the eye movements. >> For the **referent**, the predicted and real looks both rise steadily from about 300 milliseconds and approach 1 by 1,000 milliseconds. >> For the **cohort** competitor, both rise early, peak near 0.17 to 0.2 around 500 milliseconds and then fall. >> For the **rhyme** competitor, both rise later and stay small, peaking near 0.1. || The **granularity** of the simulation is fine, which means it predicts activity moment by moment and not only the final choice. **Eye movements give us an idea about the changes in “activity” for different competitors over time**. That makes it possible to **see cognitive processing before a final response is issued**. || This parallels the **graded effects of category goodness** in the categorical perception task. Sounds that are better or worse examples of a category produce different amounts of competitor activity, even when the final label is the same. >> The developing pattern of looks reveals which words became active along the way and when each one lost out.
