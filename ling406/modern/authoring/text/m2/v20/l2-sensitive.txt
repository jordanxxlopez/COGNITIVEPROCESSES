2|**Second-language learning**, also called **L2 learning**, works as a **model system** for sensitive period research. A model system is a common, easily studied situation that is used to answer questions about a process that is hard to study directly. **Lenneberg (1967)** developed an influential theory that the sensitive period had to do with the development of **cerebral dominance**, which is also described as developmental changes in **language laterality**. >> **Cerebral dominance** means that one hemisphere, usually the left, becomes specialized for language. **Language laterality** is how much language relies on one side of the brain instead of both. >> On this theory, the window for learning language closes once language becomes fixed in the left hemisphere. || Two kinds of evidence motivated the theory. The first was a **rough alignment** of the literature on **brain development** with new insights about **language milestones**. The second was a review of the literature on **resilience after brain injury or surgery**. >> Cortical neurons grow quickly for about two years and then level off, and no measurements were available from age 2 to 18. Lenneberg tied the end of fast brain growth to the start of language learning. >> Children who lost the left hemisphere often went on to develop language, while adults did not regain it. || The theory is about the brain's **maturational state**, which means its stage of physical development. A young, less specialized brain can move language to other tissue, but a mature, specialized brain has fewer ways to do that, so language learning becomes harder.
3|Much of what is known about **first-language (L1) sensitive periods** comes from research on **idiosyncratic cases** or relatively **rare phenomena**. Idiosyncratic means particular to one individual. The evidence includes children recovering from surgery that removed a hemisphere, and Genie, who was cut off from language until age 13. >> Every one of these cases carries other unusual circumstances, such as injury, illness or abuse, so it is hard to tell which factor caused the outcome. || In contrast, **many people learn second languages at different times in their lives**, often with **somewhat limited success**. Second-language learners are common, so researchers can study **large groups** instead of single rare cases. They begin at every age from early childhood to late adulthood, and their final success can be measured. >> Comparing early starters with late starters tells researchers how the age of first exposure relates to final skill. || The **limits of L2 success** have also been used to reason about the **timing of the sensitive or critical period**. If later starters reliably end up at lower levels, then the age where the decline appears could mark when the sensitive period closes. >> The **first language complicates** the comparison, because every L2 learner already knows a language and that knowledge shapes how the new one is learned.
4|**Johnson and Newport (1989)** studied **critical period effects in second-language learning**, and their paper is about the influence of **maturational state** on the acquisition of English as a second language. They started from **Lenneberg (1967)**, who hypothesized that language could be acquired only within a **critical period** running from early infancy until puberty. In its basic form, that hypothesis only has consequences for first-language acquisition, so it was important to find out whether it extends to second-language acquisition too. >> If it does extend, young children should be better second-language learners than adults and should reach higher final levels of proficiency. || They tested that prediction by comparing the English proficiency of **46 native Korean or Chinese speakers** who had arrived in the United States **between the ages of 3 and 39**. By the time of testing, these people had lived in the United States for between 3 and 26 years. They were tested on a wide variety of structures of English grammar with a **grammaticality judgment task**. || Earlier arrivals had a **clear and strong advantage** over later arrivals. Test performance was **linearly related to age of arrival up to puberty**, so each extra year of age at arrival meant a lower score. After puberty, performance was **low but highly variable and unrelated to age of arrival**. >> The age effect was not an accidental result of differences in amount of experience with English, motivation, self-consciousness or American identification. >> The effect appeared on **every grammatical structure tested**, although the structures varied a lot in how well later learners mastered them. The authors concluded that a critical period for language acquisition **extends its effects to second-language acquisition**.
5|**Johnson and Newport (1989)** devised a **grammaticality judgment test**, or **GJT**. A grammaticality judgment is a decision about whether a sentence follows the rules of the language. Participants **listened to recorded sentences** and only had to say whether each sentence was grammatical or not. The test was **untimed**, so participants could take as long as they needed to decide. || **Can you determine what is being tested in each of these example sets?** Each item came in a grammatical version and an ungrammatical version marked with an asterisk, and each pair tests one rule of English. (1a) “The farmer bought two pigs at the market” and (1b) “The farmer bought two pig at the market” test **plural marking** on nouns. (2a) “The little boy is speaking to a policeman” and (2b) “The little boy is speak to a policeman” test the **progressive -ing** form of the verb. || Several pairs on the test target articles and other **determiners**. (6a) “Tom is reading a book in the bathtub” and (6b) “Tom is reading book in the bathtub” test whether a needed article is present. (7a) “The boys are going to the zoo this Saturday” and (7b) “A boys are going to the zoo this Saturday” test whether the determiner agrees with the noun in number. (8a) “Larry went home after the party” and (8b) “Larry went the home after the party” test a spot where English does not allow an article. || Other pairs on the test target questions. (23a) “When will Sam fix his car?” and (23b) “When Sam will fix his car?” test **wh-question** word order, where the helping verb must come before the subject. (24a) “What do they sell at the corner store?” and (24b) “What they sell at the corner store?” test the need for the helping verb “do” in a question. >> Taken together, the pairs cover many different rules of English grammar.
6|The data relate each participant's score on the grammar test to their **age of arrival**, or **AOA**. The score is the number of items correct out of 276, and the participants are split at puberty, which is the end of the critical period in Lenneberg's proposal. Splitting the data this way tests whether the age effect changes after puberty. >> A **correlation**, written r, measures how closely two variables are related, on a scale from −1 to +1. A value near −1 means one variable falls steadily as the other rises, and a value near 0 means little relationship. || **What patterns do we see in these data?** Among **early arrivals**, aged **3 to 15**, the correlation was **−0.87**, so scores fall steadily as age of arrival goes up. Children who arrived between about 3 and 7 score near the maximum, around 270. Scores fall to around 250 for arrivals near age 10 and to around 210 to 230 for arrivals near age 15. >> The strong negative correlation means that age of arrival predicts most of the score in this group. || Among **late arrivals**, aged **17 to 39**, the correlation was only **−0.16**. Scores are **lower overall and widely scattered**, from about 160 to 250. Some late arrivals score around 250, while others of similar age score below 180. >> The weak correlation means that age of arrival predicts little about who does well among adults. >> The whole pattern combines a **strong age effect in childhood** with **lower, highly variable performance in adulthood**.
7|The **Johnson and Newport (1989)** data have three main features. There is a **strong relationship between AOA and accuracy**, where AOA is age of arrival and earlier arrival goes with higher scores. **Early learners, up to about age 8, are often near-perfect**, which means **native-like**, because their scores sit close to those of native English speakers. Late arrivals have a **nearly random distribution of accuracy**, because among adults age of arrival barely predicts the score. || The first two features are about learners who arrived as children. The youngest arrivals reach native-like grammar, so near-perfect scores are the clearest sign of an early advantage. Arrivals between about 8 and 15 reach steadily lower levels, so the **decline begins well before puberty**. >> Adult arrivals scored lower still, with wide differences between individuals. || The phrase **“nearly random distribution”** has a specific meaning here. It means that among late arrivals, age of arrival and score are only weakly related. >> Late arrivals scored **well above chance**, but individual scores varied enormously. Two adults who arrived at the same age could end up far apart.
8|**Johnson and Newport (1989)** argue that these data are consistent with a **sensitive period** for L2 learning, although they use the term **“critical period.”** Their argument has two parts. || First, the **dependence of ultimate attainment on AOA** is consistent with a **loss of language-learning ability over development**. **Ultimate attainment** is the final level of skill a learner reaches. Scores fall steadily as arrival age rises through childhood, so the ability to learn language seems to decline as children mature. >> The timing is **not entirely consistent with Lenneberg's hypothesis**, because Lenneberg put the end of the critical period at puberty and the decline here starts around age 7 or 8. >> Lenneberg was working with a **large gap** in the data between early childhood and adolescence, with no brain measurements between ages 2 and 18, so his timing was only a rough estimate. || Second, **attainment in late acquisition is all over the place**, because adults who arrived at the same age reach very different levels. Johnson and Newport argue that after the sensitive period closes, attainment depends on **idiosyncratic learning abilities** and not on the usual language acquisition processes. Idiosyncratic learning abilities are individual differences in skills such as memory, motivation or analytical skill. >> Children share one natural acquisition process, so they reach similar levels at similar ages. >> Adults have lost that process and must lean on their own abilities, which differ a great deal between people.
9|There are **two versions of the “critical period” hypothesis**, and both start from the same claim that early in life humans have a **superior capacity for acquiring languages**. The **exercise hypothesis** says that if the capacity is not exercised during this time, it will disappear or decline with maturation. If the capacity is exercised, however, further language-learning abilities will remain intact throughout life. >> On the exercise hypothesis, learning a first language early keeps the ability alive, so an adult who learned an L1 normally should still learn an L2 as well as a child does. || The **maturational state hypothesis** says that the capacity disappears or declines with maturation, whether or not it has been used. On this version, even someone who learned an L1 normally loses the superior capacity while growing up, so that person will learn an L2 less well as an adult. || **Which of these do you think JN89 see as more consistent with their data?** **Johnson and Newport (1989)** see the **maturational state hypothesis** as the better fit, and it is the idea named in the title of their paper. Every one of their participants had a normal first language, yet the later starters still learned the L2 less well. >> The exercise hypothesis predicts that these adults would learn as well as children, because they had already exercised the capacity. || **Does this change our interpretation of the L1 critical period data?** The L1 data cannot choose between the two versions, because in cases like Genie the capacity was never exercised, and both hypotheses predict poor learning for such cases. Only second-language data can tell the two versions apart, because only L2 learners have already exercised the capacity before learning another language.
10|**Test design** raises a **methodological issue**, because the **grammaticality judgment task is very different from fluent language use**. In fluent use, people speak and understand quickly without stopping to think about rules. In a grammaticality judgment task, people consider each sentence and decide whether it is correct. >> One thing people worry about is the use of **“metalinguistic” or “explicit” knowledge**. **Metalinguistic knowledge** is conscious knowledge about language, such as knowing the rule that English plurals add “-s.” || **Ellis (2005)** contrasts **implicit knowledge** and **explicit knowledge** on several characteristics. Implicit knowledge brings **intuitive awareness** of linguistic norms, and explicit knowledge brings **conscious awareness**. Implicit knowledge is **procedural**, which means knowing how to use rules and fragments, and explicit knowledge is **declarative**, which means knowing facts about grammatical rules and fragments that can be stated. Implicit knowledge is variable but systematic, and explicit knowledge is often anomalous and inconsistent. >> Implicit knowledge is reached through **automatic processing** and is used in **fluent performance**. Explicit knowledge is reached through effortful **controlled processing** and is used when a speaker is **planning** or having difficulty. >> Implicit knowledge is **nonverbalizable**, so people cannot explain it, and explicit knowledge is **verbalizable**. Implicit knowledge is potentially **only learnable within the critical period**, and explicit knowledge is learnable at **any age**. || An untimed GJT lets people draw on explicit knowledge, so it may miss the implicit knowledge that the critical period is supposed to affect. A learner who consciously applies a taught rule can earn the same score as a learner who knows the rule implicitly, so the score is the same whichever kind of knowledge produced it.
11|One suggestion is that the **task demands of the untimed GJT** make it a better measure of **explicit learning of “metalinguistic” knowledge** than of grammar acquisition. **Ellis (2005)** compared the **design features** of five tests. >> **Imitation** and **oral narrative** rely on feel, are done under time pressure and focus on meaning, so they require no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **timed GJT** relies on feel and is done under time pressure, but it focuses on form, and it requires no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **untimed GJT** relies on rules, has no time pressure and focuses on form, so it does allow metalinguistic knowledge. The **metalanguage** test, which asks people to state rules, also relies on rules, has no time pressure, focuses on form and requires metalinguistic knowledge. || Ellis then related each test to two background measures, which were the **age when people started learning** and their **years of formal instruction**. Only two relationships were significant. Starting age was related to the **timed GJT**, with a correlation of **−0.23**. Years of formal instruction were related to the **untimed GJT with ungrammatical items**, with a correlation of **0.27**. >> The other tests had only weak relationships with either measure. || Consistent with this suggestion, **untimed GJT performance is correlated with years of formal education in L2**. Formal classroom instruction teaches explicit rules, and those rules help on the untimed test. Starting age matters for the timed test, because time pressure limits the use of explicit rules. >> An age effect found with an untimed GJT may partly reflect explicit, taught knowledge and not natural grammar acquisition.
12|**Transfer** raises another issue that is both methodological and theoretical. Transfer means using first-language knowledge while learning a second language. Languages that are **typologically more similar** can benefit more from transfer, where typological similarity is similarity in structure, such as word order and word forms. >> Cognates and loan words, such as French **auto**, **rivière** and **bicyclette**, and Japanese **korokke** and **monoreru**, give learners familiar words to build on. || **Johnson and Newport (1989)** tested people with **English as their L2** and **Chinese or Korean as their L1**. Chinese and Korean are very different from English in grammar and vocabulary, so these learners could get little help from transfer. || **What do you suppose would happen if the languages were more similar?** Speakers of a language close to English could reuse much of what they already know, so they would get much more help from transfer. Their scores might then relate to age in a different way. >> If that happens, the age pattern in the Johnson and Newport data depends on the particular languages involved as well as on age.
13|**Not all aspects of the grammar are equally difficult**. **Johnson and Newport (1989)** compared the mean percentage of errors on **12 types of English rules**. The comparison covered native speakers and groups that arrived at ages 3 to 7, 8 to 10, 11 to 15, 17 to 24 and 25 to 39. The rule types are determiners, plurals, verb subcategorization, past tense, pronouns, particles, auxiliaries, third-person singular, wh-questions, yes/no questions, word order and progressive -ing. >> **Verb subcategorization** is the set of rules for which kinds of words or phrases a verb can take after it. || Native speakers and the youngest arrivals make very few errors on any rule. The latest arrivals, aged 17 to 24 and 25 to 39, make the most errors on **determiners**, around 65 to 68%, and on **plurals**, around 45 to 55%. Errors on **word order** and **progressive -ing** are low for every group, under about 10%. || Some of this looks like the **order in which children learn language**, because progressive -ing and word order are easiest for late learners and are also among the first structures children master. Some of it could be **transfer**. **Chinese has no determiners**, so Chinese speakers have no L1 category for “a” and “the,” and that makes English articles especially hard. >> Children learn **questions** very late, but questions are among the **easiest structures for late learners**. Adults may bring general cognitive skills or L1 knowledge to question forms. >> How hard a structure is depends on the structure itself and on what the learner's first language provides.
14|**Birdsong and Molis (2001)** repeated the Johnson and Newport study with **Spanish speakers learning English**. For the **Chinese and Korean speakers** in the Johnson and Newport data, early arrivals aged 3 to 15 had a strong correlation with age of **−0.87**, and late arrivals aged 17 to 39 had a weak one of **−0.16**. For the **Spanish speakers**, early arrivals aged 3 to 16 had only a weak correlation of **−0.23**, and late arrivals aged 17 to 44 had a strong one of **−0.69**. >> The Spanish speakers had almost the reverse pattern. >> Spanish-speaking **early arrivals** scored near the maximum regardless of age, with only a slight decline even for those arriving around 16. >> Spanish-speaking **late arrivals** declined strongly with age. Their scores fell from around 260 for those arriving near 18 to around 160 for those arriving after 40. || **What are the consequences of this for the idea that AOA effects on GJT performance reflect maturational constraints?** **Maturational constraints** are limits on learning caused by the brain's development. If a fixed maturational schedule controlled learning, the pattern should look the same for every first language. The pattern changed with the learners' first language instead, which a purely maturational account does not predict. >> Spanish is much closer to English than Chinese or Korean, so **transfer** may help Spanish speakers reach native-like grammar even when they arrive late in childhood. The age effect depends on the **language pairing** as well as on age.
15|It is not clear that these findings can even be called “conclusions.” What does hold across studies is that, overall, **L2 attainment is lower for older learners**. >> The age effect in second-language learning is real, but it depends on many factors that are hard to separate. || How much lower depends on three things. It depends on **what constructions are tested**, because some structures, such as determiners, are far harder for late learners than others, such as word order. It depends on **how they are tested**, because timed and untimed tests can disagree when the untimed test leaves room for explicit knowledge. It depends on **language pairings**, because Spanish speakers and Chinese or Korean speakers have different age patterns in learning English. || It is also **hard to dissociate** several factors that usually go together. **Age of acquisition** is hard to separate from the **amount and quality of experience**, because younger arrivals usually get more years of exposure and more immersion, such as schooling in the language. **Transfer effects** are hard to separate from **L2 acquisition**, because L2 performance reflects what was learned and also what carries over from the L1. **Performance** is hard to separate from **competence**, generally speaking. >> **Competence** is underlying knowledge of a language, and **performance** is how well that knowledge is used on a given task. A test score measures performance, so it may not reveal competence directly.
