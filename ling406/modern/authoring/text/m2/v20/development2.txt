2|Speech is **messy** because **there are no physical boundaries between words** in the speech signal. **Word segmentation** is the job of working out where one word stops and the next one starts in continuous speech. In an utterance such as “I hope it's in my desk,” the sound energy of each word flows straight into the next word, so no silent gap separates the words. >> The utterance lasts about 1,800 milliseconds, and the energy of one word runs into the next with no blank space between them. || Where there are **pauses** in the speech stream, they come from **closures** that are **as likely to happen within words as between words**. A **closure** is a moment when the vocal tract is fully blocked, as for the consonants /p/, /t/, /d/ and /k/. Very little sound escapes during a closure, so a brief silence appears. >> In “hope” and “it's,” these silences fall inside words or at their edges, so they do not match the word boundaries. >> A listener who treated every silence as a boundary would split some words and merge others. || We are still **really good at finding words** in this “sea of sounds,” and adults hear separate words with no effort. **How do we learn to do this?** That is the harder question, because adults can lean on the words they already know. >> Infants have to discover the words in the stream before they can learn what those words mean.
3|Listeners have **several cues to word boundaries**, but no single cue works for every word. Some words **appear in isolation**, as in “Hello,” “No,” “Please,” “Go” and “Stop,” so their boundaries are obvious. **Not all words** are heard alone, so most words have to be found inside longer utterances. >> A second cue is **phonotactics**, which describes the sound sequences a language allows. **Some sequences of phonemes are very unlikely to occur within words**, so they suggest a break. >> In “wordboundary” the sequence /db/ almost never occurs inside an English word, and in “speechcode” the sequence /tʃk/ suggests a break in the same way. The word “movement” calls for caution, because its /vm/ sequence might suggest a break although movement is one word. || **Stress patterns** give another cue, because **in English most words are stressed on the first syllable**. A stressed syllable is louder, longer or higher in pitch. **Jusczyk and colleagues (1999)** familiarized **7.5-month-old infants** with one word and then played them passages. The kingdom passage kept using “kingdom,” as in “Your kingdom is in a faraway place,” and the device passage kept using “device,” as in “My new red device makes ice cream.” >> Infants familiarized with “kingdom,” which has first-syllable stress, **showed a preference for the kingdom passage**. >> Infants familiarized with “device,” which has second-syllable stress, **did not show the same kind of preference**. || In connected speech, **stress may help signal the beginnings of words**. An infant who starts a new word at every stressed syllable finds kingdom easily but cuts device in the wrong place. >> This cue **will not work in every language, or for every word in English**, because languages put stress in different places.
4|**Transitional probabilities** offer another cue to word boundaries, and infants can use them through **statistical learning**. Statistical learning means picking up patterns in how often things occur together. Consider the phrase “pretty baby,” which has a word boundary between “ty” and “ba.” >> A learner who counts how often each syllable follows each other syllable can find the joints in the speech stream. || In English words, the syllable **/ti/** is **very strongly predicted by the syllable /prɪ/**. After “pre,” the next syllable is very often “ty,” because pretty is a common word. This is **less likely to be true for syllable pairs across words**, because many different words can follow “ty,” so “ba” is poorly predicted. >> The syllables “ty” and “ba” go together only when pretty happens to be followed by baby. || The comparison can be written with **frequencies** of occurrence. Dividing the frequency of “pretty” by the frequency of “pre” gives the chance that the syllable “pre” is followed by the syllable “ty” in English. Dividing the frequency of “tyba” by the frequency of “ty” gives the chance that the syllable “ty” is followed by the syllable “ba” in English. The first value is **far greater** than the second. >> Syllables inside pretty predict each other strongly, and syllables that straddle pretty and baby predict each other weakly. >> Syllable pairs inside a word recur together again and again, while pairs that cross a word boundary recur much less reliably.
5|Stated more abstractly, **the likelihood of syllable B following syllable A is greater within words than between words**. That likelihood is the **transitional probability** from A to B. Inside a word the same syllables always come in the same order, so the probability is high. At a word boundary almost any word can come next, so the probability is lower. >> A drop in transitional probability is a good guess for where a word boundary falls. || This cue is **probabilistic**, so it works most of the time but not every time. The sentence “What's that in the road a/head?” can be heard as “a head” or as “ahead.” The sounds are identical, so the meaning of the whole sentence decides the boundary. >> “I scream you scream we all scream for ice cream” plays on “I scream” and “ice cream” having almost the same sounds. One sequence of sounds can be split into words in two ways. || Transitional probabilities tip the odds toward one way of splitting the stream, and they work alongside stress, phonotactics and words heard alone. When the cues agree, segmentation is easy. >> When cues clash or are missing, as with “a head” and “ahead,” listeners fall back on context.
6|**Saffran and colleagues (1996)** separated **statistical information from other cues**. They wanted to find out whether **transitional probabilities** alone can be used to identify words. The stimuli were **tightly controlled** so that every other cue to a word boundary was removed. >> If infants still find the words, they must have used transitional probabilities, because nothing else was left to use. || Three controls did this work. The speech was **monotone**, with **no changes in pitch, duration or amplitude**, so stress could not mark where words begin. Every syllable was a **simple CV syllable**, one consonant followed by one vowel, so there were **no phonotactic cues** to word boundaries. The syllables were **presented continuously** with **no pauses**, so silence could not signal a boundary. >> Amplitude means loudness, and it is one of the things that makes a syllable sound stressed. >> With only CV syllables no unusual consonant clusters ever occurred. || The researchers built an **artificial language** from a set of **three-syllable “words,”** such as tokibu, gikoba and gopila. These words were joined in random order into one long stream. >> The only clue to where each word began and ended was the transitional probability between syllables.
7|The stream in the artificial language has **no breaks at all**, as in “tokibugikobagopilatipolutokibu,” “gopilatipolutokibugikobagopila” and “gikobatokibugopilatipolugikoba.” This is exactly what the infant hears, with no spaces and no pauses. Four words, tokibu, gikoba, gopila and tipolu, are **hidden in the stream**, and each recurs many times in random order. >> To an adult reader the stream looks like nonsense, because nothing marks where words begin and end except the order of the syllables. || Even with no marked boundaries, the syllables follow **regular patterns**. Inside tokibu, “to” is always followed by “ki,” and “ki” is always followed by “bu.” After “bu,” though, the stream may go on with “gi,” “go” or “ti,” depending on which word comes next. >> Some syllable pairs are fully predictable and others are not. || Everything needed to find the words is in the stream. A learner who **keeps track of which syllables follow which** can discover the predictable pairs, so the words come out of the statistics of the sequence. >> The infant must discover the words only by tracking the order of syllables.
8|Marking each of the four words in its own color makes their positions in the same stream visible to a reader. **Tokibu** is red, **gikoba** is green, **gopila** is pink and **tipolu** is purple, and each appears many times. The infant gets no such marking, because the infant hears only one unbroken run of syllables. >> The colors make the statistical structure visible to us, but the signal itself contains no spaces. || Each word keeps the **same inner order** every time, while its **neighbors keep changing**. The syllables “to,” “ki” and “bu” always come in that order, but tokibu may be followed by gikoba, gopila or tipolu. Transitional probabilities are high inside each word and lower across word edges for that reason. >> At the end of each word, the next syllable could start any of the other words. || The word edges are exactly the places where **transitional probability drops**. A learner who notices where predictability drops recovers where one word ends and the next begins. >> Learning which syllables reliably belong together is how the learner finds the boundaries that the colors mark.
9|**Testing for an effect of transitional probabilities** takes some care. After listening to a long stream of syllables, it is trivial that infants are more familiar with sequences they have heard than with ones they have not. Infants could prefer heard sequences only because they are familiar, without using transitional probabilities at all. >> The test added two details that remove this possibility. || First, **half of the words were repeated more frequently** than the other half. Second, the test for familiarity compared words with **“part-words”** that were **matched for frequency of occurrence** with real words. A **part-word** is a three-syllable sequence that crosses a word boundary, so it joins the end of one word to the start of the next. >> Because frequency is equal, familiarity cannot explain any difference between a word and a part-word. || Take the stream “tokibu gikoba gopila gikoba tokibu gopila tokibu gikoba gikoba tokibu.” The word **GOPILA** and the part-word **BU-GIKO** both appear **twice**, so they are equally frequent. The probability of **GI given BU** is **2 out of 4**, while the transitional probabilities inside GOPILA are **both 2 out of 2**. >> BU occurs four times, once at the end of every tokibu, but only two of those are followed by GI, which gives 0.5. >> GO is followed by PI both times and PI is followed by LA both times, which gives 1. >> If infants treat gopila differently from bu-giko, the difference must come from how predictable the syllables are.
10|The test produced a **novelty effect**, because **infants looked longer in the direction of “part-word” stimuli**. Mean looking time was about **6.8 seconds** for words and about **7.6 seconds** for part-words. The difference was statistically significant. >> Part-words held the infants' attention longer than words did. || Longer looking toward part-words is a **novelty preference**. Infants tend to look longer at whatever seems new. The words had grown familiar during listening, so they were less interesting. >> The part-words seemed less familiar, although infants had heard them just as often as the words. >> The infants must have noticed that the syllables in part-words do not reliably go together. || This result suggests that infants **had learned the statistical regularities of the speech stream** and **were not merely picking up on simple frequency of occurrence**. Words and part-words were matched for frequency, so only their transitional probabilities could separate them. >> After a short exposure, infants pulled units out of a continuous stream using only how predictable the syllables were.
11|**Identifying word referents** is the problem that comes after segmentation. A **referent** is whatever a word refers to. **Now that children can “find” the words, how do they learn what they mean?** They need more evidence, because segmentation gives a recurring chunk of sound and says nothing about its meaning. >> A child may hear “cat” while looking at a cat. >> Recognizing the sound pattern “cat” is one achievement, and knowing that it names the furry animal is a separate one. || Any scene holds many objects, parts, properties and actions. The learner cannot tell from the sound alone which of these the speaker has in mind, so the learner needs further evidence about reference. >> Segmentation solves the problem of grouping sounds, and a separate process links that group of sounds to what it names. || Learning a word means **linking its form to the speaker's intended meaning**. The child gathers that evidence from the situation in which the word is used. >> An infant can recognize a word form such as cat before knowing what it names.
12|**The inductive problem of early word learning** is that a single experience fits many possible meanings. **Induction** means reasoning from particular examples to a general rule, and the rule here is the meaning of a word. A child hears a new word while watching a cat, and the word could mean many things. >> It might mean **CAT**, **ANIMAL** or **PET**, which are categories at different levels, or it might be **FELIX**, the name of this particular cat. >> It might name a property such as **FURRY** or **ALIVE**, an action such as **WALKING**, or a part such as **WHISKERS** or **LEG**. || All the candidate meanings together make up the **hypothesis space**. Each candidate fits what the child sees, so nothing in one scene singles out the meaning the speaker intends. >> Every one of these meanings fits the situation equally well. || This is a version of **Quine's “gavagai” problem**. In Quine's example, a linguist studying an unknown language hears a speaker say “gavagai” as a rabbit runs by. “Gavagai” might mean rabbit, but it might also mean undetached rabbit parts, a stage of a rabbit or something else. >> Children meet this problem with every new word and still learn words fast, so something must narrow the possibilities. >> **Joint attention**, **intentional cues** from the speaker and **default word learning strategies** do this narrowing.
13|**How do children acquire word meanings?** One way is through **joint attention**, which is **sharing an attentional focus with another person**. Two people look at the same object, and each knows that the other is attending to it too. >> When a child and an adult attend to the same object, a word spoken by the adult is likely to be about that object. || **Gaze following emerges around 9 months of age**. **Gaze following** means looking where someone else is looking. When an adult looks over at an object, the infant follows the adult's eyes to that object. >> Gaze following lets infants join the adult's focus. || Gaze following **signals an understanding of reference**. The infant grasps that the adult's attention is aimed at something specific. If the adult then says a word, the shared object becomes the most likely referent. >> Joint attention shrinks the **hypothesis space** for that reason, because it ties the word to what the speaker is attending to. >> Understanding that others attend to particular things is a foundation for learning that words refer to what speakers are attending to.
14|**Joint attention optimizes word learning**, according to **Baldwin and colleagues**. Two groups of infants heard a novel word while they played with an object. In the **joint attention group**, an **adult interacting with the baby** labeled the object the baby was playing with. In the **no joint attention group**, a **voice from behind a screen** labeled the object the baby was playing with. >> In both groups the word came while the infant was focused on the same object. >> The groups differed only in whether a visible person was engaged with the infant and the object. || The result was that **18-month-olds learned the novel word in the joint attention group but not in the no joint attention group**. Hearing a word while seeing an object was not enough for learning. >> The hidden voice gave the same word at the same moment, so the difference in learning came from the visible, engaged speaker. || Infants needed evidence that a **speaker meant the word to refer to that object**. The social exchange around the word supported learning, so joint attention does more than place a word next to an object. >> A word and an object occurring together did not produce learning without that evidence.
15|**How do children narrow the hypothesis space for new words?** One way is through **intentional cues from the speaker**, such as **eye gaze direction**. An **intentional cue** is a signal of what the speaker means to refer to. >> When a speaker looks toward one toy, the direction of the eyes marks which object the speaker is attending to. || **Baldwin** taught new words to **16 to 17-month-olds** and **18 to 19-month-olds** in two conditions. In **follow-in labeling**, the adult looked at the **toy that the infant was focused on** while saying the new word. In **discrepant labeling**, the adult looked at a **toy in a bucket**, a different toy from the infant's, while saying the new word. >> In follow-in labeling the infant and the adult look at the same toy, so either cue points to the right object. >> In discrepant labeling the infant's focus and the speaker's focus point to different toys. || The discrepant condition separates the infant's focus from the speaker's focus. Infants who attach a new word to whatever they are looking at would link it to their own toy. Infants who follow the speaker's gaze would link it to the toy in the bucket. >> Comparing the two conditions tests whether infants treat the **speaker's gaze as evidence** of what the word means.
16|**Gaze direction** helps a child work out which object a speaker is naming when several objects are present. A speaker who looks at one object while saying a new word ties the word to that object. The child can follow the speaker's eyes to the **intended referent**. >> A child who notices where the speaker looks can work out which of several objects a new word names. || A speaker's focus can be **different from the child's own focus**. Following it means using information about someone else's attention. The child can then attach a new word to an object the child was not looking at when the word was spoken. >> Gaze direction is a cue that works even when the child is focused elsewhere. || Gaze **narrows the possible meanings** although the sound of the word gives no clue. The child combines the new word with evidence of what the speaker intends. >> Following gaze rests on understanding that a speaker's eyes point to what the speaker means. >> The result is a far more specific mapping than linking the word to everything in view.
17|Intentional cues from the speaker also include **affective and behavioral cues**. **Affective cues** are emotional expressions, and **behavioral cues** are the speaker's actions. An expression can reveal whether an object is the one the speaker was looking for. >> An action, such as reaching for something, can reveal what the speaker wants. || **Akhtar and colleagues** found that **infants used adults' facial expression to identify the intended referent of a novel word**. The adult's emotional reaction to each object signaled whether it was the one the adult meant. Infants linked the new word to the object that the adult's expression picked out. >> These cues add to gaze as evidence of what the speaker means. || Several objects may be present when a word is spoken, and the speaker's reaction can **single out one of them**. A satisfied expression signals that the speaker found the wanted object. A disappointed expression signals that an object is not the one the speaker meant. >> Word learning involves understanding the goal behind the interaction for that reason.
18|A speaker's **emotional reaction** works as an **intentional cue** that can pick out the intended object from several candidates. A reaction of satisfaction or excitement can tie one object to what the speaker was trying to find or do. The learner can use that tie to interpret a new label. >> A word may be said near several objects that are not all equally likely referents. || **Intention is conveyed through more than gaze direction**. Facial expressions and actions also reveal what a speaker means. The helpful information lies in how the adult responds to each object. >> Connecting the label to that response shrinks the **hypothesis space** of the word, which is the set of meanings it might have. || Infants who use emotional reactions to learn words are **following the speaker's goals**. They understand that the speaker is searching for something and that the word names the thing the speaker wants. >> The word attaches to the object that meets the goal. >> Objects that disappoint the speaker are ruled out.
19|Children also bring **word learning strategies** to the task. These are **default strategies that children bring to the word learning situation**. A **default** is a starting assumption that holds until evidence says otherwise. >> These strategies **function to narrow the hypothesis space for new words**, so children have a place to start when other cues are weak. || The **whole object assumption** is one example. It leads a child to expect a new word to name **a whole object**, not one of its parts, its color, its texture or an action it is doing. >> A child who hears a new word while looking at a cat will favor “cat” over “whiskers,” “furry” or “walking.” || This assumption **rules out many unlikely meanings at once**, so it speeds learning. It also fits the way adults usually name objects for children. >> It is only a starting preference, so later evidence can override it. >> Children do eventually learn words for parts and properties once the evidence points that way.
20|**Mutual exclusivity**, from **Markman (1989)**, is another word learning strategy. **As a default, objects can have only one name**. The implication is that **new words map to nameless objects**. >> Mutual exclusivity **emerges during the second half of the second year**, which is roughly between 18 and 24 months of age. || Suppose a child knows the word “dog” but has no word for a parrot, and both animals are in view. When the child hears a new word, the child takes it to mean the parrot, because the dog already has a name. >> The child does not use any gaze or emotional cue to make this choice. >> The strategy uses the child's existing vocabulary to narrow the possibilities. || Mutual exclusivity works together with the **whole object assumption**. The whole object assumption says that a new word names an object, and mutual exclusivity says that it names an object with no name yet. >> Mutual exclusivity is a default, so later learning can override it when an object turns out to have more than one name.
21|Under **mutual exclusivity**, a child who hears an unfamiliar word gives it to an object that **has no known name yet**. An object the child can already name is an unlikely target for a new word. The choice comes from comparing the objects that are in view. >> The child works out the meaning from the objects present and the words already known. || **Prior vocabulary** decides which objects count as already named. Two children in the same scene can guess differently if they know different words. >> The strategy builds directly on what the child has already learned. || Vocabulary growth **builds on itself** for that reason. A child who knows many names can rule out many objects. The unnamed objects that remain become the likeliest referents for new words. >> Every word the child knows helps narrow the meaning of the next new word.
22|**How do children start putting words together?** In production, it can be hard to tell what is going on when children produce only **one, two or three words at a time**. Very different processes can produce the same short utterance, so the question has no quick answer. >> A child might say “Up!” or “Pick you up!” or “Mommy up!” || **Menn** distinguishes among four kinds of early utterances. The first is **“just a bunch of relevant words,”** where the child strings together words that fit the situation with no grammar linking them. The second is **formulas**, which are phrases memorized as whole units without the child knowing they have separate parts. The third is **word combinations**, in which the child joins separate words productively to make new combinations. The fourth is **stable constructions**, which are consistent patterns the child uses to build utterances and are **not necessarily adult-like**. >> “Pick you up!” sounds more advanced, but it may be a formula copied whole from adult speech. >> “Mommy up!” is shorter, but it may be a true combination of two separate words. || Utterance length alone cannot tell which process produced an utterance. In practice, **a full description of the context** in which an utterance is produced is **necessary but often not sufficient** to distinguish among these. >> Context reveals what the child intends and how the utterance is used across situations. >> Even with full context, the source of a given utterance can stay uncertain.
23|**Comprehension** and **production** develop at different rates. **Even children at the one-word stage in production are able to use basic transitive word order in comprehension**. In the **one-word stage** a child speaks only in single words. >> A **transitive** sentence has a verb with a subject and an object. English transitive order puts the actor first and the one acted on after the verb. || The **structure-mapping account** of **early syntactic bootstrapping**, from **Gertner and Fisher**, describes how children do this. **Syntactic bootstrapping** means using sentence structure to help learn meaning. From the input sentence “The boy is tickling the girl!” the child builds a **partial sentence representation** that records the verb tickle with boy as the first noun and girl as the second noun. >> From the observed scene of a boy tickling a girl, the child builds **conceptual representations** of possible events. These are giggling with the girl as agent, tickling with the boy as agent and the girl as patient, and playing with the boy or the girl as agent. >> An **agent** performs an action, and a **patient** is affected by it. || A **one-to-one mapping between nouns and participant roles** lines up the two nouns with the two participants in the tickling event. The sentence has two nouns, so it fits the event with two participants, and the order of the nouns suggests who does what. >> A child who says only single words can still use noun order to tell who did what, so comprehension runs ahead of production. >> Matching nouns to the participants in a scene helps the child work out what the verb means.
24|A **generalization test** checks whether children have truly learned the **transitive structure**. If children have really learned the transitive structure, they will **generalize it to new verbs**. A child who has only memorized particular sentences will be stuck with an unfamiliar verb, but a child who knows the structure can use word order with any verb. >> Choosing the scene that fits “The duck is gorping the bunny” from two novel actions requires knowing that the first noun is the doer and the second is the one acted on. >> The verb “gorp” is made up, so only the order of the nouns can help. || **Gertner and colleagues (2006)** found that **two-year-olds can understand this**. Two scenes appeared side by side in each of two 8-second test trials. In one scene a person dressed as a duck pushed a person dressed as a bunny in a wagon, and in the other scene the bunny acted on the duck. Children **looked significantly longer at the duck acting on the rabbit** than at the reverse. >> Before the first trial, during a 5-second blank screen, children heard “Hey look! The duck’s gonna gorp the bunny!” >> While the two scenes were in view, children heard “The duck is gorping the bunny! See?” as they watched. >> Before the second trial, during a 4-second blank screen, children heard “The duck gorped the bunny! Find gorping!” || Children at this age typically have a **mean length of utterance (MLU)** of **around 2**. MLU is the average number of **morphemes**, which are meaningful words or word parts, in each utterance. Their three-morpheme utterances might be like “Duck gorp bunny.” >> Their own speech is short and incomplete, yet they understand the full transitive structure, so comprehension of grammar runs ahead of production.
