2|**Babies are overrated** as language learners. When people talk about **sensitive periods** for a second language, or **L2**, they often say that infants, toddlers and children are “better” at learning language than adults, and it is important not to get carried away with that idea. A typical child by 18 months uses at least 20 words, understands “in and out” and “off and on,” and answers simple questions such as “What’s that?” with words or gestures. >> Imagine an adult who only knew about 20 words after 18 months of language instruction. That adult would be considered a very poor learner. || **Adults initially learn more quickly than children**. This is due largely to **transfer from L1**, the first language, and to **better general cognitive skills**. >> Transfer lets adults build on what they already know about how language works. General cognitive skills include memory, attention and reasoning. || Where adults do worse is **ultimate attainment**, which is the final level of skill a learner eventually reaches. Adults **rarely become native-like** in a second language, and this may also be due in part to transfer. >> The first language that helps adults start quickly may also hold them back from native-like skill. Speed of early learning and final level of success are different measures, so children and adults compare differently on each.
3|Some of the **language milestones** of a toddler are actually **general conceptual abilities**. Examples are the ability to deal with **spatial relations between objects** and the ability to form **event representations**, which are mental representations of who does what in an event. A toddler who reaches these milestones is building new ideas about the world as well as new words. >> Understanding “in and out” and “off and on” depends on understanding spatial relations between objects. Pretend play, such as giving teddy a drink, depends on event representations. || **Later learners of a second language** have a lot more of this conceptual knowledge in place. Because the concepts are already there, later learners can focus on learning how those concepts are represented in the **target language**, which is the language being learned. >> An adult learning Spanish already understands what “in” and “on” mean as concepts. The job of the adult is mainly to learn how the new language expresses them, which is a smaller job than building concepts and words together. || Later learners also **already know another language**, and that has an impact on how the second language is learned. The first language provides ready-made ways of expressing concepts. >> Some of those ways fit the second language and help, while others clash with it and cause errors.
4|**Transfer** is the use of native language (L1) processes and representations for a second language (L2). A learner comes to the L2 with a fully working language system already in place. Because of that, the learner brings L1 habits for hearing sounds, building sentences and finding words into the L2. >> Every habit built for the first language comes along into the second, whether it helps or hurts. || Transfer can have both **positive and negative impacts** on second-language learning and performance. **Positive transfer** happens when an L1 pattern matches what the L2 requires, so shared sounds, words and structures give the learner a head start. **Negative transfer** happens when an L1 pattern conflicts with the L2. >> With negative transfer the learner makes errors or finds the L2 pattern hard to learn. The errors appear where the two languages differ, because the habits of the learner point the wrong way there. || A cartoon of two men at a bar has one of them asking “Do you not be happy with me as the translator of the books of you?” The sentence uses English words, but its structure follows the grammar of another language. >> Word-by-word translation carries over the word order and phrasing of the first language, so the sentence is an example of negative transfer in grammar.
5|Transfer affects **phonetic and phonological learning**, which is the learning of speech sounds. **Categorical perception** leads to **acquired similarity** for sounds in the same category. Acquired similarity means that sounds a language groups into one category come to sound more alike through experience with that language. >> Sounds from many, many languages can be heard at the UCLA phonetics archive. || Hindi has **minimal pairs** that make the problem clear, where a minimal pair is two words that differ in only one sound. In each pair one word has a **dental** consonant, made with the tongue against the teeth, and the other has a **retroflex** consonant, made with the tongue curled back toward the roof of the mouth. >> Two words both sound like “tap” to English ears, and one means hot while the other means a kind of beat. Two words both sound like “dab,” and one means pressure while the other means a kind of fruit. Two words both sound like “ari,” and one means saw, the tool, while the other means crooked. || **Native English speakers** tend to use the English **alveolar** category to interpret both dental and retroflex consonants. An alveolar consonant, such as English /t/ or /d/, is made with the tongue on the ridge just behind the teeth. English has only the alveolar category, so both Hindi sounds get mapped onto it and seem the same. >> In this case **transfer makes learning the L2 contrast more difficult**. The English category was learned successfully, but it now hides a difference that Hindi uses to tell words apart.
6|Transfer also affects **grammar learning**. **Classifiers** are a somewhat unusual grammatical feature across languages. A classifier is a word that must appear between a number or demonstrative and a noun, and it is chosen according to the kind of thing the noun names. >> The structure is a demonstrative pronoun, then a nominal classifier, then a noun, as in **yí gè rén**, “one person,” where **gè** is the classifier. || Chinese has many classifiers, and each one is used with a particular kind of noun. **Gè** is the general classifier, **bǎ** is used with objects that have a handle, **běn** with books and similar objects, and **kuài** with objects that are divided into pieces. **Liàng** is used with wheeled vehicles, **tái** with devices and gadgets, **zhāng** with flat objects, and **zhī** with long, thin objects. >> **Xiē** is used to indicate an indeterminate amount, or with the plural of pronouns and demonstratives. || **Native English speakers learning Chinese have difficulty learning classifiers**. In English, when we want to say “a chair,” “that dog,” “the car” or “one book,” we do not worry much about noun semantics when picking a determiner or pronoun. In Chinese, different classifiers are required by different nouns, so the learner must notice properties such as shape and function. >> One way to think about this is that English speakers have **learned to ignore the different cues for classifiers** as part of learning English grammar. Learning Chinese requires paying attention again to cues that English taught them to ignore.
7|**Grammatical gender** is another example where transfer causes problems. Grammatical gender is **different from social or cultural gender**, because it is a system that sorts nouns into classes, often called masculine and feminine. There is nothing “male” about a book or “female” about liberty, yet in French **le livre**, the book, is masculine and **la liberté**, liberty, is feminine. >> Other masculine examples are le cheval (the horse), le chien (the dog) and le bruit (the noise). Other feminine examples are la colombe (the dove), la chemise (the shirt) and la maison (the house). || Grammatical gender has implications for **agreement**, which means that related words must match the gender of the noun. Pronouns, adjectives and some forms of verbs all need to be coordinated with noun gender. English has no grammatical gender on nouns, so English speakers have no habit of tracking it. >> A learner has to know the gender of each noun in order to use the right forms of all the related words. || French is famous for having **synonyms with different gender**. **Auto** and **voiture** both mean car, **rivière** and **fleuve** both mean river, and **vélo** and **bicyclette** both mean bicycle, and in each pair one word is masculine and the other is feminine. Words with the same meaning can have different genders, so gender has to be **learned word by word**. >> Common word endings give clues to gender. Masculine nouns often end in -age, -r, -t or -isme, as in le fromage (the cheese), le professeur (the teacher), le chat (the cat) and le capitalisme. Feminine nouns often end in -ie, -ion, -ité or -tié, -ance, or -nne, -mme and -lle, as in la boulangerie (the bakery), la nation, la fraternité (brotherhood), la balance (the scales) and la fille (the girl).
8|**Transfer is not all bad**. Vocabulary learning in some L1 and L2 situations is aided by the **similarity of words across languages**. French **auto** resembles English “auto” and refers to a car, French **rivière** resembles English “river,” and French **bicyclette** resembles English “bicycle.” >> Japanese **コロッケ (korokke)** refers to a croquette, and Japanese **モノレール (monoreru)** refers to a monorail. || Sometimes the similarity is due to **shared etymology**, where etymology is the history of where a word comes from. Words with a shared origin are called **cognates**. French rivière and English river are cognates because both come from the same older word. >> Sometimes the similarity is due to more recent **“borrowing.”** A **loan word** is a word that one language takes from another, and Japanese korokke and monoreru were borrowed from European languages with their sounds adapted to Japanese. || Cognates and loan words give **positive transfer** for vocabulary. The learner already knows a similar form with a similar meaning, so the new word comes with a familiar anchor. >> A learner who knows river can quickly link it to rivière, because the two words share sounds and meaning. That link makes the new word easier to store and to recall.
9|**Not all similar-sounding words are cognates**. The **bias** to interpret L2 words as related to similar-sounding L1 words can be very strong, and sometimes the result is embarrassing. A word that looks or sounds like a word in another language but means something different is called a **false friend**. >> Spanish “Arnold está embarazado” does not mean that Arnold is embarrassed. Spanish **embarazado** means **pregnant**, so an English speaker relying on the similar sound of embarrassed would get the meaning badly wrong. || False friends reveal how automatically learners link L2 words to similar L1 words. The link happens before the learner checks the meaning, so a familiar-sounding word is assumed to mean the familiar thing. >> Embarazado sounds like embarrassed, so English speakers assume that it means embarrassed. || The same tendency drives both **helpful and harmful transfer**. When the meanings match, as with cognates, the link speeds up learning. When the meanings differ, the same link leads to errors. >> A helpful cognate and a misleading false friend differ only in whether the meanings actually match.
10|**De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** tested in the lab whether people **use cognates** in “second language” learning. They used **paired-associate learning** of made-up words. In paired-associate learning, participants study pairs of items and later have to recall one item when they are given the other. >> The participants were Dutch speakers. The Dutch words stood in for their first language, and the made-up words stood in for a new second language. || The made-up words varied in their **similarity to their Dutch associates**, so some were cognate-like and some were not. They also varied in **concreteness**. A concrete word names something that can be seen or touched, and an abstract word names an idea. >> Crossing the two factors gave four kinds of pairs, so the effect of each factor can be measured separately. The pairs were **gitaar** and **gettar** (“guitar,” concrete, cognate), **tijger** and **duunze** (“tiger,” concrete, non-cognate), **domein** and **domaan** (“domain,” abstract, cognate), and **herstel** and **groop** (“repair,” abstract, non-cognate). || Participants were simply presented with pairs of Dutch words and their non-word paired associates and asked to learn these pairs. They were then **tested a week later on retention**. >> Retention means how well they remembered the pairs after a delay without further study.
11|**De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** found that **cognates are learned more rapidly and also remembered more easily**. Recall was tested over three training sessions, and session 4 came after one week with no training. Recall increased across the three training sessions for every kind of word, but the starting points were very different. >> **Concrete cognates** were recalled 69.7% of the time in session 1, and **concrete non-cognates** started at only 43.7%. **Abstract cognates** started at 42.7%, and **abstract non-cognates** started lowest at just 19.0%. By session 3 concrete cognates reached 93.4%, concrete non-cognates 92.0%, abstract cognates 86.0% and abstract non-cognates 72.7%. || The week without training separated the kinds of words again. Concrete cognates dropped only to 78.4% and concrete non-cognates to 69.0%. Abstract cognates dropped to 68.4%, while abstract non-cognates fell sharply to **36.0%**. >> Responses were also faster for cognates in every session. In session 1 concrete cognates took about 1,880 milliseconds and abstract non-cognates took about 3,641 milliseconds. || This is evidence that **cognate status can contribute to the very earliest stages of word learning** in a second language. The cognate advantage was largest in the first session, when learning had just begun, and it reappeared after a week, when non-cognates were forgotten more. >> Concreteness also had a large effect, and the two factors add together, so abstract non-cognates were the hardest of all. One explanation for concreteness effects is called **context availability**, the idea that concrete words are easier to link with a supporting context.
12|**Is this a good experiment?** It is, because **De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** did not rely only on a test that cognates could pass without real learning. If they had only tested **receptive vocabulary**, meaning how easily participants could produce the Dutch word given its associate, the result would not be very impressive. In receptive testing the learner sees the new word and has to supply the familiar Dutch word. >> For cognate-like pairs the new word already looks like the Dutch word, so a participant could answer correctly by noticing the resemblance without having learned the pair. || The meaning of **gitaar**, **tijger** and **domein** might be guessed without any Dutch training, because they resemble English guitar, tiger and domain. The concrete cognates in the study were just as close to their Dutch partners. >> The pairs included broer and breur, brood and brodde, jongen and jange, lepel and lippel, lichaam and lechem, naald and nadel, paraplu and parredu, and rivier and riffer. They also included schaar and skare, stier and stire, trein and trinen, varken and vark, viool and vialo, and vrouw and vrauk. || The study also involved a test of **expressive vocabulary**, which the authors call **productive testing**. In productive testing the learner sees the Dutch word and has to produce the new made-up word. The learner has to remember the exact new form, such as gettar, out of many similar possibilities. >> Productive testing removes the shortcut, so a cognate advantage there reflects **real learning of new forms**.
13|**What did participants actually learn in the study?** They learned the new word forms themselves, and **De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** could tell because they compared **productive testing** with **receptive testing**. Both were measured immediately after learning, in session 3, and at retest a week later, in session 4. The measure that matters is how much recall dropped over the week. >> Productive scores were lower than receptive scores, because generating a new form is harder than recognizing a familiar one. || In **productive testing**, learners had to produce the new word. Concrete words fell from 92.7% to 73.7%, a drop of 19.0 points, and abstract words fell from 79.3% to 52.2%, a drop of 27.1 points. Cognates fell from 89.7% to 73.3%, a drop of **16.4 points**, while non-cognates fell from 82.3% to 52.5%, a drop of **29.8 points**. >> In **receptive testing**, learners had to give the Dutch word. Concrete words dropped 8.8 points, from 97.7% to 88.9%, and abstract words dropped 26.6 points, from 91.5% to 64.9%. Cognates dropped only **7.8 points**, from 98.5% to 90.7%, while non-cognates dropped **27.7 points**, from 90.7% to 63.0%. || Cognates and concrete words were **forgotten less in both directions**, so their advantage extends from initial learning to long-term retention. The cognate advantage held up in productive testing, where the learner had to recall the new form. >> Similarity to a known word helped learners form a lasting memory of the new word.
14|**Are similar L1 words always activated when processing L2?** This is a central research question, and there have been many, many studies of it. When a bilingual person hears a word in the second language, words in the first language that sound similar might also become active. That activation could happen automatically, even when the person is trying to use only the second language. >> If L1 words become active even then, the two languages are never fully separate in the mind. || One nice example comes from a **visual world experiment** by **Marian and Spivey (2003)**. In a visual world experiment, participants hear spoken instructions while viewing a display of objects, and their eye movements are recorded. >> Looks toward an object whose L1 name sounds like the spoken L2 word would reveal that the L1 word was activated. || Eye movements are useful here because they reveal activation without asking the person to report it. The participant still goes on to choose the correct object, so the looks capture what was active along the way. >> The answer from this experiment is that Russian L1 words were activated while native Russian speakers listened to English, their L2.
15|This kind of experiment was used earlier for **categorical perception** by **McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)**. They studied gradient effects of within-category **voice onset time (VOT)** on **lexical access**. Participants saw a display with a lamp, a ship, a bear and a pear, and they heard a word from a continuum between “bear” and “pear.” >> Bear and pear differ only in their first consonant, so they compete during recognition. The lamp and the ship are unrelated fillers. || **Eye movements** can tell us about **competition** between the possible responses that participants are considering. A participant could click on the bear after having looked briefly at the pear. Those looks mean that “pear” was active as a competitor. >> Eye tracking therefore measures how strongly an alternative word is active, even when the final choice is correct. || The amount of competition between BEAR and PEAR seemed to **depend on distance from the category boundary**. Sounds closer to the boundary between /b/ and /p/ produced more looks to the competitor, and sounds farther from the boundary produced fewer. >> Applying the same method across languages lets researchers ask whether a word in one language competes with a similar word in another.
16|**Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** found the **cohort effect**. A **cohort** is the set of words that share the same beginning sounds. Participants viewed a grid containing pictures and shapes and followed spoken instructions. >> The display included a **beaker**, which was the target, a **beetle**, which shares its beginning with beaker, a **speaker**, which rhymes with beaker, and a **carriage**, which is unrelated. || The measure was the probability of fixating each item over the first 1,000 milliseconds after the target word began. Looks to the beaker and the beetle **rose together early**, because both fit the first sounds of the word. Looks to the beetle peaked near 0.2 at around 500 milliseconds and then fell once the sounds that distinguish the words arrived. Looks to the beaker kept rising and approached 1 by about 1,000 milliseconds. >> Looks to the speaker rose later and stayed smaller, with a peak near 0.1, because its overlap comes at the end of the word. Looks to the carriage stayed near zero. || The cohort effect is this **early competition** from words that share a beginning with the target. Words compete as soon as they match the incoming sounds, and they drop out when the input stops matching them. >> Eye movements track this rise and fall moment by moment, even when listeners end up choosing correctly.
17|**Marian and Spivey (2003)** examined the cohort effect with **cross-language competitors**. A cross-language competitor is an object whose name in the other language shares its beginning with the spoken target word. They hypothesized that if **L1 words are always activated**, native Russian speakers should look at a book of matches when the target is “speaker” in English. >> English **speaker** begins with the same sounds as Russian **спички (spichki)**, which means matches. If Russian is active while the participant listens to English, the matches should attract looks. || For English targets, each target was paired with an English competitor and a Russian competitor. **Speaker** was paired with spear and spichki (matches), boot with book and buben (tambourine), and shark with shovel and sharik (balloon). Chair was paired with chess set and cherepaha (turtle), marker with marbles and marka (stamp), and barbed wire with bark and barhat (velvet). Plug was paired with plum and platye (dress), gun with gum and gaika (nut), and lock with lobster and lak dlea nogtei (nail polish). >> For Russian targets the pairing was reversed. Russian spichki was paired with English spear and Russian spitsi (knitting needles), marka with marker and marlya (cheese cloth), and platye with plum and plashch (raincoat). || The connection between a target and its cross-language competitor is **only in sound**, because speakers and matches share no meaning. Both competitors overlap with the target in sound, but only the overlap of the Russian competitor depends on knowing Russian. >> Looks to the matches during an English task are therefore a direct measure of the activation of spichki.
18|**Marian and Spivey (2003)** first had Russian-English bilinguals follow instructions in English, which tests **competition during L2 spoken word recognition**. The measure was the percent of trials on which participants looked at each competitor. When no competitor was present, participants looked at a control filler object on only **7%** of trials. >> When only the **between-language competitor** was present, participants looked at it on **18%** of trials. When only the **within-language competitor** was present, participants also looked at it on 18% of trials. || When only one competitor was present, participants made eye movements to **both within-language and between-language competitors to the same extent**. The Russian name of an object pulled the eyes as strongly as a similar English word did. >> When both competitors were present, participants looked at the within-language competitor more, on 19% of trials. They **still looked at the between-language competitor a lot**, on 13% of trials, which is about twice as often as in the control condition. || These results mean that **the first language stays active during second-language listening**. Bilinguals keep their L1 active while they process their L2. >> They still select the correct object, but L1 words compete along the way.
19|The same bilinguals in **Marian and Spivey (2003)** then did the experiment in Russian, which tests **competition during L1 spoken word recognition**. When only the between-language competitor was present, which was an object with a similar English name, participants looked at it on **8%** of trials. With no competitor, looks to the control filler happened on **5%** of trials. >> When only the within-language competitor was present, participants looked at it on 14% of trials. When both were present, they looked at the between-language competitor on 8% of trials and at the within-language competitor on 13%. || **English words do not seem to be activated** when native Russian speakers do this experiment in Russian. The small numerical difference between the between-language and control conditions, 8% compared with 5%, is **not statistically significant**. A difference that is not statistically significant is within the range of chance variation. >> The within-language competitor still produced a reliable cohort effect in Russian. || The pattern across the two experiments is **asymmetric**. Russian words became active during English tasks, but English words did not become reliably active during Russian tasks. >> The asymmetry fits the idea that the first language is more strongly established and more easily activated than the second.
20|The **Revised Hierarchical Model** of **Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** explains interactions between L1 and L2 in terms of **spreading activation**. Spreading activation is the idea that activating one representation partly activates the representations connected to it. The model has an **L1 lexicon**, which is the first-language vocabulary, an **L2 lexicon**, which is the second-language vocabulary, and a store of **concepts**, which holds meanings. >> **Lexical links** connect the L1 and L2 words directly. **Conceptual links** connect each lexicon to the concepts. || The L1 lexicon is assumed to be **larger** and **more strongly connected to relevant concepts**. The conceptual link between L1 and the concepts is strong, while the conceptual link between L2 and the concepts is weak. >> First-language words therefore have strong links to their meanings, and second-language words have weaker links to their meanings. || The L2 lexicon is assumed to have **strong forward connections to the L1 lexicon**. The lexical link from L2 to L1 is strong and the lexical link from L1 to L2 is weak, so **activation can spread more readily from an L2 word to an L1 word** than the other way. This occurs because this direction is emphasized in learning. >> When people learn a second language, they usually learn each new word by connecting it to its L1 translation. The link from L2 words directly to concepts stays weaker, at least early in learning, and it can strengthen as proficiency grows.
21|The **Revised Hierarchical Model** of **Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** can explain the results of **Marian and Spivey (2003)**. Consider what happens in the model when the task is performed in L2, with the target “speaker.” The concept for “matches” **partially activates the Russian word for “matches,”** spichki, because L1 words are strongly connected to their concepts. >> Spichki is also partly activated by the sounds of the English word speaker, since the two words begin alike. The link between speaker and spichki is only sound overlap, because speakers and matches share no meaning. || This activity **feeds back to the conceptual activation** for “matches.” The extra activation of spichki strengthens the concept of matches. Conceptual activation for “matches” then leads to **eye movements toward the L1 competitor**, which is the matches. >> Strong L1 conceptual links are what let a Russian word drive the eyes toward its object. || When the task is done in L1, we might imagine that the **conceptual links with L2 are too weak** to create this kind of interference reliably. In the Russian version, an English competitor would need strong links from the English word to its concept. In the model, L2 words have only weak conceptual links, so they cannot drive looks to their objects. >> The unequal strength of the links explains why the effect appears in one direction and not in the other.
22|The **Revised Hierarchical Model** makes a **counter-intuitive prediction** even for **balanced bilinguals**. A balanced bilingual is a person who is about equally skilled in both languages. One might expect a balanced bilingual to work like “two monolinguals in one,” with each language running separately, but the model predicts the opposite. >> The reason is that in a balanced bilingual the **conceptual links are similarly strong for both languages**. Every concept strongly activates its word in both languages, so both vocabularies become active whenever either one is used. || Because both words are active, **competitive dynamics and inhibition will be engaged all the time**. Competitive dynamics means that the words of the two languages constantly compete for selection. **Inhibition** is the suppression of the word from the language that is not being used. >> The bilingual must constantly select the right language and suppress the other. || This prediction has led to a lot of work on the advantages of bilinguals in non-linguistic **cognitive control** tasks. Cognitive control is the ability to manage attention and suppress irrelevant responses. >> If bilinguals constantly practice suppressing one language, they might become better at controlling attention in general, including in tasks that have nothing to do with language.
23|So far we have considered one particular **sequence of second-language learning**. A person begins learning L1, later learns L2, and at the same time continues learning and using L1. Both languages are kept in use from then on. >> In this sequence the first language is learned early and is also kept in use. || **But what happens when you stop using L1 after L2 is introduced?** The answer from adopted adults and childhood overhearers is that L2 can appear to completely replace L1, although some residual knowledge may remain that later learning can unmask. In this second sequence the person begins learning L1 and later learns L2, but does not continue learning or using L1. >> L1 is no longer heard or used, while L2 takes over. || This situation separates the effect of **learning a language early** from the effect of **continuing to use it**, which usually go together. If early learning leaves a permanent foundation, traces of L1 should remain even without use. If continued use is needed, L1 might fade or disappear. >> Comparing the two sequences reveals whether early learning alone leaves a lasting mark.
24|**Pallier, Dehaene, Poline, LeBihan, Argenti, Dupoux and Mehler** studied brain imaging of **language plasticity in adopted adults**, and they asked whether a second language can replace the first. They tested adults aged 20 to 32 who had been adopted into French homes from Korean orphanages between the ages of 3 and 8. These adults had learned Korean as young children and then moved into homes where only French was spoken. >> They stopped using Korean entirely after adoption. By adulthood they had spoken only French for many years, so they are a natural test of what happens when L1 use stops. || The researchers tested whether the adoptees had any **residual knowledge of Korean**. Residual knowledge is any remaining trace of the earlier language. Any Korean that survived would reveal what early learning leaves behind when the language is no longer used. >> Monolingual French speakers with no Korean experience served as the comparison group. If the adoptees did better than the French speakers on Korean-related tasks, some Korean knowledge would have survived.
25|Korean has a **three-way stop contrast**. A **stop** is a consonant made by fully blocking the airflow and then releasing it, such as /p/, /t/ or /k/. English and French distinguish two kinds of stops at each place in the mouth, such as /b/ and /p/, while Korean distinguishes three kinds at each place. >> The three kinds are **aspirated**, **plain** and **tense**. An aspirated stop is released with a strong puff of air, a plain stop has a lighter release, and a tense stop is produced with a stiffened, tightened throat. || The contrast appears in three series, in recordings from the UCLA Phonetics Archive. The **bilabial** series, made with the lips, has **pʰul** “grass,” **pul** “fire” and **p’ul** “horn.” The **dental** series, made with the tongue at the teeth, has **tʰal** “mask,” **tal** “moon” and **t’al** “daughter.” The **velar** series, made with the back of the tongue, has **kʰo** “nose,” **kok** “music” and **k’ok** “exactly.” >> The three words in each series are made at the same place in the mouth and differ only in the type of stop. A listener must hear the three-way difference to tell the words apart. || The contrast is a sensitive test of **retained Korean**. French speakers have no such contrast, so they tend to hear the three Korean sounds as two, while a Korean speaker hears all three as different. >> Adoptees who kept any Korean sound knowledge should hear the differences better than French speakers do.
26|**Ventureyra and colleagues** found that Korean adoptees in France were **no better than monolingual French speakers** at the Korean contrasts. Adoptees, French native speakers and Korean native speakers heard pairs of sounds, and the measure was how often each group responded “different.” >> For the **plain and tense** contrast for p and k, Korean speakers responded “different” on about 96% of trials, while adoptees did so on about 8% and French speakers on about 3%. For the **plain and aspirated** contrast for p and k, Korean speakers scored about 90%, adoptees about 19% and French speakers about 26%. || The other Korean contrasts gave the same pattern. For the **tense and aspirated** contrast for p and k, Korean speakers scored about 95%, adoptees about 23% and French speakers about 20%. For the plain and tense contrast for s, Korean speakers scored about 79%, adoptees about 26% and French speakers about 23%. >> Two control conditions checked general performance. On a **vowel** contrast all three groups said “different” on about 97 to 99% of trials, and when the two sounds were identical all groups rarely said “different,” at about 3 to 13%. || The control conditions confirm that the adoptees **could do the task**. The difficulty was specific to the Korean stop contrasts, where the adoptees heard the differences at the same level as people who had never heard Korean. >> Their early Korean experience left **no measurable advantage** for these sounds.
27|In a more extensive study, **Pallier and colleagues** found that adoptees were **no better than monolingual francophones** on three tasks. The first task was **distinguishing Korean from a collection of largely unrelated languages**, which taps familiarity with the overall sound of Korean. Participants rated on a scale of 1 to 7 how much each sentence sounded like Korean. >> Both groups rated Korean sentences around 4, but they also rated Japanese sentences around 4 to 5. Polish sentences got about 2, and Swedish and Wolof sentences got about 2.5 to 3, so adoptees did not pick out Korean any better than French speakers did. || The second task was **identifying the correct Korean translation of French words**, which taps memory for Korean vocabulary. Adoptees scored about 55% correct and French speakers about 50%, which is close to chance for both. >> The third task was **determining whether a short excerpt was repeated from a recorded sentence they had just heard**, which taps sensitivity to Korean sound sequences. Both groups detected the excerpts in French sentences about 95% of the time, and both scored about 80% for Korean, Japanese and Polish sentences, with no adoptee advantage for Korean. || These are **not standard psycholinguistic tasks**. Pallier and colleagues were looking for **any remaining trace of Korean**, so they used tasks designed to catch even weak knowledge. They did not find any. >> On every measure, the adoptees looked like people who had never learned Korean.
28|**Pallier and colleagues** also used brain imaging. They found that listening to French activated the **classical language network**, including **superior temporal** and **inferior frontal** regions, in both groups. >> The superior temporal region is the upper part of the temporal lobe, and it is involved in processing speech sounds. The inferior frontal region is the lower part of the frontal lobe, and it includes Broca's area. || The comparisons subtracted brain activity for other languages from activity for French. French minus Polish, French minus Japanese and French minus Korean were computed for the French group and for the Korean group, who were the adoptees. In every comparison, French produced more activity in the language network than the foreign language did. >> This held for both groups, so both processed French as their own language. || **Subtracting Korean from French** might have revealed some difference between the groups if there were any **residual processing ability for Korean** left. If adoptees still processed Korean as a language, Korean would activate their language network more than it did for French speakers, so the difference between French and Korean would be smaller in adoptees. The difference was not smaller in the adoptees. >> The brains of the adoptees responded to Korean as a foreign language, much as they responded to Polish and Japanese. The brain imaging results therefore agree with the behavioral results.
29|Interactions between L2 and L1 can also be thought of in terms of **competitive dynamics**. Imagine that **L1 is represented as a set of connections** within and between different brain regions. These connections are built and maintained through use. >> Connections that are used get stronger, while connections that are not used can be taken over by competitors. || The same process occurs in vision with **monocular deprivation** in mice. After 2 to 3 days, the connections of the deprived eye show **increased spine motility**. After 3 to 4 days, there is **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration**, which is the breakdown of cut-off nerve fibers. After more than a week, the fibers of the other eye show **axon sprouting and new spine birth** and take over the lost space. >> The eye that keeps receiving input gains control of the connections that the deprived eye loses. || If the L1 connections are never activated again, they could be **“taken over” by L2**. L2 receives constant use, so its connections strengthen and spread. L1 receives no use, so its connections weaken and can be claimed by L2. >> On this account, L2 replaces L1 through competition for the same neural resources.
30|**Can a second language replace the first?** The answer from the adoptees is **“Apparently YES!”** The adoptees showed no behavioral or brain evidence of their childhood Korean. As far as these tests could measure, French had completely replaced Korean. >> No test, behavioral or brain-based, found any trace of Korean. || **Are traces of L1 left behind that can be “salvaged,” though?** Salvaging a trace would mean recovering or using some leftover effect of the earlier language. Work with childhood overhearers of Spanish suggests that such traces can remain, because their early listening gave them an advantage in pronunciation when they studied Spanish later. >> A trace might not appear on a test of current ability but could still make **relearning** easier. || Testing current recognition and testing later learning ask different questions. One asks what the person can do with the language now. The other asks whether new learning benefits from something left over from the past. >> Relearning is the measure that could reveal a trace that the adoptee tests could not detect.
31|**Au, Knightly, Jun and Oh**, from the group of Terry Au at UCLA, examined this question in a study called “Overhearing a language during childhood.” They studied undergraduate students who **overheard Spanish frequently as children** but never learned to speak the language. All participants started classroom instruction of Spanish at around age 14, and they were tested at the end of a Year 2 college Spanish course. >> The **childhood overhearers** were compared with native speakers and with typical late L2 learners. || On **pronunciation**, the overhearers were far ahead of the late learners. **Voice onset time** for word-initial voiceless stops was 19.3 milliseconds for native speakers, 19.6 for overhearers and 36.2 for late learners, so overhearers matched native speakers while late learners produced longer, English-like values. Word-medial voiceless stops gave 22.4, 23.4 and 31.2 milliseconds. >> **Lenition** is a softening of a consonant, which Spanish applies to voiced stops between vowels. Word-initial voiced stops were lenited 43% of the time by native speakers, 24% by overhearers and 1% by late learners, and word-medial voiced stops 72%, 63% and 23%. >> Accent ratings on a 5-point scale for voiceless stops were 4.3 for native speakers, 3.5 for overhearers and 2.2 for late learners, and for voiced stops they were 4.4, 3.3 and 2.7. Overhearers also knew some childhood slang, with 17% correct in production and 22% in comprehension, compared with 3% for late learners and 79% and 88% for native speakers. || On **grammar**, the overhearers had little or no advantage. Gender agreement was 93% for native speakers, 70% for overhearers and 68% for late learners, and number agreement was 92%, 88% and 85%. Grammaticality judgment accuracy was 94%, 64% and 63%, with reaction times of 1,042, 2,475 and 2,209 milliseconds. >> Childhood overhearing gave a clear advantage in pronunciation but not in grammar. Early listening left traces in the sound system that were **unmasked** when the students later studied Spanish, while grammar seems to need more than overheard input.
32|Taking the data from the **Korean adoptees** together with the data from the **overhearers**, two things follow. If L1 is removed from the environment entirely, **L2 can appear to completely replace it**. The adoptees showed no trace of Korean on any test of current ability. >> Complete loss of exposure can make a first language seem to vanish. || But this replacement is **not necessarily complete**. There may be some **residual knowledge** that can be “unmasked” with later learning, as with the pronunciation of the Spanish overhearers. >> The overhearers showed a clear advantage once they began learning Spanish. || There are also **potentially important differences between these populations**. The adoptees were tested on their current knowledge without any new Korean instruction. The overhearers were tested after classroom Spanish that ran from around age 14 through a second-year college course, which gave hidden knowledge a chance to appear. >> These and other differences between the groups make the two sets of results hard to compare directly.
33|Several **potentially important differences between these populations** could explain why one group kept traces of an early language and the other did not. **Korean is rarely heard or encountered in France**, which is not so for Spanish in Los Angeles. The overhearers may have kept hearing Spanish throughout their lives, and that continued exposure could have kept their Spanish sound knowledge active. >> A **heritage language** spoken by other family members also differs from a language not spoken by anyone you know. The overhearers often heard Spanish from relatives, while the adoptees lost all contact with Korean speakers. || The languages themselves differ too. **Cognates** are common between Spanish and English but rare between Korean and French, so shared vocabulary could help overhearers relearn. >> Spanish and English also have a **shared writing system**, because they use the same alphabet, while Korean uses a different script from French. || This is a **general issue** with work on second language acquisition and bilingualism. Languages and learning situations differ in many, many ways that are **hard, perhaps impossible, to control experimentally**. Researchers cannot assign people to grow up hearing particular languages in particular settings. >> Untangling these factors would require comparing populations that differ in only one way, which is rarely possible with real language histories.
