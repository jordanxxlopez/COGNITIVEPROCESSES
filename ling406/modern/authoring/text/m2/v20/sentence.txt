2|**“The horse raced past the barn fell”** is a grammatical English sentence, even though it usually feels broken the first time you read it. The trouble comes from the opening words, because they push the reader toward the wrong structure. >> Most readers take **raced** as the **main verb**, so “the horse raced past the barn” seems to be a complete event in which the horse did the racing. >> When **fell** arrives, that structure has no room left for it, so the sentence seems to fall apart. || **What events are described in this sentence?** The sentence describes **two events**. Someone raced the horse past the barn, and then the horse fell. >> In this reading, “raced past the barn” tells which horse is meant, and **fell** is the main verb that says what happened to that horse. || **How could you disambiguate it?** You can **disambiguate** it, which means making only one reading possible, by putting back the missing words, as in **“The horse that was raced past the barn fell.”** The words “that was” make the modifying relationship explicit, so raced can no longer be read as the main verb. >> Understanding a sentence depends on working out the relationships among its words, and knowing what each word means is not enough. || **Could this be the sentence that launched a thousand experiments?** It fairly could be, because researchers have used it again and again to study how readers build structure as the words come in.
3|**“The horse raced past the barn fell”** is almost as famous as **“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously.”** The two sentences are famous for different reasons. The colorless green ideas sentence is **grammatical but meaningless**. The horse sentence is grammatical and meaningful, but it is **very hard to process**. >> A comic strip jokes that nobody would ever say the horse sentence, and the reply is that farmers might say it if their horses kept falling over. The joke only works because the sentence really is grammatical, even though it sounds wrong. || The horse sentence is the classic case of the **“garden path” effect**. A **garden path sentence** steers the reader into one reading early on, and later words reveal that this reading was wrong. The name comes from the saying “to lead someone down the garden path,” which means to mislead someone. >> Raced looks like the main verb, and fell then clashes with the structure already built, so the reader has to go back and treat “raced past the barn” as “that was raced past the barn.” >> Garden paths happen because readers build structure word by word and commit to one analysis before the sentence is over. A reader who waited until the end of the sentence would never be led astray. || **Grammaticality and processing difficulty are different things**. A sentence can have a perfectly acceptable structure and still be very hard to follow as it unfolds, because the difficulty depends on which analysis the reader picks first and how hard that analysis is to replace.
4|**Syntactic explanations** of the garden path effect start with a **parser**, which is a mental process whose job is to build a **syntactic tree** for each sentence we hear or read. A syntactic tree records how the words group into phrases and how those phrases relate to each other. >> The trees for the horse sentence and the dinosaur comic both come from a Language Log piece about this sentence. || In the **correct tree**, the subject is a large noun phrase, or **NP**, that holds “the horse” plus a reduced clause. The reduced clause means “that was raced past the barn,” and the words “that” and “was” are silent in the actual sentence. The clause also has an empty subject position. Inside the clause, raced takes the prepositional phrase, or **PP**, “past the barn,” so the main verb phrase, or **VP**, contains only **fell**. >> In the **failed parse**, “the horse” is an ordinary NP and “raced past the barn” is the main VP, with raced as the main verb. **Fell** is then left with nowhere to attach, so the parse breaks down. || Any parser has to work inside a cognitive system with **limited memory** and **time constraints**. Words arrive quickly, one after another, so the parser cannot wait for the end of the sentence before it starts building structure. It can also keep only a limited number of structures in play at once. >> An account of processing has to explain how the parser copes with these two limits when a sentence is ambiguous.
5|The **grammatical version** of the horse sentence is **much more complex syntactically** than the failed parse, because it has **many more nodes**. A **node** is a point in a syntactic tree that stands for a grammatical grouping, such as an NP, a VP or a sentence. >> The correct tree needs extra nodes for the embedded clause and for the silent “that” and “was.” >> The failed parse makes “raced past the barn” the main VP, which takes far fewer nodes. || One strategy that saves space is to **initially prefer smaller trees**, and this strategy is called **“minimal attachment.”** Under minimal attachment, the parser fits each new word into the existing structure with the fewest possible new nodes. Fewer nodes means less structure to build and hold in memory at that moment. || In the horse sentence, minimal attachment favors **raced as the main verb**, because that analysis needs the fewest nodes. The reading in which “raced past the barn” modifies horse needs an embedded relative clause, so the parser passes it over at first. >> The smaller structure lightens the immediate load, but it leads the reader down the garden path. When fell arrives, it requires the larger structure that was passed over, so the reader has to rebuild the tree.
6|**“Late closure”** is another parsing strategy in this framework. It is a bias to **keep the current clause “open” as long as possible**, so new words get attached to the phrase that is being built right now. >> Keeping a clause open means that the parser keeps adding incoming words to it, and does not close it off and start a new structure. || In **“The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite,”** the phrase **“with the dynamite”** wants to associate with the big **verb phrase** that starts with **“blew open.”** **Frazier (1987)** contrasts two structures for this sentence. >> In the **preferred** structure, the PP attaches to the **VP**. This **verb-phrase attachment** makes the dynamite the **instrument** that the burglar used to blow open the safe. >> In the **unpreferred** structure, the PP attaches to the **NP** “the safe.” This **noun-phrase attachment** makes the phrase say which safe was blown open, namely the safe that had the dynamite. || Both attachments are grammatical, but they say different things. Late closure keeps the verb phrase open, so the new phrase joins it. Minimal attachment prefers the same attachment, because it needs fewer nodes than building an extra NP around “the safe with the dynamite.” >> For this sentence the preferred parse is also the sensible one, so readers reach the right meaning without any difficulty.
7|Late closure can be seen at work in a sentence that **requires the “unpreferred” parse to be sensible**, such as **“The burglar blew open the safe with the jewels.”** Jewels are an implausible tool for blowing open a safe. The sensible meaning is that the burglar blew open **the safe that contained the jewels**, so “with the jewels” has to attach to the noun phrase “the safe,” which is the unpreferred NP attachment. || In **careful tests of reading time and eye movements**, people are **slower to read sentences where the sensible reading requires the unpreferred parse**. **Reading time** measures how long readers spend on each part of the sentence. **Eye movement** records track where readers look and when they jump back to reread earlier words. >> The extra time appears right at the point where the parse has to change. || Readers **momentarily entertain the idea that the jewels were used to blow open the safe**, and then they have to **retrace their steps**. The parser first attaches the phrase to the verb phrase, because that is its usual preference and it is what it did for the dynamite. The meaning of jewels conflicts with the instrument reading, so the parser moves the attachment to the noun phrase. >> The final meaning is easy to understand, but getting there carries a measurable **processing cost**, because the repair takes time.
8|This syntactic explanation rests on a **parser that deals exclusively with the abstract syntactic structure** of the sentence. It parallels the **“lumber mill”** model of thresholded processing from speech perception. >> In a lumber mill, each stage works on its own material and hands a finished product to the next stage. >> In the same way, the parser builds structure from grammatical categories alone, without checking meaning or context, and meaning is worked out afterward from the finished structure. || The parser has **its own operating characteristics**. It wants to make **the simplest tree possible**, which is **minimal attachment**. It does this partly by **leaving clauses open as long as possible**, which is **late closure**. >> Both preferences limit how much structure has to be built and held in memory at once. || This is one way of dealing with ambiguity **very efficiently**, because the parser can **ignore ambiguity until it becomes a problem**. It commits to one likely structure and builds only that one, and most of the time the first choice is right, so no extra effort is needed. >> When a later word does not fit, the structure has to be revised, and that revision is the source of garden path difficulty. Garden paths are the price of an efficient strategy that works well on average.
9|A **purely syntactic explanation** runs into **problems**. One problem is that **syntactically “difficult” sentences are not always difficult to parse**. Many sentences in published research articles contain a **reduced relative clause**, which is a relative clause with “that was” or “that were” left out, as in “the horse raced past the barn fell.” || In each of the following sentences, the verbs in capital letters introduce a reduced relative. **Trueswell, Tanenhaus and Garnsey (1994)** wrote “a noun phrase FOLLOWED by a MORPHOLOGICALLY AMBIGUOUS VERB … will be temporarily ambiguous.” **Britt, Perfetti, Garrod and Rayner (1992)** wrote “Referential information PROVIDED by the discourse is of no help.” >> **Frazier and Rayner (1987)** wrote “Recent research REPORTED by FERREIRA AND CLIFTON (1986) has demonstrated that syntactic processing is quite independent and that the initial syntactic analysis ASSIGNED to a sentence is little influenced by the semantic information ALREADY ANALYZED.” >> **Altmann and Steedman (1988)** wrote “The pattern of results OBTAINED with global reading times is reflected by differences LOCATED on the critical disambiguating phrase.” || Every capitalized verb begins a reduced relative with the same structure as the horse sentence, **but these are not impossibly difficult sentences**. Readers follow them easily, with no feeling of being led down a garden path. >> In “recent research reported by Ferreira and Clifton,” reported describes research that someone reported, so research is not the one doing the reporting. || **MacDonald, Perlmutter and Seidenberg (1994)** advanced an alternative argument, which is that **semantic factors and statistical regularities interact with parsing from very early on**. **Semantic factors** concern meaning, and **statistical regularities** are patterns in how often words occur in particular structures. >> On this view, particular words and their familiar uses can support a reduced relative reading from the start, so the sentence is easy to read.
10|The collection of reduced relatives is a **wonderfully passive-aggressive** table. **Many of the examples are drawn from papers that use the difficulty of parsing reduced relatives as evidence for an autonomous parser**. An **autonomous parser** is a parser that builds syntactic structure without help from meaning or context. >> The authors of those papers argued that reduced relatives are hard to process. Yet they wrote reduced relatives in their own articles, and readers understood them without trouble. || More examples from the same collection make the same point. In each one the reader attaches the capitalized verb to the right noun with no effort. >> In “a noun phrase FOLLOWED by a morphologically ambiguous verb,” followed modifies noun phrase. In “the initial syntactic analysis ASSIGNED to a sentence,” assigned modifies analysis. In “differences LOCATED on the critical disambiguating phrase,” located modifies differences. >> **Ferreira and Clifton (1986)** wrote “A person PRESENTED with the evidence EXAMINED by the lawyer may interpret the evidence as a theme, not an agent, because examined requires an animate agent.” Here presented modifies person, and examined modifies evidence. || The examples cited were **not the only reduced relatives** in those articles. Reduced relatives turned up at a rate of about **one per journal page** across this sample of articles. So reduced relatives are routine in scientific writing, and in a supportive context a formally complex structure is easy to understand.
11|The argument is that several factors besides syntax are at work when we read **“Recent research reported by…”** One factor is **semantics**. Research is an **inanimate** object, which means it is not a living thing, so it is unlikely to be the **subject** of a verb like **report**. Taking research as the thing that was reported fits the meanings of the words. >> A second factor is **lexical statistics**, which are learned patterns of how often particular words appear in particular structures. **“Reported” occurs frequently in reduced relatives**. >> A third factor combines lexical statistics with **“world knowledge.”** “Reported” occurs very frequently in reduced relatives **in scientific papers, especially when following the word “research,”** and world knowledge here means knowing how people actually write in that setting. || These and **many other factors interact with the syntax of the sentence very early on**. They act from the first moments of reading, so they help decide which structure is chosen in the first place. || Consider **“The burglar arrested in the parking lot was charged today”** and **“The policeman arrested in the parking lot was charged today.”** **Why is the policeman sentence harder to read?** Burglars are likely to be arrested, so arrested is easily read as the start of a reduced relative that describes the burglar. Policemen are likely to be the ones doing the arresting, so arrested is first taken as the main verb, and “was charged” then causes a garden path. >> The grammar of the two sentences is identical, so the difference in difficulty comes from what we know about burglars and policemen.
12|**“World knowledge” can be very local**, because it can come from the visual scene right in front of us as well as from general knowledge. **Spivey and colleagues (1995)** tested this with a **visual world** task. They gave people a display of real objects, which were **a pencil, a box, an empty towel, and an apple sitting on a towel**. People then heard the instruction **“Put the apple on the towel in the box”** and carried it out while their eye movements were recorded. || With **minimal attachment**, “the towel” would temporarily seem like it should be the end of the clause **“put the apple on the towel.”** In that reading, “on the towel” names the **destination**, the place where the apple should go. >> In the intended reading, “on the towel” says **which apple** to move, the one already on a towel, and **“in the box”** is the destination. || People **do seem to look at the empty towel while planning their movements**, as though they are considering it as a target, before they plan a movement to the box. With one apple in view and the ambiguous instruction, a typical sequence of looks goes to the apple at about 1,000 milliseconds, then to the empty towel shortly after, and then to the box at about 2,000 milliseconds. >> The comparison was the unambiguous instruction **“Put the apple that’s on the towel in the box.”** With that instruction, the eyes go from the apple straight to the box, with no look to the empty towel. >> The listener recognizes every word correctly. The error comes from taking the location phrase as the destination, which briefly changes the planned action.
13|In a **two-referent context**, the display holds **two apples**. One apple sits on a towel and the other sits somewhere else, and the empty towel and the box are still there. “The apple” alone no longer says which apple to move, so the phrase **“on the towel”** now does useful work, because it picks out one apple from the two. || In this context, participants usually look first at the apple on the towel and then at the box. Looks to the empty towel become much less common. >> **The sequence and the timing of eye movements**, measured relative to the nouns in the speech, **did not differ** between the ambiguous and the unambiguous instructions. >> So the ambiguous instruction is processed just like the explicit instruction with “that’s on the towel.” || The two-apple display gives “on the towel” a clear job before the listener even reaches “in the box.” With two apples in view, the listener needs to know which apple is meant, so a phrase that singles one out is expected. Listeners take “on the towel” as a description of the apple from the start, so the destination reading never takes hold. >> The same sentence gets a different grammatical analysis because the visual context is different.
14|Now consider what happens when there are **two apples** in the display. It suddenly becomes **felicitous**, which means natural and appropriate in the situation, to give more information about **which apple** you are talking about. >> With only one apple, there is no need to say which apple, so “on the towel” seems like extra information and is easily taken as a destination. >> With two apples, “on the towel” has a clear job, which is to identify the right apple. || So **people do not look at the empty towel as much**. The measure is the proportion of trials with eye movements to the **incorrect destination**, which is the empty towel. In the **one-referent context**, ambiguous instructions led to such looks on about **55%** of trials, and unambiguous instructions led to almost none. In the **two-referent context**, ambiguous instructions led to such looks on only about **17%** of trials, which is close to the roughly **12%** for unambiguous instructions. >> The words of the instruction were identical in both contexts, so the visual context alone changed how often listeners were misled. || This is an effect of **referential context**, which is the set of objects that a phrase could refer to. Referential context influences which grammatical relationship listeners assign while they are still hearing the sentence. >> The large cost of ambiguity appears only when the display contains one apple.
15|The results from **Spivey and colleagues (1995)** are **a big problem for purely syntactic accounts**. **Different parses of the same sentence are favored under different semantic and pragmatic conditions, and this effect happens very early**. >> **Semantic** conditions have to do with meaning. >> **Pragmatic** conditions have to do with how language is used in a particular situation, such as whether a speaker needs to specify which object is meant. || The same ambiguous sentence leads to looks at the empty towel on about **55%** of trials with one apple, but on only about **17%** of trials with two apples. The words are identical in both conditions. Only the visual situation changes, and that change alters the parse the listener adopts first. || The difference appears in **eye movements during the sentence**, before the action is carried out, so it captures the parse while it is being built. If context only repaired the parse afterward, listeners in both contexts should first look at the empty towel. Those looks drop with two apples, so context must have shaped the **first parse**. >> A parser that always starts with the same structure cannot predict that the garden path nearly disappears with two apples.
16|More recently, researchers have begun using data from **mouse movements** to look at these questions. **Farmer and colleagues (2007)** tracked the path of the computer mouse while participants carried out spoken instructions in a version of the apple and towel display. Mouse tracking records the whole path of the hand, so a competing interpretation can pull the movement toward another location before the hand reaches its target. >> It is not just eye movements but **the actual execution of arm movements** that can be influenced by **syntactic ambiguity** and its **interaction with the visually defined context**. || In the **one-referent context**, the hand moved from the apple toward the box, but with ambiguous instructions its path **curved toward the empty towel**, compared with the path for unambiguous instructions. The two paths separated most in the middle of the movement, and the hand still ended at the correct place. >> In the **two-referent context**, the ambiguous and unambiguous paths were nearly identical. The second apple makes the location phrase useful for picking out an apple, so it is no longer taken as a destination. || The researchers also measured the **Euclidean distance between the ambiguous and unambiguous trajectories** at each moment, which is the straight-line distance between the two paths. In the one-referent context, the distance rose to a peak of about 200 pixels around 40% of the way through the movement. In the two-referent context, it peaked lower, at about 140 pixels, and earlier, around 30%, and then dropped off. >> Comprehension and action planning interact while both are underway. A competing interpretation bends the path of the hand even when the movement ends at the correct place.

18|**The** is a **determiner**, which is a word that opens a **noun phrase**. Once the parser meets “the,” it expects a noun to follow soon, possibly with an adjective or another modifier in between. >> “The” does not name anything by itself. >> It limits which kinds of words can come next, so the parser can anticipate the shape of the phrase. || This expectation is about a **grammatical category**, and it is not about one particular word. Many different nouns could follow, such as pizza, dog or book. An adjective could also come first, as in “the hot pizza.” >> The parser predicts the form of the phrase while its specific meaning is still open. || Taking a sentence one word at a time reveals how each word sets up expectations for the next word. Words that fit the expectation are processed smoothly, while words that break it cause difficulty.
19|**Pizza** is a **noun**, and it completes the noun phrase that “the” opened. It is the **head of the noun phrase**, which is the central word that the rest of the phrase is built around. >> “The” combines with “pizza” to form “the pizza,” which can now serve as the subject of the sentence. >> With a complete noun phrase in place, the parser expects a verb or verb phrase that says what the pizza did or what happened to it. || Recognizing the word makes two kinds of information available. Its **category**, noun, tells the parser how it can combine with other words. Its **meaning**, a kind of food, sets up expectations about plausible events, such as being eaten or baked. >> Word recognition supplies more to sentence processing than a sound or a spelling. >> Both **grammatical expectations** and **meaning-based expectations** guide how the next words are processed.
20|**Was** is a past-tense form of the verb **be**. It can act as an **auxiliary**, which is a helping verb that combines with another verb to mark grammatical information such as tense or voice. >> In “the pizza was eaten,” was helps form a **passive** with the participle eaten. >> In “the pizza was in the oven,” was links the subject to a phrase that gives a location. || When “was” arrives, the sentence could continue in several ways, so several possibilities stay open. The sentence could describe where the pizza was, what it was like or what was done to it. The parser has to keep the developing structure available while more words arrive, because the grammatical role of was depends partly on what comes next. >> A location phrase would come next with a preposition, an adjective would say what the pizza was like, and a passive would come next with a participle such as eaten.
21|**In** is a **preposition**, which is a word that expresses a relationship such as location or containment. It begins a **prepositional phrase**. >> “In” does not name the location by itself. >> It sets up the expectation of a noun phrase that will supply the location, as in “in the oven” or “in the box.” || After “the pizza was,” the arrival of “in” narrows the possible structures. The sentence is now heading toward a statement about where the pizza was. The parser expects a noun phrase that names a place or container, because that noun phrase is what completes the prepositional phrase. >> This expectation is grammatical, so the next word must be able to start a noun phrase. Words like “the,” “an” or a noun would fit the expectation. >> Understanding the phrase requires combining the preposition with that noun phrase.
22|**The** begins a new **noun phrase** inside the prepositional phrase, which reads “in the …” so far. It is the start of the phrase that will name the location. >> “The” does not supply the location by itself. >> A noun is still needed, possibly with modifiers before it, as in “in the hot oven.” || The second “the” confirms that a noun phrase has started, so the parser now strongly expects a **noun**. This expectation concerns a **word category**, and it is more than an expectation about a sensible topic. Whatever comes next must work grammatically as part of a noun phrase. >> Knowing that the sentence is about pizza makes words like oven or box likely, but the grammatical requirement holds whatever the meaning is. >> The grammatical frame is now very specific, so any word that cannot be part of a noun phrase will clash with it.
23|**Eaten** is the **past participle** of the verb **eat**. It is a verb form and not a noun, so it cannot complete the noun phrase that “in the” has set up. The phrase **“in the eaten”** puts a verb form exactly where a noun is required. >> This is a **word-category violation**, which means a word of the wrong grammatical category appears in a position that demands a different category. || A category error is different from a strange but grammatical meaning. “The pizza was in the ocean” is odd in meaning but grammatically fine, because ocean is a noun. No unusual situation can rescue “the pizza was in the eaten,” because the problem lies in the grammatical type of the word. >> A strange but grammatical word such as ocean only makes the meaning odd. A word of the wrong category such as eaten breaks the structure itself. || This contrast between **category errors** and **meaning errors** is what makes such sentences useful for studying the brain. Brain responses to the two kinds of error can be compared, so researchers can see whether grammar and meaning are processed differently.
24|The **early left anterior negativity**, or **ELAN**, is an **ERP** phenomenon elicited by **word class violations**. An **ERP**, or **event-related potential**, is electrical brain activity recorded at the scalp, averaged over many trials and time-locked to an event such as the onset of a word. A **word class** is a grammatical category such as noun, verb or preposition. || Classic ELAN stimuli are sentences with word-category violations. One is **“The pizza was in the eaten,”** where a participle appears where a noun is expected. Another is **“The scientist criticized Max’s of proof the theorem,”** where the preposition **of** appears where a noun is expected after “Max’s.” || The ELAN has three main features. It is **early**, appearing about **100 to 200 milliseconds** after the critical word. It is **left and anterior**, which means it is strongest over the left front of the scalp, and this location has raised the question of whether it is specific to language. It occurs **specifically for word category violations**, such as a noun when a verb is expected, or a preposition in place of a noun. >> Incorrect sentences produce an early negative response over left frontal sites soon after the critical word, and correct sentences do not. >> The response comes so fast that it was taken as evidence for a very quick syntactic analysis that checks word categories before meaning is processed.
25|With visually presented words, **the ELAN came from visual cortex**, which is the region at the back of the brain that processes what we see. This finding comes from **Dikker, Rabagliati, Farmer and Pylkkänen (2010)**, whose paper is titled “Early occipital sensitivity to syntactic category is based on form typicality.” The **occipital** lobe is where visual cortex is located. >> For **bimorphemic nouns**, which are nouns that end in an identifiable noun suffix, the response to **“The beautifully princess …”** was larger than the response to **“The beautiful princess …”** at about **120 to 130 milliseconds** after the noun appeared. >> Beautiful is an adjective, which correctly predicts a noun. Beautifully is an adverb, which predicts a verb or an adjective, so princess is unexpected. || The finding suggests that when we are expecting a verb, for example, **that prediction can feed back down to the earliest parts of the visual system**. There it predicts visual patterns that are consistent with **the way verbs are written**, because a predicted grammatical category implies likely visual forms, such as typical word endings. >> The very early response to the error might reflect **a mismatch between that predictive process and what finally appears on the screen**. || **Does this only work for items that are morphologically marked as nouns or verbs, or for open-class versus closed-class words?** A word is **morphologically marked** when part of it, such as a suffix, signals its category. **Open-class** words are content words such as nouns and verbs, which keep gaining new members, and **closed-class** words are function words such as prepositions and determiners, which form a small fixed set. >> The later comparison of noun types answers the first part, because the early effect appears for nouns with a noun suffix and for nouns with a typical noun form, but not for neutral nouns like “infant.”
26|Many English words can function as either a **noun** or a **verb**, and the rest of the sentence decides which. In “Chris and Ben are glad that the bird perches seem easy to install,” **perches** is a **noun** that means the rods birds sit on, and seem is the verb. In “Chris and Ben are glad that the bird perches comfortably in the cage,” perches is a **verb** that says what the bird does. >> In “The teacher told the principal that the student needs were not being met,” **needs** is a **noun** that means the student’s requirements. >> In “The teacher told the principal that the student needs to be more focused,” needs is a **verb**. || Recognizing the word form alone does not settle its grammatical role. The **continuation** after the ambiguous word resolves it, so the reader has to wait for words such as “seem” or “comfortably.” || Words also differ in **phonological form typicality**, which is how much a word’s sound pattern resembles the sound patterns of typical nouns or typical verbs. A corpus analysis of **3,158 words** measured each word’s mean distance in phonological feature space from all nouns and from all verbs, where a small distance means the word sounds similar to that category. >> Nouns tend to sound closer to other nouns, and verbs tend to sound closer to other verbs. >> Some words go against the trend. Besides **noun-like nouns** and **verb-like verbs**, there are **verb-like nouns** and **noun-like verbs**, which sound like one category while belonging to the other.
27|In terms of **form typicality**, **“perches” is noun-like** and **“needs” is verb-like**. These are learned statistical tendencies in how words sound, and both words can still appear in either role. >> Among the 3,158 words in the analysis, perches falls among the noun-like forms and needs falls among the verb-like forms. || For **perches**, the noun-like form matches one sentence and clashes with the other. In **“the bird perches seem easy to install,”** perches is used as a noun, which matches its form. In **“the bird perches comfortably in the cage,”** perches must be a verb, which goes against its form. || For **needs**, the pattern is reversed. In **“the student needs to be more focused,”** needs is used as a verb, which matches its form. In **“the student needs were not being met,”** needs must be a noun, which goes against its form. || Each word appears once as a noun and once as a verb, so the same word form can be compared in a **matching** use and a **mismatching** use. This comparison separates what a word’s form suggests from what the grammar of the sentence requires. >> If form typicality guides parsing, the mismatching uses should be harder to process, even though all four sentences are grammatical.
28|Reading is **slower when a word’s required category conflicts with its form typicality**. The measure is reading time at **disambiguation** minus reading time at the **point of ambiguity**, adjusted for how long the words are. >> The **point of ambiguity** is the ambiguous word itself, such as perches or needs. >> **Disambiguation** is the point where the continuation makes the intended category clear, such as “seem” or “comfortably.” A larger increase from one point to the other signals more difficulty. || For **noun-like homonyms** such as perches, sentences that resolved the word as a noun added only about **50 milliseconds**. Sentences that resolved it as a verb added much more, about **120 milliseconds**. So a noun-like form is **harder to resolve as a verb**. || For **verb-like homonyms** such as needs, the pattern reversed. Sentences that resolved the word as a noun added about **110 milliseconds**, while sentences that resolved it as a verb added almost nothing, under 10 milliseconds. So a verb-like form is **harder to resolve as a noun**. || The ambiguous word stays the same in each pair, and only the continuation changes. The extra cost appears specifically when the continuation demands the category that does not match the form. >> Parsing uses learned statistics about how word forms sound, along with the structure the sentence finally requires.
29|The **ELAN effect for nouns where a verb is expected** is limited to cases where there is either **syntactic information** or **statistical form-based information** that is **physically available** in the word. Syntactic information here means **morphemes** that indicate noun-ness, and a morpheme is the smallest meaningful part of a word, such as a suffix that marks a noun. Statistical form-based information means **“nouniness,”** which is how much a word’s form resembles typical nouns even without a noun suffix. || The early brain response studied here is the **M100**, which peaks about 100 milliseconds after a word appears. Three kinds of nouns were compared, each in an expected and an unexpected version. >> For **bimorphemic nouns**, the comparison was “The beautiful princess …” and “The beautifully princess …” Princess carries a noun-marking suffix, and the unexpected version produced a significantly larger response. >> For **typical nouns**, the comparison was “The tasteless soda …” and “The tastelessly soda …” Soda has a statistically noun-like form, and the unexpected version again produced a significantly larger response. >> For **neutral nouns**, the comparison was “The cute infant …” and “The cutely infant …” Infant has neither a suffix nor a typical noun form, and the two versions did not differ. || So **no effect was observed for neutral nouns like “infant” that lack strong cues**. An abstract category mismatch alone is not enough to produce this earliest visual response. An expected category generates predictions about what the next word will look like, so the early response appears only when the arriving word carries **physical evidence** that conflicts with that prediction.
30|**Sentence processing can be summed up in four sentences**, and each one carries one main idea. The first is **“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously.”** **Syntax is independent of semantics to some extent**. In the limit you can have sentences that are grammatically correct but meaningless, because words can fill a grammatical structure without describing a sensible event. || The second is **“The horse raced past the barn fell.”** The syntactic structure of a sentence is often highly **ambiguous**, or more exactly **vague**, but we do not notice except where things fail. >> **Reading times** for preferred and unpreferred structures suggest that syntactic processes **take shortcuts to save memory**, and do not activate every possible parse of a sentence. >> When a shortcut picks the wrong structure, as in the horse sentence, the reader is led down the garden path. || The third is **“Put the apple on the towel in the box.”** **Context, pragmatics and other factors seem to interact with syntactic processing from very early on**. >> With two apples in view, listeners take “on the towel” as a description of the apple and not as a destination, so the visual situation shapes the parse while the sentence is still unfolding. || The fourth is **“The bird perches were comfortable.”** **The very earliest syntactic effects observable in the brain are driven in part by form typicality**, not necessarily by **morphosyntactic** structures, which are grammatical structures signaled by word parts and word order. These effects seem to be related to **predictions about the physical forms of words**. >> Perches has a noun-like form, so its use as a noun here matches the expectation that its form sets up.
