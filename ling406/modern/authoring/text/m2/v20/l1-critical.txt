2|**Critical periods** and **sensitive or “optimal” periods** are two ways to describe how the timing of experience affects learning, and they differ in how strict the time limits are. A **critical period** has a **discrete, “programmed” onset and offset**, so the window opens and closes at fixed points in development. Change in response to the environment, which is learning, is **strictly limited to this period**, so there is **no sensitivity to the environment before or after** it. >> Experience outside the window has no effect at all, so a critical period predicts that late exposure produces no learning. || A **sensitive or “optimal” period** is a looser link between timing and learning. **Learning is possible throughout the life span**, but it is **optimal during particular windows**, when it is easiest or works best. These windows might have a **gradual onset and offset**, and their timing **can itself be affected by experience**. >> A sensitive period predicts that late learning is harder but still possible, with a lasting advantage for early learners. || The two ideas differ as impossibility differs from reduced effectiveness. Any late improvement counts against a strict critical period, while a lasting early advantage supports a sensitive period. >> Even the classic critical period in vision fits the term sensitive period better, and the same question comes up for the evidence on language learning.
3|**Amblyopia** is **poor vision in one eye despite normal peripheral function**. **Peripheral function** means the working of the eye itself, so the eye can be healthy while vision through it stays poor. The trouble lies in how the brain handles the input from that eye. || Three conditions can lead to amblyopia. **Strabismus** is a misalignment of the eyes, so the two eyes point in different directions and the brain receives two conflicting images. A **cataract** is a clouding of the lens of the eye, so the light reaching the back of the eye is blocked or blurred. **Anisometropia** is a large difference in focusing power between the two eyes, so one eye's image is much blurrier than the other's. >> Each condition keeps the brain from getting normal visual input for some period of time. || **Periods of deprivation from normal visual input** caused by these conditions can result in **poor vision long after the conditions themselves have been remediated**, which means corrected. Straightening the eyes, removing the cataract or correcting the focus restores the input from the eye, but the brain may still fail to use that input well. >> A temporary problem early in life can leave a lasting effect, so the timing of the problem matters as much as the problem itself. >> A cataract removed early may leave little lasting damage, while a cataract removed late may leave poor vision even after the lens is clear.
4|**Amblyopia occurs because of competitive dynamics between cortical inputs**. Both eyes send input to **visual cortex**, and the two inputs **compete** for control of the neurons there. When both eyes work normally, they share the cortical neurons in balance. **Abnormal vision in one eye** can lead to **“colonization”** of the cortical representation of that eye by the other eye, so the stronger eye takes over neurons that would have served the weaker eye. || **Monocular deprivation**, which means blocking or degrading the input to one eye, reveals how the takeover unfolds in mice. The changes come in three steps over a little more than a week. >> Within **2 to 3 days** there is **increased spine motility**. **Dendritic spines** are the small projections where neurons receive connections, and increased motility means they become unstable and move around. >> Within **3 to 4 days** there is **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration**. Connections from the deprived eye are lost, and **Wallerian degeneration** is the breakdown of nerve fibers that have been cut off from their source. >> After **more than a week** there is **axon sprouting and new spine birth**, because the open eye's fibers grow new branches and form connections in the space the deprived eye lost. || When normal function is restored to the deprived eye, it **remains at a competitive disadvantage**, because the other eye now holds the connections. **Normal cortical responses cannot be restored by normal experience**, because ordinary viewing with both eyes open keeps favoring the stronger eye.
5|**Treatments for amblyopia are based on competition**. One of the most effective treatments involves **intermittently patching the “good” eye**. A patch over the stronger eye blocks its input for part of the day, so the brain has to rely on the weaker eye. >> **Why patch the stronger eye?** The stronger eye is winning the competition in visual cortex, so covering it removes its advantage for part of the day. || Patching **restores the competitive balance between the eyes** and permits the brain's own **plasticity** to **restore normal function**. Plasticity is the brain's ability to change its connections in response to experience. With the strong eye covered, the weaker eye's input can win back cortical connections. >> The patch does not repair the eye itself. It changes the experience the brain receives, and the brain's own plasticity does the rest. || This treatment is **most effective early in development**, and it **may be ineffective by late childhood, around 10 years**. Young brains are highly plastic, so rebalancing the input leads to rapid reorganization. As the visual system matures, the same treatment has less and less effect. >> The timing of treatment therefore matters as much as the treatment itself.
6|**Levi and Polat (1996)** studied **human adult plasticity after the critical period**. Experiments with adults have begun to find that **plasticity is not abolished entirely, even well into adulthood**. Some of these patients were **in their 50s**, an age normally considered past the point where treatment for amblyopia works. || The adults did **long-term, intensive training on specific psychophysical tasks** with the affected eye. A **psychophysical task** measures the relationship between a physical stimulus and what a person perceives, such as the smallest contrast a person can detect. The training led to **improvements in both task performance and general visual function**, so the benefits spread beyond the trained task to vision more broadly. >> Training was counted in **kilotrials**, and one kilotrial is a thousand trials. The training ran from 0 to about 50 kilotrials. || Improvement was measured as a falling **threshold**, which is the smallest stimulus a person can reliably detect, so a lower threshold means better vision. The threshold changed in three stages as training went on. >> Over the first **19 kilotrials** the threshold **dropped steadily**, and this change was highly significant. >> From about **20 to 35 kilotrials** the threshold **leveled off** with little further change. >> Around **36 kilotrials** the threshold **dropped again** to a new, lower level and stayed there. || The adult visual system therefore keeps some **experience-dependent plasticity**. The right kind and amount of training can still produce change after ordinary late treatment stops working.
7|**Li, Ngo, Nguyen, Lam and Levi (2008)** found that **playing video games enhances visual acuity and visual attention in adult amblyopia**. The adults played an action video game, a first-person shooting game set in a battle scene, using their amblyopic eye. >> **Visual acuity** is the sharpness of vision, the ability to see fine detail, so better acuity means clearer vision through the weak eye. >> **Visual attention** is the ability to select and focus on the relevant parts of a visual scene, such as targets in a busy battle. || Action games demand fast visual judgments and constant attention, so they push the visual system to use the amblyopic eye's input. The participants were adults whose childhood visual development was long over, so their improvement came from the training itself. Intensive visual experience improved their vision well after the age when patching usually works best. || Video game training adds to the evidence for **plasticity** in adult amblyopia. A demanding, engaging visual task can drive change in a mature visual system. >> An early optimal window can close without removing every possibility of improvement.
8|**Amblyopia is a classic example of a sensitive period**, and three facts about it support that. A **peripheral disturbance in early experience** can lead to **life-long disruption in visual function**, so an early problem with the eye itself permanently affects vision. The long-term loss of function is the **sequela** of a **temporary deficit during experience-dependent organization of the brain**. Treatment is **most effective during a limited developmental window**. >> A **sequela** is a lasting result of an earlier condition. The brain organized itself around the abnormal input, and that organization stays after the eye is corrected. >> Patching works best early and loses its effectiveness by late childhood. || However, **new evidence suggests that the right kind of experience can reverse deficits late into adulthood**. Intensive perceptual training and video game play improved vision in adults. Later learning is harder and requires special conditions, but it still happens. || This pattern fits a **sensitive period** better than a strict critical period. A strict critical period would rule out any later improvement, and adults with amblyopia did improve. >> Amblyopia combines a strong early window with some capacity for change later in life, which is what defines a sensitive period.
9|**How can the vision evidence be related to language?** Two lines of work connect them. The first is **Lenneberg's** early attempts to synthesize **developmental neurology** with the notion of a **“language acquisition device”**. The second is **studies of early deprivation**, with data from **neglected children** and from **Deaf children with limited input to sign**. >> **Developmental neurology** is the study of how the nervous system develops. A **language acquisition device** is a proposed innate capacity for learning language. || Lenneberg asked whether changes in the developing brain could open and close a window for acquiring language. He linked language milestones to stages of brain development. If such a window exists, language learning depends on the state of the brain as well as on the input. || Deprivation studies look at people who first met an accessible language unusually late. Neglected children were cut off from normal language input, and some Deaf children have no access to sign language in early childhood. >> Comparing these cases with typical learners tests whether the age of first exposure affects the final outcome.
10|In **Biological Foundations of Language**, **Lenneberg (1967)** was attempting to synthesize **contemporary linguistics** with what was known about **brain development**. Studies of language development were starting to find a fairly robust and **stage-like pattern** of language development. Data from Austrian, British and American children between 8 and 36 months of age follow the same order. >> **Single words** appear first, mostly between about 10 and 18 months. >> **Two-word phrases** come next, mostly between about 18 and 26 months. >> **Sentences of five words or more** come last, between about 28 and 36 months. || Lenneberg paired these language milestones with motor milestones at each age. At each age, typical children reach a motor achievement and a language achievement on a similar schedule. >> At **8 months** infants stand holding on and pick up small objects, and **reduplicated** babbling, such as repeated syllables, becomes frequent. >> At **10 months** infants creep efficiently and pull to standing, and their vocalizations mix with sound play such as gurgling. They try to imitate sounds and begin to tell words apart. >> At **12 months** infants walk with one hand held, and words such as **mama** or **dada** emerge. They show definite signs of understanding some words and simple commands. >> At **18 months** children have a definite vocabulary of more than three but fewer than fifty words, and comprehension progresses rapidly. || The idea that language might have **maturational milestones** was seen as consistent with the **unfolding of a biological program**. Maturational milestones are developmental achievements tied to physical growth. Language milestones line up with motor milestones, so the two seem to share a biological timetable. >> Lenneberg proposed that brain maturation sets the pace for both, so the timing of language learning depends on the developing brain.
11|In Lenneberg's time, **data on typical brain development were sparse**, because they were almost entirely based on **post-mortem analyses of tissue**. **Post-mortem** means that the tissue was studied after death. Researchers could not follow the same children's brains as they grew, so they had to piece development together from the brains of children who had died at different ages. || Measurements of **neuron size** give the cell body volume of neurons in layers III, IV and V of the cerebral cortex at different ages. Neuron size grows rapidly in the first two years and then levels off. >> **No data were available between ages 2 and 18**, so the measurements have a large gap. That gap overlaps much of the period of **language acquisition**. || Nonetheless, Lenneberg observed that **abrupt changes in neuron size and packing density before age two** might be **permissive for the beginning of language acquisition**. He made this argument from the measurements for very young children and for adults. >> **Packing density** is how closely neurons are crowded together in the tissue. >> **Permissive** means these changes make language acquisition possible without directly causing it. || On this view, **the critical period seems to open after this initial period of rapid development**. The brain first goes through a burst of physical growth, and once that growth settles, the window for learning language opens. >> Lenneberg filled the gap in the data with a reasonable guess about how the brain matures, so later research would be needed to test it directly.
12|More extensive data were available from **studies of recovery from stroke**. **Aphasia is surprisingly rare in children with brain lesions**, where a **lesion** is an area of damaged brain tissue. Damage that leaves an adult with lasting aphasia often has much milder effects on language in a child. || In extreme cases a **hemispherectomy** was performed, which is surgery that removes or disconnects one entire **cerebral hemisphere**, typically to relieve seizures in people with epilepsy. **Children in whom the left hemisphere is removed nonetheless seem to develop typical language abilities**. **Adults do not recover language after this procedure**. || Lenneberg's summary of hemispherectomy outcomes, based on **Basser (1962)**, sorts the cases by the age when the lesion was acquired. The outcome depends strongly on that age. >> Before the teens, **49** children with the **left** hemisphere removed had speech that was **not affected or improved**, and only **3** had permanent aphasia, all of whom had aphasia before the operation. >> Before the teens, **38** children with the **right** hemisphere removed had speech not affected or improved, and 5 had permanent aphasia, all of whom had aphasia before the operation. >> During puberty, a single case of left removal had **slow improvement**, with some residual aphasia to the end of life, 27 months after surgery. >> Among **adults** with the **left** hemisphere removed, **none** had speech unaffected or improved, and **6** had **permanent aphasia**. Among adults with the right hemisphere removed, 25 had speech unaffected or improved, and none had permanent aphasia. || These outcomes, among other things, led Lenneberg to hypothesize that a key factor in **closing the critical period** was the development of adult-like **cerebral dominance**. Cerebral dominance is the specialization of one hemisphere, usually the left, for language. >> In young children language is not yet fixed in the left hemisphere, so the right hemisphere can take over. Once dominance is established in adulthood, the right hemisphere can no longer take over, so removing the left hemisphere leaves permanent aphasia.
13|**As always, things are more complicated.** Systematic studies of children with **perinatal strokes** add important detail to the findings on recovery. A perinatal stroke happens between the **prenatal period and 28 days after birth**. Outcomes for these children are **clearly much better than for adults with similar injuries**, but the children still have some effects of the injury. || The first complication is that **early language deficits appear in both right-hemisphere and left-hemisphere patients**. In adults, language problems mainly follow damage to the left hemisphere. In young children, damage to either side causes early language delays. >> **Some deficits are worse for children with right-hemisphere damage**, which is the opposite of the adult pattern. || The second complication is that **language outcomes are generally in the low-normal range**. That suggests a **persistent delay rather than a catching up**. Children recover far better than adults, but they often do not reach the level of children without injury. >> Early plasticity is therefore real but limited. A young brain can reorganize to support language, but the reorganized system may not work as well as the original one would have.
14|**Curtiss and colleagues (1974)** studied the **effects of early deprivation** through the case of **Genie**. Genie was born in **April 1957**. When researchers first met her she was **13 years and 7 months old**, a painfully thin child who looked six or seven. || Her early life involved **extreme isolation and abuse**. **From the age of 20 months** she was confined to a small room under conditions of increasing physical restraint. Most of the time she was harnessed to an infant's potty chair, and otherwise she was confined in a homemade sleeping bag in an infant's crib covered with wire mesh. >> She received minimal care from a mother who was rapidly losing her sight, and she was fed only infant food. >> She was **physically punished by her father if she made any sounds**. Her father and older brother did not speak to her, but at times they barked at her like dogs. >> There was no television or radio in the home, and her contact with other people was extremely limited. || When she was hospitalized for malnutrition, Genie **could not stand up straight or chew food** and was **not toilet trained**. She **did not speak, cry or produce any vocal sounds**. >> Her case is a test of what happens when first-language learning starts in adolescence. >> Her lack of language came together with severe physical, social and emotional deprivation, so the effect of missing language input cannot be separated from her other hardships.
15|**What did Genie learn** during the **four years** that her language was observed? Genie **learned quite a bit** in that time. She came to **comprehend hundreds of words**. She came to comprehend **some aspects of morphology**, and she was **particularly good with plurals**. She also came to comprehend **some prepositions**. >> **Morphology** is the system of meaningful word parts, such as the plural ending, so she learned to use word endings to tell one from many. >> Prepositions are words for spatial relationships between objects, so her learning went beyond names for objects. || A test of prepositions used **buttons and plastic boxes** of different colors and sizes, and Genie had to move them according to spoken instructions. A sample item was “Put the red box behind the blue box.” The test covered **under, over, in, on, behind** and **in front of**, along with **in back of** and **next to**. || Between October 1972 and August 1973, Genie gave **more correct than incorrect responses for every preposition**. Her scores were strongest for in and on. >> **In** had 37 correct and 4 incorrect, and **on** had 36 correct and 9 incorrect. >> **Over** had 11 correct and 2 incorrect, and **under** had 17 correct and 7 incorrect. >> **In front of** had 20 correct and 6 incorrect, **in back of** had 7 correct and 3 incorrect, **next to** had 7 correct and 1 incorrect, and **behind** had 12 correct and 5 incorrect. || Some language can therefore be **acquired late**. This learning took place after puberty, long after the window Lenneberg proposed, but it was **uneven** across different parts of language.
16|**What didn't Genie learn** in the years she was observed? Genie's **production lagged severely behind her comprehension**. **Some lag is typical of development**, because all children understand more than they can say. Genie had also been **punished for making sounds** as a child, which could limit how much she spoke. >> Her speech therefore gave away less than she actually knew. || Her **grammatical abilities developed much more slowly than her vocabulary**. In a test of **conjunctions**, **five familiar objects in a row** were placed in front of her, and she was asked to point to one or more of them. The items included “Show me the fork and pencil,” “Show me the crayon or the knife” and “Show me either the spoon or the crayon.” >> **And** requires pointing to every named object. **Or** requires pointing to just one of them, which depends on understanding a logical relationship. || In her **early responses** Genie treated **or** sentences the same as **and** sentences, so she pointed to both objects. Her **later responses** distinguished and from or, but she still did not understand the meaning of **either/or**. >> Between October 1971 and December 1973, **and** sentences produced **100 correct**, 3 incorrect and 0 unanalyzable responses. >> **Or** sentences produced only **7 correct**, **51 incorrect** and 6 unanalyzable responses. || Genie grasped and almost perfectly but failed or almost entirely, despite a vocabulary of hundreds of words. Her strong vocabulary and weak grammar fit the idea that **grammar depends more on early exposure than vocabulary does**.
17|Genie's case leaves **lots of questions**. One question is **whether Genie was learning disabled**. She might have had difficulties from birth that would have affected her language even without deprivation. Another question is **what the residual impacts of neglect and abuse were on her social development**. Severe trauma could affect learning in many ways beyond the lack of language input. >> Disability, abuse and late exposure to language could each affect grammar learning, and no single case can separate them. || A further limit is that **after four years, funding to study her was pulled**. Nobody can know **what she might have learned after a longer time**. >> The evidence covers only what she learned within those four years. || Nonetheless, the **disparity between her vocabulary development and her grammatical development** is widely interpreted as suggestive that **grammar learning in a first language is specifically impaired after a sensitive period**. She learned hundreds of words but made little progress with grammar. On this interpretation, vocabulary can be learned at any age, while grammar depends on early exposure. >> Her pattern of strong vocabulary and weak grammar became an influential piece of evidence for a sensitive period in first-language learning.
18|**Mayberry, Lock and Kazmi (2002)** studied deaf people who acquired a first language late. **Many deaf children do not get sign language input** but have an **otherwise typical upbringing**. Unlike Genie, these children grow up in ordinary, caring homes, so their only unusual experience is a lack of accessible language early in life. >> That makes them a cleaner test of first-language timing. || **Children aged 9 to 15 have difficulty learning American Sign Language (ASL)** if this is their **first time learning a language**. A **comparison group** of children who went deaf after initially learning a spoken language **do much better**. Both groups learned ASL late, but only one group had an earlier language. **These differences persist into adulthood**. || Two tasks measured the outcome in adults. One task tested ASL and the other tested English. >> ASL performance was measured by **recall of complex ASL sentences**. Deaf adults with **no early language** scored about **36%**, while deaf adults with an **early spoken language** scored about **78%**. >> English performance was measured by **judgments of whether complex English sentences were grammatically correct**, where chance is 50%. Deaf adults with **no early language** scored about **52%**, which is close to chance. Deaf adults with an **early signed language**, ASL in infancy, scored about 80%, and hearing adults with an early spoken language other than English also scored about 80%. || The advantage comes from **having any language early**, whatever its type. Early ASL helped later English learning as much as an early spoken language did, and people with no early language struggled with both ASL and English. >> Early accessible language, whether signed or spoken, provides the foundation for learning languages later.
19|**So, is there a “critical period” or a “sensitive period” for L1?** The evidence supports age effects in learning a first language, or **L1**, but it leaves the exact nature of the window uncertain. >> The window does not wait for language to arrive, and the unusual cases make a firm answer hard to reach. || **Recovery from stroke, and certainly hemispherectomy, looks very, very different in childhood and adulthood**. Children can develop language after damage that would leave adults with permanent aphasia. Age therefore has a strong effect on the brain's ability to reorganize for language. || **Deprivation of input does not appear to extend the critical period**. It might seem that a child without language input would keep the window open until input arrives. Instead, Genie and the deaf adults who learned sign late both have lasting difficulty, especially with grammar. >> The window seems to follow a maturational schedule, whether or not language has been experienced. || **All of these data are from highly unusual cases with lots of other things going on**, so it is **difficult** to reach firm conclusions. Stroke, brain surgery, abuse and neglect each bring complications beyond the timing of language input. >> There is also a **lack of animal models**, because animals do not learn human language. >> Experiments such as **gene knockouts**, which disable specific genes to test their role, cannot be done with people.
