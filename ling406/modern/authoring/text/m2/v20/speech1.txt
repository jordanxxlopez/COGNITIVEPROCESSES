2|**Speech perception** is the process of recovering a linguistic message from the sound of speech. Listeners take an acoustic signal that seems like a mess and somehow pull out a message as clear as “I can see you.” >> In the acoustic signal for “I can see you,” the energy of each sound runs into the next, so nothing in the signal cleanly separates letters or even words. >> Real-time MRI of the **vocal tract** from USC SPAN explains why this happens. The tongue, lips and other articulators move without pause, and the movement for one sound overlaps the movement for the next, so their acoustic effects overlap as well. || Listeners also extract **the same message from very different physical stimuli**. Voices and vocal tract sizes differ, so one word has different acoustic properties from each speaker, yet listeners still hear the same word. >> Across **76 speakers**, each of **ten vowels** covers a broad range of first and second formant values instead of a single point. >> Many of those ranges **overlap**, so one pair of formant values can be one vowel for one speaker and a different vowel for another speaker. || Recognizing speech means finding a **stable linguistic identity** inside enormous physical variation. Matching a recording against one fixed sound pattern for each word would fail for many ordinary productions, because the message depends on how the sounds function in the language.
3|The starting premise is that listeners somehow have to **turn a highly variable, analog signal into something more abstract**, so that the language system can operate on it. An **analog signal** is one that varies continuously, so its frequency, loudness and timing can each shift by tiny amounts. >> A continuous signal has no built-in markers of where one speech sound stops and the next one starts. >> Many small changes leave the speech sound the same, so two physically different productions can still count as the same sound in the language. || The more abstract form is a **symbolic representation**, which identifies a language unit such as a particular consonant or vowel and sets its fine physical detail aside. The unit here is the **phoneme**, which is a sound category that distinguishes words in a language. >> /b/ in “bat” and /p/ in “pat” are different phonemes, because swapping them changes the word. >> Many slightly different acoustic versions of /b/ all count as one phoneme, so the language system can work with a category that stays constant. || **Categorical perception** is thought to reflect this kind of **“translation” from physical to symbolic**. In categorical perception, listeners are less sensitive to differences inside a category and more sensitive to equally large differences that cross a category boundary. >> A change stands out when it alters the identity the listener gives the sound, and a change of the same physical size can pass almost unnoticed when both sounds remain the same phoneme. >> Categorical perception is one account of the translation, and alternative ideas about it exist.
4|The **vocal tract** is the passage that sound travels through in speech, from the larynx through the throat and mouth to the lips. Its shape changes as the tongue, jaw and lips move. Following **Ladefoged (1996)**, the shape of the vocal tract is related in systematic ways to **which resonant frequencies get amplified**. >> A **resonant frequency** is a frequency that a space naturally strengthens, so a tube of a given shape boosts some frequencies and weakens others. >> Changing the shape of the vocal tract changes which frequencies get the biggest boost, so the sound a listener receives depends partly on that shape. || **Formants** are the resonant frequencies that the vocal tract emphasizes, and every vowel has its own combination of them. Each vowel pairs a vocal tract shape with its own pattern of amplified frequencies. >> Across **heed, hid, head and had**, the tongue moves from high and front toward low. Heed has its second formant far above its first, and the two formants draw closer together through hid, head and had. >> Across **hod, hawed, hood and who’d**, the tongue moves back and the lips round more. The amplified frequencies gather at the low end, so the first and second formants sit close together. >> A vowel is defined by the **relationship among its resonances**, which stays similar across speakers even when the exact frequencies differ. || A **formant transition** is a change in a formant’s frequency over time. It happens because the vocal tract is moving into or out of a sound, so as the mouth moves from a consonant into a vowel, the resonances glide from one set of values to another. >> The steady part of a vowel carries information about the vowel, and the moving part carries information about how the neighboring sound is made.
5|A **spectrogram** maps acoustic energy over time and frequency, with time running left to right, frequency running bottom to top, and darker shading marking stronger energy. Each vowel has several dark bands of strong energy, and these bands are its **formants**. >> The vowel recordings come from Rob Hagiwara’s samples of English vowels. || The words **bead, bid, bade, bed, bad, bod, baud, bode, “bood” and booed** all start with /b/ and end with /d/. The consonants are the same in every word, so the differences between the spectrograms come mainly from the vowels. >> **Bead** has a very low first formant and a very high second formant, above 2,000 Hz, so the two bands are far apart. >> Through bid, bade, bed and bad, the first formant rises and the second formant falls, so the two move closer together. >> In the back rounded vowels such as **bode, “bood” and booed**, the second formant drops to about 1,000 Hz or below and sits near the first formant. || **F1** is the lowest formant and **F2** is the next one up, and both run through the whole vowel at the same time. The pattern of these two lowest formants carries most of what tells one vowel from another. >> Bead pairs a **low F1 with a high F2**, while booed pairs a **low F1 with a low F2**.
6|**Source-filter synthesis** builds speech sounds by treating them as two parts. The **source** is the sound energy that the voice produces, and the **filter** is the way the vocal tract shapes that energy. Pulling the two apart makes it possible to change one while the other stays fixed. >> The work is done in **Praat**, which is free software for speech analysis available at praat.org. Praat can display recordings, measure formants and resynthesize sounds. || The recording is a speaker saying **/u/**, the vowel in “booed.” A **waveform** records changes in air pressure over time, and a **spectrogram** of the same sound breaks it into its frequency components. >> The waveform captures timing and loudness, while the spectrogram captures the resonance pattern. || This /u/ has its **first formant at about 300 Hz**, and its **second formant is very low, around 900 Hz**. Both resonances run together through the whole vowel. >> Low F1 and low F2 together fit the back rounded quality of /u/. >> If changing only the filter changes the vowel, then the vowel’s identity must lie in the filter, which is its pattern of formants. These measured formants become the starting point for building new filters.
7|**Praat can find formants automatically**. It follows each resonance through the recording and marks its frequency moment by moment. In this recording of /u/ it has identified **five formants**. >> Several resonances run at the same time, and each one is a frequency component of the same spoken sound. >> Automatic tracking measures the resonance pattern without anyone estimating each frequency by eye. || **Only the first three formants are usually important for phonemic processing**. F1, F2 and F3 carry most of the information that separates one phoneme from another, while the higher formants depend more on the individual voice. >> The numbers give the order of the resonances from low frequency to high frequency, so F1 is the lowest, F2 is the next and F3 is the third. || This example deals with only **the first two formants**, which sit near 300 Hz and 900 Hz in this /u/. Changing only these two is enough to turn one vowel into another, because they carry most of the vowel information. >> F3 and the higher formants stay fixed, so any change that listeners hear comes from F1 or F2 alone.
8|The **source** can be **extracted** from the recorded /u/. Praat builds a model of the filter from the measured formant frequencies, and that model is used to remove the filter’s effect from the recording. What is left is a **more-or-less “unfiltered” version of the voice**. >> What remains is the sound energy that the vocal folds supplied. >> It is only more or less unfiltered, because the separation is an estimate. || The extracted source **sounds like a buzz**. It keeps its energy and its pitch, but it loses the resonance pattern that made it sound like /u/. >> Its energy is spread across many frequencies, without the strong low formants of /u/. >> Pitch survives because it comes from the vibration of the vocal folds, and vowel quality disappears because it comes from the filter. || The separation is **a little bit like doing a multiple regression analysis**. In multiple regression, the contribution of one predictor is estimated and removed so that the rest can be examined. Here the contribution of the filter is estimated and removed, and what is left is treated as the source. >> With the source isolated, new filters can be applied to it. Keeping one source while swapping filters makes the effect of the resonance pattern testable.
9|Three different **filters** can be applied to the same source. A filter specifies which frequencies get more emphasis and which get less, so every filter is a resonance pattern. >> The **first filter** is just the first four formants from the original recording of /u/, so applying it should bring back the original vowel. >> The **second filter** raises **the first formant a little** and leaves the other formants roughly where they were. >> The **third filter** raises **the second formant a lot** and leaves F1 and the higher formants roughly in place. || Each filter changes one part of the resonance pattern instead of shifting every frequency together. In the original, F1 and F2 are both low, which fits /u/. Raising F1 moves only the lowest resonance up, and raising F2 moves only the second resonance up, by a much larger amount. >> Each filter spans 0 to 2.5 seconds in time and 0 to 5,000 Hz in frequency. || The **sound source stays the same** for all three filters, so any change in vowel quality comes from the filter. No new speaker and no new recording are needed. >> Comparing the outputs isolates what a change in the lowest resonance does compared with a change in the second resonance.
10|**Multiplying the source by the filters gives new vowels**. In source-filter terms, the output sound is the source shaped by the filter. Each filter boosts a different set of frequencies, so each combination comes out as a different vowel. >> The source provides the raw sound energy, and the filter decides how much of that energy stays strong in each frequency region. || One source and three filters give three results. The source is identical each time, so the three results differ only in their filters. >> With the **original filter**, F1 and F2 stay low and the result keeps the quality of /u/. >> With **F1 raised a little**, the lowest resonance rises and the vowel quality changes. >> With **F2 raised a lot**, the second resonance rises far and the vowel changes in a different way, because raising F2 moves the back rounded /u/ toward a more front vowel. || **Source-filter synthesis** pulls apart two contributions that ordinary speech combines. The voice provides the sound, and the vocal tract resonances shape its quality. All three outputs share the same buzz, the same pitch and the same timing, yet they sound like different vowels. >> A researcher can change vowel quality by swapping only the filter, without asking a speaker to record each version.
11|Formant values are only **partly** set by the vowel a speaker intends. They are also shaped by **anatomical features** that differ between speakers of the same language, by **different rates of speech**, and by **context**, such as the phones produced before and after a vowel. >> **Anatomy** matters because vocal tracts vary in length and shape, and a longer vocal tract gives lower resonances overall. >> **Speaking rate** matters because fast speech gives the articulators less time to reach their targets. >> **Context** matters because the tongue and lips are already heading toward the next sound or leaving the previous one. A **phone** is one particular spoken realization of a speech sound. || Across 76 speakers, each of ten vowels covers a wide range of formant values, and those ranges overlap. The same F1 and F2 values can be different vowels for different speakers, so no single pair of frequencies defines a vowel for everyone. || Formant values can **nonetheless** give a pretty good description of **vowel space**, especially for **a specific talker speaking carefully**. Vowel space is the layout of a language’s vowels according to their formant frequencies. Within one careful talker, the vowels separate far more cleanly than they do across many talkers. >> All else being equal, **reasonable approximations of vowels** can be produced **by manipulating steady-state formants**. A **steady-state formant** is the resonance in the stable middle of a vowel, as opposed to the transitions at its edges.
12|The **pattern playback synthesizer** turns a painted spectrogram pattern back into sound. Researchers paint bands on a transparent sheet wherever they want acoustic energy in time and frequency, and the machine plays the pattern aloud. Changing the painting changes the sound. >> Researchers could build controlled, speech-like sounds this way instead of relying on natural recordings that vary in many uncontrolled ways. || The machine works by shining light through the painted pattern. The light is first given many frequencies, then filtered by the painting, and then converted to sound. >> Light from a **light source** passes through a **cylindrical lens** onto a spinning **tone wheel**, whose rings modulate the light at many frequencies at once. >> A lens and a **mirror** send that light onto the painted **spectrogram**, and the painted areas pass along the matching frequencies. >> A **light collector** and a **photocell** turn the light into an electrical signal, and an **amplifier** and a **loudspeaker** turn that signal into sound. || A researcher can alter a single formant band or a single transition and hear the result right away. Every other part of the pattern stays exactly the same, so any change in what listeners hear can be traced to the **one painted change**. >> Series of patterns that differed in tiny steps revealed how formant transitions signal consonants.
13|**Stop consonants are distinguished by formant transitions**. The stops **/b/, /d/ and /g/** are made by briefly closing the vocal tract and then opening it. This is sensible given how they are produced, because the audible part of a /b/, /d/ or /g/ happens **while the jaw is opening**. >> During that opening the vocal tract changes shape quickly. The changing shape makes the formants glide from the consonant’s position toward the vowel’s position, so the consonant’s identity lies in how the formants move. || **Liberman and colleagues (1957)** used the **pattern playback synthesizer** to create speech-like sounds that replicated these formant transitions. Each painted pattern had two formants. >> The **lower formant** was nearly the same in every stimulus, rising briefly and then staying steady near 600 cycles per second or below. >> The **upper formant** started at a different frequency in each stimulus and then settled at the same steady vowel level near 1,800 cycles per second. >> In some stimuli the upper formant started low and **rose** into the vowel, in others it was nearly **flat**, and in others it started high and **fell** into the vowel. || The **fourteen patterns** kept the vowel the same and changed only where the upper transition began. A rising transition was heard as **/b/**, a roughly flat one as **/d/** and a falling one as **/g/**. >> Only the transition differed, so the responses of listeners could be tied directly to it.
14|A **speech continuum** is a series of sounds that change step by step along one controlled acoustic property. These stimuli are constructed by **keeping F1 constant** and **varying the F2 onset frequency linearly** from stimulus 1 to stimulus 13. The **F2 onset frequency** is the frequency at which the second formant begins its transition. >> Holding F1 constant removes one possible difference between the stimuli. >> Varying the F2 onset **linearly** means that it changes by the same amount every time, so **adjacent stimuli differ by the same amount across the entire continuum**. || The F2 transition shifts gradually along the series. Early in the series, F2 starts well below the vowel frequency, so the transition rises. As the onset climbs, the rise shrinks until the transition is nearly flat, and later F2 starts above the vowel frequency and falls. || Every step is physically equal, so the continuum is a fair test of whether perception follows the acoustics or the categories. Some neighboring pairs fall inside one consonant category, and others straddle a boundary. >> A purely acoustic listener would hear every step as the same size of change. >> If perception is categorical, steps within a category will sound alike and steps across a boundary will sound different, so any advantage for boundary pairs comes from the boundary itself.
15|An **ideal observer model** predicts how a listener would respond if perception worked according to a particular account. This simple version assumes that perception is **non-categorical**, which means that it tracks the acoustic signal gradually, with no sharp category boundaries. >> The model works as a baseline, because real data can be compared against it to see whether perception is more categorical than a gradual account allows. || The first prediction is that **categorization would get “blurry” as you move away from category prototypes**. A **prototype** is the clearest example of a category. The endpoints of the continuum are /b/ and /g/, with /d/ in the middle. >> Between these points, **stimulus ambiguity should scale with the distance from the prototype**. The farther a stimulus is from the /b/ prototype, the less /b/-like and the more /d/-like it sounds. >> **Stimulus 2** would be heard as **75% /b/ and 25% /d/**. It sits a quarter of the way from the /b/ prototype toward the /d/ prototype, so the responses split in that same proportion. || Under this account, **labeling** changes smoothly across stimuli 1 to 11. The /b/ responses fall in a straight slope to 0 at stimulus 6, where /d/ responses peak at 1, and /d/ then falls to 0 at stimulus 10, where /g/ reaches 1. >> Two neighboring labels are equally likely, at 0.5, halfway between their prototypes. Every change is gradual, with no sudden jump from one label to the next.
16|The same simple ideal observer can assume that perception is **linear**, which means that equal acoustic differences are perceived as equally large. The other important prediction is that **discrimination performance should be independent of labeling**. >> **Labeling** asks a listener to identify a sound as /b/, /d/ or /g/. >> **Discrimination** asks only whether two sounds are **“same” or “different.”** These are separate tasks, even when they use the same continuum. || Labeling **has to have a sort of “peaked” function**. There are three categories and listeners are choosing between labels, so the responses for each category rise toward its prototype and fall away from it. These peaks come from the structure of the labeling task itself. || Discrimination works differently under the linear account. The pairs compared are two steps apart, from stimuli 1 and 3 through stimuli 9 and 11, and every pair has the same physical separation. If perception of differences in F2 onset were constant over the whole continuum, **discrimination accuracy would also be constant**, at about 0.8 for every pair. >> A flat pattern would mean that category boundaries play no role in hearing differences. A peaked pattern would mean that the boundaries make some differences easier to hear. >> A listener can be unsure which name to choose and still hear that two sounds differ.
17|**Liberman and colleagues (1957)** measured actual labeling and discrimination along the continuum from /b/ through /d/ to /g/. Listeners labeled each stimulus, and they also judged pairs of stimuli that were **one step**, **two steps** or **three steps** apart. The results departed sharply from the non-categorical, linear predictions, and this pattern is **categorical perception**. || Labeling had **sharp changes** in identification. Stimuli 1 through about 3 were heard as /b/ close to 100% of the time. Around stimuli 4 and 5 the responses switched almost completely to /d/, and around stimuli 9 and 10 they switched just as quickly to /g/. >> These switches are far more abrupt than the gradual slopes that the ideal observer predicted. >> The narrow region where the label flips is the **category boundary**. || **Listeners were best at discriminating sounds near the category boundaries**. Accuracy peaked at the boundary between /b/ and /d/ and again at the boundary between /d/ and /g/. Pairs inside one category were discriminated poorly, close to **chance**, which is the level expected from guessing. >> Larger steps raised accuracy overall, but the peaks stayed at the boundaries. || Liberman and colleagues also predicted discrimination from the labeling data, on the assumption that listeners can tell two sounds apart only when they give them different labels. The predictions captured much of the real pattern, and between the peaks the predicted accuracy falls to about 50%, which is chance. >> Real accuracy was often a little higher than predicted, especially within categories, so listeners keep some sensitivity to acoustic differences beyond the category labels alone.

19|Data from students in prior years tested categorical perception along a continuum from **beach** to **peach**. The two words differ in their first consonant, which is /b/ in one and /p/ in the other. Each listener completed an **identification** task, labeling each of stimuli 1 to 9, and a **discrimination** task, judging pairs two steps apart from stimuli 1 and 3 through stimuli 7 and 9. || For the first listener, identification stays on one word for the early stimuli and then **switches to the other word** around the middle of the continuum. Responses are mixed only in a small region near the switch. The acoustic change is gradual, but the label changes within a narrow region. >> That listener discriminates best for the pairs that straddle the identification boundary, which are stimuli 4 and 6 and stimuli 5 and 7. >> Pairs well inside one category are hard to tell apart, even though their physical separation is the same. >> The pair of stimuli 6 and 8 falls in between, because stimulus 6 itself received mixed labels. || The second listener **switches word labels later** along the continuum and discriminates best near that later boundary. Different people can place their category boundaries at different points. >> For each listener, the **best discrimination falls close to that person’s own identification boundary**.
20|The data from prior years support a few general thoughts. **Not everyone has exactly the same category boundary**, so a sound near the boundary between beach and peach for one listener can fall squarely inside a category for another listener. >> A pair that straddles one person’s boundary may stay inside one category for someone else. >> When individual boundaries differ, averaging across the group can flatten the boundary peak. || **In general, discrimination is best for stimuli perceived as being in different categories**. A listener may miss a physical difference between two versions of the same phoneme. The same size of difference becomes far easier to hear when the two sounds are heard as different words. || To make the lab doable in a short amount of time, the discrimination experiment included **only “different” trials**. Every pair held two physically different stimuli, so the correct answer on every trial was **“different,”** provided the listener was able to discriminate them. >> Answering “same” meant missing a real difference. >> Accuracy is the share of pairs heard as different. Someone who always said “different” would score perfectly, so low accuracy on within-category pairs means that listeners often failed to hear differences that were really there.
21|More results from prior years compare a continuum from **bear** to **pear** with the continuum from **beach** to **peach**. For bear and pear, identification switches fairly clearly from one word to the other. Discrimination, however, jumps up and down from one pair to the next. >> Accuracy is 0 for the first two pairs and jumps to 1 for stimuli 3 and 5. It then drops back to 0 for stimuli 4 and 6, returns to 1 for stimuli 5 and 7 and falls to 0 for stimuli 6 and 8. >> Another listener’s data for beach and peach fluctuate even more. Identification zigzags instead of switching once, and discrimination bounces between high and low values. || The one iron-clad law of experimental research is that **stuff happens**. Responses to a carefully built continuum can still bounce around. >> Identification records which word a listener chooses, while discrimination records whether a difference is detected, and these two behaviors can vary in different ways across trials and people. >> An isolated rise or drop in discrimination reflects noise in a few responses. || **Can you think of some ways this experiment was maybe not the best, and how would you do it differently?** Only “different” trials were used, so there was no measure of how often listeners said “different” when the sounds were identical. A short session also gave each listener few trials per pair, so a single response could move accuracy a great deal. >> The real test is whether better discrimination lines up with the place where identification changes, across the whole continuum.
22|**How would categorical perception help, or what is the advantage of being worse at hearing some differences between sounds?** The advantage is **stable word recognition**. Categorical perception makes listeners worse at hearing differences within a category, which at first seems like throwing away useful detail. >> Every production of a phoneme differs physically from every other one, because of speakers, speaking rates and neighboring sounds. >> If each small acoustic change were heard as a new identity, everyday versions of one word would sound like unrelated sounds. || Reduced sensitivity within a category lets many acoustic versions count as **one phoneme**. Variation inside a category is tolerated because it leaves the word unchanged. A listener who noticed every variation would struggle to recognize that two productions were the same word. || Differences **between categories** stay easy to hear, so the distinctions that change words are kept. Ignoring small differences within a category frees the listener to focus on the differences that change meaning. >> Being worse at detecting some physical changes is compatible with being better at recognizing a stable message.

24|**Categorical perception in infants** can be tested even though infants cannot name sounds. Infants react to novelty, so researchers watch for a behavior that changes when an infant notices something new. || In the **conditioned head turn procedure**, from Janet Werker’s infant studies lab, an infant learns to turn the head toward a sound when the sound changes. A correct head turn earns an interesting visual reward, such as a moving toy. >> After the infant learns this link, a head turn following a change means that the infant detected the change. || The infant results on categorical perception come from **high-amplitude sucking**. The infant sucks on a pacifier attached to a recording device, and strong sucks make a sound play. >> As the same sound repeats, the infant gets used to it, which is called **habituation**, and the sucking slows. >> If a new sound makes sucking **increase again**, the infant has noticed the change. || Both methods make perception testable before an infant can talk. The experimenter controls exactly which sound difference is presented. A head turn or renewed sucking then reveals sensitivity to that difference without any labeling, even in infants who are months from saying words.
25|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** tested categorical perception in **4-month-old infants** with **high-amplitude sucking**. The sounds varied in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**, which is the delay between the release of a consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration. >> Adults hear a short VOT as a voiced consonant such as /b/ and a longer VOT as a voiceless consonant such as /p/. >> In a syllable with a VOT of +100 milliseconds, voicing begins much later after the release than in a syllable with a VOT of +10 milliseconds. || Infants heard one synthetic syllable over and over until their sucking slowed, and then the sound either changed or stayed the same. The study had three conditions. >> In condition **20D**, the VOT changed from **+20 to +40 milliseconds**, a 20-millisecond change that **crossed** the adult boundary between two phonemic categories. >> In condition **20S**, the VOT also changed by **20 milliseconds**, but both sounds came from the **same** adult category, either minus 20 and 0 or +60 and +80 milliseconds. >> In condition **0**, the control group kept hearing the same sound. || In every group, sucking rose at first and then dropped to around 30 to 40 responses per minute as the sound became familiar. In 20D, sucking **recovered** sharply after the change, back up near 50. In 20S it kept falling with little recovery, and in the control group it kept declining toward about 15. || The two changed conditions were matched at 20 milliseconds, so the difference comes from crossing the boundary. Infants noticed a change that adults would hear as a new consonant, but they did not notice an equally large change inside one category. >> Even at four months, before they can produce or label speech, infants **perceive voicing categorically**.
26|**Perceptual narrowing** is the process in which experience tunes perception to the sounds of the surrounding language, while sensitivity to some distinctions from other languages declines. **Werker and Tees (1984)** tested how age and experience affect the ability of infants to discriminate two **non-English contrasts**. >> The **Hindi contrast** is a distinction between two “ta”-like sounds that English does not use. >> The **Salish contrast** is a distinction between “ki” and “qi” in Nthlakampx, a Salish language, which English also does not use. || The measure was the percentage of infants who reached a **discrimination criterion** at each age. English-learning infants were tested at three ages and compared with infants learning Hindi or Salish. >> At **6 to 8 months**, most English-learning infants discriminated both contrasts, about 95% for Hindi and about 80% for Salish. >> At **8 to 10 months**, fewer succeeded, about 67% for Hindi and about 52% for Salish. >> At **10 to 12 months**, very few succeeded, near 20% for Hindi and near 10% for Salish. >> **Hindi-learning and Salish-learning infants** met the criterion close to 100% of the time for the contrast from their own language. || These results are described as **perceptual reorganization during the first year of life**. Young infants can detect these nonnative contrasts, but with experience of English they become much less likely to discriminate them by about their first birthday. >> Infants who hear the contrast in their own language keep it, so the decline depends on language experience. Contrasts that the language uses are kept, and contrasts that it lacks become hard to hear.
27|These findings feed **a very compelling narrative** about speech perception, which is built from three linked claims. The first claim is that speech perception depends on **categorical perception abilities that are a special property of the language system**. On this claim, the mechanism that sorts sounds into categories is specialized for language. || The second claim is that these abilities are **present very early in development**. The infant experiments with sucking and head turns are taken as evidence that the mechanism is in place long before children talk. || The third claim is that children **start off as “citizens of the world,”** able to hear **all** contrasts, and then **rapidly tune their perceptual systems to their specific language**. On this claim, development is mostly a matter of losing contrasts the language does not use. >> As “citizens of the world,” infants could learn any human language and are ready to hear the distinctions of every one. >> Tuning means that the contrasts of the surrounding language stay available while contrasts from other languages fade, as in the Werker and Tees (1984) results. || The narrative covers both the **starting state** and **developmental change**. It proposes broad, language-specific abilities at birth, and it treats development mostly as narrowing from that broad start. >> Each early result seems to fit, because infants discriminate categorically before talking, hear contrasts from many languages and lose nonnative contrasts with experience.
28|The compelling narrative meets **some issues**, and three of them concern **categorical perception**. Each one weakens a different part of the narrative. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to language**. It also appears with nonspeech sounds, which undercuts the claim that it is special to language. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to humans**. Nonhuman animals also perceive speech sounds categorically, which undercuts the claim that it is special to humans. >> Categorical perception is also **task-dependent** in its results. Results look more or less categorical depending on what the listener is asked to do, and choosing between labels makes responses more categorical than a task that measures fine acoustic differences. || Another issue concerns **infant speech perception**, which is **not actually “universal.”** Some contrasts are harder for infants to detect than others from early on. Development includes initial differences in sensitivity as well as changes driven by experience, so not all contrasts are equally available at birth. || Together, these issues weaken the strong claim that every speech distinction starts out equally available in a mechanism devoted only to language. Task dependence also suggests that some categorical patterns come from the way listeners are asked to respond.
29|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** tested categorical perception with **non-speech sounds**. The sounds varied in **rise time**, which is how quickly a sound reaches its full loudness after it begins. >> A sound with a **rapid rise time** sounds **plucked**, like a plucked string. >> A sound with a **slow rise time** sounds **bowed**, like a bowed string. || The stimuli included **sawtooth waves**, **sine waves** and **speech syllables**, each made with rise times such as **10 milliseconds** and **70 milliseconds**. With a 10-millisecond rise the sound reaches full strength almost at once, and with a 70-millisecond rise it builds up gradually. >> Pluck and bow are labels for two kinds of sound onset, and they are not phonemes. || For the sawtooth waves, listeners labeled short rise times as **pluck** and long rise times as **bow**. Pluck responses started near 100% and fell steeply around 30 to 50 milliseconds, where bow responses rose toward 100%. >> Discrimination of pairs that differed by 10 milliseconds stayed around 55 to 60% for most pairs, only a little above chance. >> Discrimination **peaked near 75%** for the pairs around 30 to 50 milliseconds, right where the labels switched. || The link between a label boundary and better discrimination appears for a sound distinction that has nothing to do with language. The physical continuum is gradual, yet the preferred label changes over a narrow region, and discrimination improves there.
30|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** tested whether **infants** have categorical perception of **non-speech sounds**. They used high-amplitude sucking to measure whether a change in sound was detected. >> Infants heard one sound repeatedly until their sucking declined. >> The sound was then shifted, and the change was compared across three conditions. || The three conditions were **no change**, a change **within categories** and a change **between categories**. In every condition, sucking started near 20 per minute and declined to about 12 before the shift as the sound became familiar. >> After **no change**, sucking stayed low, near 12. >> After a **within-category** change, sucking also stayed low. >> After a **between-category** change, sucking jumped back up to about 18 and then declined again as the new sound became familiar. || Infants responded more strongly to a non-speech change that crossed a category boundary than to a change within a category. This mirrors the pattern that Eimas and colleagues (1971) found for speech. >> Infants cannot be drawing on knowledge of words or phonemes when they respond to plucked and bowed sounds. Their categorical response must come from **more general auditory processing**, which could also underlie their categorical response to speech.
31|Categorical perception of speech sounds has also been found in **chinchillas**, by **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and in the earlier work of **Kuhl and Miller (1975)**. The animals were trained with **operant conditioning**, which is a form of learning in which consequences change how often a behavior occurs. >> Each chinchilla learned that either the **0-lag** stimulus or the **80-millisecond lag** stimulus was an **SD** for food. **SD** stands for **discriminative stimulus**, which is a signal that a response will be rewarded. >> The lag is **voice onset time**. Humans hear a 0-millisecond lag as /ga/ and an 80-millisecond lag as /ka/. || After training, the chinchillas were tested with intermediate VOTs along the continuum from /ga/ to /ka/, and human listeners were tested on the same sounds. Short VOTs were labeled /ga/ almost every time, and long VOTs almost never. The switch happened over a fairly narrow range, mostly between about 30 and 70 milliseconds, and the chinchilla curves had the **same steep, categorical shape** as the human curves. || The study also varied the **burst** and **aspiration noise**. The **burst** is the brief noise at the release of a stop consonant, and **aspiration** is the breathy noise that follows a voiceless release. >> Making the burst and aspiration louder shifted the boundary toward shorter VOTs, made the switch steeper, or both. >> This happened for one chinchilla and for one human listener, so both species responded to these sound properties in a similar way. >> Animals without human language can sort a speech continuum into categories, which argues against the view that categorical perception is special to human language.
32|Some speech contrasts are **less salient than others** for infants. A **salient** contrast is one that is easy to notice. >> The contrasts tested here differ in **place of articulation**, which is where in the mouth a sound is made. >> In **[ma]**, the nasal is made with the lips. In **[na]**, it is made with the tongue near the ridge behind the teeth. In **[ŋa]**, which begins with the sound at the end of “sing,” it is made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate. || The study compared the **looking time** of infants on **same** trials, where the syllable stayed the same, and on **change** trials, where it switched. Longer looking on change trials means that the infant noticed the change. >> At **4 to 5 months**, English-hearing infants looked about 3.3 seconds on same trials and about 5.4 seconds on change trials for the native contrast between **[ma] and [na]**. >> For the nonnative contrast between **[na] and [ŋa]**, the same infants looked about 4.5 seconds on same trials and about 4.2 seconds on change trials. They did not detect this change, and English never uses [ŋ] at the start of a syllable. || **Filipino-learning infants** hear the contrast between [na] and [ŋa] in their own language. At **6 to 8 months**, they looked about 3.8 seconds on both kinds of trials, so they did not yet detect the change. By **10 to 12 months**, looking rose from about 3.3 seconds on same trials to about 4.8 seconds on change trials. >> Some contrasts start out hard for infants to hear, even when they belong to the native language. Experience with the language **strengthens** sensitivity to such contrasts over the first year, so development includes gains as well as losses.
