54|The **Revised Hierarchical Model** of **Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** explains interactions between a first language, L1, and a second language, L2, in terms of **spreading activation**. Spreading activation means that activating one representation partly activates the representations connected to it. The model connects an L1 vocabulary, an L2 vocabulary and a shared store of concepts. >> **Lexical links** join words across the two languages, and **conceptual links** join each vocabulary to meanings. L1 words have strong links to concepts, while L2 words have weaker conceptual links. || Consider what happens in this model when the task of **Marian and Spivey** is performed in L2, when Russian-English bilinguals hear the English instruction to pick up the speaker. The concept for “speaker” **partially activates the Russian word for “matches,”** which is spichki. The link between speaker and spichki is sound overlap, because the two words begin with the same sounds and share no meaning. >> This activity **feeds back to the conceptual activation** for “matches,” because L1 words are strongly connected to their concepts. Conceptual activation for “matches” leads to **eye movements toward the L1 competitor**, although the listener can still pick up the speaker correctly. || When the task is done in L1, we might imagine that the **conceptual links with L2 are too weak** to create this kind of interference reliably. An English competitor would need a strong link from its English name to its concept to attract the eyes during a Russian task. >> The unequal strength of the links explains why the other language interferes in one direction but not in the other.
