2|The question of **nature and nurture in child language** asks how much of language comes from built-in capacities and how much comes from experience. **Nativist positions** grew out of **Chomsky's critique of Skinner**. Skinner was a behaviorist who explained behavior, language included, with general learning mechanisms such as reward and punishment, and Chomsky argued that those mechanisms could not explain how children acquire grammar. >> For Chomsky, the important feature of language is its **generativity**, which is the ability to produce and understand an unlimited number of new sentences. Acquiring a language therefore means **identifying the abstract grammar** that produces the sentences of the language. || The **general learning mechanisms** studied by behaviorists were **not up to this task**, for two reasons. First, grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are **not "similar"** in any straightforward way, because one small change can make a sentence ungrammatical while two grammatical sentences can look nothing alike. Second, the context for language acquisition does not look like **operant conditioning**, which shapes behavior with rewards and punishments that follow it. >> Children are not reliably rewarded for grammatical sentences or punished for ungrammatical ones, so the **reward and punishment contingencies do not hold**. || Nativist approaches assert two things instead. Children have **innate knowledge of what language is like**, called **Universal Grammar**, which is a built-in set of principles that limits the grammars a child will consider. Children also use **language-specific acquisition processes**, which are learning mechanisms built specifically for language. >> Chomsky put the argument this way, "The fact that all normal children acquire essentially comparable grammars of great complexity with remarkable rapidity suggests that human beings are somehow specially designed to do this, with data-handling or 'hypothesis-formulating' ability of unknown character and complexity." >> Every typical child reaches a complex grammar quickly even though experiences differ widely, so the speed and uniformity suggest something built in.
3|**Categorical perception** is the pattern in which listeners hear differences between speech sounds in terms of categories. Early theories treated it as a **language-specific** and **species-specific** phenomenon. Language-specific means it was thought to occur only for language, and species-specific means it was thought to occur only in humans. >> At the time, **only humans** were known to show categorical perception, and **only for speech sounds**, so it looked like a unique feature of human language. || The classic evidence comes from a synthetic consonant continuum of fourteen stimuli. The upper formant transition changes in equal steps from one stimulus to the next while the lower formant stays the same. In **labeling**, listeners switch sharply from **/b/** to **/d/** around stimuli 4 and 5, and from /d/ to **/g/** around stimuli 9 and 10. >> In **discrimination**, accuracy **peaks near those boundaries** and drops inside each category, whether the pairs are one, two or three steps apart. || Categorical perception is a compelling example of how **similarity between stimuli is defined in linguistic, rather than physical, terms**. Two sounds that differ by the same acoustic amount can sound almost identical if they belong to the same category. Two sounds with that same acoustic difference can sound clearly different if they fall on opposite sides of a category boundary. >> What counts as similar therefore depends on the categories of the language, and these patterns were taken as signs of a specialized speech system.
4|**Categorical perception in infants** can be measured through behaviors that change when an infant notices something new. Head turns and sucking reveal what an infant hears without the infant saying anything. Neither method asks the infant to name or produce a sound. >> The experimenter decides exactly which acoustic difference is presented, so a head turn or renewed sucking tells whether the infant detected that difference. || In the **conditioned head turn procedure**, used in Janet Werker's infant studies lab, an infant learns to turn toward a sound when it changes. The infant sits on a parent's lap while an experimenter holds the infant's attention forward with toys. A correct head turn after a change earns an interesting visual reward, such as a lit-up moving toy. >> Once the infant has learned this link, a head turn after a sound change means **the infant heard the change**. || **High-amplitude sucking** relies on the infant's natural response to something new. The infant sucks on a pacifier attached to a pressure recorder, and strong sucks make a sound play. As the same sound repeats, the infant grows used to it, which is called **habituation**, and sucking slows down. >> If a new sound makes sucking **increase again**, the infant has noticed the change. >> The head turn depends on a learned link between a sound change and a reward, while sucking depends on a natural response to novelty, so the two methods give complementary evidence.
5|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** tested categorical perception in **4-month-old infants** using **high-amplitude sucking**. The sounds varied in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**, which is the delay between the release of a stop consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration. In a syllable with a long VOT of +100 milliseconds, voicing starts much later after the release than in a syllable with a short VOT of +10 milliseconds. >> Infants heard a syllable over and over until their sucking slowed down, and then the sound either changed or stayed the same. || The experiment compared **three conditions**. In **20D**, the two sounds differed by **20 milliseconds** of VOT, +20 and +40 milliseconds, and came from **different adult phonemic categories**. In **20S**, the sounds also differed by 20 milliseconds, either −20 and 0 or +60 and +80 milliseconds, but came from the **same category**. In **0**, the **control** condition, the same sound continued throughout. || In every condition, sucking rose above baseline and then declined as the sound became familiar. In 20D, sucking **jumped back up** after the change, to around 50 responses per minute. In 20S, sucking showed **little recovery** and kept falling, and in the control condition it kept declining to about 15 responses per minute. >> Infants responded to a change that **crossed an adult phoneme boundary** but not to an equally large change within a category. >> The two changed conditions were matched in acoustic size, so the difference comes from crossing the boundary, and the control condition establishes what happens with repetition alone.
6|**Why was Eimas's result a big deal?** Categorical perception was considered to be the **first step in turning speech into a symbolic format** that later linguistic processes could use. A symbolic format stores a sound as a category, such as /b/. >> Finding this ability in infants suggested that the starting point for language processing was already in place. || **Four-month-olds are really little**, and their other abilities are only beginning. Some **gross motor milestones** for that age, meaning large movements of the body, are manipulating objects with two hands and holding the head and chest up while prone, which means lying face down. Their **color vision and general visual acuity**, the sharpness of vision, are still "in progress." They are just starting to produce **vowel-like sounds** instead of creaky whines. >> Basic perception in other areas is still developing, and four-month-olds cannot yet make speech sounds well. || This is **very, very early in development** for an apparently language-specific ability to emerge, so it **seemed like evidence for innateness**. Infants sort speech sounds into categories before they can produce them and while other skills are immature. >> Because speech categorization ran so far ahead of other abilities, it looked built in instead of learned through long experience.
7|**Perceptual narrowing** for speech categories is the decline in sensitivity to sound distinctions that are not used in the language a child hears. At around six months, **English-learning infants** show evidence of discriminating speech contrasts they have never heard before. Over the course of development, the ability to perceive these contrasts **declines**. >> **Werker and Tees (1984)** tested English-learning infants on two non-English contrasts. The **Hindi contrast** is a distinction between two "ta"-like sounds, and the **Salish contrast** is a distinction between "ki" and "qi" from Nthlakampx, a Salish language. || They measured the share of infants at each age who reached a **discrimination criterion**. At **6 to 8 months**, about 95 percent of English-learning infants discriminated the Hindi contrast and about 80 percent discriminated the Salish contrast. At **8 to 10 months** these fell to about 67 percent and 52 percent, and at **10 to 12 months** they fell to about 20 percent and 10 percent. Hindi-learning and Salish-learning infants discriminated their own native contrast close to 100 percent of the time. >> This is **perceptual reorganization during the first year of life**. Infants who hear the contrast in their language keep it, so the decline **depends on language experience**. || **How is this related to categorical perception?** As infants form the categories of their native language, sounds that their language groups together become harder to tell apart. >> A nonnative distinction that falls within one native category comes to be heard as a **within-category difference**.
8|The infant findings support **a very compelling narrative**. In it, speech perception depends on **categorical perception abilities** that are a **special property of the language system**. >> These abilities are **present very early in development**, as the infant studies suggest. || Children start off as **"citizens of the world,"** able to hear **all contrasts**. That means they have **innate, language-general knowledge** about speech, which is knowledge that applies to the speech sounds of every language and not one particular language. Children then **rapidly tune their perceptual systems** to the specific language in their environment. >> Calling infants "citizens of the world" captures the idea that a newborn is ready to learn any human language. || The **role of learning**, then, is to **select** among the many possible **innately specified phonetic contrasts**. Learning narrows down the **inventory**, which is the full set of phonetic contrasts the child can perceive, to just those that are meaningful in the child's native language. >> Learning works by elimination, because it drops the contrasts the native language does not use and keeps the innate categories the language needs. >> Infants start with a full set of possible contrasts, so the native language decides which innate categories survive.
9|The citizens-of-the-world view faces **some problems**. Three of them concern **categorical perception**. Categorical perception is **not limited to language**, because it also occurs for nonspeech sounds. It is also **not limited to humans**, because animals without language perceive speech sounds categorically. >> Categorical perception is also **task-dependent**. How categorical the results look depends on what the listener is asked to do, such as naming a sound or judging whether two sounds differ. || The fourth problem concerns **infant speech perception**, which is **not actually "universal."** Infants do not begin able to hear every contrast equally well. >> Some contrasts are hard for young infants to detect, even contrasts that occur in their own language. || Each problem targets a different part of the narrative. The nonspeech and animal findings question whether categorical perception is special to language, and the task effects question how categorical perception really is. Uneven infant sensitivity questions whether all contrasts are innately available. >> A full account of development has to explain contrasts that **improve with experience** as well as contrasts that are lost, so learning must **build categories** as well as remove them.
10|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** tested categorical perception with **non-speech sounds**. The sounds varied in **rise time**, which is how quickly a sound grows to full loudness after it starts. A **short rise time** produces a sound like a **plucked** string, and a **long rise time** produces a sound like a **bowed** string. >> The stimuli included **sawtooth waves**, speech syllables and sine waves, each made with a 10-millisecond rise time and a 70-millisecond rise time. >> With a 10-millisecond rise the wave reaches full size almost at once, and with a 70-millisecond rise it grows gradually. || For the sawtooth waves, listeners identified each sound as a pluck or a bow and also discriminated pairs that were 10 milliseconds apart. **Pluck responses** started near 100 percent at short rise times and dropped steeply between about 30 and 50 milliseconds, while **bow responses** rose in the same region to near 100 percent. Discrimination stayed near 55 to 60 percent for most pairs. It **peaked near 75 percent** for pairs around 30 to 50 milliseconds, **where the labels switched**. || The link between a label boundary and better discrimination appears here for a sound distinction that has **nothing to do with phonemes**. Pluck and bow are labels for kinds of sound onset and are not speech categories. >> Categorical perception can therefore arise in **general auditory processing**, without a mechanism specific to language.
11|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** tested whether **infants** show categorical perception of **non-speech sounds**, using high-amplitude sucking. Infants heard a sound over and over until their sucking slowed down. The sound was then changed, and three conditions were compared. >> The conditions were **no change**, a change **within categories** and a change **between categories**. || In all three conditions, sucking started near 20 per minute and declined to about 12 before the change, as the sound became familiar. After no change, sucking stayed near 12. After a within-category change, sucking also stayed near 12. After a **between-category change**, sucking **jumped back up** to about 18 and then declined again as the new sound became familiar. >> Infants responded more strongly to a non-speech change that **crossed a category boundary** than to a change within a category. || This is the **same pattern** that Eimas and colleagues found for speech. Adults show categorical labeling and discrimination for plucked and bowed sounds, and infants show categorical discrimination for nonspeech changes. >> Neither group needs language knowledge to show the pattern, so early categorical perception reflects a **general auditory ability**.
12|**Chinchillas** also show categorical perception of **speech sounds**, following **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and the earlier work of **Kuhl and Miller (1975)**. The animals were trained using **operant conditioning**, a form of learning in which a behavior becomes more likely when it is followed by a reward. Each chinchilla learned that either the **0-millisecond lag** stimulus or the **80-millisecond lag** stimulus was an **SD** for food. >> SD stands for **discriminative stimulus**, which is a signal that a response will be rewarded. >> The lag is the **voice onset time**, and humans hear a 0-millisecond lag as /ga/ and an 80-millisecond lag as /ka/. || After training, the animals were tested with intermediate VOTs along the continuum from /ga/ to /ka/, and human listeners heard the same sounds. The measure was the proportion of stimuli labeled /ga/. The /ga/ responses stayed near 1 at short VOTs and then fell steeply to near 0, mostly between about 30 and 70 milliseconds. The chinchillas' labeling had the same **steep, categorical shape** as the human listeners' labeling. || **Increasing the intensity of the burst and aspiration noise** shifted the category boundary to a **shorter VOT**, made the switch steeper, or both, for one chinchilla and one human listener. The **burst** is the brief noise at the release of a stop, and **aspiration** is the breathy noise that follows a voiceless release. Both species responded to these acoustic properties in a similar way. >> Animals without language sort a speech continuum into categories, so categorical perception of speech is **shared with other species**.
13|**Narayan and colleagues (2010)** found that **some contrasts are less salient than others for infants**. A **salient** contrast is one that is easy to notice. The contrasts they tested involve **nasal consonants** in the **onset**, which is the start of a syllable. >> The nasals differ in **place of articulation**, which is where in the mouth the sound is made. The sound **/m/** is made with the lips, **/n/** with the tongue just behind the teeth, and **/ŋ/**, the final sound in "sing," with the back of the tongue against the soft palate. || Infants heard either the same syllable repeated or a syllable that switched, and the researchers compared looking time on same trials and change trials. Longer looking on change trials means the infant noticed the change. For the native contrast between **[ma] and [na]**, English-hearing infants at 4 to 5 months looked about 3.3 seconds on same trials and about 5.4 seconds on change trials. For the nonnative contrast between **[na] and [ŋa]**, the same infants looked about 4.5 seconds on same trials and about 4.2 seconds on change trials, so there was **no increase** for the change. || A **place contrast for nasals in onset**, /na/ and /ŋa/, is **not readily perceived by four-month-olds, independent of language experience**. In contrast, /ma/ and /na/ is **relatively salient**. >> The gap comes from the sounds themselves and not from experience, because some contrasts are harder to hear from the start. >> That undercuts the idea that every contrast begins equally available.
14|**Narayan and colleagues (2010)** also found that **between 6 and 10 months**, exposure to the contrast between /na/ and /ŋa/ in a **Filipino language environment increases its salience**. Filipino treats [na] and [ŋa] as different syllables, so infants learning Filipino hear the contrast regularly. >> At **6 to 8 months**, Filipino-learning infants looked about 3.8 seconds on both same and change trials, so the change had no measurable effect. >> At **10 to 12 months**, looking rose from about 3.3 seconds on same trials to about 4.8 seconds on change trials, so by then they noticed the switch. || English-hearing infants at 4 to 5 months showed no response to this contrast, while they did respond to [ma] and [na]. So it **cannot be that all speech contrasts are available innately**. Sensitivity to [na] and [ŋa] starts out weak, even for infants whose language uses it, and hearing the contrast over the first year makes it easier to detect. || **Exposure to contrasts in the environment is necessary for acquisition in some cases**. Learning can strengthen a contrast as well as preserve it. >> Development therefore includes **building up sensitivity** to hard contrasts, not only losing sensitivity to unused ones, so experience plays an active role in shaping speech categories.
15|**What is the alternative to the nativist approach?** The alternative is the **emergentist approach**. Emergentist approaches assume that **associative learning mechanisms** are sensitive to **statistical regularities in the environment**. >> **Associative learning** is learning that two things go together because they repeatedly occur together. **Statistical regularities** are patterns in how often things occur and which things occur together. >> On this view, language structure emerges from general learning mechanisms working on these patterns in rich input. || Classical conditioning is the simplest example of associative learning. It can be described with three neurons, a **bell** neuron, a **food detector** neuron and a **salivation** neuron. The food detector already connects strongly to salivation, so food produces salivation. When the bell repeatedly occurs together with food, the **synapse** between the bell neuron and the salivation neuron, which is a connection between neurons, is **strengthened**. After enough pairings, the bell alone can produce salivation. || **How can this extend beyond conditioning to support learning for pattern recognition?** **Pattern recognition** is the ability to identify a recurring structure, such as a speech sound, across many varied examples. The same strengthening of connections could link acoustic features that tend to occur together. >> Repeated co-occurrence in speech could then build up categories that were never specified in advance. >> Applied to speech, infants would learn categories by tracking which sounds occur together and how often, instead of starting with a full set of built-in categories.
16|The **American vowels** give an example of statistical regularities. When **Kuhl and others** talk about **learning the statistics of distributions in the environment**, they mean patterns like these. A **distribution** describes how often each value occurs across many examples. || A **vowel space** is determined by the **first and second formants**. The first formant runs from 300 to 1,200 Hz and the second formant from 600 to 3,400 Hz. Each production of a vowel is one point in this space, so each vowel forms a cloud of points across many productions. >> The clouds **overlap** heavily, so a single pair of formant values can belong to more than one vowel. >> That overlap is why speech categorization is difficult, because listeners must learn where each vowel is centered and how far it spreads. || Maybe we learn the vowels of our language by **extracting the statistics of these distributions**. Over many exposures, the listener tracks where productions of each vowel tend to cluster, so the categories reflect the shape of the input. A **"prototypical" /a/** would then be just the **mean**, or perhaps the **mode**, of the distribution of all the /a/ sounds we hear. >> The mean is the average of all the values, and the mode is the most frequent value. >> Either way, the prototype comes from experience, so it can differ across languages and listeners.
17|**Does anything like that actually happen?** **Maye and colleagues (2002)** tested whether infants actually learn from distributions. They had infants **listen passively** to stimuli from a continuum from [da] to [ta] with eight steps that differed in **voice onset time**. >> Passive listening means the infants simply heard the sounds, with no task or reward. || Two groups heard different distributions during familiarization. In the **bimodal distribution**, stimuli 2 and 7 were presented most often, 16 times each, while the middle stimuli were rare. Two peaks give **evidence for two different categories**. In the **unimodal distribution**, stimuli 4 and 5 in the middle were presented most often, while the ends were rare. One peak gives **evidence for a single category**. || At test, infants listened to **stimuli 3 and 6** in either **alternating** or **non-alternating** trials. In alternating trials the two stimuli switched back and forth, and in non-alternating trials one stimulus repeated. If infants treat 3 and 6 as different sounds, the two kinds of trials should hold their attention differently. >> After unimodal exposure, six-month-olds looked 4.85 seconds on alternating trials and 4.53 seconds on non-alternating trials, and eight-month-olds looked 4.98 and 5.20 seconds. >> After bimodal exposure, six-month-olds looked 5.66 and 6.41 seconds, and eight-month-olds looked 5.45 and 6.15 seconds. || **Only infants pre-exposed to the bimodal distribution** were sensitive to the difference between conditions. The unimodal infants looked about equally long on both kinds of trials, as if they had **learned to treat stimuli 3 and 6 as members of the same category**. >> A few minutes of exposure to a distribution changed how infants grouped the sounds, which supports statistical learning of speech categories.
18|There is experimental evidence that **social interaction is important for learning**. In one experiment, **nine-month-old American infants** were exposed to **Mandarin Chinese** in twelve 25-minute sessions. Some infants had **live exposure**, with a Mandarin speaker playing and reading with them in person. Others had auditory or audiovisual exposure through audio or television, and a control group had English exposure instead. >> After exposure, infants were tested on a **Mandarin phonetic contrast** using the head-turn technique. Infants with live Mandarin exposure scored about 65 percent correct, above the English control group at about 56 percent. >> Infants with TV or audio exposure scored at about the same level as the controls, close to chance. The same exposure to a Mandarin speaker is therefore **more effective when it happens in person and is interactive** than when it is viewed as a video. || A second experiment manipulated **social responses**. **Eight-month-old infants** received feedback from their mothers in response to their vocalizations. In the **contingent condition**, mothers responded right after the infant vocalized, so the response depended on the infant's behavior. In the **yoked-control condition**, mothers responded on a schedule copied from another infant, so the responses were **noncontingent**. >> Each session had 30 minutes of familiarization, then a 10-minute baseline, a 10-minute social response phase and a 10-minute **extinction** phase, in which the responses stopped. || The measure was the proportion of syllables among the infants' vocalizations. At baseline both groups produced about 0.05 to 0.08. During the social response phase the contingent group rose to about **0.32** while the noncontingent group stayed near 0.05. During extinction the contingent group stayed higher, at about 0.2 compared with about 0.1. >> **Contingent social feedback increased the quantity and complexity of infants' vocalizations**, so the responses changed what the infants produced. || Social interactions contingent on the production of different sounds seem to function much like a **reinforcing stimulus**, which is something that makes a behavior more likely when it follows that behavior. One idea is that humans find social interaction with other humans **innately reinforcing**. Another is that we are innately good at using **social cues** to learn from other people.
19|**Statistical learning with constraints** means learning from patterns in the input in a way that is guided and limited. **What do we mean by "constraints"?** The word covers a lot of things, and three kinds stand out. || The first kind limits **which stimulus properties can be contrastive**. A **contrastive** property is one that distinguishes words, such as voicing, which separates /b/ from /p/. Speaker identity and speech rate change the sound a great deal but do not change which word is said, so they are **not contrastive**. >> A learner who tracked every statistical pattern would form categories for different voices and speeds, so learning has to focus on the properties that matter for meaning. || The second kind is **based on earlier learning**. What a learner has already acquired shapes what is easy to learn next. Once native categories are in place, new distinctions that cut across them become harder to notice and learn. >> Constraints of this kind are central to **sensitive periods**, in which earlier experience limits later learning. || The third kind comes from **social and interactive contexts for learning**. The same sounds are learned more effectively in live, responsive interaction than from a recording, and contingent responses from caregivers shape infants' own vocal behavior. >> Learning depends on the patterns in the input, on what the learner already knows and on the social situation in which the input arrives. >> Together, these constraints help explain why learning is fast and why it focuses on the distinctions that matter.
