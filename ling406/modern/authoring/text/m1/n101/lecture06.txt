2|**The Cognitive Revolution brought internal mental states back into explanations of language.** There is a very old tradition of describing languages in abstract, formal terms like those used in Artificial Intelligence. >> A **formal symbolic system** (abstract symbols plus rules) is one way to describe a language. >> Experimental psychology in the 1950s and 1960s was actively hostile to talk of an **internal state**. >> Behaviorism explained learning through observable stimuli, responses and consequences instead. || Two works framed the debate. >> **Skinner's Verbal Behavior** tried to explain language learning by **operant conditioning** (learning through reinforced responses). >> **Chomsky's (1959) review** of Verbal Behavior challenged that account. >> The review is better remembered than the book itself. >> It was an important moment in **the Cognitive Revolution**. || For Chomsky, a **grammar** is an internal system. >> It characterizes sentences a person has never encountered.
3|**The formal tradition grew out of early computers, which raised the question of how machines differ from human intelligence.** Interest in **Artificial Intelligence** dates back to the beginning of the digital computer. >> **Alan Turing** thought about this question a lot. >> He developed decryption devices for the British Army during World War II. || Computers were invented as **universal function approximators** (machines that carry out many kinds of calculations and logical operations). >> No machine had done so many different kinds of operations before. >> Earlier machines did one fixed task. >> This flexibility raised the question of how the computer differed from human intelligence, if at all. || The question links language to AI. >> If language is symbols and procedures, a computer can explore what those procedures accomplish.
4|**The Turing test counts a computer as intelligent if it can replace a player in the imitation game without the examiner noticing.** The **imitation game** has three players. >> A and B are a man and a woman. >> C is an examiner whose job is to figure out the gender of A and B. >> A tries to fool C. >> B tries to help C guess correctly. || In the test, a **computer** replaces either A or B. >> If C does not notice, the computer has demonstrated intelligence. >> C never inspects the program. >> C judges only the conversation. || The test turns intelligence into **observable conversational performance**. >> **Natural language** is the medium of the judgment.
5|**Turing's proposal centers on ordinary language use and on learning, not on expert skills.** The test has several interesting features. >> It depends on **natural language**. >> It involves **deception**, because one player tries to fool the examiner. >> Turing explicitly excluded **expert behaviors**, such as calculating large sums or composing poetry. || Turing also concluded how best to build an intelligent machine. >> Build something with the cognitive capabilities of a **child**. >> Let it learn to be more like an **adult**. >> Intelligence then develops through experience. >> It is not supplied as a finished list of adult answers.
6|**Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver solved formal problems by applying procedures to symbols.** It is a later example of **GOFAI** (the approach that models intelligence as operations on explicit symbols). >> It takes an abstract description of a formal problem. >> It applies procedures to symbols to find a solution. >> Each configuration is a **state**, and a legal move leads to another state. >> A solution is a path of legal moves to the **goal state**. || It was very good at games and puzzles. >> The **Tower of Hanoi** is one example. Each arrangement of disks is a state. >> **Chess** is another example. || It was also good at mathematics and logic. >> It handled **geometry proofs**. >> It handled **algebra word problems**.
7|**Early AI favored formal problems because of limited technology and because of a belief about what intelligence is.** Two reasons drove the emphasis. || The first reason was **technology**. >> Inputs were still very limited, such as punchcards and machine language. >> No one could input a high-resolution image and ask the computer to find the face. || The second reason was **theoretical commitments**. >> Intelligence was thought to be exemplified by **rational behavior** of this kind. >> You can train a **pigeon** to guide a missile, but it does not know what it is doing. >> Whether AI programs know what they are doing is a deep argument too. >> AI programs at least **represent goal states** and choose procedures for achieving them.
8|**Chomsky argued that knowing a language means having an internalized grammar, not a set of conditioned responses.** A **grammar** (a system that enumerates sentences and gives each a structural description) captures remarkable abilities. >> Speakers tell **sentences from nonsentences**. >> Speakers understand **new sentences**, in part. >> Speakers notice **ambiguities**. || New sentences are not related to familiar ones by any simple **similarity**. >> The similarity is not formal, semantic, statistical or a shared grammatical frame. >> So Chomsky called talk of **generalization** pointless and empty. >> We recognize a new sentence because our grammar **generates** it. >> We understand it partly by working out how the grammar **derives** it. || All normal children acquire comparable complex grammars with remarkable speed. >> Chomsky took this to mean humans are **specially designed** for it. >> He proposed a **hypothesis-formulating** ability of unknown character.
9|**Linguistic knowledge is a grammar, so people can judge whether any new sentence is grammatical.** A **grammar** is a set of abstract rules that produce grammatical sentences. >> The number of grammatical sentences is **infinite**. >> People still make a **grammaticality judgment** (a decision about whether a sentence is well formed) for any new sentence. >> That ability is remarkable because the sentence was never heard. || The grammar also handles ambiguity. >> One string of words can get more than one structure. >> So the words alone do not fix the meaning. || These judgments become the main evidence. >> People's **intuitions** about grammaticality are the key data for the study of language.
10|**Grammatical sentences share no obvious similarity, so stimulus generalization cannot explain how people handle new ones.** **Stimulus generalization** (a trained response spreading to similar stimuli) is the behaviorist account of novelty. >> It needs a similarity between old and new items. >> Grammatical sentences have no obvious similarities to one another. >> A grammar can generate an infinite number of sentences. >> Generalization cannot cover that infinite set. || Chomsky offered another account. >> We recognize a new sentence because our **internalized grammar** generates it. >> It does not have to match a familiar sentence. >> Spreading a trained response and recognizing an abstract grammatical relation are different processes.
11|**Conditioned responses generalize along concrete dimensions like color, and it is unclear how that reaches abstract concepts.** A **pigeon** trained to peck for a **green light** also pecks at similar colors. >> Pecking is strongest near green. >> Pecking falls as the color becomes less similar. >> This pattern is a **generalization gradient** (response strength falling with distance from the trained stimulus). || Another pigeon does a **magnitude discrimination** task. >> It responds according to amounts. >> Number is another concrete dimension. || Generalization in operant conditioning depends on concrete similarity. >> Examples are **color** and **number**. >> Grammar has no concrete dimension like these. >> So it is not clear how conditioning extends to **abstract concepts**.
12|**Stimulus control explains an utterance only if the controlling stimulus can be named apart from the utterance itself.** **Stimulus control** is Skinner's idea that an aspect of the environment controls a response. >> Skinner's typical example is a response to a piece of **music**. >> The music is the stimulus. >> The utterance is the response. || Chomsky said other examples only add to the **general mystification**. >> Skinner treated a **proper noun** as a response to a stimulus. || The trouble is finding the stimulus. >> A piece of music has many properties. >> A speaker can respond to any of them. >> If the utterance picks out the stimulus, the utterance ends up deciding its own cause.
13|**Skinner posed the wrong question, because what people say depends on far more than the stimuli around them.** Chomsky's point is that we do not know the current stimuli until the speaker responds. >> So stimulus control cannot predict speech. >> Naming the stimulus after the response makes the account **circular**. || What a person says depends on many factors. >> **Social interactions** matter. >> **Cognitive processes** matter. >> **Previous experiences** matter. >> Much of this has nothing to do with language. || The **tractable problem** (one that can actually be solved) is different. >> Study the **structure** of what people say. >> Derive the set of **rules** that produces that structure.
14|**A sentence can be grammatical even when its meaning makes little literal sense, and that judgment does not come from having heard it.** Chomsky (1955) compared two sentences. Neither sentence, nor any of their parts, had occurred in an English speaker's experience. >> Sentence (a) is "colorless green ideas sleep furiously". >> Sentence (b) is "furiously sleep ideas green colorless". >> Sentence (a) is **grammatical**. >> Sentence (b) is **not grammatical**. || The two sentences use the same words. >> In (a), adjectives come before the noun, followed by a verb and adverb. >> In (b), the order breaks that structure. >> So the difference lies in **structure**, not in familiarity. || The reverse case also holds. >> The warning "Car! Getting towed! Yours!" is **ungrammatical**. >> Yet the stimulus that likely controlled it is perfectly clear.
15|**Chomsky describes language at Marr's computational level of analysis.** **David Marr** was a visual neuroscientist. >> He said cognitive systems have three **levels of analysis**. || The three levels answer different questions. >> The **computational level** states what problem the system solves. >> The **algorithmic level** states which representations and procedures solve it. >> The **implementational level** states how the procedures are physically carried out. || A claim can be useful at one level without answering the others. >> Chomsky's abstract grammar is a computational description. >> It states what language processing must achieve.
16|**A cash register performs the same function no matter what machine carries it out.** This example borrows heavily from **Sharon Goldwater**. || Each level gives a different description. >> At the **computational level**, it takes item prices and determines the amount due. Given the payment, it computes the change. >> At the **algorithmic level**, it uses addition for the price and subtraction for the change. >> At the **implementational level**, the machinery does not matter for these functions. || Many devices count as cash registers. >> A mechanical register works. >> A credit card reader works. >> A computer program works. >> For the same prices and payment, each gives the same change.
17|**Treating language as a Type I problem lets researchers study grammar while ignoring how it is implemented.** **Type I problems** (Marr's term) break down cleanly into his levels. >> The computational level for language is **Universal Grammar**. >> The algorithmic level covers specific processes and how they apply in different languages. >> The implementational level can be set aside. || Setting aside implementation ignores several factors. >> **Errors** are ignored. >> **Memory limitations** are ignored. >> **Associative learning** is ignored. || The chronology matters. >> Chomsky's critique came about a decade before Marr's theoretical work. >> Both were influential in AI and in **GOFAI** theory.
18|**A Type 2 problem is solved by many interacting processes and cannot be split into a clean computational theory.** Marr linked his computational level to Chomsky. >> Chomsky's **competence theory** for English syntax is what Marr meant by a computational theory. >> Both care little about the details of algorithms. >> One must know what algorithms should do before devising them. >> A problem that divides this way has a **Type 1 theory**. || Many problems of biological information processing have a Type 1 theory. >> There is no reason they all should. || Some problems have a **Type 2 theory**. >> Many processes act at the same time. >> Their interaction is its own **simplest description**.
19|**Marr suggested that the grammar of natural language may have a Type 2 theory rather than a Type 1 theory.** Marr described what language does. >> A speaker holds a **data structure** that is not one-dimensional. >> Language turns it into a **sequential utterance** (a one-dimensional form). >> The listener rebuilds a rough copy in their head. || This changes how syntax might be explained. >> There may be no Type 1 theory of English syntax of the kind **transformational grammar** tries to define. >> Its constraints may be **wired-in conventions** for doing this tedious but vital operation. >> They may not be deep principles about intelligence. || An abstract theory of syntax may be an **illusion**. >> It may only approximate the real processes. >> **Julesz's second order statistics theory** approximates texture vision in the same way.
20|**How language is implemented in the brain might be unavoidable in a theory of language.** If language comes from interacting processes, those processes belong in the theory. >> Learning then matters to the theory. >> Memory then matters too. >> Perception and action matter as well. || A whole alternative approach to language research rests on this hypothesis. >> The first approach starts with an abstract grammar for the brain to carry out. >> The alternative starts with the learner and the processing system. >> It asks what language-like organization can develop from them.
21|**Emergentist approaches make their strongest claims at the implementational level.** The approach is also called **statistical learning** or **parallel distributed processing**. || Its commitments are specific. >> It uses **distributed representations** (non-hierarchical activity spread over many units). These are like activity patterns over groups of neurons. >> It uses **gradual learning** (slowly adjusting connection strengths). This is like adjusting the strength of synaptic connections. >> It focuses on **microstructure** (the many small interactions that produce a larger regularity). >> It draws a more **graded division** between levels of analysis. || The learning mechanism is expected to shape the apparent rules in behavior.
22|**Elman (1990) built simple recurrent networks that learn language patterns only by predicting the next item.** **Simple recurrent networks** capture different aspects of language performance in this framework. >> All the model does is predict the next item. >> It gradually learns to do this with experience. || Information flows through four groups of units. >> The **input** feeds the **hidden units**. >> The hidden units feed the **output** prediction. >> A copy of hidden activity goes to **context units**. >> The context units feed back into the hidden units with the next input. || Some connections learn and one does not. >> Trainable connections adjust to improve prediction. >> The copying connection is fixed. >> So the prediction depends on the current item and what came before.
23|**A letter-prediction network finds word boundaries without ever being given spaces.** The model receives "Manyyearsagoaboyandgirllivedbytheseatheyplayedhappily". >> That string becomes "many years ago a boy and girl lived by the sea they played happily". || The model only predicts the next letter. >> **Prediction error** (how far the guess is from the real next letter) is tracked letter by letter. || The error follows the words. >> At the start of a word, error is high. >> Within a word, error goes down. >> At the next word, error rises again. >> So the error peaks mark the **word boundaries**.
24|**A network trained only to predict words in simple sentences ends up grouping words by grammar and meaning.** The categories are a **side effect** of learning to predict sequences. >> No one labels the words. >> Elman examined the **hidden-unit activation patterns** for each word. >> Words used in similar contexts got similar patterns. || The patterns cluster by **grammatical class**. >> **Nouns** form one group. >> **Verbs** form another. || The patterns also carry **semantic information**. >> Nouns split into animates and inanimates. >> Animates split into humans and animals. >> Inanimates include food and breakable things. >> Verbs split by how they take a **direct object**.
25|**A statistical model trained on text can tell "colorless green ideas sleep furiously" from its reversed form.** Many engineering solutions in **Natural Language Processing** work this way. >> They analyze **massive corpora** (collections of texts and transcriptions of spoken language). >> They find statistical patterns in them. || **Peter Norvig** discussed Chomsky's critique. >> He agreed neither sentence appeared in the published literature before 1955. >> But every two-word part had occurred. >> "Colorless green" appeared in 1942. >> "Green ideas" appeared in 1922. >> "Ideas sleep in books" appeared in 1912. || **Pereira (2001)** tested a **finite-state model** with word categories. >> It was trained by **expectation maximization** on newspaper text. >> It computed that sentence (a) is **200,000 times more probable** than sentence (b).

27|**Psycholinguistics has two traditions that disagree about what linguistic knowledge is.** The first is the **GOFAI, modular or Chomskyan** tradition. >> Linguistic knowledge is essentially abstract and symbolic. >> Linguistic representations differ in kind from other knowledge. >> They must interface with messy perceptual, motor, memory and other cognitive capacities. >> The psycholinguist studies this **interface**. || The second is the **emergentist or functionalist** tradition. >> Symbol and rule-like behavior are **emergent properties** of statistical learning. >> Perceptual, motor and cognitive processes actually shape linguistic representations. >> The psycholinguist studies **language use** and relates it to general psychological phenomena.

30|**Messiness like the exceptions to the "i before e" rule appears at every level of every language.** This messiness motivates the **emergentist or statistical learning** approach. >> **Phonetic categories** are defined probabilistically over multiple dimensions. >> **Words** are ambiguous in every way imaginable. >> **Morphological paradigms** have exceptions. "Add -ed" for the English past tense fails for RUN-RAN and SLEEP-SLEPT. >> **Syntactic rules** vary across dialects and individuals and change over time. || A strong pattern and its exceptions coexist. >> Adding -ed works for many verbs. >> Irregular forms stay part of what speakers know.
31|**Moravec's paradox says tasks that are easy for computers are hard for humans, and the reverse.** This is a problem for the **GOFAI** approach. >> For computers, proving theorems and solving geometry problems is comparatively easy. >> For computers, recognizing a face is extremely difficult. >> For computers, crossing a room without bumping into anything is extremely difficult. >> For humans, the opposite is true. || The paradox raises a question about language. >> Characterizing language in formal terms, as for a computer program, may be a **false trail**.
32|**Fixed categories and rules are often fuzzy in practice, and this has been known for a long time.** **Wittgenstein (1953)** made the point in **Philosophical Investigations**. >> His example is the category of **games**. >> Board games, card games, ball games and Olympic games are all games. >> No single feature belongs to every one. || The members are linked by **family resemblance** (overlapping similarities with no one shared feature). >> One pair shares one feature. >> Another pair shares a different feature. >> A category does not need one strict list of necessary properties.
33|**Many phenomena in language are probabilistic, just as games share overlapping features rather than one definition.** **Wittgenstein (1953)** compared many games. >> Chess and noughts and crosses are not amusing in the same way. >> Patience has no competition between players. >> Ball games have winning and losing, but a child throwing a ball at a wall does not. >> Skill in chess differs from skill in tennis. >> Ring-a-ring-a-roses keeps amusement but loses many other features. || Similarities crop up and disappear from game to game. >> They form a complicated network, **overlapping and criss-crossing**. >> Some are overall similarities. >> Some are similarities of detail.
34|**Psychologists produced results that made a lot of cognition seem less suited to formal, rational interpretation.** Their work stressed **family resemblances**, **similarity-based generalization** and **hunches**. >> **Roger Shepard** worked on **perception**. >> **Eleanor Rosch** worked on **categorization**. >> **Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky** worked on **decision-making**. >> Many others did similar work. || Their results pointed away from a purely **formal, rational** account of the mind. >> This supports asking whether language also reflects similarity-based generalization.
