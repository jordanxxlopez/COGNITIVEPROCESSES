2|**Below the functional level, three levels turn chosen words into spoken movements.** >> The **positional level** places the chosen words into a grammatical structure. >> The **phonological encoding level** turns them into a string of **phonemes** (speech sounds that tell words apart). >> The **speech gestural level** turns that string into movements made by the motor system. || Speech errors support **abstract units**. >> **Phonemes**, **lemmas** and **noun phrases** cannot be heard directly. >> Errors move them or leave them in place in regular ways, so the units earn a place in the theory. || Collected errors can be sorted by level. >> Purely **observational research** has weaknesses. >> So **experimental methods** are needed too.
3|**The functional level knows the verbs and their roles, and the positional level must express them in syntax.** The sentence is the error "Where'd I bring it when I put it in?" It was earlier evidence that **lemmas** (words chosen for meaning and grammar, before their sounds) are useful. >> The functional level knows there are two verbs, PUT and BRING. >> It knows I/ME is the **Agent** (the doer) of both verbs. >> It knows IT is the **Undergoer** (the thing acted on) of both verbs. || Other facts are still open. >> The **temporal relationship** between the verbs is not yet set. The word when expresses it. >> The question form and the verb tenses are not yet set. >> The **positional level** expresses all of these in syntax.
4|**The positional level builds a frame with slots, and each slot sets the form of the verb that fills it.** Without the question steps, the frame is roughly "Where'd I [verb, root form] it when I [verb, past] it in?" >> The first clause needs the **base form**. It took real syntax, with **Wh- movement** and an **auxiliary verb**. >> The when clause needs the **past form**. >> The frame also holds the other **particles** the grammar requires. || The error arises while lemmas from the functional level fill the slots. >> BRING went into the root form slot, so it came out as bring. >> PUT went into the past slot, so it came out as put. >> Each verb took the form of the slot it landed in.
5|**In "a language needer learns," two lemmas switched like bring and put, but the noun shows something new.** The intended phrase is "a language learner needs." >> Similar, because LEARN and NEED got **switched** while being fed into the positional level. >> Similar, because the frame stayed mostly the same, a noun phrase then a verb. || Different, because the noun is a **verb plus an affix**. >> Learner is learn plus -er. >> Needer is need plus -er. >> Adding -er to a verb is **derivational morphology** (making a new word from an existing one).
6|**The roots NEED and LEARN swapped slots, but the endings stayed in their slots.** The positional level itself stayed mostly the same. >> The noun slot is a **verb plus -er**. This **derivational affix** (an ending that makes a new word) turns a verb into a doer noun. >> The verb slot asks for the **third person singular** form with -s. This is **inflectional morphology** (an ending set by grammar). || The tree shows where each ending lives. >> The noun phrase (NP) holds the noun, built as a verb plus -er. >> The verb phrase (VP) holds a third person singular verb. || The result follows from the slots. >> Need landed in the noun slot, so it became needer. >> Learn landed in the verb slot, so it became learns. >> If whole words had swapped, the endings would have moved too, so the affixes are **stranded**.
7|**"He did that easy enoughly" puts the ending -ly on the wrong word.** The intended phrase is "He did that easily enough." >> In easily, -ly attaches to easy. >> In the error, -ly attaches to enough. >> Easy is left bare, and the odd form enoughly appears. || Two questions follow. >> How is this error similar to "a language needer learns"? >> How is it different? || Answering both means comparing the intended and produced forms word by word.
8|**"Easy enoughly" shares a stranded affix with "needer," but it also changed how the positional frame was set up.** >> Similar, because both have a **stranded affix** (an ending that stays in its slot while the root changes). >> Here the affix is **-ly**, which makes an **adjective** into an **adverb**. || Different, because the frame itself changed. >> In "needer," the positional frame stayed the same. >> In "enoughly," something went wrong **while setting up the positional level**. || Two adjective phrase (AP) trees compare the frames. >> The intended frame has the adverb slot first, built as adjective plus -ly, then the adjective slot. >> The starred error frame has the adjective slot first, then the adverb slot. >> Easy filled the first slot and enough filled the second, so enough got the -ly.
9|**Errors like needer and enoughly show that affixes float around at the positional level, so derivational morphology is real.** >> Another view says derived words are just separate stored words linked to their roots by rule-like relations. >> The lexicon would hold sets like needy, need and needless, or unlearn, learn and learner. >> Then affixes would need no separate representation, and no quirky word-building rules would be needed. || But errors make **non-words** like "needer" and "enoughly" on the fly. They suggest three things. >> **Lemmas and affixes** are represented **separately**. >> **Affixes have a slot** in the positional level. >> **Derived words** can be built by a **grammatical process**.
10|**An affix added at the positional level still leaves phonological work, because the sounds of the new word must be worked out.** >> Easy /’i.zi/ becomes easily /’i.zIl.i/. >> Enough /i.’nəf/ becomes enoughly /i.’nəf.li/. || Some derivations change the base too. >> The word certificate is /sər.ˈtI.fɨ.kɨt/. >> The word certification is /ˌsər.tɨ.fɨ.ˈkeI.ʃən/. >> The word recertification is /ˈri.sər.tɨ.fɨ.ˌkeI.ʃən/. >> **Vowels change** and **stress moves** across these forms. || The positional level only names a process like adding -er or making an adjective into an adverb. >> A later level still turns that plan into sounds.
11|**Phonological encoding turns lemmas, affixes and particles into a string of phonemes, and its errors reorder phonemes neatly.** >> For "easy enoughly," this level worked fine. >> The speaker did not say "easy /inəvɪli/" or garble the phoneme sequence. || Errors at this level reorder phonemes. >> Fish grotto came out as gish frotto. >> Chomsky and Halle came out as Chomsky and Challe. >> With this ring I do wed came out as with this wing I do red. || Menn calls these errors **"neat."** >> They follow English **phonotactic patterns** (rules for allowed sound sequences). >> As far as anyone knows, they are **clearly produced**.
12|**The speech gestural level carries out the planned phonemes as movements, and its errors make speech unclear.** >> The **motor system** carries out a series of **gestures**. >> Unlike phonological encoding errors, these errors are **not necessarily "neat."** >> They lead to **unclear or imprecise speech**. || **Many errors are ambiguous.** >> Ad hoc came out as odd hack, with /æd hɑk/ becoming /ɑd hæk/. >> /æ/ is a low front vowel, as in bat, and /ɑ/ is a low back vowel, as in hot. || Two levels could explain it. >> It could be **metathesis** (two sounds switching places) at the phonological encoding level. >> It could be a **miscuing of tongue position** at the gestural level.

14|**Some collected errors change word forms, and others change sounds.** >> Buyed in, for bought in, puts the regular past ending on an irregular verb. This is **morphological regularization**. >> Arising came out as "arriving," with one consonant changed in a similar word. || Two errors change sounds. >> In "It's pretty close to here," close was said [kloʊz]. The final consonant became voiced. >> Thoroughly came out as "throfly," with several sounds changed. || Each error is placed by comparing intended and produced sounds. >> The unit that changed points to the level.
15|**Some collected errors move whole words, and others move single sounds.** >> In "Wash my teeth and brush my face," the **verbs** switched and the objects stayed. || Some errors move sounds. >> Following vowel came out as "vollowing fowel," with the first consonants **switched**. >> Seat heaters came out as "heat seaters," with the plural ending staying on seaters. >> Speech-language pathology came out as "speech plath... speech plang," with attempts and repairs. || Some bring in a familiar sequence. >> Lana del Rey came out as "Marina del Rey." >> "For all intensive purposes" puts different words into a familiar phrase. >> "It's in the stimulus" is another collected error.

17|**In a talk about hot yoga, "a hundred-dollar room" is an error in choosing a word for the meaning.** The speaker said "you enter a hundred-dollar room and you do yoga." >> In a talk about cost, the phrase would be normal. >> In a talk about **hot yoga**, the hundred could describe the room's heat, not its price. >> Dollar is a real word that does not fit the intended meaning. || The rest is fine. >> The clause is **grammatical**. >> The words are easy to pronounce. >> So the error is in **word choice**, not in sounds or movements.
18|**"Law and Order SUV" switches the last two letters of SVU into a familiar abbreviation.** >> **SVU** belongs to the show's title. >> **SUV** is a familiar vehicle abbreviation. >> The same three letters appear in a new order. || The produced form is familiar. >> So a competing familiar form may have been **selected**. >> The switch should not be assumed to be a simple motor slip. >> The result is still easy to say.
19|**"He cooked his kitchen" for chicken mixes sound similarity with meaning context.** >> **In sound**, kitchen and chicken are very similar, with first and middle consonants in opposite positions. >> **In meaning**, both relate to cooking. || The grammar stayed intact. >> Both words are nouns that fit the slot after his. >> So the sentence keeps its **grammatical frame**. >> The analysis has to use both the word forms and the situation.
20|**What an observer listens for and expects can change which speech errors get heard and reported.** >> Observers listen for the kinds of errors they expect. >> Learning about speech production changes those expectations. >> So expectations can affect what is heard and what is reported. || Placing an error in the model needs **context**. >> The analyst must reconstruct the speaker's intended message. >> The analyst must know the situation of the error. >> A form can be fine alone but wrong for what the speaker meant.
21|**Speech perception biases which errors get noticed, so observers may miss whole classes of errors.** This is **theory-dependent observation**, as philosophers of science call it. >> Listeners are poor at hearing sounds **"halfway" between two phonemes**, especially **consonants**. >> Listeners are **biased against** hearing **phonotactically illegal strings** (sound sequences the language does not allow). >> Listeners are **biased toward** hearing ambiguous sounds **as words**, so something between "giss" and "kiss" sounds more like "kiss." || **Experimental methods** and careful measurement help. >> They fix some of these problems. >> They do not fix all of them.
