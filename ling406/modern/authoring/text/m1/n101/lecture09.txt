2|**Frequent object names are produced faster, but frequent words are also shorter, so frequency is confounded with length.** **Oldfield and Wingfield (1965)** asked people to name pictures of objects. >> They found a **log-linear relationship** (a straight line once frequency is on a logarithmic scale) between frequency and response time. >> More frequent names had shorter response times. >> The example line drawings come from a later set by Snodgrass and Vanderwart (1980). || Frequency is related to **word length**, even in this tiny sample. >> A **confound** (a second variable that changes along with the tested one) gives another explanation for the result. >> Either frequency or length could cause the faster naming. >> A web-based tool lets researchers check this relationship with numbers.
3|**A lexical database lets researchers measure the properties of candidate words instead of guessing.** The **English Lexicon Project** is one such database. >> It gives **lexical characteristics** (properties of words such as frequency and length). >> It gives **behavioral data** from naming and lexical-decision tasks. || Its homepage has two kinds of links. >> One kind generates word and nonword lists. >> The other kind gives trial-level lexical-decision and naming data. >> The first serves choosing stimuli, and the second serves studying responses. || Measurements reveal hidden differences. >> A list that seems "frequent" may also differ in length. >> The database exposes that difference before the materials are chosen.
4|**The query tools can either build a new word list or look up the properties of an existing one.** Both ways make stimulus properties explicit. >> **Generate Lists of Items with Specific Lexical Characteristics** builds a new list. >> **Submit lists of Items to retrieve Lexical Characteristics** looks up an existing list. || Each way fits a different starting point. >> Generating a list helps when a study needs a certain range of frequency or length. >> Submitting a list helps when the words were chosen for another reason, such as a semantic category. >> Either way can reveal an unintended difference between conditions. || Stimulus design often moves back and forth between searching and checking.
5|**A query separates the properties to report from the limits on which words enter the list.** The reported properties are **fields**, and the limits are **constraints**. >> Checkboxes request fields such as **length** and frequency measures. >> Minimum and maximum boxes constrain the chosen frequency field. || Retrieving both length and frequency reveals whether they change together. >> The frequency constraints pick candidate words. >> The returned lengths tell whether more selection is needed. || The researcher turns a design goal into measurable criteria. >> The database does not choose the comparison by itself. >> The chosen fields decide which confounds can be checked.
6|**English word frequency drops off very steeply, and length goes up as frequency goes down.** The 200 most frequent words of an old corpus were put into ten "bins" of twenty words each. >> Mean frequency drops very dramatically from the top bin to the next. >> This steep drop is **Zipf's law** (a small number of words account for a large share of usage). || Length is **inversely related** to frequency (as one goes up, the other goes down). >> The most frequent bins have the shortest words on average. >> Less frequent bins have longer words. || This is why frequency and length are hard to separate. >> Picking very frequent words also picks many short words.
7|**High-frequency words are much shorter than low-frequency words chosen at random.** The comparison used the **467 most frequent words (HF)** and **467 random low-frequency words (LF)**. >> A **boxplot** (a summary of a whole distribution) shows each group's lengths. >> The **median** (the middle value) of LF is higher than that of HF. || **Fewer than 25%** of HF words are as long as the median LF word. >> The difference covers most of each sample. >> It does not come from one odd word. || Random sampling does not fix the confound. >> Random LF words keep the natural link between frequency and length.
8|**Low-frequency words should be chosen deliberately, not at random, so that length can be matched.** HF words are generally shorter than LF words. >> So random LF words will always be longer. || The fix is to pick from where the two distributions overlap. >> Select long-ish HF words. >> Select short-ish LF words. >> The groups then **match on length** while still differing in frequency. || Matching has limits. >> It changes only the experimental sample. >> It does not claim frequency and length are unrelated in the language. >> The final groups must be checked on both frequency and length.
9|**Oldfield and Wingfield matched their words for length and still found a robust frequency effect.** They concluded that **frequency effects are not merely length effects in disguise**. >> If length were the whole cause, matching it would remove the naming difference. >> The difference stayed. >> So frequency affects naming beyond length. || Much psycholinguistics follows this logic. >> Researchers try to show an effect is not just an effect of a **confounding variable** (one that changes along with the variable of interest). >> Controlling the confound turns a correlation into a real test.
10|**Matching on many dimensions is good design, but it can leave odd, artificial and small sets of words.** **Cutler (1981)** called this "Making up materials is a confounded nuisance." >> **Cortese and Simpson (2000)** did a very good matching job on regular and irregular words. >> They matched frequency, familiarity, imageability, length and sound and spelling measures. || Fine-grained matching has costs. >> The chosen items may be somehow "odd." >> Their statistics may be inappropriate. >> The frequency-length confound is a fact about English and most languages. >> Avoiding it creates a somewhat **artificial stimulus environment**. >> Few items may be left. This study had ten words per cell, which is small for word naming.
11|**Most interesting psycholinguistic effects happen below conscious awareness, so the tasks can be simple.** A person naming line drawings does not usually think about word frequency or length. >> The effects still show up in naming times. || Psycholinguists need less craftiness than some researchers. >> Researchers studying **racial stereotyping** need craftier designs. >> Researchers studying **altruism** also need craftier designs. || **Norming studies** (studies that collect ratings of words) just ask people to rate words on some dimension. >> Researchers hope for the best. >> Some studies still add **fillers** (extra items mixed in with the test items).
12|**Good dependent measures capture behavior that participants do not consciously control.** A **dependent variable** is the outcome a researcher measures. >> **Eye-tracking** records where and when people look. >> **EEG** and **fMRI** measure brain activity. >> **Reaction time** and **accuracy** are usually not under direct control either. >> A **tachistoscope** (19th century) presented stimuli briefly. || Brain activity is useful mainly because participants cannot usually control it. >> It matters less for what it says about the brain itself. >> Different brain responses to two kinds of stimuli, with no overt task, suggest the stimuli differ to the participant.

14|**Belke, Meyer and Damian (2005) found that naming is slower when the objects in a set are semantically related.** They used **cyclic semantic blocking** (naming the same sets of objects again and again). >> **Homogeneous** sets had related names. >> **Heterogeneous** sets had unrelated names. || Three experiments located the cost. >> **Experiment 1** found blocking after the objects had been named once. >> **Experiment 2** showed objects together. Blocking appeared in naming latency and gaze duration, but not significantly in the gaze-to-speech lag. >> **Experiment 3** found blocking for new names from the same categories. || The study has nice design features. >> It varies presentation, adds eye tracking and adds new items.
15|**Roelofs' WEAVER++ model describes producing a single word, so it only roughly matches a general sentence-production model.** WEAVER++ has **conceptual**, **syntactic** and **word-form** levels. >> The general model has message, functional and positional levels, then phonological encoding and articulation. || The syntactic levels differ. >> WEAVER++ focuses on activation for single words. >> Its syntactic information is not "positional." >> It holds single-word features such as number and gender. || The conceptual and **lemma** levels matter most for blocking. >> A lemma is the word candidate with its syntactic features. >> Word-form encoding comes after lemma selection.
16|**Spreading activation can make naming slower, not just faster.** In a **spreading-activation model**, related concepts share connections. >> Activating **duck** also activates features such as animal, bird and swim. >> Those features link to **fish**, **snake** and **mouse**. >> Each concept sends activation to its lemma. || A naive view predicts faster naming. >> A block of similar pictures would make every name more active. || The model predicts slower naming in some cases. >> Naming requires choosing one candidate. >> A related set makes the target more available. >> It also makes the competitors more available.
17|**Activating related words helps them become available but slows down selecting the right lemma.** Spreading activation from **duck** to **fish** means activating one helps activate the other. >> This causes problems when one lemma must be selected from many active ones. || **WEAVER++** sets selection time by a **ratio**. >> The top is the activity of the most active lemma. >> The bottom is the overall activation of all lemmas. >> A bigger total makes the ratio smaller and selection slower. || Feedback from the **category level** adds **collateral activation** (extra activation for other items in the same category). >> This raises the total activation. >> So lemma selection slows down.
18|**The same items were named in related and unrelated blocks across eight cycles.** In each **cycle**, all of the stimuli were presented. >> **Homogeneous (HOM)** blocks had related items, such as "duck", "mouse", "fish", "snake". >> **Heterogeneous (HET)** blocks had unrelated items, such as "duck", "saw", "chair", "bus". || The same stimulus switched blocks. >> "Duck" appeared in HOM and HET blocks in alternating cycles. >> So an item is HOM or HET only because of the other items in its set. || The design allows two comparisons. >> Across cycles, response times change with repetition. >> Between blocks, response times differ with semantic context.
19|**Two effects happen at once, a big identity priming effect and a semantic blocking effect.** They come from different comparisons. >> **Identity priming** (faster naming of an item named before) appears across cycles. >> Both HOM and HET times drop sharply after the first cycle. || **Semantic blocking** (slower naming in related blocks) appears between conditions. >> In later cycles, HOM is slower than HET. || The effects do not contradict each other. >> A condition can get faster over time. >> It can still stay slower than the other condition.
20|**Belke and colleagues ruled out visual similarity as the cause of blocking in two ways.** Related objects might look alike, so blocking could arise in **visual object recognition**. >> First, the stimuli were **normed** so that same-category objects were not more visually similar on average than different-category objects. >> Second, a **control experiment** blocked visual similarity. || The control found a different result. >> Visual-similarity blocking appeared in the first block of trials. >> Semantic blocking appeared only after the first cycle. || So the effects in Experiment 1 are not likely due to visual similarity.
21|**Belke and colleagues tested whether blocking depends on seeing objects one at a time.** **Hermens and colleagues** showed several objects simultaneously and found no effect. >> Maybe something is odd about seeing objects in serial. || The new task separated seeing from speaking. >> Participants viewed **displays of four items simultaneously**. >> They produced the names in serial. >> One display held duck, snake, fish and mouse. || Blocking still appeared. >> It showed up in naming latency and gaze duration. >> So the effect does not depend on serial viewing.
22|**Eye tracking splits the naming of each object into separate time intervals.** Belke and colleagues measured **eye movements** during naming. >> The participant looks at one object. >> The eyes move on to the next object. >> The first name is spoken after the eyes have moved. || The intervals have labels. >> **GD** is **gaze duration** (time spent looking at an object). >> **Art** is the onset of articulating the name. >> The **lag** is the time from the end of looking to the start of the name. || These intervals can be compared across HOM and HET displays.
23|**Gaze duration tracks lemma selection, and lag tracks the later steps that need no visual support.** Both are **dependent variables** in the study. >> **Gaze duration** is the time spent looking at an object. >> There is evidence that **lemma selection** is coordinated with eye movements. || The **lag** is the time after the eyes leave an object and before its name begins. >> **Phonological encoding** (building the sound form) happens then. >> **Gesture planning** (planning the speech movements) happens then. >> These need no visual support once the eyes have moved on. || The two measures split stages that overall naming time combines.
24|**Eye-mouth coordination links where a speaker looks to the words the speaker says.** **Griffin, Bock and colleagues** made demonstrations of it. >> One example is accurately labeling a patient in "The horse is kicking a cow." >> The **agent** (the one acting) is the horse. >> The **patient** (the one affected) is the cow. || The eye record is lined up with the spoken words. >> It reveals when each part of the event matters for building the sentence. >> It extends object naming to whole sentences.
25|**Of the three eye-tracking measures, only gaze duration shows a blocking effect.** The three measures had different results. >> **Overall speech duration** decreases over the experiment but shows no blocking effect. >> **Gaze duration** shows blocking, but only after the first cycle. >> **Lag** shows no effect. || The lag result needs care. >> Numerically, its blocking effect seems to go in the opposite direction. >> That difference is not significant. || Practice and semantic context are separate. >> A measure can shrink across cycles without differing between HOM and HET.
26|**The gaze-duration result places semantic blocking at lemma selection.** Each measure maps onto a production stage. >> **Speech duration** maps onto the **gesture level** and shows no blocking. >> **Gaze duration** maps onto **lemma selection** and shows blocking after the first cycle. >> **Lag** maps onto **phonological encoding** and shows no significant blocking. || Longer looking in HOM displays fits more competition among related lemmas. >> No effect on lag gives no evidence of a cost after the eyes move.
27|**Blocking generalizes to new items from the same category.** **Experiment 3** introduced **new items** in **Cycle 5**. >> The repetition priming effect went away in Cycle 5. >> Overall performance slowed down dramatically. >> The blocking effect was still there. || The new items can be compared to the items in Cycle 1. >> In Cycle 1, nothing had been named yet, and there was no blocking. >> In Cycle 5, the new items came from categories already named, and blocking appeared. || So blocking comes from the semantic category, not from repeating particular names.
28|**Belke and colleagues isolate the blocking effect by ruling out three other sources.** Each source is a different point between seeing an object and saying its name. >> It is not **visual recognition**. >> It is not **phonological encoding**, since the words are not similar in sound. >> It is not **item-specific**, since it generalizes to new items. || Together these controls narrow the explanation. >> What remains is **lemma selection** among semantically activated competitors.
29|**Each rival explanation for blocking has its own test.** The tests point to lemma selection. >> **Visual recognition** is ruled out by normed and matched stimuli and a control experiment. >> **Phonological encoding** is ruled out, with a question mark, because the words are not phonologically similar. Gaze duration is affected, but eye-speech lag is not. >> **Repeating specific lists** is ruled out because the effect generalizes to new items. || The question mark reflects that timing measures support an interpretation. >> They do not directly observe a hidden stage.
30|**A good experiment idea starts by asking whether an effect could just be something else.** The question to ask is "Couldn’t it just be …?" For **stimulus manipulations**, many word features are correlated with frequency. >> **Length** is one. >> **Age of acquisition** is another. >> **Concreteness** or **imageability** is another. >> **Contextual diversity** is another. >> So frequency effects might be a catch-all for these features. || For **experimental manipulations**, even a simple blocking study involves many processes. >> **Object recognition** is one. >> **Response selection** is another. || Each alternative must predict a different result. >> Matching, direct manipulation, new items and new measures test those predictions.
