4|**The consonant chart sorts every consonant by place, manner and voicing.** >> **Place of articulation** (where the vocal tract closes) runs along the horizontal axis, from front to back. >> **Manner of articulation** (the degree of closure) runs along the vertical axis, from closed to open. >> **Voicing** (when the vocal folds start vibrating) separates the two sounds in each pair. || Voicing pairs differ in timing. >> In the voiced member, voicing starts before or during the closure. >> In the voiceless member, voicing starts after the closure. || The chart reads from front to back. >> Bilabial sounds like p and b sit at the left. >> Velar and other back sounds sit farther right.
5|**/p/ and /b/ share place and manner, and differ only in voicing.** Features describe sounds in a precise and well-defined way. >> /p/ is plus **plosive**, plus **bilabial**, minus voiced. >> /b/ is plus plosive, plus bilabial, plus voiced. || The two sounds share two features. >> They have the same **manner** (both are plosives, with a full closure that is released). >> They have the same **place** (both are bilabial, made at the lips). || They differ in one feature. >> That feature is **voicing** (the timing of phonation relative to the release of the closure). >> **Phonation** is vibration of the vocal folds. || The same contrast repeats elsewhere. >> /t/ and /d/ differ in the same way. >> So features allow a general class, **the voiced stops** /b/, /d/ and /g/. >> This general class is helpful for phonological descriptions.
6|**Minimal pairs prove that two sounds belong to different phonemes.** >> A **minimal pair** (two words that differ in one sound and in meaning) shows **contrast**. >> Two phones are **contrastive** when swapping them changes meaning. >> Contrastive phones are examples of different **phonemes**. || Testing for two low back vowels follows the same steps. >> Find two words that differ only in those two vowels. >> **Caught** and **cot** work for a speaker who keeps the vowels apart. >> The consonants stay the same, so the vowel carries the meaning difference. >> For a speaker who merges the vowels, caught and cot sound alike and prove nothing. || The evidence is meaning. >> A change in meaning counts, not just a change in sound.
7|**Minimal pairs also reveal that many English contrasts depend on the single feature of voicing.** >> /b/ and /p/ differ in **voicing**. >> /t/ and /d/ differ in voicing. >> /k/ and /g/ differ in voicing. || Each pair holds place and manner constant. >> Inside a pair, only voicing changes. >> Across the pairs, the place changes from bilabial to alveolar to velar. || The evidence is language specific. >> It comes from comparing minimal pairs in each language. >> A contrast that holds in English may not hold in another language.
8|**Speech is a continuous signal, so phonemes are not letters lined up in the sound.** Literate people easily imagine speech as an orderly stream of letter-like units. It is nothing like that. >> A **spectrogram** (a display of sound energy over time) makes this clear. >> Time runs left to right. >> Frequency rises from bottom to top. >> Darker regions mean stronger energy. || The sentence "A sore back is a real pain" has no clean breaks. >> Neighboring sounds blend through transitions. >> One phoneme has no single fixed shape across contexts. || Transcription and sound differ. >> A transcription is a row of separate symbols. >> The signal itself is continuous and overlapping.
9|**Hockett (1955) compared speech to raw Easter eggs smashed in a wringer, with the hearer as an inspector.** >> Raw eggs of various sizes and colors ride a moving belt. >> The belt carries them through a **wringer**, which smashes them and rubs them together. || Each part maps onto speech. >> Eggs before the wringer are the impulses from the **phoneme source**. >> The mess after the wringer is the output of the **speech transmitter**. >> The **inspector** is the **hearer**. || The inspector's job is only to identify. >> He judges from broken and unbroken yolks, spread-out whites and bits of shell. >> He does not rebuild the eggs, which would be physically impossible. >> So a listener **identifies** phonemes from a smeared signal without separating the pieces.

11|**Derivational morphology builds new words out of existing parts called morphemes.** >> **Morphemes** are the parts (units) that words are built from. || The word morphology is built this way. >> **morph-** is from Greek for "form". >> **-ology** is from Greek for something like "the study of". >> Together they mean "the study of the forms of words". || The same process gives other words. >> It gives **morpheme**. >> It gives **phoneme**, with **phone** as a Greek morpheme probably meaning "speech". || The pattern can misfire. >> Applying it to units in text leads to **graphology**, an awkward result.
12|**Derivational affixes are productive, but they apply unevenly from word to word.** Affixes differ in how widely and how regularly they apply. >> Stem plus **-hood** means a state of being linked to the stem noun. || Some -hood words fit the pattern. >> **Brotherhood** fits. >> **Sisterhood** fits. >> **Knighthood** fits oddly. Brothers share a brotherhood, but each knight has his own knighthood. || Some -hood words break the pattern. >> **Neighborhood** is a fine word, but -hood does a different job entirely. >> **Friendhood** is not the usual form. Friendship is used instead, for no apparent reason. >> **Enemyhood** is only a proposed form. English uses formal words like "enmity" or "antagonism" for that idea. || Some examples come from Liberman and Prince.
13|**Zero derivation changes a word's category with no affix, yet the meaning changes in complicated ways.** **Zero derivation** (a category change with no added affix) still affects meaning. >> Dish, spoon, fork and table are strong associates of the noun **knife**. >> As verbs or in fixed phrases, their meanings are not similar. || Each use has its own meaning. >> "She can dish it out but she can't take it" uses dish as a verb with a learned meaning, not a plain reference to a dish. >> "He was born with a silver spoon and a forked tongue in his mouth" uses fixed figurative phrases. >> "They decided to table the issue after a day of inconclusive negotiations" uses table as a verb about an issue. || The meanings must be learned one by one. >> Figurative phrases like silver spoon are studied in **Cognitive Linguistics**.
14|**Adding a derivational affix often changes how the rest of the word is pronounced.** It changes the spelling too. >> certificate /sər.ˈtI.fɨ.kɨt/ has main stress on the second syllable. >> certification /ˌsər.tɨ.fɨ.ˈkeI.ʃən/ has main stress on the fourth syllable. >> recertification /ˈri.sər.tɨ.fɨ.ˌkeI.ʃən/ has main stress on the first syllable. || These changes affect spelling. >> They are one reason for **"The Chaos"** of English spelling. >> Spelling closer to the spoken form would not necessarily read better. || Noun and verb pairs often differ mainly in **stress** (which syllable is most prominent). >> **record** changes stress between its noun and verb. >> **invite** does too, as in "He did not receive an invite" and "We did not invite him". >> **entrance** does too, but its noun and verb are not actually related.
15|**Inflectional morphology adds grammatical information in a more predictable way than derivation.** >> **Inflection** (a change for number, agreement or tense) keeps the same word. || Inflection comes in three kinds. >> **Plurals** turn knife into knives, one fork into two forks, and one mouse into a lot of mice. >> The **third person singular present** turns knife into knifes, I walk into she walks, and you run into he runs. >> The **past** turns knife into knifed, I walk into I walked, and she sings into she sang. || Menn makes a simple point. >> He says it would be silly to have separate dictionary entries for singular and plural nouns. || The sound side is less regular. >> Forms like mice and sang show **irregularity**. >> This irregularity matters for **language learning models**. >> It also matters for kinds of **developmental language impairment**.
16|**Syntax can require an inflected form, but no syntactic process forces a derived one.** >> In "two [noun]+pl", whatever noun fills the slot must be pluralized somehow. >> A regular noun gives two forks. >> An irregular noun gives two knives. || Derivation has no such syntactic process. >> This holds even for very productive derivational forms. >> The closest case is the formula "Once you [verb] it, you can un-[verb] it". >> That formula is not exactly syntax. || This difference has a use. >> It is important for models of **speech production**.

18|**A simple lexical network treats each word as a node that the matching word can activate.** >> A **node** (a lexical representation in the network) responds to one specific word. || The example network is very small. >> It has three nodes, **lion**, **tiger** and **stripes**. >> The input is the printed word **LION**. || Two levels are involved. >> The printed word gives perceptual evidence. >> The node stands for the word that could be recognized.
19|**Activation can be partial, so a node can be weakly active before a word is recognized.** >> **LION** contrasts poorly with a **noise mask** (visual noise that hides the word). >> The input is weak evidence. >> The lion node gets only **weak activation**. || The node ends up partly active. >> It is somewhat active but not yet identified. >> Its activation can keep building as evidence comes in.
20|**When the word is clear enough, its node becomes active enough to count as identified.** >> The **signal-to-noise ratio** (SNR, the word's strength compared with the background noise) becomes very favorable. >> The lion node becomes strongly active. >> The network has "identified" lion. >> Tiger and stripes stay as unidentified alternatives. || This models the **perceptual identification task**. >> Participants push a button as soon as they can identify the item. >> The measure is time to identification.
21|**Experience may speed up frequent words by tuning how their activation rises over time.** >> **Activation dynamics** (how activation rises and falls over time) are assumed in most models of this kind. >> A **frequency effect** (frequent words being identified faster) can come from this tuning. || Each encounter with a word might change one of three things. >> It might raise the word's **baseline** activation. >> It might strengthen its **incoming connections**, so it activates more quickly. >> It might change a **threshold** parameter. || This is a very simple form of **learning**. >> Different computational models simulate frequency effects in different ways. >> One is **Rumelhart and McClelland's (1986) Interactive Activation model**.
22|**Repetition priming happens because activation from a recent word has not fully faded.** >> **Repetition priming** (faster identification of a word seen very shortly before) follows from activation dynamics. || The process runs in steps. >> Activation does not rise and fall instantly. >> It falls more slowly than it rises. >> The word appears again while activation is still high. >> Its baseline is already higher than usual. >> It reaches identification faster.
23|**Two effects need links between words, so the simple network must be extended.** >> **Neighborhood effects** depend on how similar a word is to other words. >> **Associative, semantic and phonological priming** depend on whether a similar or related word was just seen. || The simple network cannot explain them. >> Its nodes are separate. >> Each node changes only its own activation. >> Connections between nodes are needed for one word to affect another.
24|**Spreading activation lets an active word partly activate the words it is associated with.** >> Association strength comes from word association **norms**. >> **Connection strength** (or distance) between nodes is set by association. || The spread follows the connections. >> Activating lion sends some activation along a weighted connection to tiger. >> A stronger connection passes more activation. || The example network links related concepts. >> Vehicle links to car and bus. >> Fire engine links to vehicles and to fire-related concepts. >> One active concept partly activates several related ones. >> This partial activation is the basis of **semantic priming**.
25|**Balota and Lorch found that priming through a go-between word appeared in pronunciation but not in lexical decision.** >> They asked whether activation spreads beyond directly associated concepts. >> Prime **lion** and target **stripes** connect only through the **mediating concept** **tiger**. >> Conditions were directly related, indirect through tiger, neutral and unrelated. || The first task was **lexical decision** (judging whether a string is a word). >> Direct pairs like lion and tiger sped responses. >> Lion before stripes did not beat neutral or unrelated primes. || The second task was **pronunciation** (reading the target aloud). >> Direct pairs sped responses. >> Lion before stripes also sped responses. || They drew two conclusions. >> Activation spreads beyond directly related concepts in semantic memory. >> Features of the lexical decision task **masked** the indirect effect.
26|**Activation flowing through the network lets lion prime stripes even though they are not directly linked.** >> Lion connects to tiger. >> Tiger connects to stripes. >> Lion and stripes have no direct association. || Activation follows a route. >> Activating lion activates tiger. >> Tiger passes some activation on to stripes. >> Stripes becomes easier to process. || This indirect priming through a **mediating** node is evidence for spreading activation.
27|**The indirect priming result is typical psycholinguistics, with careful design, small effects and a counterintuitive finding.** >> **Stimulus onset asynchrony** (SOA, the time from prime onset to target onset) was 250 ms or 500 ms. >> The measure was **pronunciation latency**. || Mean latencies across both SOAs differ by condition. >> Related primes gave 549 ms. >> Primes linked through tiger gave 558 ms. >> Neutral primes gave 577 ms. >> Unrelated primes gave 575 ms. || The effect is small but steady. >> The indirect advantage is only 17 to 19 ms. >> It appears at both SOAs. || The result is counterintuitive. >> Stripes and lion share no direct link. >> The only route between them runs through tiger.
28|**In WEAVER++, spreading activation among similar items can make naming slower, not faster.** >> **Belke and colleagues (2005)** used the **WEAVER++** model. >> The **upper network** links duck, fish, snake and mouse through shared concepts like animal, bird, swim and scales. >> The **lower lemma nodes** are the word candidates (**lemmas**) for duck, fish, snake and mouse. || A naive view makes one prediction. >> A block of similar pictures should prime each other. >> Naming should get faster. || The model makes the opposite prediction. >> Naming requires **selecting** one lemma. >> Similar pictures activate many lemmas at once. >> The lemmas compete, so naming can get **slower**.

30|**Broca's patient Tan could not speak but seemed to understand what was said.** >> In **1861**, neurologist **Pierre Paul Broca** presented the case. >> He presented it to the newly established **Society of Anthropology**. >> He was also its founding president. || Tan lost speech but kept other abilities. >> Tan had lost speech after a brain injury decades earlier. >> He could only produce one jargon syllable. >> He seemed otherwise "in possession of his senses". >> He seemed to understand what was being said. || Tan was not the first observed case of a specific loss like this.
31|**Tan's large frontal lesion gave Broca's area its name, but locating damage is not locating speech.** >> Tan's brain is on display at the **Musee Dupuytren**. >> It has a **large lesion**. >> The lesion definitely includes the **inferior frontal gyrus** (the third convolution of the frontal lobe). || This case gave rise to a claim. >> **Broca's area** is a portion of the left frontal lobe important for speech. || The claim drew criticism. >> **Hughlings Jackson (1874)** said "to locate the damage which destroys speech and to locate speech are two different things". >> Damage in one place can stop speech without that place holding speech.
32|**Lichtheim and Wernicke explained aphasias as disconnections between language centers.** >> In the **1870s**, these German neurologists observed **aphasia** (language loss after brain damage) more systematically. >> They ascribed specific losses to **disconnections** between **centers**. || Lichtheim's house has three centers. >> A is sound images. >> B is concepts. >> M is movement images. || Each disconnection causes a different loss. >> A cut between A and B could cause loss of understanding spoken words. >> A cut between A and M could cause loss of verbatim repetition. || This centers approach is an ancestor of **modularity**. >> Eling and Whitaker (2009) review this history.
33|**Freud argued that the brain may not be organized by our psychological categories.** >> **Sigmund Freud** criticized the **"diagram-drawing" school** of aphasia research. >> He thought it naive to expect brain organization to match categories like "word forms" or "concepts". >> He proposed that language emerged from **fields** linking perceptual and motor abilities to **drives**. || This is a **holist critique**. || It names a durable tension in **cognitive neuroscience**. >> Can brain area functions be directly related to cognitive functions? >> Is the brain really organized by our descriptions of cognitive phenomena? >> A useful psychological category has to be tied to the brain by evidence.
34|**MRI showed that Broca's famous cases had deep damage that reached far beyond Broca's area.** >> **Dronkers and colleagues** used **MRI** to assess Broca's landmark cases very precisely. >> One of these brains is **Leborgne**'s. || The MRI found extensive damage. >> The lesion is very **deep** and includes the **insula**. >> There is heavy **white matter damage** to the **superior longitudinal fasciculus** (a pathway connecting the front and back of the cerebral cortex). || Other work by this group points to the insula. >> The insula, not Broca's area, is strongly associated with **speech apraxia**. >> Speech apraxia is the condition Broca described in his patients.

36|**Speakers select lemmas at the functional level, and the tense of each verb is added later.** >> A **lemma** (a word's identity before its grammatical form is set) is what gets selected. >> The term is borrowed from mathematics, where it is a proposition in an argument. || A speech error reveals this. >> The intended sentence was "Where'd I put it when I brought it in". >> The produced sentence was "Where'd I bring it when I put it in". || The error has a clear pattern. >> The verbs switched places. >> The moved verb came out as "bring", not "brought". >> So the tense was applied after the switch. >> If "brought" had been selected, it would have moved unchanged. >> So the lemma BRING was selected and later expressed in its present form.
37|**Antonyms slip into speech easily because they overlap with the intended word at many levels.** >> People often say the opposite of what they mean. || Antonyms overlap in meaning. >> Antonyms are **semantically related**. >> They are **strongly associated**. >> In **spreading activation** terms, this is a perfect storm. || Antonyms also overlap in grammar. >> They can take on the same **semantic roles**. >> They share a **word class**, so they take the same syntactic roles. >> The rest of the speech production system can handle either one. || So activation spreads easily from a **lemma** to its antonym.
38|**Semantic roles stay the same even when the sentence order changes.** >> **Semantic roles** (how each participant relates to the event) are also called functional roles. || The passive sentence has one order. >> "The plums in the icebox were eaten by me" is **Undergoer**, **Location**, Verb, **Agent**. || The active sentence has another order. >> "I have eaten the plums in the icebox" is Agent, Verb, Undergoer, Location. || Lemmas can then be slotted into roles. >> The verb is TO EAT. >> The agent is ME. >> The undergoer is PLUM. || Order has **syntactic consequences**. >> It decides whether the lemma ME becomes I or me. >> That happens at the **positional level**.
39|**The functional level settles the message, the lemmas and their roles, but builds no clause yet.** >> A **message** to convey is decided. >> Appropriate **lemmas** are selected. >> The lemmas are associated with **semantic roles**. || Much is still missing. >> The clause is not put together. >> Word order and word forms are not set. >> Nothing is said out loud.
40|**The positional level turns the functional plan into syntax, including how the two events relate in time.** >> The example sentence is "Where'd I bring it when I put it in?" || Some facts are known after the functional level. >> There are two verbs, **PUT** and **BRING**. >> I/ME is the **agent** of both. >> IT is the **undergoer** of both. || Other facts are still needed. >> The speaker needs the **temporal relationship** between the verbs. The putting in happened in the past, so its clause needs a past form. >> The speaker needs the question structure. || Expressing all of this syntactically is the **positional level**'s job.
41|**The positional level builds a frame of slots, and each slot sets the form of the lemma that fills it.** >> The frame is "Where'd I [verb, root form] it when I [verb, past] it in?" >> It also holds other particles the grammar requires. || The two clauses have different slots. >> The first clause needs **Wh- movement** and an **auxiliary verb** to form the question. >> Its verb slot requires the **base form**. >> The next clause's slot requires the **past form**. || The error happens during slot filling. >> "Where'd I bring it when I put it in" arises while slots are filled with lemmas. >> BRING landed in the base slot, so it became bring. >> PUT landed in the past slot, and its past form is put.
42|**In "a language needer learns," two roots swapped while the affixes stayed in their slots.** >> Two lemmas got switched when fed into the positional level. >> The positional level stayed nearly the same. >> This matches the bring and put error. || The difference is in the noun. >> The noun is a **verb plus an affix**, need plus -er. >> Adding -er is **derivational morphology** (building a new word from parts). >> The verb slot kept its **inflectional** ending -s, so learn became learns.
43|**"He did that easy enoughly" has a stranded affix and also a reversed positional frame.** >> A **stranded affix** (an affix left in its slot while the root changes) appears here too. >> The affix is **-ly**, which makes an adjective into an adverb. || The two errors share one property. >> Both this error and "a language needer learns" strand an affix. || This error also differs. >> Something also happened when setting up the **positional level**. >> The intended frame is an adverb (adjective plus -ly) followed by an adjective. >> The error frame is an adjective followed by an adverb (adjective plus -ly). >> So easy took the plain slot and enough took the -ly.

45|**Oldfield and Wingfield found that objects with frequent names are named faster, but frequency is tangled with length.** >> **Oldfield and Wingfield (1965)** asked people to name pictures of objects. >> They found a **log-linear relationship** (a straight line against the logarithm of frequency) between frequency and response time. >> The line drawings come from **Snodgrass and Vanderwart (1980)**. || The finding has a problem. >> Frequency is related to **word length**, even in this tiny sample. >> Frequent words tend to be shorter. >> Length is a **confound** (a second variable that changes along with the studied one). >> Either variable could explain naming time. || This is a classic cautionary tale.
46|**Word frequency falls off steeply from the top words, and length rises as frequency falls.** >> The 200 most frequent words of an old corpus were put into ten bins of twenty. || Frequency changes across the bins. >> The first bin averages above 15,000 per million. >> The second bin drops below 4,000 per million. >> Later bins are much smaller, roughly 2,000 per million or less. >> This steep drop is **Zipf's law** in action. || Length changes the other way. >> The first bin averages under three letters. >> The last bins average about five letters. >> Length is **inversely related** to frequency. || Real words fit this pattern. >> Top words like the, of, and, to and a are three letters or fewer. >> Later high-frequency words like century and Washington are longer.
47|**To separate frequency from length, pick the words on purpose instead of at random.** >> High-frequency (**HF**) words are generally shorter than low-frequency (**LF**) words. >> Random LF words will always be longer. || The fix is deliberate selection. >> Choose long-ish HF words. >> Choose short-ish LF words. >> The groups are then **matched on length** while frequency is manipulated.
48|**With length matched, the frequency effect stayed, so frequency is not just length in disguise.** >> **Oldfield and Wingfield** eventually matched their high and low frequency words for **length**. >> They still found a **robust frequency effect**. >> They concluded that frequency effects are not merely length effects in disguise. || This logic is general. >> A lot of psycholinguistics tries to show that an effect is not just a **confounding variable** at work.

55|**Blocking generalized to new items from the same category, so it is not tied to repeating particular items.** >> **Experiment 3** introduced new items in **Cycle 5**. || Cycle 5 changed some things but not others. >> Naming slowed down dramatically overall. >> The **repetition priming** effect went away. >> The **blocking effect** stayed, with homogeneous sets slower than heterogeneous sets. || Cycle 5 is best compared with Cycle 1. >> In Cycle 1, no category had been named yet, and there was no blocking. >> In Cycle 5, the items were new but their category was not, and blocking appeared.
56|**Belke and colleagues ruled out three alternatives to place the blocking effect at lemma selection.** >> It is not **visual recognition**. The stimuli were **normed and matched**, and a **control experiment** was run. >> It is probably not **phonological encoding**, which is marked with a question mark. The words were not phonologically similar. Gaze duration, not eye-speech lag, was affected. >> It is not just a specific effect of **repeating lists**. Blocking generalizes to new items.
