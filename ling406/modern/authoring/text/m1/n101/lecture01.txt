7|**Alphabetic letters are not phonemes, because each language has its own rules for turning spelling into sound.** English spelling is especially ambiguous, or irregular. Even regular writing systems cause problems when languages are compared. >> **Pinyin** (a relatively recently invented system for writing Mandarin) is perfectly regular. >> The phrase "Wo qu tushuguan" (我去图书馆) uses familiar letters, but English rules do not give its pronunciation. >> The Chinese characters and the Pinyin write the same Mandarin phrase in two writing systems. || **French** spelling is also very regular. >> The sentence "Comment tu t’appelles?" also needs the French rules to be read aloud. >> A writing system can be regular inside one language without being a sound code for all languages. >> So comparing speech sounds must start from what speakers actually say.
8|**The International Phonetic Alphabet is a standard system for writing the sounds of all languages, showing their similarities and differences.** Its symbols stand for properties of pronunciation, not the spelling habits of one language. >> Sounds with different ordinary spellings can be compared directly. >> Sounds that ordinary writing spells with one letter can be told apart. || The **IPA consonant chart** arranges symbols by how the sounds are produced. >> Rows include **plosive**, **nasal** and **fricative**. >> Columns include **bilabial**, **alveolar** and **velar**. >> Symbols in related positions stand for related ways of producing a sound. >> So the chart is a system of comparisons, not a list of symbols to memorize.
9|**The IPA consonant chart is organized by three features, which are place, manner and voicing.** A consonant can match another on two features and differ on the third. >> **Place** (where the vocal tract is constricted) runs across the chart from front to back. >> **Manner** (the degree of closure) runs down the chart from closed to open. >> **Voicing** (when vocal fold vibration starts relative to the closure) shows as pairs of symbols. || Each feature has a clear case on the chart. >> Bilabial sounds sit at the left, because the constriction is at the lips. >> Velar sounds and sounds made farther back sit farther right. >> A **plosive** has a complete closure. A **fricative** has a narrower passage. >> Voicing can start before or during the closure, or after it. >> In a pair, the right-hand symbol is voiced. So p and b share the bilabial plosive position.
10|**/p/ and /b/ have the same place and manner of articulation but differ in voicing.** Features describe sound similarities and differences in a relatively precise and well-defined way. >> /p/ is plosive, bilabial and not voiced. >> /b/ is plosive, bilabial and voiced. >> Both are bilabial plosives, so the lips close and then release. >> They differ in **voicing** (the timing of phonation relative to the release of the closure). || The same difference separates **/t/ and /d/**. >> That pair is made at a different place. >> It still pairs a voiceless stop with a voiced stop. >> So features allow general statements about **voiced stops** such as /b/, /d/ and /g/.
11|**The voiced and unvoiced stop contrast is produced differently across languages and across contexts within a language.** The IPA writes these differences compactly. >> **[pʰ]** is plosive, bilabial, not voiced and **aspirated** (followed by a puff of air after the release). >> **[p]** is plosive, bilabial, not voiced and not aspirated. >> **[b]** is plosive, bilabial and voiced. || English and French build the contrast from different pairs. >> In English, word-initially, "peer" often starts with [pʰ] and "beer" often starts with unaspirated [p]. >> A hand in front of the mouth makes the puff easy to feel. >> In most varieties of French, the contrast is really [p] in "pou" against [b] in "boue". >> So the labels voiced and unvoiced do not mean the same timing in every language.
12|**Slashes mark broad transcription of phonemes, and square brackets mark narrow transcription with as much detail as possible.** The two notations answer different questions about the same utterance. >> **Broad transcription** writes the phonemes of an utterance, such as /p/ and /b/. >> It keeps the distinctions that organize words in the language. || **Narrow transcription** records how a sound was actually produced, such as [pʰ], [p] and [b]. >> [pʰ] is marked aspirated, and [p] is marked not aspirated. >> It can keep a difference like aspiration even when that difference does not distinguish words. || Two broad labels sit beside three narrow forms. >> More phonetic detail does not create more phonemes. >> So a **phone** (an actual speech sound) and a **phoneme** (a contrastive category) are not the same.
13|**Consonant features are anchored to how speech is produced, and place of articulation names where the vocal tract is constricted.** The lips can close together. Parts of the tongue can approach or touch different regions of the mouth. >> These movements change where the outgoing air is blocked or narrowed. || Three places use different articulators. >> **Bilabial** means the upper and lower lips close at the front of the mouth. >> **Alveolar** means the tongue tip rises toward the ridge behind the upper teeth. >> **Velar** means the back of the tongue rises toward the soft palate. || Place is separate from manner. >> A complete closure can happen at several different places. >> So finding the place means locating the main constriction, not reading the letter.
14|**Consonant features describe both the route of the airflow and which articulators make the constriction.** All of these arrangements happen in the same vocal tract. >> A **nasal** consonant lets air out through the nose while the mouth is closed at some point. >> The passage between mouth and nose is open, so air keeps flowing despite the closure. >> For a nasal at the lips, the lips are closed and the route to the nasal cavity is open. >> So a nasal can share its place with a stop and differ in the airflow route. || Two places involve the teeth at the front of the mouth. >> **Labiodental** means the lower lip meets the upper teeth. >> **Dental** means the tongue is at the front teeth. >> Tracking the airflow and locating the constriction are separate steps.
15|**Vowels are organized by height, by front or back tongue position, and by lip rounding.** The vowel chart runs from close at the top to open at the bottom, and from front at the left to back at the right. >> **Close** vowels tend to have a higher jaw position and tongue than **open** vowels. >> The tongue is generally farther forward for **front** vowels than for **back** vowels. >> **Rounded** vowels have rounding of the lips. In a pair, the right-hand symbol is rounded. || **/y/** is the vowel in French "tu" and Mandarin "qu" (去). >> It can sound like /u/ to English speakers. >> /i/ and /y/ share the close front region and differ only in rounding. >> Making an /i/ and rounding the lips changes it toward /y/. >> The tongue does not move back as for /u/, so lip shape is separate from tongue position.
16|**A vowel category is a pattern of articulation, not one fixed tongue shape.** The whole vocal tract shapes the sound, not the tongue alone. >> The center of the **tongue body** gives one reference point per vowel. >> That point captures one important difference among vowels. >> Whole tongue outlines can differ in more than that one point. || Some vowels are very **dynamic**. >> The **diphthongs** (vowels that change during their production) in "boat" and "bait" move through time. >> A single point cannot give the path the tongue takes. >> So a dynamic vowel is understood by following its change over its duration.
17|**Speakers of the same language can differ in which vowels they distinguish.** Even within American English, vowels are very salient and differ across **dialects**. >> The test case is the two vowels in the lower back corner of the chart, rounded /ɔ/ and open back /ɑ/. || The two kinds of speaker give opposite results for "caught" and "cot". >> A mid-Atlantic speaker from the outer boroughs of New York City distinguishes the two vowels. >> For that speaker, "caught" and "cot" are different words. >> Most people from other parts of the country do not distinguish them. >> For them, "caught" and "cot" are **homophones** (words that sound the same). || Both are English, so the difference is a systematic dialect pattern. >> Whether a speaker has the contrast is found by listening to that speaker.
18|**The lack of invariance problem is that one speech category sounds physically different across speakers and contexts, yet listeners still understand it.** The problem comes up again and again in speech perception. >> Some people say "Aaron" and "Erin" with the same vowel, and listeners who keep them apart still understand. >> A listener can hear such a difference for some time and only notice once attention is drawn to it. || Measured vowels give the same problem. >> Each vowel is placed by its **first formant (F1)** and **second formant (F2)**, in cycles per second. >> Many productions of one vowel spread over a wide region. >> The regions for neighboring vowels overlap. >> So no single pair of F1 and F2 values identifies a vowel.
19|**A minimal pair is two words whose difference in meaning depends on one sound, and it is the evidence that two phones are different phonemes.** Two phones are **contrastive** when swapping them changes the meaning. Contrastive phones are different phonemes. >> The two low back vowels had to be established with two words that differ only in those vowels. || "Caught" and "cot" work this way for one kind of speaker. >> For a speaker who distinguishes the vowels, "caught" and "cot" are a minimal pair. >> The consonants stay the same, so the vowel alone separates the words. >> For a speaker who merges the vowels, the pair sounds the same. >> So that pair does not establish two vowel phonemes for that speaker. >> The evidence is a difference in meaning, not just a difference in sound.
20|**Minimal pairs show that several English stop contrasts all depend on the same feature, voicing.** The evidence comes from comparing minimal pairs in each language. >> **/b/ and /p/** are bilabial stops that differ in voicing. >> **/t/ and /d/** are alveolar stops that differ in voicing. >> **/k/ and /g/** are velar stops that differ in voicing. || Each pair keeps place and stop manner the same. >> Voicing changes within each pair. >> Place changes across the pairs. >> So one feature describes all three contrasts. >> Two phones existing physically does not make them a contrast in every language.
21|**Allophones are different phones that are different realizations of the same phoneme.** There is a great deal of variability in how phonemes are realized. Some of it is very predictable. >> The variation depends on position and neighboring sounds, so it is systematic. >> It does not change which word is being said. || Recording three phrases in **Praat** (a program for recording and analyzing speech) compares /p/ in three contexts. >> In "pot that", /p/ starts the word before the vowel. >> In "spot that", /p/ comes after /s/. >> In "stop that", /p/ ends the word. >> The release and aspiration can differ, while each one is still /p/.
22|**A recording of "pot that", "spot that" and "stop that" makes the allophones of /p/ visible over time.** The contexts differ, so closure, release, aspiration and the following voiced sound can differ in timing. >> These differences fit with all three words containing the same phoneme /p/. || The recording is read in two forms. >> The **waveform** (top) gives the amplitude of the signal over time. >> The **spectrogram** (bottom) gives acoustic energy over time and frequency. >> Time runs left to right in both, so the same event lines up vertically. >> Whether two phones are different phonemes depends on whether the difference distinguishes words.
23|**Unreleased [p̚] and aspirated [pʰ] at the end of "stop" are allophones, because no minimal pair separates them.** Speakers sometimes say [stɑpʰ]. >> This happens when stressing the word at the end of a sentence. >> It also happens in careful speech, called **citation form**. >> There is no difference in meaning between [stɑp̚] and [stɑpʰ]. >> Allo means different, so allophones are different phones for the same phoneme. || The forms also alternate across **morphological forms** of the word. >> "Stop", "stops", "stopped" and "stopping" each put something different after the /p/. >> That changes the environment of the /p/. >> So whether the /p/ is released and aspirated depends on the form. >> A difference that can be heard is not always a phoneme difference.
24|**The word voicing is used in two different ways, one for phones and one for phonemes.** That is why it can be confusing. >> In **narrow transcription** (describing specific phones), a sound is voiced if the vocal folds vibrate during the whole gesture or sound. >> This is a claim about one actual production. || In **broad transcription** (describing phonemes), voicing is **realized** in a number of different ways. >> One way is presence or absence of **aspiration** at the beginnings of words. >> Another way is the relative duration of voicing to closure in "writer" and "rider". >> So one phoneme contrast can have more than one physical cue. >> Ask which level is meant when a sound is called voiced.
25|**Phonotactics is, roughly, which phonemes can occur together in syllable structures, and it reshapes clusters that English does not allow.** Rules about syllable structure turn out to be complex. They still seem to shape speech production. >> The name Sbarro comes out as [spɑroʊ] or [zbɑroʊ]. >> It never comes out as [sbɑroʊ], whatever speakers think they are saying. || Foreign names force speakers to deal with such clusters. >> Speakers tend to insert vowels. >> The Polish name Zbigniew becomes [zɨ’bIg.nʲu]. >> The Kru name Gbagbo becomes [gə.bɑ.gə.boʊ]. >> The changes follow English syllable patterns, not an inability to say each consonant.
26|**Phonemes are not letters, and they are not even specific sounds or gestures.** A phoneme is "the smallest phonetic unit in a language that is capable of conveying a distinction in meaning." This can be complicated in practice. >> A phoneme is defined by the contrasts it makes, which ties it to minimal pairs. >> One phoneme can have several allophones. || Three things belong to three descriptions. >> A **letter** is part of a writing system. >> A **phone** is an actual speech realization. >> A **phoneme** organizes contrasts that carry meaning. || The **International Phonetic Alphabet** lets speech scientists communicate about similarities and differences between sounds across languages. >> So it is easy to think of it as an alphabet of phonemes. >> That would be a mistake, because phonemes are not letters.
27|**Speech is a continuously changing signal, not an orderly stream of letter-like units.** Literate people easily imagine speech as a row of letters, but it is nothing like that. >> A **spectrogram** of "A sore back is a real pain" makes this visible. >> Time runs left to right, and frequency rises upward. >> Darker regions have stronger acoustic energy. || The patterns flow into each other. >> Frequency patterns change over time, and transitions connect neighboring segments. >> The labels and boundary markers are added on top and do not cut the signal into pieces. >> Phonemic categories do not match isolated blocks with one fixed acoustic shape. >> So recognizing the sentence means interpreting changing patterns.
28|**Speech is made by coordinated movements of the lips, jaw, tongue and other parts of the vocal tract.** Their changing configuration shapes the sound that comes out. >> A side view from USC SPAN shows the articulators in action. >> It locates the tongue in the mouth and the passage toward the throat. || Each articulator does a different job. >> The **tongue** changes the inner shape of the oral cavity. >> The **lips and jaw** change its opening. >> The movements run as one ongoing sequence. >> So a speech **gesture** is part of a moving system, not one posture repeated for each phoneme.
29|**Hockett (1955) compared hearing to identifying smashed eggs, because speech mixes its units together and the hearer only has to identify them.** The analogy has three stages. >> Unboiled Easter eggs of various sizes and colors ride a moving belt. >> This flow stands for the impulses from the **phoneme source**. || A wringer smashes the eggs and rubs them more or less into each other. >> The mess stands for the output of the **speech transmitter**. || An inspector studies the yolks, the spread-out albumen and the bits of shell. >> He decides what flow of eggs reached the wringer. >> The inspector stands for the **hearer**. >> He does not rebuild the eggs, which is physically impossible. He only identifies them.
30|**People have started to question whether phonemes are real, either psychologically or linguistically.** A phoneme is useful for describing contrasts. That does not settle how words are stored or how speech is recognized. >> **Lotto and Holt (2000)** wrote about the illusion of the phoneme. >> **Beckman and Edwards (2000)** wrote about how phonological categories develop and the primacy of lexical learning. >> **Port (2007)** asked how words are stored in memory, beyond phones and phonemes. >> **Goldinger and Azuma (2003)** wrote about puzzle-solving science in speech perception. || The shared question is about mental representation. >> A descriptive notation and a model of mental processing answer different questions. >> So the open issue is whether phoneme-sized units describe the mind itself.
