2|**Phrenology tried to read brain functions from skull shape, and its chain of premises was only partly wrong.** A simplified phrenological chart assigns head regions to language, memory, thinking, love and other categories. >> Premise one says different parts of the brain do different processes. >> Premise two says aptitude relates to brain region size. >> Premise three says brain region size shapes the skull. >> Careful skull measurements were then supposed to reveal brain functions. || No one believes in phrenology anymore, but its premises are not equally wrong. >> Premise one stays a serious idea, because brain parts can contribute differently to behavior. >> Premise two needs a dependable link from ability to region size. >> Premise three fails, because **skull shape is not a sensitive measure of brain shape**.
3|**Phrenology failed because it measured the wrong thing and defined functions too loosely.** >> The first problem was **inappropriate anatomical measurement** (using the skull to stand for the brain). >> Skull shape is not a sensitive measure of brain shape. || The second problem was an **unconstrained definition of functions** (broad social labels treated as single mental functions). >> Studies of prisoners aimed to locate "criminality". >> Studies of women with different numbers of children aimed to locate "philopropogenitiveness". || A sound structure and function study needs both sides done well. >> It needs a valid anatomical measure. >> It needs a clear account of the process being measured.
4|**Flourens attacked phrenology with lesion experiments and argued that lesion size mattered more than location.** He was an early and vociferous critic of phrenology. >> He used **experimental lesions** (damaging brain tissue and observing behavior). >> He worked mainly with pigeons and rabbits. || His results had two parts. >> He found **distinct functions** for the cerebral cortex, the cerebellum and mid-brain structures. >> He **failed to find** any match between specific cortical regions and specific senses. || He insisted that the **size of the lesion** was critical. >> When one sense was lost, all were lost together. >> Removing most of the cortex makes sight, hearing and other senses disappear together.
5|**In 1861 Broca presented Tan, a patient who could not speak but seemed to understand.** Pierre Paul Broca was a neurologist studying **aphasia** (language loss after brain damage). >> Tan lost speech after a brain injury decades earlier. >> Broca presented the case to the newly established Society of Anthropology. >> Broca was also its founding president. || Tan's abilities came apart. >> Speaking was limited to **one jargon syllable**. >> His senses seemed otherwise intact. >> His **understanding** of what was said seemed preserved. || Tan was not the first observed case of a specific loss of speech. >> His case still suggested that speech production and comprehension can be damaged separately.
6|**Tan's lesion gave the name Broca's area to part of the left frontal lobe, but finding damage is not finding function.** >> Tan's brain is on display at the Musee Dupuytren. >> The lesion was **large**. >> It definitely included the **inferior frontal gyrus** (the third convolution of the frontal lobe). || **Broca's area** is a portion of the **left frontal lobe** important for speech. || **Hughlings Jackson (1874)** objected at the time. >> He wrote that "to locate the damage which destroys speech and to locate speech are two different things". >> Damage in one spot can stop speech. >> That does not show where speech itself is produced.
7|**Lichtheim and Wernicke explained different aphasias as disconnections between language centers.** In the 1870s these German neurologists made more systematic observations of aphasia. >> A **center** is a proposed brain location for one function. >> A **disconnection** is a break in the pathway between two centers. || In Lichtheim's house model, each letter is a center. >> A stands for sound images. >> B stands for concepts. >> M stands for movement images. || Each loss maps to a broken link. >> A loss of understanding spoken words may come from an A to B disconnection. >> A loss of verbatim repetition may come from an A to M disconnection. || Describing centers for specific functions is an ancestor of **modularity**.
8|**Freud doubted that the brain is organized by our psychological categories.** He critiqued the "diagram-drawing" school of aphasia research in his early neurological work. >> He felt it was naive to expect brain areas for "word forms" or "concepts". >> He proposed that language emerges from **"fields"**. >> These fields connect perceptual and motor abilities to **"drives"**. || This is a **holist critique** (language spread over connected systems, not set in centers). || It raises a durable tension in cognitive neuroscience. >> Can brain area functions be related directly to cognitive functions? >> Is the brain really organized according to our descriptions of cognition?
9|**MRI showed that Broca's original lesion was much deeper than Broca's area.** **Dronkers and colleagues** used MRI to assess damage in Broca's landmark cases very precisely, including Leborgne's brain. >> The lesion is very deep. >> It includes the **insula**. >> It damages much white matter in the **superior longitudinal fasciculus** (a tract connecting the front and back of the cortex). || Other work by this group points away from Broca's area. >> The **insula**, not Broca's area, is strongly associated with **speech apraxia**. >> Speech apraxia is the condition Broca described in his patients.
10|**Broca's description of behavior and his anatomy were both crude because of the methods of his time.** >> Broca is still discussed 150 years later despite these limits. || His **behavioral** description was crude. >> Linguistics then focused on historical issues, such as how languages were related. >> Linguists also worked on reconstructing ancient languages. >> Experimental psychology had not really been invented yet. >> Observations of behavior were necessarily informal. || His **anatomical** localization was crude and probably wrong. >> Fine-grained descriptions of the cortex were still in their infancy. >> **Brodmann's map** was not published until 1909.
11|**Broca's presentation of Tan made linking functions to brain regions a respectable pursuit.** Other researchers showed similar things around the same time. >> **The Daxes** reported related findings. >> **Wernicke** reported a patient who was **fluent** but produced **meaningless gibberish**. >> Wernicke's data are, if anything, more interesting because of that contrast with Tan. || For a variety of reasons, Broca's case is still seen as a **watershed moment** in neuropsychology. >> It turned relating specific functions to specific brain regions into a serious research question.

13|**Cognitive neuropsychology needs cognitive psychology to define the functions it relates to brain damage.** >> **Cognitive neuropsychology** (the study of how brain damage changes mental functions) supplied much 20th century knowledge about structure and function. >> It relied on experimental observations of brain-damaged individuals. || Cognitive theory proposes which operations might be separable. >> One question asks if recognizing **abstract word forms** separates from computing **meaning**. >> **Physical identity** matching compares letters by appearance. >> **Name identity** matching compares letters by name. >> **Rule identity** matching sorts letters as vowels or consonants. || **Quantitative measurements** test such questions. >> They allow **statistical comparison**. >> This is critical because **deficits are rarely complete**.
14|**Anatomical imaging locates injuries in living patients, but it still shows damage, not normal function.** >> Scientists no longer wait for patients to "come to autopsy" to find a lesion. >> A scan of **H.M.'s** middle temporal lobes is one example. >> His damage resulted from surgery. || The warning from **Hughlings Jackson (1874)** still holds. >> Locating the damage that destroys speech differs from locating speech. >> Patient studies show behavior **after** a region is damaged. >> They tell only **indirectly** what the intact region does.
15|**PET and fMRI measure brains in action only indirectly, slowly and coarsely.** **Posner and colleagues** described **PET** studies that used radio-labeled oxygen as an activity marker. >> These methods are **metabolic** (they track an index of oxygen consumption, not neural activity). >> They are **slow**, since metabolic processes unfold over seconds. >> Important neural events can occur in milliseconds or less. >> They have only **fair spatial resolution**, on the order of millimeters. >> Important events happen at the scale of microns. >> FMRI has better resolution than PET.
16|**Imaging needs a control condition because the whole brain is active all the time.** >> A claim that "x part of the brain is active during y task" cannot stand alone. >> Activity occurs all over the brain all of the time. || **Subtraction logic** (task image minus control image) isolates the difference. >> The control shares most demands with the task. >> A motor image minus a rest image leaves a much smaller region. || **Posner and colleagues** applied this logic to language. >> Their tasks shared visual input and responses. >> The tasks differed in whether **word meaning** had to be accessed. >> Subtracting the simpler task isolates semantic processing from word and letter form identification.
17|**Semantic tasks activated a left inferior frontal region that roughly matches Broca's area.** **Posner and colleagues** compared three meaning tasks with simpler tasks. >> Monitoring words for dangerous animals was compared with passively viewing words. >> Generating uses for written words was compared with repeating them. >> Generating uses for heard words was compared with repeating them. || All three contrasts activated the **left inferior frontal** region. >> The activity appeared for both seen and heard words. >> It links the region to **semantic processing** of words. >> The region corresponds **roughly to Broca's area**.
18|**Imaging can be better than phrenology, but only when its measures and definitions are sound.** The question "Is this better than phrenology?" is rhetorical but worth exploring. || Phrenology failed on two points. >> Its measure, skull shape, was invalid. >> Its functions, such as "criminality", were undefined. || Imaging improves both points in part. >> It measures an index of brain activity. >> It defines operations through **controlled task contrasts**. || An image of an active region still proves nothing alone. >> The contrast must isolate the proposed operation. >> The measurement must support linking that operation to the region.
19|**Posner and colleagues localized component operations, not whole tasks.** >> The operations work on **visual**, **phonological** and **semantic codes**. || Whole tasks involve many coordinated computations. >> Reading a word is one such task. >> Forming a visual image is another. >> Shifting attention is another. >> No whole task is assigned to one brain area. || The PET evidence **complemented lesion evidence**. >> It linked cognitive distinctions to activity during performance. >> Averaged blood-flow patterns suggested **consistency** across participants. >> The participants were right-handed and good readers.
20|**Language suits localization research because it splits into separable, testable abilities.** Language has turned up at key moments in debates about localization of function. >> **Broca's presentation of Tan** helped establish **lesion-behavior analysis**. >> Some first **PET** and **fMRI** studies used activation of **Broca's area** in language tasks. >> Those studies treated it as evidence that cognitive functions could be localized. || Language abilities come apart in clear ways. >> Tan lost speaking but seemed to keep understanding. >> Understanding, repeating and judging meaning place different demands on processing. >> These differences give clear contrasts for lesion studies and control conditions.
