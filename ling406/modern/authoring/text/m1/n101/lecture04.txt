2|**Association norms record which words people produce when they see a cue word.** **Association norms** (summaries of the responses people give to a cue) are collected with a simple task. >> A participant sees a word on a screen for a few seconds. >> The participant writes down the first few words that come to mind. >> The task asks for immediate associations, not a definition. || Responses are then compared across people. >> The same cue can bring up different associates in different people. >> Some associations recur across many people. >> Other responses are unusual.
3|**LION is a cue, and the participant supplies the words it brings to mind.** >> The responses reveal connections in **lexical knowledge** (what a person knows about words). >> TIGER is one possible response. >> TIGER would connect two animal concepts. >> No participant has to give TIGER. || The cue starts the response. >> The association itself comes from the participant.
4|**TRIPE looks like STRIPES, but the two words do not share a meaning.** >> The two words overlap in **form** (spelling and sound). >> They do not overlap in **meaning**. >> Similar form and connected meaning are different relationships. || The pair makes the **arbitrariness** of form and meaning concrete. >> Overlapping letters can occur in words with very different meanings. >> The form of a word does not tell you what it means.
5|**FREE is a cue, and the participant generates the response instead of choosing from a list.** >> The participant writes words that come to mind from FREE. >> No list of answers is offered. || Recording the response makes an internal association available to study. >> Responses to FREE can then be compared across people. >> The norm for FREE comes from what many people produce on their own.
6|**STRIPES names a property, so its associates can be a different kind of thing.** >> STRIPES is a **visual property** (a feature you can see, here a pattern). >> An association can link a property to an animal that has it. >> STRIPES and TIGER can be associated. >> STRIPES names a pattern, while TIGER names an animal. || This link is not **similarity** (sharing properties). >> LION and TIGER are similar animals. >> STRIPES and TIGER are associated but not similar.
7|**PEAR and BEAR look alike in form but name different things.** >> PEAR names a fruit. >> BEAR names an animal. >> Their forms overlap. || The pair separates two relationships. >> A **formal relationship** (shared sound or spelling) links PEAR and BEAR. >> A **semantic relationship** (shared meaning) does not link them. >> A shared spelling pattern is not evidence of similar meaning.
8|**BEAR is another cue whose form overlaps with PEAR while its meaning does not.** >> BEAR resembles PEAR in form. >> BEAR does not resemble PEAR in meaning. || **Association norms** record the responses people actually give. >> The experimenter does not decide in advance whether a response will be based on form or meaning. >> The norm captures whatever connection the cue brings to mind.
9|**TREE is a cue, and its norm summarizes the different associates that different people give.** >> The first responses show which words are readily connected with TREE. >> That evidence holds for the task context it came from. || The cue stays the same physical word. >> Different people can supply different associates. >> The **norm** (the summary of all responses) does not treat one response as the only association.
10|**TIGER links to LION through similar meaning and to STRIPES through a characteristic pattern.** >> TIGER and LION are related as members of the same animal category. >> TIGER and STRIPES are related as an animal and one of its properties. || Both kinds of link can be part of a **lexical network** (a network of connected word representations). >> The two links are different relationships. >> So association and similarity are not interchangeable.

12|**LION, TIGER and STRIPES are tabulated because they show two kinds of meaning link that matter later.** >> LION and TIGER name similar animals. >> TIGER and STRIPES link an animal with a characteristic property. >> Their association can be measured by counting how often participants give one word in response to another. || The pairs TRIPE and STRIPES, and PEAR and BEAR, make a separate point. >> Their forms overlap. >> Their meanings do not. >> The link between a word's form and its meaning is largely **arbitrary** (not predictable from the form). || Three relationships stay separate. >> One is similarity of form. >> One is similarity of meaning. >> One is the tendency of one word to bring another to mind.
13|**Being associated is different from being similar, and norms depend on how the task was set up.** **Association** is a tendency for one item to bring another to mind. **Similarity** is about shared properties. >> TIGER and STRIPES are associated. >> TIGER and STRIPES are not similar objects. || Participants may have used a deliberate **strategy**. >> A strategy can change which words they write. || The **list structure** could influence the answers. >> A list of only wild animals could encourage one set of responses. >> A list of only household appliances could encourage a different set. >> A norm is evidence about responses under specific conditions.
14|**A few metaphors from neuroscience are the basis for most theory and experimental design in psycholinguistics.** Menn's chapter is called "How Brains Work". A better title might be "Some basic metaphors from neuroscience that are useful in psycholinguistics". >> **Activation** is how active a representation is. >> **Spreading activation** passes activity over **weighted connections** to linked representations. >> **Distributed representations** encode an item as a pattern across many units. >> **Associative learning**, such as **Hebbian learning**, strengthens links between units that are active together. >> **Bottom-up processing** runs from sensory features up to words, and **top-down processing** runs from word knowledge back down. || Together these ideas connect how knowledge is organized with the time course of recognizing and producing language.
15|**A single neuron can respond most strongly to one specific combination of line orientation and movement.** **Hubel and Wiesel (1968)** related neural activity to sensory input, at the same time as the **Cognitive Revolution**. >> They recorded from **simple cells** in **primary visual cortex (V1)**. >> A simple cell responds to lines of particular orientations in its **receptive field** (the part of the sensory input it responds to). || They varied the orientation and movement of a line. >> Upward movement of a line at about **315 degrees** gave the **strongest response**. >> Other orientations and movements gave much weaker responses. || The cell's **electrochemical activity** tells us about its representational and computational properties. >> The cell can be described as **representing** that combination of features.
16|**Cells further up the visual processing stream respond to complex patterns, such as another monkey's face in profile.** >> In data adapted from **Gross and colleagues (1985)**, a cell in **inferotemporal cortex** fires strongly for near-profile views of a monkey's face. >> It fires much less for frontal and rear views. >> Its responses are highest around 80 to 100 degrees of face angle. || The cell seems to **represent** something complex, not line segments. >> Its activation is **graded** (it can be a little active or very active). || In psycholinguistics, **activation** extends this idea metaphorically. >> It applies to **perceptual representations**. >> It applies to **conceptual representations**.
17|**A simple model treats each word as a node that a specific word can activate.** >> Imagine an incredibly simple set of **lexical representations** (stored representations of words). >> Each one is a **node** (a unit with an activation value) in a network. >> The network has three nodes, for lion, tiger and stripes. || The input and the nodes are different levels. >> The printed word LION supplies perceptual evidence. >> The lion node is the word the system could recognize from that evidence.
18|**One word node has to respond to many printed forms of the same word.** >> LION, lion and Lion all name the same word. >> Changes in typeface and size alter the display too. >> All of them should activate the lion node. || This is the **lack of invariance problem** (one identity, many different sensory forms). >> The same problem came up with **phonemes**. >> The **receptive fields** of word nodes would eventually have to be very complicated to handle it. >> For now, imagine nodes that words can activate.
19|**A node can be only partly active when the input is weak.** >> LION appears inside a **noise mask** (visual noise around the word). >> LION contrasts poorly with the mask. >> So the input gives weak evidence. >> So the lion node gets only **weak activation**. || Partial activation describes the state before recognition. >> The node can be partly active while recognition is still uncertain. >> Its activation can rise as more evidence comes through.
20|**As the word becomes clearer, the activation of its node increases.** >> In the input, LION becomes easier to tell apart from the noise. >> In the model, the lion node becomes **more active**. || Stronger evidence supports the same word more strongly. >> Recognition can be modeled as a response that grows with input strength. >> Changing the display can change how a word is processed.
21|**When the signal is clear enough, activation is strong enough to count as identifying the word.** >> The **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)** (the strength of the word signal relative to the noise) becomes very favorable. >> The lion node becomes strongly active. >> The simple network has **identified** lion. >> Tiger and stripes are not identified. || This is the **perceptual identification task**. >> Participants push a button as soon as they can identify the item. >> The time to identify it is the measure. >> Easier conditions lead to an earlier response.
22|**Both the word itself and the experimental setting can change how quickly a word is identified.** >> **Features of the stimulus itself** concern the target word. >> How often it is encountered is one. >> How similar it is to other words is another. || **Features of the experimental environment** concern what surrounds the target. >> Having just seen the same word is one. >> Having just seen a similar or related word is another. || The same target can be tested in different contexts. >> Different targets can be compared under the same task.
23|**Frequency, similarity to other words and recent exposure all change how quickly a word is identified.** >> **Word frequency** (how often a word is encountered) makes a word easier to activate. >> Similarity to other words creates **neighborhood** relationships. >> **Repetition priming** (faster identification after just seeing the same word) comes from recent exposure. >> Just seeing a similar or related word can also prime it. || Frequency and repetition priming differ. >> Frequency reflects experience built up over time. >> Repetition priming reflects a very recent presentation. >> A related prime changes the context without repeating the target.
24|**Words that are encountered more often are easier to activate because experience tunes up their activation dynamics.** >> Most models of this kind assume that activation **unfolds over time**. >> **Activation dynamics** (how activation rises and falls over time) are **tuned up by experience**. >> Frequent words become more sensitive. >> That is one way to explain **frequency effects**. || The **Interactive Activation model** of **Rumelhart and McClelland (1986)** simulates this kind of process. >> Activation for CAVE rises above competing words while the input supports it. >> Then it gradually falls. >> Different computational models simulate frequency effects in different ways.
25|**Seeing a word again soon makes it faster to identify because its activation has not fallen back yet.** >> Activation does not rise and fall instantly. >> It may **fall off more slowly than it rises**. >> Some activation remains after the word is gone. || The word appears again while activation is still high. >> Its **baseline** (starting activation level) is higher than usual. >> So it can be activated more easily. >> This is **repetition priming**, a basic effect in psychology.
26|**Neighborhood effects and related-word priming need connections between word nodes.** >> **Neighborhood effects** come from how similar a word is to other words. >> Priming can come from a similar or related word. >> That priming can be **associative**, **semantic** or **phonological**. || These two factors were left out so far. >> Isolated nodes can only change their own activity. >> Repetition involves the same word twice. >> These effects involve one word changing a different word.
27|**Many language phenomena require the nodes of the network to be connected.** After all, it is a network. >> Lion is connected to tiger. >> Tiger is connected to stripes. || Activation can now arrive in two ways. >> The printed word activates lion **directly**. >> A connection carries activity from lion to tiger **indirectly**. >> A connection changes how one node responds when another is active.
28|**Activating one word sends some activation along weighted connections to associated words.** This is **spreading activation**. >> Connection strengths between nodes are set by their association, as in the norms. >> Activating lion sends some activation along a **weighted connection** to tiger. >> Different connections pass different amounts of activation. || This is a spreading activation account of **semantic priming** (faster processing of a word after a related word). >> Tiger is already partly active from lion. >> So tiger is easier to recognize. || A semantic network links many concepts. >> Vehicle links to car and bus. >> Fire engine links to the vehicles and to fire concepts.

31|**As the contrast of STRIPES rises, more of its letter pattern stands out from the noise.** >> The letters are faint but easier to pick out. >> The input gives **stronger support** for the word. || The word itself has not changed. >> Only the quality of the evidence has changed.
32|**STRIPES becomes more identifiable as its contrast improves.** >> The letters now stand apart from the noise. >> The evidence for the word keeps growing. || The participant responds when the evidence is enough to recognize the word. >> Stimulus quality decides when that response becomes possible.
33|**The clearer STRIPES gives enough word information for the participant to respond.** >> The noise field is the same kind as before. >> The letter pattern is clearer. || The participant can now make the **identification response**. >> The response reflects recognizing the word. >> It is not a judgment about whether stripes go with a particular animal.
34|**At high contrast, STRIPES is easy to identify, and identifying it activates the stripes node.** >> The dark letters stand out from the gray noise. >> Recognizing the word activates its node in the network. || The identified word can act as a **prime** (an earlier item that may change how a later item is processed). >> Its effect on another word depends on the connections between their nodes.
35|**LION is the target in a second identification sequence, and it starts out hard to read.** >> LION has weak contrast against the noise mask. >> The letter pattern gives little usable information. >> The short word sits in the center of the noise. || The target can be separated from its recent context. >> A **priming** account asks whether earlier experience changes how quickly LION is recognized.
36|**As the contrast of LION rises, the evidence for the word gets stronger.** >> LION is now faintly visible in the center. >> The letters start to separate from the noise. || The same target produces a **changing internal response**. >> This happens because the quality of the sensory evidence changes. >> Recognition becomes more likely.
37|**The time at which LION is identified is what a priming comparison measures.** >> LION is clearer inside the mask. >> The four letters have more contrast than before. >> A response can happen once the evidence supports recognizing the word. || A priming comparison asks whether recognition comes **sooner** after one preceding context than after another. >> LION is the common target in that comparison.
38|**LION is now legible, and both its clarity and any leftover activation from context help its node.** >> The noise mask is still present. >> The stimulus gives substantial evidence for lion. >> That evidence raises the activation of the lion node. || Two sources add to the lion node's state. >> One is the **clarity** of the target. >> The other is **activation left by the context**.
39|**The clear LION display completes the sequence, and its node can be activated in two ways.** >> The strong word signal is easy to tell apart from the noise. || The two ways matter for the experiment. >> The printed word activates the lion node **directly**. >> Other nodes can influence it **through connections**. >> The experiment asks whether STRIPES helped LION through the network.
40|**Activating stripes primes lion even though the two words are not directly associated.** Activity flows throughout the network. >> Stripes is connected to tiger. >> Tiger is connected to lion. >> Activation from stripes reaches lion by way of tiger. || This is called **mediated priming** (priming that passes through a middle word, here tiger). >> A model with only direct associations has no route from stripes to lion. >> So the effect is **compelling evidence for spreading activation**.
41|**The stripes and lion experiment is typical of psycholinguistics, with careful design and a small, counterintuitive effect.** >> It uses **careful study design**. >> It finds **small effects**. || The result is **counterintuitive**. Why would stripes prime lion? >> Stripes and lion are not obvious direct associates. >> Activity travels from stripes through tiger to lion. >> A model with only direct associations cannot explain the effect. >> So a small effect can tell the two accounts apart. || Three broader issues remain. >> They are top-down and bottom-up processing, learning and distributed representations.
42|**Word knowledge can feed back down and help complete a letter that is only partly visible.** **Bottom-up processing** (from visual features up to letters and words) and **top-down processing** (from an active word back down to its letters) work together. >> The **Interactive Activation network** after **McClelland and Rumelhart (1981)** has features at the bottom. >> It has letters in the middle. >> It has words such as WORK, WORD, WEAR and WEAK at the top. || In the example, blots cover part of the last letter of WORK. >> The visible features fit more than one letter. >> WORK becomes active and sends activation back down to K. >> The output for **K rises strongly** over time, while R and D stay low. || This is one way of modeling **pattern completion**, which Menn discusses at length.
43|**Associative learning strengthens connections between units that are active together.** **Hebbian learning** is summed up as "neurons that fire together, wire together." >> The real process is more complicated. >> The neural learning mechanisms that are relatively well understood are **associative**. || In the bell and food example, a bell signal and a food signal both feed a salivation unit. >> The bell and food inputs are active together. >> The **synapse** from the bell is strengthened. >> The bell then has more effect. || These mechanisms were once considered **insufficient** to learn anything important about language. >> Recent arguments ask what role they play. >> They also ask how **innate knowledge** might constrain them. >> **Large language models (LLMs)** learn many structural features of language with little more than this.
44|**Distributed representations let an associative model generalize what it learns to similar items.** A **distributed representation** (an item encoded as a pattern across many units) can capture similarity. >> **Generativity** is very important for language. >> To generalize, a model must represent **similarity**. >> Similar items have overlapping patterns. || What you know about robins and canaries can **generalize** to new birds as you learn about them. || The network takes an **item** input and a **relation** input. >> For canary with the relation can, the outputs include grow, move, fly and sing. >> Hidden layers connect the item and relation to those properties.
45|**Five neurally inspired metaphors give psycholinguistics a vocabulary for how word knowledge works.** >> **Activation** changes as input is processed. >> **Spreading activation** lets one representation influence another. >> **Top-down and bottom-up processing** describe the direction of influence between levels. >> **Associative learning**, such as Hebbian learning, changes connection strengths. >> **Distributed representations** support similarity and generalization. || **Priming experiments** help reveal what people's **lexical networks** might look like. >> They measure how processing one item changes the processing of another.
