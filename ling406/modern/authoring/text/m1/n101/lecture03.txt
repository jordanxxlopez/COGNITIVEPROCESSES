4|**The form knifes is a real word because knife can be a verb, and the verb takes a different ending than the noun.** The case comes from an argument over Scrabble. >> One player says knifes is not a word because the plural of knife is knives. >> The other answers that Tony knifes Bernardo in West Side Story. || Three ideas explain it. >> **Inflectional morphology** (changing a word's form for grammar) gives the plural **knives** from the noun. >> **Derivational morphology** (making a new word from an existing one) gives the verb knife from the noun. This case is **zero derivation** (a change in part of speech with no affix added). >> **Phrases and clauses** set the verb's ending. "He knifes him" is a singular noun, a verb and a noun, so the verb needs the **third person singular** form. || Knives is the noun's plural. Knifes is the verb's form after he.
5|**Derivational morphology derives new words from existing parts, and those parts are called morphemes.** A **morpheme** contributes meaning or grammatical information. >> A morpheme is not a syllable. >> A morpheme is not a single speech sound. || The word **morphology** has a fairly transparent derivation. >> The part morph- (from Greek, meaning form) comes first. >> The part -ology (also from Greek, here meaning something like the study of) comes second. >> Together they mean "the study of the forms of words". || A similar process gives other unit names. >> **Morpheme** is the unit of word form. >> **Phoneme** uses phone, another Greek morpheme (probably meaning speech). >> A unit in text should get a name built the same way. >> Graphology looks like it should name the study of those units, but the word does not mean that.
6|**A word made by derivation has a meaning that is only partly predictable from its base.** **Derivational morphology** is **productive** (able to make new words), but only in limited ways. >> Made-up words are hard to guess the meaning of. >> The meaning of the final form is only **partially predictable**. || **Zero derivation** (changing the part of speech with no affix) usually works with a **narrow sense** of the base word. >> The noun knife covers many objects. Image search results for knife include several cutting tools and tableware. >> A search for prison shiv gives an improvised weapon. >> The verb knife covers one kind of action, which is attacking somebody with a knife. >> The weapon-like knives, such as the prison shiv, are the ones a person would use to knife somebody. >> A table knife is a knife, but it is not a natural fit for the verb.
7|**The suffix -hood is productive, but it does not apply to every stem and does not always do the same job.** **Derivational morphology** is limited in how widely and how regularly **affixes** (parts added to a stem) are applied. >> The pattern is a stem plus -hood. >> The result is a state of being that is associated with the stem noun. || Some words follow the pattern. >> **Brotherhood** and **sisterhood** fit it. >> **Knighthood** fits it oddly. Two brothers share their brotherhood, but each knight has his own knighthood. || Some words do not follow the pattern. >> **Neighborhood** is a perfectly good word, but -hood does a different job in it entirely. >> **Friendhood** is not used. English says friendship instead, for no apparent reason. >> **Enemyhood** is not used, though maybe it ought to be. The available words are less common ones such as enmity or antagonism. || Some of these words come from Liberman and Prince's introductory linguistics course at Penn. >> Knowing an affix includes knowing which words actually take it.
8|**Zero derivation leaves the word's form alone, but the verb's meaning can be far from the noun's meaning.** **Zero derivation** changes a word's category with no visible affix. It still does complicated things with **semantics** (meaning). >> Dish, spoon, fork and table are strong associates of the noun knife. >> Their meanings as verbs are **not similar**. || Each sentence uses a learned meaning. >> "She can dish it out but she can’t take it" uses dish as a verb. >> "He was born with a silver spoon and a forked tongue in his mouth" uses forked for a shape, with an established figurative meaning. >> "They decided to table the issue after a day of inconclusive negotiations" uses table as a verb. || None of these is a literal reference to a utensil or to furniture. >> The meaning comes from **conventional usage**, not from naming the object. >> The silver spoon expression is the kind of thing studied in **Cognitive Linguistics**.
9|**Adding an affix can change how the rest of the word is pronounced, especially where the stress falls.** **Derivational morphology** more commonly changes the pronunciation and spelling of a word than leaves them alone. >> Certificate is /sər.ˈtI.fɨ.kɨt/. >> Certification is /ˌsər.tɨ.fɨ.ˈkeI.ʃən/. >> Recertification is /ˈri.sər.tɨ.fɨ.ˌkeI.ʃən/. >> The **stress** sits on different syllables in the three words, and some vowels differ too. || This effect on **phonology** (the sound pattern of a word) is one reason for "The Chaos" of English spelling. >> Higher fidelity to the spoken form does not necessarily improve readability. || Some noun and verb pairs differ mainly in **stress**. >> Record is stressed differently as a noun and as a verb. >> Invite is stressed differently in "He did not receive an invite" and "We did not invite him". >> Entrance is stressed differently too, but its noun and verb are not actually related to each other.
10|**Inflectional morphology changes a word in a predictable way and does not make a new dictionary word.** English has three inflections here. >> **Plural** gives knife(n) and knives, one fork and two forks, one mouse and a lot of mice. >> **Third person singular present** gives knife(v) and knifes, I walk and she walks, you run and he runs. >> **Past** gives knife(v) and knifed, and today I walk and yesterday I walked. It also gives today she sings and in the past, she sang. || Menn writes that “...we’d think it was silly to have separate dictionary entries for the singular and plural forms of nouns.” >> The singular and the plural are forms of one word. || The phonological process of inflecting words often has some **irregularity**. >> Regular forms add an ending, as in forks and walked. >> Irregular forms change the word, as in mice and sang. >> Both kinds give the same grammatical meaning. >> Irregularity becomes important for language learning models and for kinds of developmental language impairment.
11|**Syntax can require a word to be inflected, but it does not require a word to be derived.** **Inflectional morphology** depends on the **syntax** (how words are organized into phrases and sentences) of the rest of the sentence. >> The phrase "two [noun]+pl" has a slot for a noun. >> Whatever noun goes there must be pluralized somehow. >> A regular noun gives two forks. >> An irregular noun gives two knives. || **Derivational morphology** has no syntactic process like this, even for very productive forms. >> The closest case is a formula, “Once you [verb] it, you can un-[verb] it.” >> That formula is not syntax. >> No sentence requires a verb to have an un- form.
12|**English uses inflection in only a few places, and many other languages use it for much more.** English **inflection** covers plurals, the third person singular present, and the past. >> Plurals are knives, two forks and a lot of mice. >> Third person singular present forms are knifes, she walks and he runs. >> Past forms are knifed, walked and sang. || Many languages inflect more than English. >> Many languages conjugate verbs much more elaborately for **person**. >> Many languages do the same for **tense**. >> Many languages form the **plural** differently for small and large numbers. >> Many languages have **case markings** (noun markings that encode information about syntactic or semantic roles).
13|**Derivation creates new words, and inflection gives a word the form its sentence requires.** Both kinds of morphology share two properties. >> Both create new phonological forms out of old parts. >> Both change the meanings of base forms. || **Derivational morphology** has two properties of its own. >> It creates **new words**. >> It is productive, but not always, and typically in a limited way. || **Inflectional morphology** has three properties of its own. >> It changes how words interact syntactically with other words. >> It is **highly productive**, but not always regular. >> It is **required by syntax**. || The knife forms give one case of each. >> The verb knife from the noun knife is derivation. >> The form knifes after he is inflection.
14|**Words combine into phrases, and phrases use function words as well as content words.** Above the word are **phrases**, **clauses** and **sentences**. >> **Content words** include nouns and verbs. >> Other content word classes are modifiers such as adjectives and adverbs. >> **Function words** come in once more than a couple of words are put together. >> Determiners and prepositions are function words that organize how content words relate. || The sentence “The video for “More than Words” came out before most of you were born” contains two **noun phrases**. >> The first is The video for “More than Words”. >> The second is most of you. >> They differ in length, so a noun phrase is not a fixed number of words.
15|**The video for “More than Words” is a noun phrase built from a determiner, a noun and a prepositional phrase.** Its structure is written **Det + N + PP**. >> **The** is the **determiner** (the function word that begins the noun phrase). >> **Video** is the head noun. >> **For** is the **preposition** (the function word that begins the prepositional phrase). >> The prepositional phrase lets the song title specify which video is meant. || The title “More than Words” is not really part of the sentence. >> It is being used like a noun. >> With the quotes removed, it is not a phrase. >> It is then a bunch of words strung together in a row.
16|**Phrases with completely different words can share the one abstract structure Det + N + PP.** The structure is incredibly **abstract** because it names categories, not words. >> "two guys from the early ‘nineties" is two, then guys, then from the early ‘nineties. >> "a band with only one hit" is a, then band, then with only one hit. >> "one and only one phrase unrelated to early ‘nineties one-hit wonder Extreme" is one and only one, then phrase, then the rest. || The parts differ inside. >> A **determiner** can be several words, as in one and only one. >> The **head nouns** are guys, band and phrase. >> The first two modifiers are **prepositional phrases** beginning with from and with. >> The third modifier begins with the adjective unrelated, followed by a to phrase. >> That third modifier is an adjective phrase with a prepositional phrase inside it.
17|**Abstract categories such as noun phrase are needed to compare different phrases and different languages.** The abstraction leaves out the individual words. >> Without it, very different surface forms could not be called similar. >> Without it, similarities and differences between languages could not be stated coherently. || All languages have **NPs** (noun phrases), but they can form them very differently. >> English has the cat in the coffee shop, with a determiner first and the PP after the noun. >> Chinese has 咖啡店裡面的小貓, glossed as coffeeshop inside + cat. >> The Chinese order is **PP + N**. >> The Chinese NP has **no determiner**. || The missing determiner helps explain a learning problem. >> Speakers of Chinese languages find determiners very difficult to learn in a second language (L2).
18|**Every prepositional phrase here is a preposition followed by a noun phrase, written P + NP.** Breaking down three **prepositional phrases** gives the general structure. >> From the early ‘nineties is from plus the NP the early ‘nineties. That NP is Det + Adj + N. >> With only one hit is with plus the NP only one hit. That NP is given as P + Det + N. >> Unrelated to early ‘nineties one-hit wonder Extreme is unrelated to plus an NP. That NP is given as Adv + Adj + Adv + Adj + N. || The **NP** inside can be short or long. >> It still counts as one unit. >> The preposition relates that whole unit to the rest of the expression.
19|**Phrase structure is hierarchical, which means smaller phrases sit inside larger ones.** The phrase from the early ‘nineties has several levels. >> The determiner, the adjective and the noun are inside the **noun phrase**. >> The noun phrase is inside the **prepositional phrase**. >> The prepositional phrase is inside a sentence, or inside yet another noun phrase. || Curly brackets and parentheses in a flat row are a clumsy way to write these levels. >> **Trees** are diagrams that give each phrase a node above the parts it contains. >> Hierarchy is part of why people started using trees to diagram sentences. >> Hierarchy is also an important feature of languages.
20|**A verb phrase is a verb plus the phrases that complete it, and those phrases vary.** The **verb phrase** came out before most of you were born is **V + PP**. >> The V is came out. >> The PP is before most of you were born. >> The PP is hard to unpack because most of you were born looks like a verb phrase. >> It specifies a point in time that complements before. || Many verb phrases also contain **noun phrases**. >> Sang the song is **V + NP**. >> Worked himself to death is **V + NP + PP**, with himself as the NP and to death as the PP. >> Threw a party for him is **V + NP + PP**, with a party as the NP and for him as the PP.
21|**A clause is a verb phrase together with its subject, and the subject is the noun phrase that controls the verb.** The sentence “The video for “More than Words” came out before most of you were born” has two parts. >> The NP is The video for “More than Words”, which is Det + N + PP. >> The VP is came out before most of you were born, which is V + PP. || The **clause** pairs that VP with its **subject**. >> The whole first NP is the subject. >> The **head noun** is video. >> The whole sentence is **one clause**.
22|**A verb agrees with the head noun of its own subject, even when another noun is closer.** This agreement is what **controls** means. >> “The plums that were in the icebox were delicious” is a simple sentence with one clause. >> Its parts are given as Det + N + PP and then VP. >> The verb agrees with plums, not icebox, because plums is the **head** of the noun phrase. || “The plums in the icebox, which you were probably saving for breakfast, were delicious” is a **clause within a clause**, set off with commas. >> You is the subject of were probably saving for breakfast. >> Plums is the subject of were delicious. >> Changing you to my roommate changes were probably saving to was probably saving. >> Were delicious does not change, because its subject is still plums. || "Forgive me" has an **implicit subject** (a subject that is understood but not said). >> The speaker wants whoever shares his icebox to forgive him for eating the plums.
23|**The same event can be said with different subjects, so the subject is not always the one who acted.** Two sentences mean the same thing but are very different **syntactically**. >> The subject is the phrase the verb agrees with. || "I have eaten the plums in the icebox" is the **active** sentence. >> I is the **subject** and appears first. >> A verb phrase follows. >> The plums in the icebox is an NP inside the verb phrase. It is the **object** of the verb. || "The plums in the icebox were eaten by me" is the **passive** sentence. >> The plums in the icebox becomes the **subject**. >> The verb were eaten agrees with the plural noun, which is the evidence. >> By me is just a **PP** that fills out the VP.
24|**Semantic roles stay the same in the active and passive sentences, and only the syntactic roles change.** **Semantic roles** (what each participant does in the event) do the job of telling "who did what to whom". **Syntactic roles** (subject, object) do that job less. >> The **agent** is the eater. >> The **undergoer** is the plums. >> The **location** is the icebox. || The passive order is Undergoer + Location + Verb + Agent. >> The plums is the undergoer, in the icebox is the location, and by me is the agent. >> The subject is the undergoer. || The active order is Agent + Verb + Undergoer + Location. >> I is the agent, the plums is the undergoer, and in the icebox is the location. >> The subject is the agent. || English usually aligns the syntactic subject with the agent. >> That habit is why it feels counterintuitive that me is not the subject of the passive sentence. >> The relationship between syntactic and semantic roles also influences sentence processing.
25|**Language is highly generative, and speakers use that ability mostly without thinking about it.** **Generative** means familiar parts can be combined into new words and new sentences. >> **Derivational morphology** makes new words. >> **Syntax** organizes words into clauses, clauses into phrases, and phrases into sentences. >> **Inflectional morphology** modifies words for different syntactic roles. || People do all of this mostly without explicit thought. >> A speaker can form a plural or build a phrase without naming a grammatical category. >> One of the hardest things to understand about linguistics is what there is to explain. >> Some of the most complex phenomena are hiding in plain sight.
