2|**Speech errors are linguistic evidence because the form of each error reveals how an utterance was planned.** >> An error can split a word into parts. >> An error can move a sound into another position. >> An error can replace the intended word with a related one. || People make speech errors because many items are active at once. >> **Noise**, **frequency**, other active material and **similarity** can each let the wrong item win. || **Fromkin**'s title "Speech errors as linguistic evidence" is gutsy. >> If **competence** (knowledge of the language) is studied apart from **performance** (actual speaking), an error is just something to set aside. >> Fromkin instead treats the structure of errors as evidence about the language system. >> Comparing the error with the intended form reveals which parts changed and which stayed.
3|**In spreading activation models, an error happens when an unintended item gets enough activation to beat the intended one.** Four key concepts explain where that activation comes from. >> **Noise** (fluctuation and interference in activation) is not only literal acoustic noise, though that can cause problems too. >> **Frequency** matters because common words are easier to activate than less common ones. This seems to affect many levels of speech production. >> **Other sources of activation** include what was just said and what is planned next. Thoughts the speaker is trying not to say may add activation too. >> **Similarity** lets activation spread more easily between related items. It can be semantic, syntactic or phonological. || These sources can combine on one competitor. >> That is how an unintended candidate can enter the utterance.
4|**A tip-of-the-tongue state is partial activation of a word's meaning and sound that falls short of the threshold for saying it.** The person knows the meaning but cannot produce the word. >> Less frequent words are most susceptible, because their forms are harder to activate. >> A **prime** (a related word read first) can help, because it adds activation to the missing sound form. || **Lise Abrams** reviewed an example with the target "contraband" (goods traded illegally). >> People in a **tip-of-the-tongue (TOT) state** read a prime and then tried the target again. >> First-syllable primes such as "contact," "connotation" and "conscious" resolved about **50 percent** of TOT states. >> Middle-syllable primes gave intermediate resolution. >> Final-syllable primes were close to the unrelated condition. >> Unrelated words resolved only about **one quarter**. || Sound overlap at the start of the word helps recovery most.
5|**Slips of the tongue are classified by which direction a sound moves between the intended and the produced form.** Spooner supposedly once said "Mardon me padam, this pie is occupewed. Can I sew you to another sheet?" >> These examples come from Fromkin. || The three types answer the same question, which is where the misplaced sound came from. >> **Anticipation** (a later sound comes too soon) turns "week long race" into "reek long race." >> **Perseveration** (an earlier sound carries forward) turns "John gave the boy" into "John gave the goy." >> **Metathesis** (an exchange, where two sounds trade places) turns "keep a tape" into "teep a cape." The k and t swap, and the rest stays put. || The type depends on the relation between the two forms. >> Whether the result makes sense does not matter.
6|**Other sources of activation are the spreading activation concept most obviously at work in these slips.** Material just said and material planned next are both active while a person talks. >> In anticipation, planned material is active too early, so the r in "reek long race" arrives before its place. >> In perseveration, spoken material stays active, so the g in "John gave the goy" lingers after its place. >> In an exchange, two elements in the same plan interfere with each other's positions. || The other concepts still play a part. >> **Similarity** and **frequency** decide which candidates compete well. >> **Noise** can decide which candidate gets picked. || Planning reaches past the sound being said right now. >> This lets the speaker prepare what comes next. >> It also lets the wrong active element fill a position.
7|**A mondegreen is a slip of the ear, in which a listener groups the speech sounds into different words than the speaker meant.** >> The term comes from hearing "laid him on the green" as "Lady Mondegreen." >> "Hold me closer, tiny dancer" gets misheard as "Hold me closer, Tony Danza." It is "Tiny Dancer," like the song title. >> **Andrew Nevins** is analyzing crowdsourced mondegreens from kissthisguy.com. || Speech is ambiguous because it arrives as a continuous stream. >> Listeners have to infer the **word boundaries** (where one word ends and the next begins). >> "The good candy came anyways" and "The good can decay many ways" sound nearly alike. >> **Context** is what tells them apart. || Context matters most in songs. >> In songs, **prosody** (the rhythm and melody of speech) is less helpful. >> So a familiar name or phrase can take over.
8|**A malapropism like this is a perceptual error, because the speaker says exactly what they intend to say.** >> The speaker once heard a word like "congenital" (present from birth). >> The speaker decided the intended word must have been a more familiar one, like "congenial." >> The speaker now says "a congenial heart defect" and means it. || This is **top-down processing** (known words and expectations shaping what is heard). >> A familiar word helps organize the heard sound. >> The familiar word wins over the less familiar target. >> Other examples include "lettuce days," "decaffeinated him" and "Utopian tubes tied." || A production slip and a mislearned expression can sound equally odd. >> They come from different processes.
9|**Fromkin's errors break up consonant clusters, so single segments are units in speech planning.** At the time, people argued about whether the **phoneme** (or phone) was a planning unit. >> If whole **clusters** always moved together, errors would look like "gish frotto" or "clesh freer water." >> Fromkin presents evidence that clusters get broken up all the time instead. || Each error moves only one sound of a cluster. >> "Fish grotto" became "frish gotto." The r left gr and joined the f. >> "Fresh clear water" became "flesh queer water." The r and l exchanged, and f and k stayed put. >> "Split pea soup" became "plit spea soup." Only the s moved. || A model therefore needs access to each individual segment.
10|**Fromkin's 1971 model traces how a meaning to be conveyed becomes an utterance through a fixed series of stages.** It is an early version of the model Menn describes. >> First, syntactic and semantic generators build **syntactic-semantic structures**. >> Next, an intonation contour generator adds **primary stress** and **intonation**. >> Then the **lexicon** supplies words specified for features and order of segments. >> This gives strings of segments divided into syllables. >> **Morphophonemic rules** and **phonetic rules** fully specify the sounds. >> Last, **motor commands** to the muscles produce the **utterance**. || Each stage is a separate representation. >> So an error can hit one stage while the others stay organized. >> Different errors give evidence about different stages.
11|**The basic model of speech production has five levels, from the message down to the speech gesture.** >> The **message level** holds what the speaker intends to convey. >> The **functional level** picks **lemmas** (word identities before their final form) and assigns **semantic roles**. >> The **positional level** arranges them into a clause. >> The **phonological encoding level** specifies the sounds. >> The **speech gesture level** prepares the movements. || Following Menn, the first two levels come first. >> The sound slips were already phonological encoding errors. || The levels separate things the everyday word "word" blurs together. >> Choosing a fitting word is one job. >> Choosing its grammatical form is another job. >> Assembling its sounds is a third job.
12|**The message level decides what information a speaker wants to convey, and the same event can become different messages.** Take "I won a bunch of money on the game Sunday." >> With a fan of the losing team, the listener's feelings may change how directly the win is stressed. >> With someone not interested in football, the speaker may need to explain why it matters. >> In a tweet, the short public post changes which details get included. || The event stays the same, but purpose and chosen details change. || The message level is mysterious and hard to investigate experimentally. >> Giving everyone the same event does not mean they all intend the same content.
13|**Picture-book elicitation controls what speakers see, but it still cannot fully control the message they intend.** An **elicitation paradigm** (a task built to draw out speech) such as the picture book "Frog, where are you?" gives everyone the same scene. >> The scene contains a boy, a dog and frogs. >> Descriptions can be compared because the visible material is held constant. || The intended message is still hard to define. >> Speakers can focus on different characters or actions. >> Speakers can assume different knowledge in the audience. >> Age, culture and audience may change how the message is built.
14|**The functional level selects lemmas rather than finished words, because a moved word can still change its grammatical form afterward.** A **lemma** is a word's identity before its final form is set. >> The term is borrowed from mathematics, where it means a proposition in an argument. || One error explains why. >> Intended was "Where'd I put it when I brought it in." >> Produced was "Where'd I bring it when I put it in." || The verbs switched places, but the tense was applied later. >> "Where'd" already contains the past-tense helper did, so the next verb takes the base form. >> So BRING came out as "bring," not "brought." >> If the word "brought" had been selected, it would have moved unchanged. >> PUT looks the same in both forms, so BRING gives the visible evidence.
15|**People often say the opposite of what they mean because opposites are closely related in the lexical system.** This is an **error in lemma selection**. >> A speaker said "I really like to" and corrected it to hate. >> A speaker said bottom and meant the top of the stack of books. >> A speaker said "This room is too damn hot" and corrected it to cold. >> A speaker said oral and meant the written part of the exam. || Each pair shares a domain. >> Hot and cold share temperature. >> Top and bottom share position. >> Oral and written share exam format. || The wrong word fits the sentence. >> So the sentence stays well formed while its meaning is reversed.
16|**Antonyms are quite similar at several levels, so activation spreads easily from a lemma to its antonym.** **Antonyms** (opposite words) overlap in four ways. >> They are **semantically related**. >> They are **strongly associated**. >> They can take on the **same semantic roles**. >> They belong to the **same word class**, so they can take on the same syntactic roles. || The first two give the antonym strong activation. >> In the spreading activation framework, this is a perfect storm. || The last two let the antonym pass through. >> The rest of the speech production system can handle it. >> So "hot" for "cold" still makes a normal sentence.
17|**A blend combines parts of two competing words into one produced form.** **Lewis Carroll** made such blends on purpose and called them portmanteau words. >> In The Hunting of the Snark, he explains "frumious" from "fuming" and "furious." >> A speaker leaning toward fuming says "fuming-furious." >> A speaker leaning toward furious says "furious-fuming." >> A perfectly balanced mind says "frumious." || **Fromkin** quoted Carroll because he said blends occur in natural speech. >> Carroll also proposed a hypothesis about how they occur. || Fromkin found that complete blends like this are seldom found in normal speech. >> A blend of [fjurijəs] and [fjumiŋ] is more apt to come out as [fjumijəs] or [fjuriŋ]. >> This is because the first syllables are identical.
18|**Spontaneous blends show that two alternative words can both stay active until they mix in the output.** >> "I swindged" blends **switch** and **changed**. >> Other blends mix **mainly** and **mostly**, **bother** and **trouble**, and **swinging** and **hip**. >> Others mix **spirant** and **fricative**, and **player** and **athlete**. || **Fromkin** proposed how this happens. >> The speaker selects words by matching **semantic features** to the meaning. >> With several alternatives, both go into a **buffer** (a temporary storage compartment) with their sound specifications. >> Then one is selected, or the two are blended. >> These errors seem to support Carroll's assumptions. || Speech production nearly always has a number of alternative possibilities. >> Choosing among them is an ordinary part of speaking.
19|**Semantic roles stay the same when the sentence form changes, so lemmas can be slotted into roles before the word order is set.** >> In "The plums in the icebox were eaten by me," the order is Undergoer, Location, Verb, Agent. >> In "I have eaten the plums in the icebox," the order is Agent, Verb, Undergoer, Location. || At the functional level, the lemmas fill **semantic roles** (also called functional roles). >> The verb is TO EAT. >> The **agent** (the one acting) is ME. >> The **undergoer** (the thing acted on) is PLUM. >> The **location** is the icebox. || The order of roles has syntactic consequences. >> ME becomes "I" in the active sentence and "me" after "by" in the passive. >> That choice happens later, at the **positional level**.
20|**The functional level does a lot of work, yet the speaker is still nowhere near putting a clause together, much less saying it.** By this point, three things are done. >> A **message** has been decided. >> Appropriate **lemmas** have been selected. >> The lemmas have been linked with **semantic roles**. || Much is still undecided. >> Whether the sentence is active or passive is not set. >> How the pronouns will be expressed is not set. >> The full sound sequence is not set.

23|**Many speech errors go undetected because top-down effects in perception fill in the expected message.** >> Listeners use context and expectations to recover what was meant. >> So a real error can pass without notice. >> A similar process is assumed for many malapropisms and mondegreens. || Some "errors" are made on purpose for **stylistic** reasons. >> **Paul Krugman** wrote "the upper hand is on the other foot," quoting Hot Shots Part Deux. >> It mixes "the upper hand" with the shoe being on the other foot. || A deliberate error can be **more memorable** than the correct version. >> The odd mismatch draws attention to the wording itself. >> Recognizing both familiar phrases makes the mix funny. >> So whether an error was intentional matters when it is used as evidence.
