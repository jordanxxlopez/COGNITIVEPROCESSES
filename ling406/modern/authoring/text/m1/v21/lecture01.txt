7|**Letters are not phonemes**, because letters belong to a writing system while speech sounds belong to the spoken language. >> English spelling is especially ambiguous, or irregular as Menn puts it, but even less irregular spelling systems cause problems when languages are compared. || **Pinyin** is a relatively recently invented system for writing Mandarin, and it is perfectly regular, yet an English reader cannot pronounce Wo qu tushuguan (我去图书馆) correctly from English spelling habits. >> Pinyin is regular for Mandarin only, so its letters follow Mandarin conventions and not English ones. || **French** spelling is also very regular, and Comment tu t’appelles? still has to be read with French spelling-to-sound conventions. >> Matching letters are not evidence of matching sounds, so comparing languages requires describing what speakers actually produce.
8|Comparing languages requires a **standard system** for writing the sounds of the world's languages in a way that accurately captures their **similarities and differences**. >> The **International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)** is that system, because its symbols stand for properties of pronunciation and not for the spelling conventions of any one language. || The IPA **consonant chart** arranges its symbols by how the sounds are produced, so the position of a symbol tells how that sound relates to its neighbors. >> The rows have labels such as **plosive**, **nasal** and **fricative**, and the columns have labels such as **bilabial**, **alveolar** and **velar**. >> Two sounds can be compared directly even when ordinary spelling writes them differently, and sounds that share a letter can be told apart.
9|The IPA consonant chart is organized by three **features**, which are **place**, **manner** and **voicing**. || **Place of articulation** is where the vocal tract is constricted, and it runs across the columns from the front of the vocal tract to the back. >> Bilabial sounds sit toward the left because their constriction is at the lips, and velar sounds sit farther right. || **Manner of articulation** is the degree of closure, and it runs down the rows from closed to open, so a plosive has a complete closure and a fricative has a narrower passage. >> **Voicing** separates the two symbols paired in one position by whether voicing starts before or during the closure or after it, and the right-hand symbol is the voiced one. >> The paired p and b sit in the plosive row of the bilabial column, so two consonants can share place and manner and still differ in voicing.
10|Features describe how sounds are alike and different in a relatively **precise and well-defined** way. >> /p/ is plus plosive, plus bilabial and minus voiced, while /b/ is plus plosive, plus bilabial and plus voiced. || /p/ and /b/ have the **same manner and place of articulation**, because both are bilabial plosives in which the lips close and then release. >> They differ in **voicing**, which is the timing of phonation, meaning vocal-fold vibration, relative to the release of the closure. || The difference between /p/ and /b/ is analogous to the difference between /t/ and /d/, which are made at a different place but have the same voiceless and voiced relation. >> Features therefore allow one general statement about **the voiced stops**, such as /b/, /d/ and /g/, in place of a separate description for every consonant.
11|The contrast between **voiced** and **unvoiced** stops is produced differently across languages, and across contexts within one language, and the IPA gives a compact way of writing this. || In **English**, at the start of a word, the first sound of "peer" is often an unvoiced **aspirated** stop [pʰ], and the first sound of "beer" is often an unvoiced, **unaspirated** stop [p]. >> **Aspiration** is the puff of air after the closure is released, so a hand held in front of the mouth feels the difference between "peer" and "beer". >> The superscript h in [pʰ] marks aspiration, so [pʰ] and [p] are both minus voiced and differ only in aspiration. || In most varieties of **French**, the contrast is really between [p] for "pou" and voiced [b] for "boue". >> [pʰ], [p] and [b] are three different phones, even though a language may sort them into fewer contrasting categories.
12|**Slashes** mark **broad transcription**, which writes the **phonemes** of an utterance, as in /p/ and /b/. >> Broad transcription keeps only the distinctions that organize the words of the language. || **Square brackets** mark **narrow transcription**, which includes as much detail as possible about how the sound was actually produced, as in [pʰ], [p] and [b]. >> Narrow transcription keeps differences such as aspiration even when they do not distinguish words, so recording more detail does not create more phonemes. || There are two broad labels and three narrow forms here, so a **phone**, which is a specific produced sound, and a **phoneme**, which is a sound category that distinguishes words in a language, are not interchangeable terms.
13|**Phonetic features**, especially for consonants, are **anchored to how speech is produced**, so each place label names a physical constriction in the vocal tract. || A **bilabial** closure brings the upper and lower lips together at the front of the mouth. >> An **alveolar** constriction raises the tongue tip toward the ridge behind the upper teeth. >> A **velar** constriction raises the back of the tongue toward the soft palate. || Place is separate from manner, because a complete closure can be made at several different places. >> Finding the place of a consonant means locating the main constriction and not relying on the written letter.
14|Consonant features describe both the **route of the airflow** and the **articulators** that make the constriction. || A **nasal** consonant closes the mouth at some point while the passage to the nose stays open, so the air goes out through the nose. >> A nasal can share its place in the mouth with a stop, as when the lips are closed, and differ only in the route that the air takes. || A **labiodental** constriction brings the lower lip against the upper teeth, while a **dental** constriction puts the tongue at the front teeth. >> Both are made at the front of the mouth, so the difference is whether the lower lip or the tongue approaches the teeth.
15|Vowels are described by **height**, by **front or back** tongue position, and by **lip rounding**. || **Close** vowels tend to have a higher jaw position than **open** vowels, and the tongue is higher as well. >> The tongue is generally farther forward in the mouth for **front** vowels than for **back** vowels. >> **Round** vowels are made with rounding of the lips, and where two vowel symbols are paired the right-hand one is the rounded vowel. || /y/ is the vowel in the French word "tu" and the Mandarin word "qu" (去), and it sounds like /u/ to English speakers. >> Making an /i/ and then rounding the lips produces /y/, so /y/ is a close front vowel like /i/ that differs in rounding, and it does not use the back tongue position of /u/.
16|A **dot** marks the **center of the tongue body** for each vowel, so vowels can be compared by where that center sits in the mouth. >> The colored outlines trace the whole tongue shape for each vowel, and those shapes differ in more ways than the position of one dot. || Vowels are more complicated than one fixed tongue position, because some vowels are very **dynamic**. >> The **diphthongs** in "boat" and "bait" change while they are being produced, so each is a movement through time and not one held position. || The whole vocal tract contributes to how a vowel is produced, so a single point is a useful reference and still an incomplete description of the vowel.
17|Vowels are very noticeably different across **dialects**, even within American English. || A speaker of the mid-Atlantic dialect, more specifically from the outer boroughs of New York City, distinguishes between the two vowels in the lower right corner of the vowel chart. >> Those two vowels are the rounded back vowel ɔ and the low back vowel ɑ. >> For that speaker "caught" and "cot" have different vowels, so they are **not homophones**, meaning they do not sound the same. || Most people from other parts of the country do not make this distinction, so for them "caught" and "cot" sound alike. >> A vowel inventory belongs to a particular dialect, and the difference is a systematic dialect pattern and not a pronunciation mistake.
18|A listener understands people who say "Aaron" and "Erin" with the **same vowel**, even when the listener's own dialect keeps those two vowels apart. >> Other listeners can hear a speaker produce the two different vowels for some time and only notice the difference once attention is drawn to it. || Both facts are a first encounter with the **lack of invariance problem**, which is that one speech category has different acoustic forms across speakers and contexts, so no single fixed acoustic value identifies it. >> When vowels are plotted by the frequency of the first formant and the frequency of the second formant, each vowel spreads over a wide region and neighboring vowels overlap. || Listeners still recover a stable interpretation from this variable signal, and how they do so is a problem that comes up again and again in the study of speech.
19|**Minimal pairs** are important for establishing **contrast**. >> A minimal pair is two different words whose difference in meaning depends on a difference in one speech sound in the same context. || Two **phones** are **contrastive** when a minimal pair can be found in which the difference between them results in a **difference in meaning**, and contrastive phones are examples of **different phonemes**. || Establishing that a speaker really has two different low back vowels required finding two words, "caught" and "cot", whose distinction depends on the difference between those vowels. >> The surrounding consonants are the same in both words, so the vowel carries the contrast. >> For a speaker who merges the two vowels, this pair does not establish two vowel phonemes.
20|Minimal pairs also give evidence that **phonemic contrasts depend on a particular feature**. || In English the pairs /b/ and /p/, /t/ and /d/, and /k/ and /g/ all differ on **voicing**. >> Each pair keeps place and stop manner the same and changes the voicing category, so the same feature makes the contrast at the bilabial, alveolar and velar places. || This is known from comparing **minimal pairs in each language**, so the evidence has to come from the language being analyzed. >> Two phones existing physically is not enough, because a sound difference is a phonemic contrast only where it separates words in that language.
21|There is a great deal of **variability** in how phonemes are realized, and some of that variability is very **predictable**. || The different phones that are different realizations of the **same phoneme** are called **allophones**. >> A speaker produces /p/ differently depending on its position and the neighboring sounds, without changing which word is said. || Recording the phrases "pot that", "spot that" and "stop that" in Praat puts /p/ in three environments, which are the start of the word, the position after /s/, and the end of the word. >> The release and aspiration of /p/ can then be compared across the three phrases while the consonant stays a realization of /p/.
22|A recording of "pot that", "spot that" and "stop that" makes the **allophones of /p/** observable in time. >> The timing of the closure, the release, the aspiration and the following voiced sound can differ across the three contexts, and all three words still contain the same phoneme /p/. || The upper display is a **waveform**, which gives the amplitude of the sound signal over time. || The lower display is a **spectrogram**, which gives the distribution of acoustic energy over time and frequency, with time running left to right and frequency running upward. >> The two displays line up in time, so an abrupt release or a quiet interval can be matched from one to the other.
23|The word "stop" is sometimes said as [stɑpʰ], with a released and aspirated /p/, often when the word is stressed or when someone is trying to speak clearly in **citation form**. || There is **no difference in meaning** between [stɑp̚] and [stɑpʰ], and no minimal pair can be found for these two phones in these phonological contexts in English. >> The two phones are called **allophones** for that reason, where allo means different, so they are different phones for the same phoneme. >> The small mark in [p̚] means the stop is unreleased, and the superscript h in [pʰ] means it is aspirated. || These forms also **alternate in different morphological forms**, such as "stop", "stops", "stopped" and "stopping". >> Adding material after the /p/ changes its environment, so whether the /p/ is released and aspirated depends on the form in which the word occurs.
24|The word **voicing** is used in two different ways, which can be confusing. || In **narrow transcription**, which describes specific **phones**, a sound is called voiced if the vocal folds vibrate during the whole gesture or sound. || In **broad transcription**, which describes **phonemes**, voicing is a contrast that is **realized in a number of different ways**. >> One way is the presence or absence of aspiration at the beginnings of words, and another is the relative duration of voicing to closure in "writer" and "rider". >> A phoneme can be called voiced without vocal-fold vibration through its whole closure, so it matters whether a statement is about a phone or about a phoneme.
25|**Phonotactics** is, roughly, which phonemes can occur together in syllable structures. || This is clearest with **clusters** that cannot occur in English words. >> English speakers say the name Sbarro as [spɑroʊ] or [zbɑroʊ] but never [sbɑroʊ], whatever they think they are saying. || When forced to deal with clusters that English phonotactics does not allow, as in foreign names like Zbigniew (Polish) or Gbagbo (Kru), English speakers tend to **insert vowels** and say [zɨ’bIg.nʲu] or [gə.bɑ.gə.boʊ]. >> The inserted vowels break the cluster into extra syllables, so the name fits English syllable patterns. >> Rules about syllable structure turn out to be complex, and they seem to shape speech production.
26|**Phonemes are not letters**, and they are not even specific sounds or gestures. >> A **phoneme** is the smallest phonetic unit in a language that can convey a **distinction in meaning**, so it is defined by the contrasts it makes. >> One phoneme can have several allophones and still be one category, which is part of why this is complicated in practice. || The **International Phonetic Alphabet** gives speech scientists a way of communicating about the similarities and differences between sounds across languages. || It is easy to think of the IPA as an **alphabet of phonemes**, but that would be a mistake because phonemes are not letters. >> A letter belongs to a writing system, a phone is one produced speech sound, and a phoneme is a category that separates meanings.
27|Literate people easily imagine that speech is produced as an **orderly stream of letter-like units**, but speech is nothing like that. || A **spectrogram** of the sentence "A sore back is a real pain" has frequency patterns that change continuously over time, with transitions connecting neighboring sounds. >> Time runs from left to right, frequency increases upward, and darker regions have stronger acoustic energy. >> The phonetic labels and boundary markers above it are annotations, so they organize the signal without cutting it into separate pieces. || This is why **phonemes are not letters**, because a transcription is a row of discrete symbols while the sound it describes is one **continuous** signal in which neighboring sounds overlap.
28|The **articulators** in action are the **lips, jaw, tongue** and other structures of the vocal tract, and they move in a coordinated, continuous way during speech. >> The tongue changes the shape of the space inside the mouth, while the lips and jaw change its opening, so together they shape the outgoing sound. || A side view of the vocal tract from USC SPAN locates the tongue inside the mouth and the passage leading toward the throat. || Because the movements run together as one ongoing sequence, a speech gesture is part of a **moving system** and is not one fixed posture repeated unchanged for every occurrence of a phoneme.
29|**Hockett** gave an analogy for why hearing speech is not reading off a row of separate units. >> He imagined a row of unboiled Easter eggs of various sizes and colors on a moving belt, which carries them between the two rollers of a wringer that smashes them and rubs them into each other. || The row of eggs before the wringer stands for the **series of impulses from the phoneme source**, and the mess that comes out stands for the **output of the speech transmitter**. || An inspector examines the passing mess and decides, from the broken and unbroken yolks, the spread-out albumen and the colored bits of shell, which eggs arrived at the wringer. >> The **inspector stands for the hearer**, who only has to **identify** the eggs and does not have to put them together again, which would be physically impossible.
30|Some people have started to question whether **phonemes are "real"**, either **psychologically** or **linguistically**. >> A phonemic description is useful for stating contrasts, but that does not settle how a speaker stores words or how a listener recognizes speech. || **Lotto and Holt** wrote about the illusion of the phoneme, and **Beckman and Edwards** wrote about how phonological categories develop and the primacy of lexical learning. >> **Port** asked how words are stored in memory and looked beyond phones and phonemes, and **Goldinger and Azuma** wrote about speech perception. || What these works share is the question of whether phoneme-sized units are what the mind actually uses in learning, memory and perception.
