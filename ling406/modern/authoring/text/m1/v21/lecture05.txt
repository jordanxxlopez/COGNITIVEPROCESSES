2|**Phrenology** was an early attempt to relate brain structure to function by reading mental abilities from the shape of the skull. >> A simplified phrenological chart assigns regions of the head to language, memory, thinking, love and other categories. || Its reasoning rests on three premises, that **different parts of the brain** are responsible for different processes, that **aptitude** is related to differences in brain region size, and that **brain region size influences skull shape**. Careful measurements of people's skulls should then reveal relationships between skull shape and various brain functions. || No one believes in phrenology anymore, yet the premises are not equally wrong. The first premise, that brain parts differ in what they do, is separate from the claim about skulls and stays a serious idea, while the third fails because **skull shape is not a sensitive measure of brain shape**. >> The second premise would require a dependable link from an ability to the size of a region before any skull measurement could support a psychological claim.
3|**Phrenology** failed for two important reasons, one on the side of anatomy and one on the side of psychology. || The first problem is **inappropriate anatomical measurement**, because skull shape is not a sensitive measure of brain shape, so measuring the head cannot identify a brain mechanism. || The second problem is an **unconstrained definition of functions**, because broad social labels were treated as if they already named one mental function that could be assigned to a region. >> Studies of prisoners aimed to identify regions associated with "criminality", and studies of women with different numbers of children aimed to find the locus of "philopropogenitiveness". >> The criticism is about the evidence linking those labels to regions of the head, so it leaves open the possibility that brain regions differ in what they contribute.
4|**Flourens** was an early and vociferous critic of phrenology who tested brain function with **experimental lesions**, which means damaging or removing brain tissue and then observing how behavior changes. >> He worked mainly with pigeons and rabbits, and he also wrote an anti-phrenology pamphlet. || His experiments established **distinct functions** for the cerebral cortex, the cerebellum and mid-brain structures, so he did find broad differences among large parts of the brain. || Within the cortex, however, he **failed to find any correspondence** between specific cortical regions and specific senses. >> He insisted instead that the **size of the lesion** was critical, because when one sense was lost, all were lost together, as when most of the cortex is removed and sight and hearing disappear together. >> His lesion evidence argued against a precise map of sensory centers, so it supported a more general account of cortical function.
5|The neurologist **Pierre Paul Broca** presented the case of "Tan", a man who had lost the ability to speak after a brain injury decades earlier. >> He gave the presentation to the newly established Society of Anthropology, of which he was also the founding president. >> This specific loss of language is called **aphasia**, and Tan was not the first observed case of a specific loss. || Tan **could only produce one jargon syllable**, yet he seemed otherwise to be "in possession of his senses" and seemed to **understand what was being said**. || Because speaking was severely impaired while understanding seemed preserved, the case suggests that **producing speech and understanding speech can be affected separately** by brain damage, so the loss was not a loss of intelligence or of all language ability.
6|Tan's brain, kept on display at the Musee Dupuytren, has a **large lesion** that definitely includes the third convolution of the frontal lobe, also called the **inferior frontal gyrus**. || That link between the injury and the lost speech gives us **Broca's area**, a portion of the left frontal lobe that is important for speech. >> The lesion was extensive, so the injury was not a sharply isolated speech component. || **Hughlings Jackson** raised a criticism in the same period, "to locate the damage which destroys speech and to locate speech are two different things". >> Damage can interrupt a process through the region itself or through its connections, so the lost ability tells us the tissue matters for speech without telling us everything the intact system does.
7|German neurologists of the later nineteenth century, among them **Lichtheim** and **Wernicke**, began making more systematic observations of aphasia. || They ascribed specific losses of function to **disconnections between centers**, so a pathway between centers is part of the explanation along with the centers themselves. >> In Lichtheim's "House" model, **A** is the center for sound images, **M** is the center for movement images and **B** is the center for concepts, while lowercase a is the acoustic pathway and lowercase m is the motor pathway. >> A loss of the ability to understand spoken words might be due to a disconnection between A and B, and a loss of verbatim repetition may be due to a disconnection between A and M. Each marked break in the model stands for a different form of aphasia. || Describing centers for specific functions is an ancestor of the later idea of **modularity**.
8|**Sigmund Freud**, in his early neurological research, critiqued the "diagram-drawing" school of aphasia research. >> He felt it was naive to expect the brain to be organized according to psychological categories like "word forms" or "concepts". >> He proposed instead that language emerged from **fields** connecting perceptual and motor abilities to "drives". || His position is a **holist critique**, and it marks a durable tension in cognitive neuroscience about whether brain area functions and cognitive functions can be directly related. || A psychological category can be useful for describing behavior, but it has to be connected to brain mechanisms **through evidence**, because the brain may not be organized according to our descriptions of cognitive phenomena.
9|**Dronkers and colleagues** used **MRI** to assess the extent and location of the damage in Broca's landmark cases very precisely, including the brain of Leborgne, the patient called Tan. || The lesion is **very deep** and includes the **insula**, and there is also a lot of white matter damage to the **superior longitudinal fasciculus**, which connects the front and back of the cerebral cortex. >> The cases involved disrupted connections as well as cortical damage, which an inspection of the surface alone would not reveal. || Other work by this group suggests that the **insula, not Broca's area**, is strongly associated with **speech apraxia**, the condition Broca described in his patients. >> The historical case changes from a small surface speech center to a much more extensive pattern of injury, so the deficit cannot be assigned to the surface region alone.
10|We are still talking about Broca about a century and a half later even though both sides of his case were crude. || His **characterization of the behavior was crude**, because linguistics at the time was focused on historical issues, such as how languages were related to each other and how to reconstruct ancient languages. >> Experimental psychology had not really been invented yet, so observations of behavior were necessarily informal. || His **anatomical localization was also crude, and probably wrong**, because fine-grained anatomical descriptions of the cerebral cortex were still in their infancy. >> Brodmann's map, which divides the cortex into more detailed regions, was published only in the early twentieth century. >> His lasting importance does not depend on his anatomical conclusion being a complete account of speech, because the case set up a question that later methods could study more carefully.
11|Other people, such as the **Daxes** and **Wernicke**, were reporting similar things at around the same time as Broca. >> Wernicke's data are, if anything, more interesting, because his patient was **fluent but produced meaningless gibberish**, which contrasts sharply with Tan's restricted speech. >> Contrasting cases like these encourage separating the components of language instead of treating all abnormal speech as the same loss. || For a variety of reasons, though, Broca's presentation of Tan is seen as a **watershed moment** in neuropsychology generally. >> It turned the project of **relating specific functions to specific brain regions** into a respectable pursuit, which is why Broca is still discussed today.

13|**Cognitive neuropsychology** depends on cognitive psychology for its definitions of functions, and much of what was learned about structure-function relationships in the twentieth century came from experimental observations of individuals with brain damage. || Theory about cognitive processes supplies hypotheses about which operations might be separable, such as whether the ability to recognize the **abstract forms of words** can be **dissociated** from the ability to compute their meaning. A dissociation would mean that one of the two operations is impaired more than the other. >> Reaction time studies with normal subjects argue for different internal codes, because matching letters by **physical identity** is fastest, matching by **name identity** is slower, and matching by **rule identity**, such as both vowels or both consonants, is slowest. >> The same ordering holds when pairs of words are classified as both animals or both plants. || **Quantitative measurements** allow statistical comparison, which is critical because deficits are **rarely complete**.
14|**Anatomical imaging** permits high-resolution location of brain injuries in living patients, so scientists no longer had to wait for patients to "come to autopsy" before determining the site of a lesion. >> A scan of HM's middle temporal lobes locates his damage, which was the result of surgery. >> Such a scan identifies an injury and its extent, and it does not display activity during a language task. || The warning from **Hughlings Jackson** still applies, "To locate the damage which destroys speech and to locate speech are two different things". >> Studies of brain-injured patients tell us what **behavior looks like after a region is damaged**, so they can tell us only **indirectly** what the region might do when intact.
15|**Metabolic neuroimaging** allows us to study brains in action, and **Posner and colleagues** describe PET studies that used radio-labeled oxygen as a marker for activity. || Techniques like **PET** and **fMRI** are **metabolic**, so they do not record neural activity itself and instead give an index of oxygen consumption. || They are also **slow**, because metabolic processes unfold over seconds while important neural events can occur on the order of milliseconds or less. >> They have only **fair spatial resolution**, because their resolution is on the order of millimeters while important things are going on at the micron scale, although fMRI is better than PET. >> A colored activation image is a measured metabolic signal, so it is not a direct view of thoughts or of single neurons firing.
16|The logic of cognitive psychology has to be applied to metabolic imaging data, because one cannot just say "x part of the brain is active during y task" when there is activity all over the brain all of the time. || A **control condition** is required for that reason, and **subtraction logic** takes the activity in the control condition away from the activity in the task condition, so what remains goes with whatever differs between the two. >> A scan taken during a motor task minus a scan taken at rest leaves a much smaller region than the widespread activity in either scan alone. || **Posner and colleagues** applied this logic to isolate identification of abstract word and letter forms from **semantic processing** of word meaning. >> Tasks can share visual input and response demands while differing in whether word meaning must be accessed, so the leftover activity goes with that one processing demand. >> Monitoring words for dangerous animals minus passively viewing words is one such comparison.
17|**Posner and colleagues** found an area in the **left inferior prefrontal cortex** that was active in all three of their semantic processing conditions. || Each condition was a subtraction, so monitoring visual words for dangerous animals was compared with passively viewing words, and **generating uses** for words was compared with repeating them, once for visual words and once for auditory words. >> Generating a use requires retrieving meaning beyond what repeating the word requires, so the remaining activity goes with **semantic processing**. >> The area was active whether the words were seen or heard, and each condition also activated the anterior cingulate gyrus. >> The colors mark the relative strength of activation, from purple at the minimum to white at the maximum for that condition. || Interestingly, the region tied to semantic processing of words **corresponds roughly to Broca's area**.
18|Whether modern brain imaging is better than **phrenology** depends on the reasoning behind a localization claim and not on how attractive the image of an active region looks. || Phrenology had a poor anatomical measure and loosely defined functions, so a modern localization claim is better only when it has **valid measurements** and a **clear definition of the operation** being tested. >> An image of an active region does not supply those definitions by itself. || **Controlled task contrasts** give imaging studies a different kind of evidence from assigning broad traits to skull regions. >> The question to ask of any study is whether the contrast isolates the proposed operation and whether the measurement supports relating that operation to the observed region.
19|**Posner and colleagues** speak to the comparison with phrenology in their conclusions, where they claim localization of **component operations** and not of whole cognitive tasks. || The PET data provide strong support for localization of operations performed on **visual, phonological and semantic codes**, and the PET data on lexical access complement the lesion data. >> Studies of average blood flow could localize these operations, which suggests considerable homogeneity in the neural systems involved, at least among the right-handed subjects with good reading skills who were studied. || This form of localization differs from the idea that a cognitive task is performed by a particular brain area. >> Visual imagery, word reading and shifting visual attention are **not performed by any single brain area**, because each involves a large number of component computations that must be orchestrated.
20|Language has turned up at a number of key moments in debates about **localization of function**. >> Broca's presentation of Tan helped establish the neuropsychological approach of **lesion-behavior analysis**. >> Some of the very first PET and fMRI studies used activation of "Broca's Area" in language tasks as evidence that cognitive functions could be localized. || Language suits these investigations because it offers **distinguishable abilities** that can be compared in behavior and in the brain. >> Restricted speech production, apparent comprehension, repetition and semantic judgments place different demands on processing, so an injury can affect one ability more than another. >> Language tasks can also be broken into questions about forms, meanings and responses, which gives researchers a basis for choosing control conditions.
