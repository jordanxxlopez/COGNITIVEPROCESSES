2|**Oldfield and Wingfield** asked people to name pictures of objects, a task called **picture naming**, and timed how long each name took. >> The example line drawings come from **Snodgrass and Vanderwart**, a later set of drawings, and not from the original study. || They found a **log-linear relationship** between **word frequency** and **response time**, so more frequent names were produced faster, and the relation is close to a straight line when frequency is put on a logarithmic scale. || The catch is that **frequency is related to word length**, even in a tiny sample of names, because frequent words tend to be shorter. >> So frequency and length change together and either one could explain the faster naming, which makes length a **confound** (another variable that changes along with the one being tested).
3|A **lexical database** gathers information about many words in one place so the words can be searched and compared. || The **English Lexicon Project** gives the properties of words along with behavioral data from **naming** and **lexical decision** tasks, so researchers can measure their candidate words instead of trusting their intuitions about which words are common or similar. >> It has separate links for generating word and nonword lists and for getting trial-level naming and lexical decision data. || Measuring the words before choosing them makes **correlations among word properties** visible, such as the link between frequency and length, before those correlations end up in the conditions.
4|The word-query tools give two ways to get lexical information about words. || A researcher can **generate a list of items with specific lexical characteristics**, which helps when a study needs words within particular ranges of frequency or length. || A researcher can also **submit a list of items to retrieve their lexical characteristics**, which helps when the words were already chosen for another reason, such as belonging to one semantic category. >> In both cases the measurements can reveal **unintended differences between conditions**, so they can be fixed before the study is run.
5|A database query separates the **fields to retrieve** from the **constraints** that decide which words enter the list. || **Length** and **frequency** can both be requested as output fields, while **minimum and maximum values** on a frequency measure restrict the range of words that come back. >> Getting both fields back lets the researcher check whether length changes along with frequency in the candidate words. || For a frequency comparison, the goal is items that **differ in frequency** while a likely confound such as **length** stays controlled. >> The database only supplies candidates, so the researcher still has to decide whether more selection is needed.
6|The two hundred most frequent words in an old corpus were sorted into ten **bins** of twenty words each, from most to least frequent. || The **mean frequency drops off very dramatically** from the most frequent bin to the next and keeps falling, which is **Zipf's law** in action, so a small number of words account for a large share of use. || **Length is inversely related to frequency**, so the most frequent bins hold the shortest words on average. >> Because of this, picking very frequent words automatically picks many short words, and a random set of low-frequency words will differ in length as well as in frequency.
7|**Boxplots** compare the lengths of the four hundred sixty-seven most frequent words, the **high-frequency (HF)** words, with the same number of **low-frequency (LF)** words selected at random. >> A boxplot gives the spread of a whole set, with the **median** marking the middle value and the box covering the middle half. || **Fewer than a quarter of HF words are as long as the median LF word**, so HF words are shorter across the whole set and not because of a few odd words. || Picking LF words at random keeps the natural link between frequency and length, so random sampling does not remove the **length confound**.
8|The problem is that **HF words are generally shorter than LF words**, so choosing LF words at random will always give a length difference. || The solution is to **not select LF words at random**. >> Choosing **long-ish HF words** and **short-ish LF words** lets the two sets **match on length** while still **differing in frequency**. || Matching changes only the experimental sample, because frequency and length stay related in the language itself, and it lets the frequency difference be tested with length held similar.
9|**Oldfield and Wingfield** eventually **matched their high- and low-frequency words for length**. || They still found a **robust frequency effect**, so they concluded that **frequency effects are not merely length effects in disguise**. >> If length were the whole cause, matching length should have removed the naming difference, and because the difference stayed, frequency matters beyond length. || A large share of psycholinguistics is an attempt to establish that an observed effect is **not just an effect of some other confounding variable**.
10|**Cutler** described the problem in the title "Making up materials is a confounded nuisance," because good stimuli have to be **matched on many dimensions at once**. || **Cortese and Simpson** did a very careful matching job, with regular and irregular words matched on properties such as frequency, familiarity, imageability, length and phonological or orthographic measures. || Such fine-grained matching also has costs. >> The items may be **odd** or have inappropriate statistics, because the frequency and length confound is a real fact about English and most languages, so avoiding it creates a somewhat **artificial stimulus environment**. >> Matching can also leave **very few items**, such as the ten words per cell in the study by Cortese and Simpson, which is small for a word naming experiment.
11|Most interesting effects in psycholinguistics happen **below the level of conscious awareness**, and that shapes **task design**. || A person who has not studied psycholinguistics would probably not think about **word frequency** or **length** while naming line drawings, so psycholinguists do not have to be as crafty in design as researchers who study racial stereotyping or altruism. || In **norming studies**, researchers routinely just ask people to **rate words** on some dimension and hope for the best. >> Norming studies can still include **fillers**, which are extra items mixed in among the critical ones to shape the task context.
12|Many tasks measure things that participants **do not consciously control**, and the tools range from the nineteenth-century **tachistoscope**, which controls brief presentation of stimuli, to eye-tracking, EEG and fMRI. || **Eye movements**, recorded with **eye-tracking**, can be very revealing, as in the speech production studies that follow. >> **Reaction time** and **accuracy** are also unlikely to be under direct control in most experiments. || **EEG** and **fMRI** both measure **brain activity**, which is useful mainly because it is a **dependent variable** (the measured outcome) that participants usually do not control directly. >> If different kinds of stimuli bring different brain responses even with **no overt task**, the stimuli are likely different to the participant.

14|**Belke, Meyer and Damian** used **cyclic semantic blocking** to study **picture naming**, in which participants named the same sets of objects again and again. || In **homogeneous** sets the names were semantically related, and in **heterogeneous** sets they were unrelated, and naming was **slower in related sets** once the objects had already been named once. || A later experiment showed the objects all at once and found blocking in **naming latency** and **gaze duration**, but no significant blocking in the lag between the eyes leaving an object and the start of its name. >> A further experiment found that the cost **generalized to new names** from the same categories.
15|The **WEAVER++** model from **Roelofs** splits word production into **conceptual**, **syntactic** and **word-form** levels, and it focuses on **activation for single words**. || Because of that focus, it does not line up exactly with the more general production model, which has message, functional, positional, phonological encoding and articulation levels. >> In WEAVER++ the syntactic information is not positional, because it covers single-word features such as **number** and **gender**. || The conceptual level and the **lemma** level (the stored word candidates) matter most for semantic blocking, because activation from a concept reaches the word candidates there, before their sounds are planned.
16|A naive view of **spreading activation** predicts that a whole block of similar pictures should make naming **faster**, because activating one concept helps activate related ones. >> Duck connects through shared features such as animal, bird, swim and scales to fish, snake and mouse. || The model instead helps explain how spreading activation can make naming **slower** in some cases. >> A related set activates the target but also activates its **competitors**, and naming requires choosing just one candidate, so the extra activation can make the choice harder.
17|Speech production requires a careful distinction between **activation** and **selection**. || Spreading activation from duck to fish means that activating one helps activate the other, but this causes trouble when the task is to **select the correct lemma** from many active ones. || **WEAVER++** assumes that **selection time** depends on the **ratio** of the activity of the most active lemma to the **overall activation of all lemmas**. >> Feedback from the category level adds **collateral activation** to other items in the same category, so the target is a smaller share of the total and **lemma selection slows down**.
18|**Belke and colleagues** ran the experiment for eight **cycles**, and every stimulus was presented in each cycle. || **Homogeneous (HOM)** blocks held related items such as "duck", "mouse", "fish" and "snake", while **heterogeneous (HET)** blocks held unrelated items such as "duck", "saw", "chair" and "bus". || The same stimulus, such as "duck", appeared in HOM and HET blocks in **alternating cycles**, so each item could be compared across a related and an unrelated context. >> An item counts as HOM or HET only because of the other items named with it.
19|**Two effects** happen at the same time in the naming times. || A big **identity priming effect**, which is faster naming of an item that was named before, makes naming much faster after the first cycle in both HOM and HET blocks. || A **semantic blocking effect** makes naming **slower in HOM than in HET** blocks once the objects have been named. >> The two do not conflict, because priming is a change across repeated encounters with an item, while blocking is a difference between related and unrelated contexts at the same point.
20|One competing explanation is that the **blocking effect arises at the level of visual object recognition**, because objects in the same category might look alike. || The first response is that the stimuli were **normed** so that objects in the same category were **not more visually similar** on average than objects in different categories. || The second response is a **control experiment** in which **visual similarity** was blocked instead, and it gave **different results**, because that blocking effect appeared in the **first block of trials**. >> The semantic effect appears only after the first cycle, so the effects in the first experiment are **not likely due to visual similarity**.
21|A study by **Hermens and colleagues** showed people several objects **simultaneously** and found no effect, which raises the worry that blocking depends on seeing objects **one at a time**. || **Belke and colleagues** tested this by having participants view **displays of four items at once** and produce their names **in serial**, one after another. >> A display of duck, snake, fish and mouse is homogeneous, because all four are animals. || This keeps seeing simultaneous while keeping speaking serial, so it tests whether semantic competition remains when the next objects are already in view.
22|**Belke and colleagues** **measured eye movements** while participants named the four objects in order. || **Eye tracking** records how long the eyes stay on each object, the **gaze duration** (GD), and the onset of articulation (Art) marks when each name begins. >> The eyes move on to the next object before the name of the previous one is spoken, so looking runs ahead of speaking. || This divides each naming delay into separate parts, so each part can be compared across HOM and HET displays.
23|There are two key **dependent variables** in the eye-tracking experiment. || **Gaze duration** is how long the eyes stay on an object, and there is evidence that **lemma selection is coordinated with eye movements**. || **Lag** is the time between the eyes leaving an object and the start of its name. >> Later processes such as **phonological encoding** (planning the sounds of the word) and **gesture planning** (planning the speech movements) can happen **without visual support** once the eyes have moved on. >> So a semantic effect on gaze duration points to selection, while an effect on lag would point to these later processes.
24|**Griffin, Bock and colleagues** made demonstrations of **eye-mouth coordination**, which follows where a speaker looks while describing an event. || One example is correctly naming the **patient** in "The horse is kicking a cow." >> The horse is the **agent** (the one doing the action), and the cow is the **patient** (the one the action affects). || Lining up the eye record with the spoken words reveals when information about each participant in the event becomes relevant to the sentence.
25|In the eye-tracking experiment, **overall speech duration decreases** over the course of the experiment but has **no blocking effect**. || **Gaze duration has a blocking effect**, with longer looking in HOM displays, but **only after the first cycle**. || There is **no significant effect on lag**. >> Numerically the blocking effect on lag seems to go in the **opposite direction**, but this difference is **not significant**, so the semantic cost appears clearly only in gaze duration.
26|Each measure is linked to a production stage, so **speech duration** reflects the **gesture level**, **gaze duration** reflects **lemma selection**, and **lag** reflects **phonological encoding**. || Only gaze duration has a blocking effect after the first cycle, so the result supports a **lemma-selection account**, in which related lemmas compete longer in HOM sets. || Speech duration and lag have no significant blocking effect, so they give no matching evidence for a blocking cost at the gesture level or in phonological encoding.
27|**Experiment three** asked whether **blocking generalizes to new items** in the same category. || New items were introduced in the **fifth cycle**, and the **repetition priming effect went away** there, so overall naming **slowed down dramatically**. >> Even so, the **blocking effect was still there**, with HOM slower than HET. || Comparing the new items from a blocked category with the items in the **first cycle** helps separate experience with a category from having named one particular object before. >> The first cycle has no blocking, while new items from an already blocked category do, so the effect is tied to the category and not to repeating specific items.
28|**Belke and colleagues** isolate the blocking effect in the model by ruling out three other sources. || It is **not visual recognition**, because of the stimulus norming and the visual-similarity control experiment. || It is **not phonological encoding**, because the words are not similar in sound, and it is **not item-specific**, because it **generalizes to new items**. >> What remains is **lemma selection** among semantically activated competitors.
29|The **visual recognition** explanation is ruled out by **normed and matched stimuli** and by the **control experiment** on visual similarity. || **Phonological encoding** is ruled out more tentatively, because the words are **not phonologically similar** and the effect is in **gaze duration, not eye-speech lag**. >> This one carries a question mark, because the timing measures support an interpretation of a hidden stage and do not observe that stage directly. || The effect is **not just a specific effect of repeating lists**, because it **generalizes to new items** from the same categories.
30|A good way to get an idea for an experiment is to ask whether an effect "couldn't just be" something else. || For **stimulus manipulations**, many features of words are **correlated with frequency**, including **length**, **age of acquisition**, **concreteness or imageability** and **contextual diversity**. >> So frequency effects might be just a **catch-all** for these other properties. || For **experimental manipulations**, even the simple blocking study involves several processes, such as **object recognition** and **response selection**, and each one could explain an effect. >> A design then tests each alternative by matching it, manipulating it directly, or adding a new measure.
