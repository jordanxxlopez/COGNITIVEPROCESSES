4|Consonants are organized by three separate features, and each one answers a different question about how the sound is made. || **Place of articulation** is where the vocal tract is narrowed or closed, running from the front of the vocal tract to the back across the columns of the IPA consonant chart. >> **Manner of articulation** is the degree of closure, running from closed to open down the rows, so a **plosive** closes completely while a **fricative** leaves a narrow passage. || **Voicing** is whether the vocal folds start vibrating before or during the closure or only after it. >> Voiceless and voiced sounds sit in pairs at the same place and manner, with the voiced one on the right, so two sounds can share two features and differ only in the third.
5|/p/ and /b/ are both **plosive** and both **bilabial**, so they have the same manner and place of articulation. || They differ in **voicing**, which is the timing of **phonation**, the vibration of the vocal folds, relative to the release of the lip closure. >> So /p/ is marked as minus voiced and /b/ as plus voiced, and features describe their similarity and their difference in a precise, well-defined way. || The same voicing difference separates /t/ from /d/, so we can talk in general about **the voiced stops** /b/, /d/, /g/ and so on, which is helpful for **phonological descriptions**.
6|A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in only one sound, where that single difference changes the meaning. Having two different low back vowels was established by finding two words whose distinction depended only on those vowels, such as caught and cot for a speaker who keeps them apart. || If two **phones** are **contrastive**, meaning there is a minimal pair where swapping them changes the meaning, then they are examples of **different phonemes**. >> The evidence is the change in meaning, not just the fact that two pronunciations sound different.
7|Minimal pairs also give evidence that a phonemic contrast depends on a particular **feature**. In English, /b/ and /p/, /t/ and /d/, and /k/ and /g/ all differ on **voicing**, and this is known from comparing minimal pairs for each of them. >> Each pair keeps the same place and manner and changes only voicing, so one feature is doing the same job at three different places. || This evidence has to come from minimal pairs **in each language**, because two sounds that contrast in one language may not contrast in another.
8|Phonemes are not letters, because speech is not produced as an **orderly stream of letter-like units**, even though literate people find it easy to imagine it that way. || A **spectrogram** of "A sore back is a real pain" plots time from left to right and frequency from bottom to top, with darker areas showing stronger energy. The sounds **blend and overlap** continuously, so there are no clean breaks where one phoneme ends and the next begins. >> A phonemic transcription gives useful categories, but each category does not match a separate block of sound with the same shape in every context.
9|**Hockett** compared speech to a row of unboiled Easter eggs of various sizes and colors carried on a moving belt through a **wringer** that smashes them and rubs them into each other. || The eggs going in stand for the impulses from the **phoneme source**, and the mess coming out stands for the output of the **speech transmitter**, so neighboring sounds smear together. || The **hearer** is an inspector who examines the mess and decides which eggs went in, using the broken yolks, spread albumen and bits of shell. >> The inspector does not have to put the eggs back together, which is impossible, but only has to **identify** them, so hearing speech means identifying the units rather than rebuilding them.

11|**Derivational morphology** is a process that derives new words from existing parts, and those parts are called **morphemes**. || The word morphology is built this way, from morph-, from the Greek for form, plus -ology, roughly the study of, so it means **the study of the forms of words**. >> A similar process gives morpheme, and phoneme is built on phone, another Greek morpheme, for speech. || Asking what to call a unit in text, and what graphology means, pushes the same pattern further, and the joking "oops" hints that a word built from familiar parts does not always mean what its parts suggest.
12|Derivational morphology is **productive, but in limited ways**, both in how widely and in how regularly an affix is applied. The suffix **-hood** added to a noun stem gives a state of being tied to that noun, as in brotherhood, sisterhood and knighthood. >> Even these differ, because two brothers share their brotherhood while each knight has his own knighthood. || Neighborhood is a perfectly good word, but -hood does a different job in it entirely. Friendhood is not used because English says friendship for no apparent reason, and enemyhood is missing, so the idea needs words like enmity or antagonism. >> Several of these examples come from **Liberman and Prince**.
13|**Zero derivation** turns a word into another category without adding any affix, yet it still does **complicated things with meaning**. || Dish, spoon, fork and table are strong associates of the noun knife, but their meanings as verbs are **not similar**. In "She can dish it out but she can't take it", "a forked tongue" and "table the issue", each word carries a conventional meaning that does not follow simply from the object. >> So knowing the noun does not predict what the converted form means, because each use has to be learned. || An expression like "silver spoon" is the kind of figurative meaning studied in **Cognitive Linguistics**.
14|More commonly, derivational morphology **changes the pronunciation** and spelling of a word, for example by adding affixes. In certificate, certification and recertification, each added affix moves the **stress** and changes vowels in the rest of the word. >> This is one reason for "The Chaos" of English spelling, and it is why spelling closer to the spoken form does not necessarily make reading easier. || The noun and verb forms of record and invite differ mainly in **stress**, as in "He did not receive an invite" and "We did not invite him". >> The two forms of entrance also differ in stress, but they are not actually related to each other.
15|**Inflectional morphology** changes the meaning of words in a more **predictable** way than derivation does. It marks **plurals** such as knives, forks and mice, the **third person singular present** such as walks, runs and the verb knifes, and the **past** such as walked, sang and knifed. || **Menn** says it would be silly to have separate dictionary entries for the singular and plural forms of nouns, because they are one word in different grammatical forms. || The sound changes in inflection often have some **irregularity**, as mice and sang show. >> That irregularity matters later for **language learning models** and for kinds of **developmental language impairment**.
16|Inflectional morphology also **depends on the syntax** of the rest of the sentence. In the phrase "two [noun]+pl", whatever noun goes there must be **pluralized** somehow, whether regularly as in forks or irregularly as in knives. || There are **no syntactic processes like this** for even very productive derivational forms. The closest is a formula like "Once you [verb] it, you can un-[verb] it", but that is not really syntax, because no sentence forces a verb into a derived form. >> This difference between required inflection and optional derivation matters for **models of speech production**.

18|A very simple model treats each word as a **node** in a **lexical network**, a unit that can be activated by a specific word. Here the network has three nodes, for lion, tiger and stripes. || When the printed word LION appears, it supplies evidence that activates the lion node. >> The printed input and the node are different levels, because the input is what is seen and the node is the stored word that the system can recognize.
19|Activation in this network **can be partial**, so a node can be somewhat active before its word is recognized. || When the word LION **contrasts poorly with the noise mask**, the evidence for it is weak, so the lion node gets only **weak activation**. >> The system builds toward a decision as evidence emerges from the noise, instead of switching straight from off to on.
20|When the **signal-to-noise ratio** becomes very favorable, the word stands out clearly from the noise, so activation of the lion node becomes **strong**. At that point this simple network has **identified** the word lion. || This is the **perceptual identification task**, in which participants push a button as soon as they can identify the item. >> The button press times identification, so conditions that make identification easier lead to earlier responses.
21|Most models of this kind assume that **activation unfolds over time**, rising after the input arrives and then falling. || One way to explain **frequency effects**, the faster identification of common words, is that experience tunes these **activation dynamics**. Every time you meet a word, it might raise its **baseline activation**, **strengthen incoming connections** so it activates more quickly, or change a **threshold** for recognition. >> This is a very simple form of **learning**. || Different computational models, such as **Rumelhart and McClelland's Interactive Activation model**, simulate frequency effects in different ways.
22|Activation dynamics also explain **repetition priming**, where seeing a word again very shortly after seeing it leads to **faster identification**. || Activation does not rise and fall instantly, and it may **fall more slowly than it rises**. So if the word appears again while activation is still high from the last exposure, its starting level is already **higher than usual**, and it reaches identification sooner.
23|Two factors need something added to the simple network before it can explain them. **Neighborhood effects** depend on how similar a word is to other words, and **associative, semantic or phonological priming** depends on whether you just saw a similar or related word. || A network of isolated nodes can explain changes in one word's own activity, but these effects involve one word changing another, so the model needs **connections between nodes**.
24|**Spreading activation** is one way to represent the associations measured in **association norms**. Words sit in a network where the **connection strength**, or distance, between two nodes is set by how strongly they are associated. || So when you activate lion, some of that activation travels along a **weighted connection** to tiger. >> Tiger is then partly active before it appears, which is how the network explains **semantic priming**.
25|**Balota and Lorch** asked whether activation automatically spreads **beyond directly associated concepts**. They built prime and target pairs related only through a **mediating concept**, such as the prime lion and the target stripes, which connect only through tiger. || In **lexical decision**, where people judge whether the target is a word, directly related pairs like lion and tiger or tiger and stripes sped responses, but lion and stripes **did not help** compared with a neutral or unrelated prime. || In **pronunciation**, where people read the target aloud, both the directly related pairs and pairs like lion and stripes **sped responses**. >> This second benefit is **mediated priming**, the priming of a target through a word that is linked to both the prime and the target. >> They concluded that activation spreads beyond direct associates, and that features of the lexical decision task **masked** the mediated priming effect.
26|Activity flows throughout the network, so activating lion can prime **stripes** even though the two words are **not directly associated**. || Lion activates tiger, and tiger passes some activation on to stripes, so stripes gets a head start through the shared link. This is called **mediated priming**, because the prime reaches the target through a linking word, here tiger. >> A direct association works in one step, while this kind of priming needs activation to travel two steps through the network.
27|In pronunciation, directly related primes gave the fastest responses, primes in the **mediated priming** condition, such as lion before stripes, were a little slower, and neutral and unrelated primes were slowest. >> This held at both **stimulus onset asynchronies**, the short and the longer delay between the start of the prime and the start of the target. || The benefit through tiger was only a small fraction of a second, so this study is typical of psycholinguistics, with **careful study design** and **small effects**. || The result is also **counterintuitive**, because lion and stripes seem unrelated. Even so, reading lion first speeds up saying stripes, because both words are associated with tiger.
28|**Belke and colleagues** studied a case where spreading activation **slows** naming down. A naive view of spreading activation predicts that a whole block of similar pictures presented together would lead to **faster naming**, because each picture would pre-activate the others. || In the model, duck, fish, snake and mouse share concepts and features such as animal, bird, swim and scales, and each concept connects to its own **lemma**, the word candidate. >> Activating one animal also activates its competitors, so choosing one lemma among several active ones takes longer, which leads to **slower naming times**.

30|The neurologist **Pierre Paul Broca** presented the case of **Tan**, who had lost the ability to speak after a brain injury decades earlier. He gave the presentation to the newly established Society of Anthropology, where he was also the founding president. || Tan could produce only **one jargon syllable**, yet he seemed otherwise in possession of his senses and seemed to **understand** what was being said. >> This split between lost speaking and kept understanding suggested that producing and understanding speech can be damaged separately, although it was not the first observation of such a specific loss.
31|Tan's brain, on display at the Musee Dupuytren, had a **large lesion** that definitely included the third convolution of the frontal lobe, the **inferior frontal gyrus**. || From this came **Broca's area**, a portion of the left frontal lobe that is important for speech. || **Hughlings Jackson** objected that locating the damage that destroys speech and **locating speech** are two different things. >> Damage can stop speech by disrupting one part of a larger system, so the lesion site alone does not reveal where speech itself happens.
32|In the eighteen-seventies, German neurologists such as **Lichtheim** and **Wernicke** began making more systematic observations of **aphasia**, language loss after brain damage. They ascribed specific losses of function to **disconnections** between **centers**. || In Lichtheim's model, A holds sound images, B holds concepts and M holds movement images. A disconnection between A and B could cause a loss of understanding spoken words, and a disconnection between A and M could cause a loss of **verbatim repetition**. >> Describing centers for specific functions is an ancestor of the later idea of **modularity**.
33|**Freud** criticized the "diagram-drawing" school of aphasia research in his early neurological work. He felt it was **naive** to expect the brain to be organized by psychological categories like word forms or concepts. || He proposed instead that language emerged from **fields** connecting perceptual and motor abilities to **drives**, which makes his view a **holist critique**. || This leaves a durable tension in **cognitive neuroscience** over whether brain area functions and cognitive functions can be directly related. >> A psychological category can be useful without matching one brain area, so whether the brain is organized by our descriptions has to be tested with evidence rather than assumed.
34|**Dronkers and colleagues** used **MRI** to assess the extent and location of damage in Broca's landmark cases very precisely, including the brain of **Leborgne**, the patient known as Tan. || The lesion is very **deep** and includes the **insula**, with a lot of white matter damage to the **superior longitudinal fasciculus**, which connects the front and back of the cerebral cortex. || Other work by this group suggests that the **insula**, not Broca's area, is strongly associated with **speech apraxia**, the condition Broca described in his patients. >> So the damage was far larger than a small surface speech center, which changes how the classic cases should be read.

36|At the **functional level** we select **lemmas** rather than finished words, and the term lemma is borrowed from mathematics, where it is a proposition in an argument. || In the error "Where'd I bring it when I put it in" for the intended "Where'd I put it when I brought it in", the verbs switched places, but the produced words reflect a **tense change** applied later. >> This could not have happened if the word brought had been selected, because brought would have moved unchanged. || So the speaker selected the lemma **BRING**, which carries no tense yet, and it was later expressed as **present tense** in its new position.
37|People so often say the **opposite** of what they mean because **antonyms** are similar at several levels. They are **semantically related** and **strongly associated**, which makes a perfect storm in the **spreading activation** framework. || Antonyms can also take on the **same semantic roles** and belong to the **same word class**, so they can fill the same syntactic roles. >> That means the rest of the speech production system can handle the wrong word without trouble. || So activation spreads easily from a lemma to its antonym, and if the antonym wins, the sentence still comes out grammatical.
38|**Semantic roles**, also called functional roles, describe how each participant relates to the event. In "The plums in the icebox were eaten by me", the plums are the **Undergoer**, in the icebox is the **Location**, and me is the **Agent**. || "I have eaten the plums in the icebox" gives the same roles in a different order, with the Agent first, then the verb, the Undergoer and the Location. >> With this simple event structure, lemmas can be slotted into roles, so TO EAT is the verb, ME is the Agent and PLUM is the Undergoer. || Different orders have **syntactic consequences**, including whether the lemma ME becomes I or me, and that is worked out at the **positional level**.
39|By the end of the **functional level**, the speaker has decided on a **message** to convey, selected the appropriate **lemmas**, and associated those lemmas with **semantic roles**. || That is quite a lot of work, yet the speaker is still nowhere near **putting a clause together**, much less saying it out loud. >> For an eating event, the plan knows EAT, who eats and what is eaten, but not yet whether the sentence is active or passive, how pronouns are expressed, or how it sounds.
40|For "Where'd I bring it when I put it in?", the **functional level** has already supplied two verbs, PUT and BRING, with I or ME as the **Agent** of both and IT as the **Undergoer** of both. || What is still missing is the **temporal relationship** between the verbs, which is how the bringing and the putting relate in time, along with the question structure and the tense of each verb. >> Expressing all of these things syntactically is the job of the **positional level**, which turns the roles into clauses and grammatical positions.
41|Setting aside how the question is formed and how the tenses are worked out, the **positional level** produces a frame like "Where'd I [verb, root form] it when I [verb, past] it in?" >> The first clause takes a lot of syntax, because forming the question needs both **Wh- movement** and an **auxiliary verb**. || The first clause requires the **base form** of the verb and the next clause requires the **past form**. So the positional level is a **syntactic structure with slots** for the lemmas from the functional level, plus the other particles the grammar requires. || The error "Where'd I bring it when I put it in" arises while these **slots are being filled** with lemmas, so each swapped verb takes the form of the slot it lands in.
42|The error "a language needer learns" is similar to the bring and put error, because **two lemmas got switched** as they were fed into the positional level while the **positional level stayed largely the same**. || It differs in the noun, which is actually a **verb plus an affix**, so this error involves **derivational morphology**. >> The noun slot was built as a verb plus -er, so the swapped root need became needer, while the verb slot kept its third person ending and gave learns. >> So the roots moved and the affixes stayed where the structure put them, which makes them **stranded affixes**.
43|"He did that easy enoughly" is similar to "a language needer learns" because both contain a **stranded affix**. Here the affix is **-ly**, which turns an adjective into an adverb. || It differs because something also happened while **setting up the positional level**, not only while filling its slots. >> In the faulty structure the adverb position, built as an adjective plus -ly, comes after the adjective position, so -ly lands on enough and easy is left without its ending.

45|**Oldfield and Wingfield** asked people to **name pictures** of objects and found a **log-linear relationship** between frequency and response time, so naming time dropped steadily as the logarithm of word frequency rose. >> The line drawings come from **Snodgrass and Vanderwart**. || This is a classic cautionary tale, because **frequency is related to word length**, even in this tiny sample. >> Since frequent words tend to be shorter, frequency and length change together, so either one could explain the faster naming, which makes length a **confound**.
46|Taking the two hundred most frequent words in an old corpus and putting them into ten **bins** of twenty words each reveals two patterns. || First, **mean frequency drops off very dramatically** from the most frequent bin to the next most frequent and so on, which is **Zipf's law** in action. || Second, **length is inversely related to frequency**, so the highest-frequency bins hold the shortest words on average. >> That is why choosing very frequent words automatically means choosing many short words.
47|High-frequency words are generally **shorter** than low-frequency words, so a set of low-frequency words selected at random will always end up longer on average. || The solution is to **not select low-frequency words at random**. Choosing longish high-frequency words and shortish low-frequency words lets you **match on length** while still **manipulating frequency**. >> Matching changes only the experimental sample, not the language, and the final groups must still differ in frequency while agreeing in length.
48|**Oldfield and Wingfield** eventually **matched** their high-frequency and low-frequency words **for length** and still found a **robust frequency effect**. || They concluded that frequency effects are **not merely length effects** in disguise, because if length were the whole story, matching it would have removed the difference. || Rather a lot of psycholinguistics is an attempt to prove that some observed effect is **not just an effect of a confounding variable**.

50|**Belke, Meyer and Damian** used **cyclic semantic blocking**, in which participants repeatedly name sets of objects with **semantically related names**, called homogeneous sets, or unrelated names, called heterogeneous sets. Naming is typically **slower in related sets**. >> Their first experiment found the blocking effect only after all objects of a set had been shown and named once. >> Their second showed a set's objects at the same time and found blocking in **naming latencies** and **gaze durations**, but the **gaze-to-speech lag** showed repetition priming and no blocking. >> Their third found that blocking **generalized to new**, previously unnamed items. || They proposed that the blocking effect is due to **refractory behaviour in the semantic system**.
51|In speech production, **activation** and **selection** must be kept carefully apart. Spreading activation from duck to fish means that activating one can help activate the other, but that causes problems when the task is to **select the correct lemma** from a bunch of active ones. || The **WEAVER++** model assumes that **selection time** depends on the **ratio** of the activity of the most active lemma to the overall activation of all lemmas. >> So when feedback from the **category level** adds **collateral activation** to items from the same category, the target's share shrinks and **lemma selection slows down**.
52|In the **cyclic semantic blocking** study by **Belke, Meyer and Damian**, people named the same sets of pictures again and again, either in **homogeneous sets** of related names or **heterogeneous sets** of unrelated names. || Naming took **longer in homogeneous sets**, but only once every object in the set had been named once. >> When all objects appeared together, related sets lengthened both **naming latencies** and **gaze durations**, while the lag between gaze leaving an object and speech starting showed **no blocking**. >> The cost also carried over to **new items** from the same categories. || The authors traced the effect to **refractory behaviour in the semantic system**.
53|Speech production needs a clear line between **activation**, which spreads from duck to fish and other related animals, and **selection**, which picks the one lemma that will be spoken. || In **WEAVER++**, **selection time** is set by the **ratio** of the most active lemma's activity to the total activation of all lemmas. >> **Category-level feedback** gives extra **collateral activation** to every member of the category, so the target stands out less and selecting it **takes longer**. || This is how related words that help each other's activation can still **slow down naming**.
54|The second experiment tracked three measures across presentation cycles for homogeneous and heterogeneous sets. || **Speech duration** decreased over the course of the experiment but showed **no blocking effect**, and it is linked to the **gesture level**. || **Gaze duration** showed a **blocking effect** only **after the first cycle**, with longer gazes in homogeneous sets, and it is linked to **lemma selection**. >> There was **no effect on lag**, which is linked to **phonological encoding**, and the numerical trend in the opposite direction was not significant. >> So the clearest cost falls at lemma selection, where competition among related lemmas happens.
55|The third experiment asked whether **blocking generalizes to new items** in the same category, so new items were introduced in the fifth cycle. || With the new items the **repetition priming** benefit went away and performance **slowed down dramatically**, yet homogeneous sets stayed slower, so **the blocking effect was still there**. >> Comparing the new items from a blocked category to the items in the first cycle separates experience with the category from having named that exact object before. >> In the first cycle homogeneous sets were not slower, yet the new items from an already named category were, so the cost comes from the category and not from the item. || So blocking is **not just a specific effect of repeating lists**, because it carries over to new members of a category.
56|**Belke and colleagues** isolated the blocking effect by ruling out three alternatives. It is **not visual recognition**, because the stimuli were normed and matched and a **control experiment** was run. || It is probably **not phonological encoding**, because the words were **not phonologically similar** and **gaze duration**, not the eye-speech lag, was affected. >> This point carries a question mark because the timing measures support the interpretation without observing the stage directly. || It is **not just a specific effect of repeating lists**, because the effect **generalizes to new items**, which leaves **lemma selection** as the stage where blocking arises.
