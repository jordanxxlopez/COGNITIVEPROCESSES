2|The bottom three levels of the production model are the **positional level**, the **phonological encoding level** and the **speech gestural level**, which carry a planned message down to articulated speech. >> The positional level builds the grammatical structure, phonological encoding plans the string of phonemes, and the gestural level turns that plan into movements. || Speech errors matter here because one error can keep one kind of organization while breaking another, so errors give evidence that **abstract entities** like **phonemes**, **lemmas** (the words chosen at the functional level) and **noun phrases** are useful units. >> Each collected error can then be traced to the level where it most likely happened. || Purely **observational research** on errors also has real weaknesses, and those weaknesses are the reason to study speech production with **experimental methods** next.
3|By the end of the **functional level**, the sentence "Where'd I bring it when I put it in?" already has its two verbs, PUT and BRING, and its **semantic roles**, the jobs each participant plays. >> I/ME is the **Agent** (the one doing the action) of both verbs, and IT is the **Undergoer** (the thing acted on) of both verbs. || The speaker also has to work out the **temporal relationship** between the verbs, meaning how the putting and the bringing line up in time, and that the sentence is a question. || Expressing all of those facts **syntactically** is the job of the **positional level**, so it turns verbs and roles into clauses with grammatical positions.
4|Leaving aside the work of forming the question and choosing tenses, the positional level produces a frame like "Where'd I [verb, root form] it when I [verb, past] it in?" >> Even the first clause takes a lot of syntactic work, because the question needs both **Wh- movement** (moving the question word to the front) and an **auxiliary verb**. || The structure of the first clause requires the **base form** of the lemma in English, while the next clause requires the **past form**. || So the positional level holds a **syntactic structure with slots** for every lemma activated at the functional level, plus the other particles the grammar requires. >> The error "Where'd I bring it when I put it in" can arise while those slots are being **filled with lemmas**, so BRING and PUT end up in each other's slots.
5|In "a language needer learns", the speaker meant to say "a language learner needs", so this is another error worth comparing with the BRING and PUT sentence. || It is similar because **two lemmas**, LEARN and NEED, **got switched** while being fed into the positional level, and the positional structure stayed almost unchanged. || It is different because the noun is really a **verb plus an affix**, so the ending -er stays in the noun slot and the ending -s stays in the verb slot while the two roots trade places.
6|In "a language needer learns", two lemmas got switched as they were fed into the positional level, while the **positional level itself stayed the same**. || The difference from the earlier sentence is in the noun, which is actually a **verb plus an affix**. >> This is **derivational morphology** (adding a piece that builds a new kind of word), so adding -er to the verb learn makes the noun learner. >> The verb carries the third-person singular ending -s, which is **inflectional morphology** because it marks a grammatical requirement on the verb. || Because both endings stay in their positions while the roots move, the error is **not a swap of two whole words**.
7|In "He did that easy enoughly", the speaker meant "easily enough", so the ending -ly, which builds an adverb, landed on enough instead of easy. || This is similar to "a language needer learns" because both errors leave a **stranded affix**, an ending that stays in its position while the wrong root fills that position. || It is different because something also went wrong **while setting up the positional level**, not only while its slots were being filled.
8|Both "He did that easy enoughly" and "a language needer learns" have a **stranded affix**. >> Here the affix is -ly, which you can add to turn an **adjective into an adverb**. || The difference is that something also happened **when setting up the positional level**, so the frame itself was arranged differently. >> The intended phrase puts the adverb position, built from an adjective plus -ly, before the adjective, but the starred structure puts the adverb position after the adjective. >> Because enough fills that adverb position, the speaker produces enoughly and leaves easy without its ending.
9|These errors are evidence that **derivational morphology** is a real process, because **affixes** seem able to **float around** at the positional level. || An alternative view says that **derived forms** are just other stored words related to their roots by rule, as in needy, need and needless or unlearn, learn and learner. >> That lexicon would need no separate representation for affixes, and nobody would have to remember quirky rules for making common words. || But errors create **non-words** like "needer" and "enoughly" **on the fly**, so the errors suggest that lemmas and affixes are **represented separately**. >> They also suggest that affixes have a **slot** at the positional level and that derived words can be made by a **grammatical process**.
10|Adding an affix also changes the **sound form** of the word, so easy /’i.zi/ becomes easily /’i.zIl.i/ and enough /i.’nəf/ becomes enoughly /i.’nəf.li/. || Derivational morphology can involve **complex phonological processes**, as in certificate /sər.ˈtI.fɨ.kɨt/, certification /ˌsər.tɨ.fɨ.ˈkeI.ʃən/ and recertification /ˈri.sər.tɨ.fɨ.ˌkeI.ʃən/, where **vowels change** and **stress moves**. || So when the positional level specifies a process like "add +er" or "make this adjective into an adverb", it **still leaves work to do** for the levels that plan the sounds.
11|**Phonological encoding** takes the specification of lemmas, affixes and syntactic particles and turns it into a **string of phonemes** to produce. || For "easy enoughly" this level seems to have **worked fine**, because the speaker did not say "easy /inəvɪli/" or make any other error in the phoneme sequence. || Errors at this level include "fish grotto" becoming "gish frotto", "Chomsky and Halle" becoming "Chomsky and Challe", and "with this ring I do wed" becoming "with this wing I do red". >> In each case the problem is specifically with the **ordering of phonemes**. >> **Menn** calls these errors **neat**, because they follow the **phonotactic patterns** of English (the allowed sound sequences) and, as far as anyone knows, are clearly produced.
12|At the **speech gestural level**, the sequence of phonemes planned during phonological encoding becomes a series of **gestures** actually carried out by the **motor system**. || Unlike phonological encoding errors, gestural errors are **not necessarily neat**, because they lead to **unclear or imprecise speech**. || **Many errors are ambiguous**, so "ad hoc" becoming "odd hack", with /æd hɑk/ coming out as /ɑd hæk/, could be **metathesis** (two sounds trading places) at the phonological encoding level. >> It could also be a **miscuing of tongue position** at the gestural level, and either route changes the vowels a listener hears.

14|Saying "buyed in" for "bought in" is a **morphological regularization**, because the regular past ending is added to a verb whose past form is irregular. || "arising" coming out as "arriving" changes a consonant inside a very similar word form. || "It's pretty [kloʊz] to here" for "it's pretty close to here" ends the word with a voiced consonant, and "throfly" for "Thoroughly" changes several parts of the sound sequence, so both are **sound-level errors**. >> Calling all four just wrong words would hide the **different levels** where they happened.
15|"Wash my teeth and brush my face" **exchanges the verbs** while the objects stay in place, so the clause structure survives. || "following vowel" becoming "vollowing fowel" **exchanges initial consonants**, and "seat heaters" becoming "heat seaters" moves sounds while the plural ending stays on seaters. >> These errors separate the movement of **whole roots** from the movement of **single sounds**. || "Lana del Rey" becoming "Marina del Rey" brings in a familiar related name, and "For all intensive purposes" puts different words into a familiar expression, alongside the collected error "It's in the stimulus." >> The attempt "speech plath... speech plang" for "speech-language pathology" keeps its **attempts and repairs**, which are part of the evidence.

17|The phrase "you enter a hundred-dollar room and you do yoga" was said about **hot yoga**, so the context is what makes it an error. || In a talk about cost the phrase would be ordinary, but for hot yoga the amount could concern the room's temperature, so dollar is a meaningful word in the **wrong relation to the message**. || The clause stays grammatical and the words come out clearly, so the error points to the **choice of concept and lemma**, not to phoneme ordering or articulation.
18|In "Law and Order SUV", the title letters SVU come out as SUV, so the **same three letters** appear with the last two in a different order. || SUV is a **familiar vehicle abbreviation**, so a competing familiar form may have been selected, rather than every reordering being an isolated motor slip. || The utterance is still easy to pronounce, so the analysis starts from the SVU and SUV pattern and asks which representations hold each sequence.
19|"He cooked his kitchen" was said when the speaker meant chicken. || Chicken and kitchen **share much of their sound structure**, and their first and middle consonants sit in opposite positions. || Both words also relate to **cooking**, so **sound similarity** and **meaning in context** come together in this one error. >> Kitchen still fits the noun slot after his, so the grammatical frame survives and the error lies in selecting and ordering the word form.
20|**Observational studies** of speech errors depend on **what the observer listens for**, and reading about speech production can change those **expectations** over time. || Those expectations can affect **what is heard** and **what is reported**, because someone has to notice a sound, treat it as an error, remember its wording and describe it. || Placing an error in the model also takes facts about the **situation**, such as the intended message, so context is needed to reconstruct how the error happened.
21|**Speech perception** shapes which errors observers report, because people are not very good at hearing sounds **halfway between two phonemes**, especially consonants. >> People are also **biased against phonotactically illegal strings** and **biased toward hearing words**, so a sound partway between "giss" and "kiss" sounds more like "kiss". || This is **theory-dependent observation**, so observers might miss many errors, or whole classes of errors, because of the way perception works. || **Experimental methods** and **careful measurement** can fix some of these problems, but far from all of them.
