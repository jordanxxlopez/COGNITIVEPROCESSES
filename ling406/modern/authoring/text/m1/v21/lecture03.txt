4|In a Scrabble argument, one player says "knifes" is not a word because the plural of the noun knife is "knives", and the other answers that Tony "knifes" Bernardo in West Side Story. || Both players are right, because **a word form depends on its grammatical role**, so "knives" is the plural of the noun while "knifes" is a present-tense form of the verb. >> Making the verb knife from the noun with no added affix is **zero derivation**, a kind of **derivational morphology**, which builds new words. >> Adding the ending for the plural or for the verb is **inflectional morphology**, which marks the form the grammar requires. || The sentence "He knifes him" is a **clause** made of a singular noun subject, a verb and an object noun, so the subject requires the **third person singular** form of the verb.
5|**Derivational morphology** is a process that derives new words from existing parts, and those parts, or units, are called **morphemes**. >> A morpheme is not a syllable or a single speech sound, because what makes it a unit is that it contributes meaning or grammatical information. || The word "morphology" has a transparent derivation, because morph- comes from the Greek for form and -ology, also from Greek, means something like the study of, so together they give **the study of the forms of words**. >> A similar process gives "morpheme", and "phoneme" is built on phone, another morpheme from the Greek, for speech. || By that pattern a unit in text should get a name built the same way, and "graphology" should be the study of such units. That guess goes wrong, because the meaning of a derived word is only partly predictable from its parts.
6|Derivational morphology is **productive**, meaning it can keep making new words, but only in **limited ways**, as trying to make up new words and guess their meanings makes clear. >> The meaning of the final form is **only partially predictable** from its base, so it rests partly on convention. || Even **zero derivation**, which changes the part of speech of a word without changing its form, usually works with a **narrow sense of the base word**. >> Top-ranked image search results for "knife" include different cutting tools and tableware, and a search for "prison shiv" gives an improvised weapon. >> The noun covers a range of objects, but to "knife" somebody names one narrow action, so those objects are not equally natural choices for it even though they resemble one another as cutting tools.
7|Derivational morphology is also limited in **how widely and how regularly affixes are applied**. The suffix **-hood** added to a stem noun gives a state of being associated with that noun, as in "brotherhood" and "sisterhood". >> "Knighthood" is a little odd, because two brothers share their brotherhood, but each knight has his own knighthood. || The pattern has gaps and exceptions, so a recognizable affix does not combine equally well with every sensible base. >> "Neighborhood" is a perfectly good word, but there -hood does a different job entirely. >> The form friendhood is not used because English says friendship instead, for no apparent reason. The form enemyhood maybe ought to be a word, since the only words for that idea are top-shelf ones like enmity or antagonism.
8|**Zero derivation** changes the category of a word without adding a visible affix, yet even it does complicated things with **semantics**, the meaning of the word. || Dish, spoon, fork and table are strong associates of the noun knife, but their meanings as verbs are **not similar** to one another or to the verb knife. >> In "She can dish it out but she can’t take it" and "They decided to table the issue after a day of inconclusive negotiations", the verbs have learned meanings that do not come from naming the object. >> "He was born with a silver spoon and a forked tongue in his mouth" relies on established figurative meanings, the kind studied in **Cognitive Linguistics**. || An unchanged form does not mean an unchanged meaning, because **conventional usage** decides what the converted word communicates in its sentence.
9|More commonly, derivational morphology changes the **pronunciation and spelling** of a word, for instance by adding **affixes**. >> In "certificate" /sər.ˈtI.fɨ.kɨt/, "certification" /ˌsər.tɨ.fɨ.ˈkeI.ʃən/ and "recertification" /ˈri.sər.tɨ.fɨ.ˌkeI.ʃən/, each added affix moves the **stress** and changes vowels in the rest of the word. || Adding an affix can change the **phonology** of the rest of the word, which is one reason for "The Chaos" of English spelling and why higher fidelity to the spoken form does not necessarily improve readability. || Stress is also the major change between the noun and verb forms of "record" and "invite", as in "He did not receive an invite" and "We did not invite him". >> The two words spelled "entrance" also differ in stress, but they are not actually related to each other.
10|**Inflectional morphology** changes the meaning of words in a **more predictable** way than derivation does, because it gives grammatical forms of the same word. >> **Plurals** include "knives" from the noun knife, "two forks" and "a lot of mice". >> The **third person singular present** gives "knifes", "she walks" and "he runs", and the **past** gives "knifed", "walked" and "sang". || Menn puts this succinctly, writing that we would think it silly to have separate dictionary entries for the singular and plural forms of nouns. || The change in meaning is predictable, but the **phonological process is often irregular**, as in "mice" and "sang" beside "forks" and "walked". >> That irregularity matters for different language learning models and for kinds of developmental language impairment.
11|**Inflectional morphology** also depends on the **syntax** of the rest of the sentence, so the grammar around a word can require a particular inflected form. >> In the phrase "two [noun]+pl", whatever noun fills the slot must be pluralized somehow, whether regularly as in "two forks" or irregularly as in "knives". || There are **no syntactic processes like this** for even very productive derivational forms, because no position in a sentence requires a derived word. >> The closest case is a formula like "Once you [verb] it, you can un-[verb] it", but that is **not syntax**, since nothing in the grammar requires every verb to have an un- form.
12|English is **pretty limited in its use of inflection**, and its familiar cases are plurals on nouns and the third person singular present and the past on verbs. >> The forms are few, such as "knives", "knifes" and "knifed" for knife. || Other languages put more grammatical information into the forms of words. >> Many languages conjugate verbs much more elaborately than English for **person** and for **tense**. >> Many languages have different ways of forming the plural for **small and large numbers**. || Many languages also have **case markings** for nouns, which encode information about the syntactic or semantic role of the noun.
13|Both **inflectional** and **derivational morphology** create new phonological forms out of old parts, and both change the meanings of base forms. || **Derivational morphology creates new words**, as when the verb knife is made from the noun. >> It is productive, but not always, and typically in limited ways, so its results can have partly conventional meanings. || **Inflectional morphology changes how words interact syntactically with other words**, as when "knifes" is the form needed after "he". >> It is highly productive but not always regular, and it is **required by syntax**. >> Regularity of sound cannot decide between the two, so the test is whether a change makes a new word or marks the form a sentence needs.
14|Nouns and verbs are kinds of **content words**, and other content words include modifiers such as adjectives and adverbs. || **Function words**, such as determiners and prepositions, organize how content words relate, so they only come into play once more than a couple of words are put together into **phrases, clauses and sentences**. || In "{The video for “More than Words”} came out before {most of you} were born", the curly brackets mark two **noun phrases**. >> The subject is the whole first phrase and not only the noun video. >> The two phrases differ in length, so a phrase is not defined by a fixed number of words.
15|"The video for “More than Words”" is a **noun phrase** (NP) whose structure is a **determiner**, then a **noun**, then a **prepositional phrase**, written Det + N + PP. >> The **function words** here are "The", a determiner that begins the noun phrase, and "for", a preposition. >> The preposition begins the prepositional phrase that allows the song title to specify which video is meant. || The noun video is the head, so the whole phrase can act as one unit in a sentence. || The title “More than Words” is not really part of the sentence but is used like a noun, because without the quotation marks it is only a bunch of words strung together in a row and not a phrase.
16|The description Det + N + PP for a noun phrase is **incredibly abstract**, because phrases with very different words and lengths have the **same structure**. >> "two guys from the early ‘nineties" is the determiner "two", the head noun "guys" and a prepositional phrase beginning with "from". >> "a band with only one hit" is the determiner "a", the head noun "band" and a prepositional phrase beginning with "with". || In "one and only one phrase unrelated to early ‘nineties one-hit wonder Extreme", the determiner is the several words "one and only one" and the head noun is "phrase". >> Its modifier begins with the adjective "unrelated" followed by a "to" phrase, so the broad pattern of determiner, head noun and modifier holds while the inside of the modifier differs.
17|This level of **abstraction** is needed because, without some way of abstracting away from the individual words, very different surface forms could not be described as similar. >> It would also be impossible to talk coherently about similarities and differences between languages. || All languages have **NPs**, but they can **form them very differently**, and the Chinese NP 咖啡店裡面的小貓, glossed as coffeeshop inside + cat, means the cat in the coffee shop and has the order PP then N. >> English puts "in the coffee shop" after "cat", while Chinese puts the location before the noun. || The Chinese NP also has **no determiner**, which helps explain why determiners are so difficult for speakers of Chinese languages to learn in a second language.
18|The prepositional phrases seen so far share one general structure, because each is a **preposition followed by a noun phrase**, written **P + NP**. || "from the early ‘nineties" is the preposition "from" and the NP "the early ‘nineties", and that NP is itself Det + Adj + N. >> Inside it, "the" is the determiner, "early" is the adjective and "‘nineties" is the noun. || "with only one hit" is "with" and the NP "only one hit", and "unrelated to" is treated as the P before the NP "early ‘nineties one-hit wonder Extreme". >> The NPs differ inside, but each counts as a single unit after the preposition, so one short label can stand for a larger inner structure.
19|The curly brackets and parentheses in "from + { the + early + ‘nineties }" are clumsy because what they try to represent is really **hierarchical**, meaning units sit inside other units. >> The determiner, adjective and noun are all inside the **noun phrase**, which in turn is inside the **prepositional phrase**. >> The prepositional phrase is then inside a sentence, if it is not part of yet another noun phrase. || This nesting is part of why people started using **trees** to diagram sentences, because a tree puts each unit above the parts it contains. >> Hierarchy is also an **important feature of languages**, since words that sit side by side can belong to different levels of structure.
20|A **verb phrase** (VP) is a verb together with the material that completes it. In "The video for “More than Words” came out before most of you were born", the VP is "came out" and the PP "before most of you were born", so its structure is **V + PP**. >> That PP is hard to unpack, because "most of you were born" looks like a verb phrase, but it specifies a point in time that complements "before". || Many verb phrases also contain **noun phrases**, so "sang the song" is V + NP, a verb followed by the noun phrase "the song". >> "worked himself to death" and "threw a party for him" are both V + NP + PP, with "to death" and "for him" as the PPs.
21|The sentence divides into the NP "The video for “More than Words”", which goes Det + N + PP, and the VP "came out before most of you were born", which goes V + PP. || A **clause** is defined as a **VP and its subject**, and the **subject** is defined as the NP that **controls the verb**. >> Here the whole first NP is the subject, and its **head noun** is "video". >> The song title sits inside the modifying PP, so it helps identify the video without becoming the subject. || Because there is one VP with one subject, **the whole sentence is one clause**.
22|"{The plums that were in the icebox} {were delicious}" is a simple **one-clause sentence** made of a subject NP and a VP. >> A subject **controls** its verb, which means the verb **agrees** with "plums" and not "icebox", because "plums" is the **head** of the noun phrase. || "The plums in the icebox, which you were probably saving for breakfast, were delicious" has a **clause within a clause**, set off with commas. >> "you" is the subject of "were probably saving for breakfast", and "plums" is the subject of "were delicious". >> Changing "you" to "my roommate" changes only the inner verb, to "was probably saving", because each subject controls its own verb. || In "Forgive me", the subject is **implicit**, since the speaker wants whoever shares his icebox to forgive him for eating the plums.
23|"I have eaten {the plums in the icebox}" and "The plums in the icebox were eaten {by me}" **mean the same thing**, but they are **syntactically very different**. || In the first sentence, the **subject** "I" comes first and is followed by a verb phrase, and the bracketed NP inside that verb phrase is the **object** of the verb. || In the second sentence, "the plums in the icebox" becomes the **subject**. >> The evidence is agreement, because the verb "were eaten" agrees with the plural noun "plums". >> "by me" is only a **PP** fleshing out the VP, so it names who did the eating without being the subject.
24|It can seem counterintuitive that "me" is not the subject of "The plums in the icebox were eaten {by me}", because English usually aligns the **syntactic subject** of a sentence with its **agent**, the one who does the action. >> Telling **who did what to whom** is the job of **semantic roles**, more so than syntactic ones. || In that passive sentence the order is **Undergoer**, **Location**, Verb, **Agent**, with "The plums" as undergoer, "in the icebox" as location and "by me" as agent. >> In "I have eaten the plums in the icebox" the order is Agent, Verb, Undergoer, Location. || **The semantic roles do not change** when the syntax changes, so only syntactic roles such as subject and object move. >> How different relationships between syntactic and semantic roles influence processing is a question for **sentence processing**.
25|Language is **highly generative**, which means familiar parts can be combined into new words and new sentences, and **derivational morphology** is the part that makes new words. || **Syntax** organizes words into clauses, clauses into phrases, and phrases into sentences, and **inflectional morphology** modifies words for different syntactic roles. || People do all of this **mostly without thinking explicitly** about it. >> That is why one of the hardest things to understand about Linguistics is **what there is to explain**, because some of the most complex phenomena are hiding in plain sight.
