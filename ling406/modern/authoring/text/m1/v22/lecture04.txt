2|**Association norms** are records of the words people give in response to a cue word. A participant sees a word for a few seconds and writes down the first few words that come to mind. >> The task asks for immediate associations and not for a dictionary definition. || Collecting answers from many people is called **norming**, and it reveals which associations come up again and again and which are unusual. >> The same cue can bring out several different associates across participants, so the norm is the whole pattern of responses to that cue.
3|LION is an **association cue**, a word presented so that the participant writes the first words it brings to mind. >> The responses reveal **connections in the participant's lexical knowledge**, which is their stored knowledge of words. || A response such as TIGER would connect **two animal concepts**, but it is only one possible associate and not an answer that every participant must give.
4|TRIPE is an association cue whose **form resembles STRIPES**, because the two words share a sequence of letters. || The shared letters do not give the words a shared meaning, so **similarity in form** and a **connection in meaning** are different relationships. >> This pair makes the **arbitrariness** of the relationship between form and meaning concrete, because overlapping letters occur in words with very different meanings.
5|FREE is another cue in the association task. The participant **generates** the words that come to mind from it and does not choose from a list of answers. || Writing the response down makes an **internal association** available to compare across people, which is what a norm requires.
6|STRIPES is a cue that names a **visual property**. An association can connect a property with an object or animal that has it, so associated words do not have to name the same kind of thing. || STRIPES and TIGER can be **associated** even though one names a pattern and the other names an animal. >> That link differs from the **similarity** between two animal concepts.
7|PEAR is an association cue whose **form overlaps with BEAR**, although the two words have different meanings. || The resemblance in sound or spelling does not make the fruit and the animal the same concept, so a **relationship of form** is separate from a **relationship of meaning**. >> A shared sound or spelling pattern is not evidence that two words have similar meanings.
8|BEAR is another cue for a freely generated association, and its form overlaps with PEAR. Words can **resemble one another in form** without resembling one another in meaning. || Association norms record the responses people **actually give**, so the experimenter does not have to decide ahead of time whether a response will be based on form or on meaning.
9|TREE is a cue for the association task, and the participant's first responses are evidence about which words are **readily connected** with it in that task. || The cue stays the same physical word while different people supply **different associates**. >> The norm sums up all of those responses, so no single response counts as the only association the word has.
10|TIGER connects with LION through their **similar meanings** as animals, and it connects with STRIPES through a **characteristic pattern**. >> Two animal names are related through shared category membership, while an animal and one of its properties are related in a different way. || Because TIGER has both kinds of link, **association and similarity are not interchangeable**, and both can contribute to the structure of a lexical network.

12|Three cues are critical for what comes later, and they are **LION, TIGER and STRIPES**. LION and TIGER name **similar animals**, while TIGER and STRIPES connect an animal with a **characteristic property**. >> An association is measured by counting how often participants give one word in response to another. || The pairs TRIPE and STRIPES, and PEAR and BEAR, make a different point about the **arbitrariness of the relationship between form and meaning**. >> Their forms overlap but their meanings do not, because the relationship between the sound or spelling of a word and its meaning is largely arbitrary. >> Similarity of form, similarity of meaning and the tendency of one word to bring another to mind are three separate things to measure.
13|**Associated** means that one item tends to bring another to mind, while **similar** means that two items share properties. >> TIGER and STRIPES are associated without being two similar objects. || The answers people give also depend on the **task context**. Participants may use a deliberate strategy, and the other words in the list can influence which ideas come to mind easily. >> A list of only wild animals could encourage different responses from a list of only household appliances. || A norm is for that reason evidence about responses **under particular conditions**, so reading one includes asking how the cue was presented and what other cues were present.
14|The chapter title "How Brains Work" is better read as some **basic metaphors from neuroscience** that are useful in psycholinguistics, because they are the basis for most theory and experimental design in the field. || There are five of them. **Activation** means a representation can become active, and **spreading of activation** means it passes activity to connected representations over weighted connections. >> A **distributed representation** encodes an item as a pattern across many units. >> **Associative learning**, such as Hebbian learning, changes the relationships between units that are active together. || **Bottom-up processing** runs from sensory features toward words, and **top-down processing** runs from word knowledge toward the interpretation of features.
15|**Hubel and Wiesel (1968)** recorded from **simple cells** in **primary visual cortex**, which respond to lines of particular orientations in a particular **receptive field**. >> A receptive field is the part of the sensory input that a cell responds to. || They compared a cell's activity as the orientation and movement of a line changed. One cell **activated most strongly** to upward movement of a line at an angle of about three hundred and fifteen degrees, and it fired far less in the other conditions. >> Because the cell responds selectively, its electrochemical activity carries information about that **combination of features**, so the cell can be described as **representing** it. || This work on relating neural activity to sensory input came at the same time as the Cognitive Revolution.
16|A cell from further up the **visual processing stream** responds to something more complex than line segments. In data adapted from **Gross and colleagues (1985)**, a neuron in monkey **inferotemporal cortex** fires strongly to **near-profile views** of a monkey's face and stays near its baseline rate for frontal and rear views. >> Its response is **graded**, since it fires a little to some views and much more to others. || It seems natural to describe this cell as **representing** something complex, the profile of another monkey. || **Activation** in psycholinguistics extends this idea as a metaphor to **perceptual and conceptual representations**.
17|Imagine a very simple set of **lexical representations** for the words lion, tiger and stripes. Each one is a **node** in a network, and a node is a unit that can be **activated by a specific word**. >> The printed word LION supplies perceptual evidence, while the lion node stands for the word the system could recognize from that evidence. || Treating words as nodes makes it possible to say how much activity each one receives, so the model can describe how evidence for one word changes the state of the whole system.
18|The printed word can appear as LION, lion or Lion, in different typefaces and sizes, and every one of these has to activate the **same lion node**. >> The physical display changes while the identity of the word stays the same. || This is the **lack of invariance problem**, which also arises with phonemes, and it means the sensory form of a linguistic unit varies even though the unit stays constant. >> A full account would have to say how complicated the **receptive field** of each node must be to handle that variation. For now it is enough to imagine nodes that words can activate.
19|**Activation can be partial**, so a node does not have to be either fully active or fully inactive. || When the word LION **contrasts poorly with a noise mask**, the input gives only weak evidence for that word, so the lion node receives only **weak activation**. >> The word is not yet identified at that point, because the weak input raises the node's activity without giving enough support for a confident identification. >> A graded value lets the model describe the intermediate states of processing as useful evidence emerges from the noise.
20|As the stimulus becomes clearer, **activation increases**. LION becomes easier to tell apart from the noise, so the input gives more evidence to the lion node and the node becomes more active. || Two changes go together here. The physical change is a better relation between the word signal and the noise mask, and the change in the model is **stronger activity** in the representation of the word. >> Keeping the two apart explains how a manipulation of the display can influence processing.
21|At some point the **signal-to-noise ratio**, which is the strength of the useful word information relative to the interfering background, becomes very favorable. Activity in the lion node is then strong enough to say that the simple network has **identified** the word "lion". >> The tiger and stripes nodes remain as alternatives that were not identified. || This is the **perceptual identification task**, in which participants push a button as soon as they can identify the item. >> The measure is **how long identification takes**, so a condition that makes identification easier leads to an earlier button press.
22|Two kinds of factor can influence how quickly a word is identified in the perceptual identification task. || **Features of the stimulus itself** concern the target word, and **features of the experimental environment** concern the surrounding situation, including what the participant has just encountered. >> Keeping them apart makes a testable comparison possible, because the same target can be presented in different contexts, or different targets can be compared under the same task conditions. || Identification speed for that reason depends on more than visibility alone.
23|Two **features of the stimulus itself** influence how quickly a word is identified. One is **word frequency**, which is how often the word is encountered, and the other is **how similar it is to other words**. >> A frequently encountered word can be easier to activate. || Two **features of the experimental environment** also matter. Having just seen the same word produces **repetition priming**, and having just seen a similar or related word can produce other kinds of priming. >> Frequency concerns experience built up with a word, while repetition priming concerns its recent presentation, although both make a representation easier to reach. >> A related prime changes the context without repeating the target.
24|Most models of this kind assume that activation **unfolds over time**, and **activation dynamics** is the name for how the activity of a representation changes over time. >> In the Interactive Activation model of **Rumelhart and McClelland (1986)**, activation for the word CAVE rises above the competing words while the input supports it and then gradually falls. || **Frequency effects** can be explained by assuming that activation dynamics are **tuned up by experience**. Words that are encountered more often become more sensitive, so they are easier to activate. >> Different computational models simulate frequency effects in different ways.
25|**Repetition priming** is faster identification of a word that is seen again very shortly after it was just seen, and it is a basic effect in psychology. || Activation dynamics explain it because activation **does not rise and fall instantaneously**, and it may fall off more slowly than it rises. >> If the word is encountered again while activation is still high from the last exposure, its **baseline** is already higher than usual. >> A node that starts from a higher baseline can be activated more easily, so the word is identified faster.
26|Two factors that influence the activity of a word are still unexplained. One is how similar the word is to other words, which produces **neighborhood effects**. The other is having just seen a similar or related word, which produces **associative, semantic or phonological priming**. || Both involve one word affecting a **different word**, while repetition involves the same word on two occasions. >> A set of separate nodes can explain changes in the activity of a single word but cannot explain an influence from another word, so something has to be **added to the network**. >> What has to be added is connections between the nodes.
27|Understanding many phenomena in language requires assuming that the **nodes in a network are connected**. || In the network for lion, tiger and stripes, lion is linked to tiger and tiger is linked to stripes, so the nodes are no longer separate detectors. >> The printed word LION activates the lion node **directly**, and a connection can then carry some of that influence to tiger **indirectly**. || Connections matter because they change how one representation responds when another is active.
28|One way to represent the associations from the norms is a network in which the **connection strength** between two nodes is determined by how strongly the words are associated. >> A semantic network of this kind links vehicle with car and bus, and it links fire engine both with vehicles and with concepts related to fire. || **Spreading activation** means that when "lion" is activated, some of that activation travels along a **weighted connection** to "tiger". >> Tiger is then already partly active, so it is easier to reach when the word tiger itself appears. >> Connections of different strengths pass on different amounts of activity. || This is the spreading activation account of **semantic priming**, in which processing one word makes a related word easier to process.

30|The word STRIPES sits in the center of a **strong noise mask** at the start of a perceptual identification sequence. || The contrast is low, so the word signal is hard to separate from the background and the word is barely visible. >> The stripes node can begin to receive some sensory support even while the evidence is **too weak for a confident identification**.
31|The **contrast of STRIPES increases**, so more of its letter pattern becomes available against the noise. || The input now gives stronger support for identifying the same word, although the letters are still faint. >> A change in clarity changes the **amount of evidence** for the target without changing which word the target is.
32|STRIPES becomes **more identifiable** as its contrast improves, and its letters are now visibly separate from the surrounding noise. || In the perceptual identification task the participant responds when the evidence is enough to recognize the word, so the quality of the stimulus decides **the point at which a response becomes possible**.
33|The clearer STRIPES gives **stronger word information** than the heavily masked versions, although the same kind of noise is still present. || A participant who recognizes the word can now make the identification response. >> The response reports **recognition of the target** and is not a judgment about whether stripes are associated with a particular animal.
34|At high contrast STRIPES stands out from the mask, and recognizing it **activates the stripes node** in the network account. || Identifying STRIPES also makes it a possible **prime**, which is a word seen first that can affect how another word is processed. >> Its effect on another word depends on the connections between their representations.
35|LION is the target in a **second perceptual identification sequence**, and it also begins with weak contrast against a noise mask, so its letters give little usable information. || The target and its visibility are separate from what was seen just before it. >> A priming account asks whether that preceding experience changes **how quickly LION is recognized**.
36|The contrast of LION increases, which **strengthens the perceptual evidence** for that word, although it is still only faintly visible in the center of the mask. || Recognition becomes more likely as the letter pattern separates from the noise. >> The same target produces a **changing internal response** because the quality of its sensory evidence keeps changing.
37|LION becomes clearer within the mask, and a response can occur once the evidence supports recognizing it. >> The **time of that response** is what the task measures. || A **priming comparison** asks whether recognition of the same target comes sooner after one preceding context than after another, so the visible target is what the two contexts have in common.
38|LION is now legible while the noise mask is still present, so the input gives substantial evidence for the word and **activation of the lion node keeps increasing**. || Recognition starts from a state that has two sources, which are the **clarity of the target** and any **activation left behind by the context** that came before it.
39|The clear LION completes the identification sequence. || The lion node can be activated in two ways, **directly by the word input** and **through connections with other nodes**. >> The difference between direct input and activity arriving through the network is what explains how seeing STRIPES first can prime LION.
40|Activity flows throughout the network, so activating "stripes" can **prime "lion"** even though these two words are **not directly associated**. >> Stripes is connected to tiger and tiger is connected to lion, so activation from stripes reaches lion by way of tiger. || This is called **mediated priming**, which is priming that passes through a middle word, with tiger as the intermediate node between the prime and the target. >> A model limited to direct associations has no route from stripes to lion, so it could not explain the effect. >> The effect is for that reason **compelling evidence for spreading activation**.
41|The experiment with stripes and lion is very representative of psycholinguistics in two ways, which are **careful study design** and **small effects**. || The result is also **counterintuitive**, because "stripes" and "lion" are not obvious associates, yet "stripes" primes "lion" because activity travels through the connection each word has with tiger. >> The value of the experiment comes from the relationship it tests and not from the size of the difference. >> An indirect route predicts an influence that a model with only direct associations could not explain, so a small effect can separate the two accounts.
42|**Bottom-up processing** carries information from visual features up to letters and then to words, and **top-down processing** lets an active word send activation back down to the letters that fit it. || In the Interactive Activation network, drawn after **McClelland and Rumelhart (1981)**, features are at the bottom, letters in the middle and words such as WORK, WORD, WEAR and WEAK at the top. When part of the last letter of WORK is covered, the partly visible features support several candidate letters. >> The display activates WORK at the word level, and WORK feeds activation back to K, so the output for **K rises strongly** over time while R and D stay low. || Top-down support of this kind is one way of modeling **pattern completion**, which Menn discusses at length.
43|One extremely simple but powerful idea about how all of this gets into the brain is "Neurons that fire together, wire together." >> This is **Hebbian learning**, in which units that are active together become more strongly connected, so learning changes the network that will process future input. >> In the example of the bell and the food, both signals feed a pathway for salivation, so when they are active together the synapse from the bell onto that pathway is strengthened. || The reality is more complicated, but the neural learning mechanisms that are relatively well understood are **associative**. || Historically these mechanisms were considered **insufficient to learn anything important about language**. More recent arguments are about what role they might play and how **innate knowledge** might constrain them, especially given that LLMs can learn many structural features of language without much more than this.
44|A **distributed representation** encodes an item as a pattern across many units, and it is how associative learning mechanisms can learn relatively sophisticated patterns. || **Generativity** is important for language, and for an associative model to **generalize** it has to be able to **represent similarity**. >> Similar items have overlapping patterns, so what is known about robins and canaries can generalize to new birds as they are learned about. || In the network, an item input and a relation input together produce attribute outputs. >> With the item canary and the relation can, the active attributes are grow, move, fly and sing.
45|Five **neurally inspired metaphors** give a way of talking about psycholinguistics. **Activation** changes as input is processed, and **spreading activation** lets one representation influence another. >> **Top-down and bottom-up processing** are the two directions of influence between levels. >> **Associative learning**, such as Hebbian learning, changes connection strengths, and **distributed representations** give a basis for similarity and generalization. || **Priming experiments** measure how processing one item changes the processing of another, so people have used them to reason about what lexical networks might look like.
