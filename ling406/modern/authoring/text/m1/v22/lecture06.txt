2|There is a very old tradition of describing natural languages in **abstract, formal terms**, similar to the terms used in **Artificial Intelligence**, so language is treated as symbols and rules. >> A grammar in this sense is an internal system that can characterize sentences a person has never encountered. || Experimental psychology in the fifties and sixties was **actively hostile to talking about this kind of internal state**, because behaviorists wanted to explain learning through observable stimuli, responses and consequences. || **Skinner** wrote Verbal Behavior to describe how language could be learned by **operant conditioning**, and **Chomsky (1959)** reviewed it so critically that the review is better remembered than the book. >> That review was an important moment in the **Cognitive Revolution**, which brought internal mental representations back into explanations of language.
3|The formal tradition comes from interest in **Artificial Intelligence**, which dates back to the beginning of the **digital computer**. >> **Alan Turing**, who developed decryption devices for the British Army during the Second World War, thought a lot about this question. || Computers were invented as **universal function approximators**, which means one machine could do many different kinds of calculations and logical operations instead of a single fixed task. >> No one had made a machine like that before, so people asked **how the computer differed from human intelligence, if at all**. || Because a computer runs procedures on symbols, it became a way to explore what language can do if language is also represented as symbols and procedures.
4|The **Turing test** judges a machine by its conversation, and it starts from the **imitation game**. >> In the game, A and B are a man and a woman and C is an examiner whose job is to figure out the gender of A and B. >> A is trying to fool C, while B is trying to help C guess the correct answer. || The test is that if either A or B can be replaced with a computer and **C does not notice**, the computer has demonstrated **intelligence**. >> The examiner never inspects the machine's program and only has its answers, so the machine passes by producing answers that fit the conversation.
5|Several features of the **Turing test** are worth knowing. The test runs entirely through **natural language**, and it involves **deception**, because a respondent has to handle an examiner who is trying to expose what it is. || Turing explicitly **excludes expert behaviors** such as calculating large sums or composing poetry, so the test is about ordinary conversation and not one special skill. || Turing concludes that the best way to get an intelligent machine would be to build something with the **cognitive capabilities of a child** and have it **learn** to be more like an adult. >> On this proposal, intelligence develops through experience instead of being supplied as a finished list of adult answers.
6|**Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver** is a later example of **GOFAI**, the approach that models intelligent behavior as operations on explicit symbols. >> Given an abstract characterization of a formal problem, the program **applies procedures to symbols to find a solution**. || It was very good at games and puzzles such as the **Tower of Hanoi** and **chess**, and also good at mathematics and logic such as **geometry proofs** and algebra word problems. >> These problems suit the program because their states and permitted moves can be stated explicitly. >> **Knoblock (1990)** laid out the Tower of Hanoi as connected states in work on abstracting the puzzle. In the Tower of Hanoi, each arrangement of disks is a state and each legal move leads to another state, so a solution is a path of legal moves that reaches the goal arrangement.
7|Early AI emphasized formal problems partly because of **technology**. Inputs to computers were still very limited, with punchcards and machine language, so there was no way to input a high-resolution image and ask the computer to find the face. || The other reason was **theoretical commitments**, because researchers held that intelligence is exemplified by this kind of **rational behavior**. >> A pigeon can be trained to guide a missile, but it does not know what it is doing. >> There is a deep argument about whether AI programs know what they are doing either, but they at least **represent goal states and choose procedures** for achieving them.
8|**Chomsky's (1959)** critique of Verbal Behavior makes several linked claims about what a speaker knows. A **grammar** that enumerates sentences and gives each one a structural description characterizes the ability to **tell sentences from nonsentences**, to **understand new sentences** and to notice certain **ambiguities**. || New sentences are not related to familiar ones by any simple formal, semantic or statistical similarity, so Chomsky calls talk of **generalization** pointless and empty. >> A new item is recognized as a sentence because it is **generated by the grammar** each person has internalized, and not because it matches a familiar item. || All normal children acquire comparable, very complex grammars with remarkable speed, so Chomsky suggests that humans are **specially designed** to do this, with a data-handling or hypothesis-formulating ability of unknown character.
9|In his critique, **Chomsky (1959)** characterizes linguistic knowledge as a **grammar**, which is **a set of abstract rules that produce grammatical sentences**. >> The grammar describes the ability to tell sentences from nonsentences, to understand new sentences and to notice ambiguities. || There are an **infinite number of grammatical sentences**, so it is remarkable that a person can make a **grammaticality judgment** for any new sentence. >> A grammaticality judgment is a person's sense of whether a sentence is well formed or ill formed. || In this approach, **people's intuitions about whether sentences are grammatical are the key data** for the study of language.
10|**Chomsky (1959)** argues that **grammatical sentences do not have any obvious similarities to one another**, whether in form, meaning, statistics or grammatical frame. >> A new sentence is accepted because it is generated by the grammar a person has internalized, and it is understood in part because the person can determine how the grammar derives it. || **Stimulus generalization** is the behaviorist concept in which a trained response extends to stimuli that resemble the trained one. >> Because it depends on resemblance and grammatical sentences do not resemble one another in any simple way, stimulus generalization **cannot deal with the infinite number of sentences** a grammar can generate.
11|In behaviorism, **stimulus generalization** happens when a trained response spreads to similar stimuli. A pigeon trained to peck for a **green** light also pecks for similar colors, with the most pecks at green and fewer pecks as the color gets less like green. >> Another pigeon does a **magnitude discrimination task**, where the stimuli differ in numerical magnitude instead of color. || The cases in which operant conditioning generalizes depend on **relatively concrete similarity**, such as color or number. >> It is **not clear how this extends to abstract concepts**, so a conditioning account of language would have to say what kind of similarity links grammatical sentences.
12|**Stimulus control** is the idea in Skinner's account that some aspect of the environment controls a spoken response. >> A typical example for Skinner is a person responding to a piece of music with an utterance, and in another example a proper noun is held to be a response. || Chomsky says such examples **merely add to the general mystification**. A piece of music has many properties and a speaker can respond to it in different ways, so the account must say which property controls the utterance. >> The explanation only works if the stimulus can be identified **independently of the utterance**, because otherwise the response itself decides what counts as its cause.
13|Chomsky writes that verbal behavior **cannot be predicted from the stimuli** in the speaker's environment, because nobody knows what the current stimuli are until the speaker responds. >> Naming the stimulus only after hearing the response makes the prediction **circular**. || Read this way, Skinner does not only have a bad theory of language, because he is **posing the wrong question**. >> Why people say what they do, when they do, depends on a whole matrix of social interactions, cognitive processes and previous experiences, and much of that has nothing to do with language. || The **tractable problem** is to study the **structure of what people say** and from there derive the **set of rules** that produces this structure.
14|**Chomsky (1955)** points out that neither "colorless green ideas sleep furiously" nor "furiously sleep ideas green colorless", nor any of their parts, has ever occurred in the past linguistic experience of an English speaker. >> The first is **grammatical** and the second is **not**, even though both use the same words, so the judgment cannot come from having met the sentence before. >> The first is also hard to interpret literally, so **grammatical structure and meaningfulness come apart**. || The warning "Car! Getting towed! Yours!" is **ungrammatical**, although the stimulus that likely controlled it is perfectly clear. >> A clear controlling stimulus does not make an utterance grammatical, so being understood in a situation and being grammatical are separate questions.
15|Chomsky is trying to describe language at the **computational level of analysis**. The term comes from **David Marr**, a visual neuroscientist who described cognitive systems as having three **levels of analysis**. || The **computational** level says what problem the system solves. >> The **algorithmic** level says which representations and procedures are used to solve it. >> The **implementational** level says how those procedures are physically carried out. || The three levels answer different questions about the same ability, so a claim can be informative at one level without answering the questions at the other levels.
16|A **cash register** is the worked case for the three levels. At the **computational level**, it takes the prices of items and determines the amount due for a purchase, and it takes the payment amount and computes the change. || At the **algorithmic level**, it uses the operations of **addition** for the prices and **subtraction** for the change. || The **implementational level does not matter** for these functions, because a credit card reader or a computer program does the same job and so counts as a cash register too. >> The physical devices differ, but the relation between the prices, the payment and the required answer stays the same.
17|Marr called problems that break down cleanly into these levels **Type I problems**. || Viewing **language as a Type I problem** means a researcher can work at the **computational level**, which is **Universal Grammar**, and at the **algorithmic level**, which is the specific processes and how they are applied in different languages. >> The researcher can do this **without worrying about the implementational level**, which covers errors, memory limitations and associative learning. || Chomsky's critique came about a decade before Marr's theoretical work, so Marr's terms describe Chomsky's approach and are not where it came from. >> Both were influential in artificial intelligence research and theoretical work in the GOFAI tradition.
18|Marr says Chomsky's notion of a **competence theory** for English syntax is exactly what he means by a **computational theory**. Both are little concerned with the details of the algorithms that must be run, because a person has to know what the algorithms are supposed to do before devising them. >> When a problem decomposes in this way, Marr says it has a **Type I theory**. || Marr adds that there is no reason why every problem of biological information processing should have a Type I theory. >> A problem can be solved by the simultaneous action of a considerable number of processes **whose interaction is its own simplest description**, and Marr calls that situation a **Type II theory**.
19|Marr applies the two types to the **grammar of natural language**. He says the purpose of human language is presumably to transform a data structure that is not one-dimensional into **one-dimensional form** for transmission as a **sequential utterance**. >> The listener then retranslates the utterance into a rough copy of the original. || Seen this way, there may be **no Type I theory of English syntax** of the kind transformational grammar attempts to define. >> The constraints of syntax may be wired-in conventions about useful ways of carrying out this operation, and not deep principles about the nature of intelligence. >> An abstract theory of syntax may then be an illusion that only approximates what the processes really do, so the grammar of natural language **may have a Type II theory**.
20|If grammar has a Type II theory, details about **how language is implemented in the brain** might be **unavoidable in a theory of language**. >> Facts about learning, memory, perception and action would then be part of the explanation of linguistic structure, because the processes that do the work would shape what the system can represent. || A whole **alternative approach** to language research is based on just this hypothesis. >> That approach starts with the properties of the learner and the processing system and asks how they produce the regularities seen in language use, instead of starting with an abstract grammar.
21|**Emergentism**, **statistical learning** and **parallel distributed processing** name the alternative view, and its strong commitments are at the **implementational level**. || It assumes **distributed, non-hierarchical representations**, which are like patterns of activity over groups of neurons. >> A representation is spread over many units instead of sitting in a single symbol. || It assumes **gradual learning**, which is like adjusting the strength of synaptic connections. >> It also focuses on **microstructure** and makes a more **graded division between the levels of analysis**, so properties of the learning mechanism are expected to shape the apparent rules in behavior.
22|**Elman (1990)** built **simple recurrent networks** to capture different aspects of language performance in this framework. >> All the model does is try to **predict the next item** it is going to see, and it gradually learns to do this with experience. || The current input activates **hidden units**, and the hidden units produce the output prediction. >> A copy of the hidden activity is carried into **context units**, so the context units supply information about the earlier items when the next input arrives. >> Because the same item can follow different earlier sequences, a good prediction depends on both the current item and that context.
23|A **letter-prediction model** built on the principles of **Elman (1990)** can **find words** in text that has no spaces. >> The unbroken string "Manyyearsagoaboyandgirllivedbytheseatheyplayedhappily" becomes "many years ago a boy and girl lived by the sea they played happily". || The model is only predicting the next letter, and its **prediction error goes down within words**. >> Inside a word the earlier letters limit what can come next, so prediction gets easier. >> At the start of a new word many more letters are possible, so the error often rises again, and those rises line up with the **word boundaries**.
24|When a similar model is trained on **simple sentences**, it starts to identify **grammatical classes** and even some **semantic information**. >> This happens as a **side effect** of learning to predict the sequences, because the model was never asked to classify words. || **Elman (1990)** examined the hidden activity for each word, and words that occur in similar contexts ended up with similar activity. >> **Nouns** separate from **verbs**, and the nouns divide into animate and inanimate groups with smaller clusters for humans, animals, food and breakable things. >> The verbs divide by their patterns of direct-object use.
25|Many engineering solutions in **Natural Language Processing** work this way, by statistically analyzing massive **corpora**, which are collections of texts and transcriptions of spoken language. || **Peter Norvig** agrees with **Chomsky (1955)** that neither sentence had appeared in the published literature before then, but he finds that **every two-word part had already occurred**. >> Examples are "colorless green" in **Elisabeth Finley Thomas (1942)**, "green ideas" in **William White (1922)** and "Ideas sleep" in the magazine Current Opinion. || Norvig reports that **Pereira (2001)** used a statistically-trained **finite-state model**, augmented with word categories and trained by expectation maximization on newspaper text. >> The model computes that "colorless green ideas sleep furiously" is **two hundred thousand times more probable** than the reversed order, so a statistical model **can distinguish the two sentences**.

27|Psycholinguistics has two important traditions, and the first is the **GOFAI, modular or Chomskyan** tradition. It assumes that linguistic knowledge is essentially **abstract and symbolic** and that linguistic representations are **different in kind** from other kinds of knowledge. >> All of this has to interface with messy perceptual, motor, memory and other cognitive capacities, so the psycholinguist's job is to **study this interface**. || The **emergentist or functionalist** tradition assumes that symbol-like and rule-like behavior are **emergent properties of a statistical learning process**. >> It also assumes that perceptual, motor and cognitive processes **shape linguistic representations**, so the psycholinguist's job is to study **language use** and relate it to more general psychological phenomena.

30|**Emergentist and statistical learning approaches** start from the messiness seen in the "i before e" rule, which is found all over the place in every language. || **Phonetic categories** are probabilistically defined over multiple dimensions, and **words are ambiguous** in every way imaginable. >> **Morphological paradigms** such as adding -ed for the past tense in English have exceptions like RUN-RAN and SLEEP-SLEPT. >> **Syntactic rules** are open to dialect and individual variation, innovation and change over time. || Because a strong pattern and its exceptions exist side by side at every level, these approaches let experience shape graded expectations instead of assuming exceptionless rules.
31|**Moravec's paradox** is a problem for the GOFAI approach. Proving theorems and solving geometry problems is **comparatively easy for computers**, but a supposedly simple task like recognizing a face or crossing a room without bumping into anything is **extremely difficult**. >> The opposite is true for humans. || So characterizing language in formal terms, the way a computer program would be written, **may be a false trail**. >> For language research, the paradox is a reason to take perceptual, motor and other processing systems seriously instead of treating them as simple attachments to a symbolic grammar.
32|Another problem for the formal approach is that **fixed categories and rules are often fuzzy in practice**, which has been recognized for a long time. || **Wittgenstein (1953)** asks what is common to everything called a **game**, including board games, card games, ball games and Olympic games. >> He says not to assume there must be something common, but to look and see whether there is. >> Looking does not turn up one thing common to all games, and what appears is **similarities and relationships**, a whole series of them. || Moving from board games to card games, many correspondences with the first group remain while other features change, so the category holds together without one defining feature.
33|**Many phenomena in language are probabilistic**, and the games of **Wittgenstein (1953)** are a category of that kind. From card games to ball games, much that is common is kept but much is lost. || No single feature runs through all games. >> Amusement differs between chess and noughts and crosses, patience has no competition between players, and winning and losing disappears when a child throws a ball at the wall and catches it again. >> Skill in chess differs from skill in tennis, and ring-a-ring-a-roses keeps the amusement while many other features disappear. || The result is **a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing**, sometimes overall similarities and sometimes similarities of detail.
34|**Family resemblances**, **similarity-based generalization** and **hunches** were turning up across psychology. || **Roger Shepard** worked on perception, **Eleanor Rosch** on categorization, and **Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky** on decision-making. >> They and many others were producing results that made a lot of cognition seem **less amenable to formal, rational interpretation**. || The named researchers worked in different areas and did not share one experiment, so several lines of psychology were pointing the same way. >> That makes it reasonable to ask whether language also reflects similarity-based generalization and the properties of actual processing.
