2|People make **speech errors** because many words and sounds are active while an utterance is planned, so sometimes the wrong one is selected. || Errors interest psycholinguists because their form is **evidence about planning**, since an error can split parts of a word, move a sound to another position or swap in a related word. >> Comparing the intended and produced forms reveals **which units** the production system represents and how it organizes them. || **Fromkin** titled the article “Speech errors as linguistic evidence,” which was gutsy because linguists often study **competence** apart from the slips of **performance**, so errors could simply be set aside. >> The title claims instead that the structure of an error tells us how language itself is organized.
3|**Spreading activation** models explain speech errors as competition, because activating one word also activates related items that can be selected by mistake. >> **Noise** here means not only acoustic noise but also fluctuations and interference in the activation of different parts of the system. || **Frequency** matters because common words are easier to activate than rare ones, and this seems to affect many levels of speech production. || **Other sources of activation** add competitors, since what you just said and what you plan to say stay partly active while you talk, and so can things you are trying not to say. >> **Similarity** lets activation spread more easily between related items, whether the relation is semantic, syntactic or phonological.
4|A **tip-of-the-tongue (TOT) state** is partial activation of a word’s meaning and sound form that falls short of the **threshold** for producing it, so you know the word but cannot say it. || **Less common words** are more susceptible to TOT states, because by the frequency principle their forms are harder to activate. >> In the study reviewed by **Lise Abrams (2008)**, people in a TOT state for “contraband,” defined as goods traded illegally, read a list of words before trying again. || **Priming** helps because a word that shares sounds with the target adds activation to the missing form. >> Words sharing the **first syllable**, such as “contact,” “connotation” and “conscious,” resolved **about half** of TOT states, compared with about a quarter after unrelated words. >> Middle-syllable overlap helped less, and final-syllable overlap was close to the unrelated words.
5|**Slips of the tongue** differ in the direction a sound moves, and all of these examples come from **Fromkin**. >> **William Archibald Spooner** supposedly once said “Mardon me padam, this pie is occupewed,” a slip full of moved sounds. || In **anticipation**, a later sound appears too early, as when “week long race” becomes “reek long race.” >> In **perseveration**, an earlier sound carries into a later position, as when “John gave the boy” becomes “John gave the goy.” || In **metathesis**, also called an exchange, two sounds trade places, as when “keep a tape” becomes “teep a cape.” >> Classifying a slip depends on comparing the intended and produced forms, not on whether the result makes sense.
6|**Other sources of activation** are most obviously at work in these slips, because each one borrows a sound from elsewhere in the same utterance. >> In **anticipation**, material planned for a later position is already active, so the r in “reek long race” arrives early. >> In **perseveration**, material just spoken stays active, so the g in “John gave the goy” lingers. || In **metathesis**, two elements active in the same plan interfere with each other and trade positions. || **Similarity** and **frequency** also play a part because they affect which candidates compete well, and **noise** in activation affects which one is finally selected.
7|**Mondegreens** are **slips of the ear**, in which a listener assigns the speech sounds to different words than the speaker intended. >> The term comes from mishearing “laid him on the green” as “Lady Mondegreen,” and “Hold me closer, tiny dancer” is misheard as “Hold me closer, Tony Danza.” >> **Andrew Nevins** is analyzing crowdsourced mishearings collected on the kissthisguy website. || Mondegreens happen because speech is continuous, so the listener has to infer the **word boundaries**. >> “The good candy came anyways” and “The good can decay many ways” contain nearly the same sounds, so **context** is what tells them apart. || Songs make this harder because **prosody**, the rhythm and melody of speech, is less helpful when words are sung.
8|**Malapropisms** of this kind are **perceptual errors** more than speech errors, because the speaker says exactly what they intend to say. || The speaker seems to have heard an unfamiliar word like “congenital” and decided that a more familiar word, “congenial,” must have been intended, so they later say “a congenial heart defect.” >> “Congenital” means present from birth and “congenial” means pleasing, so only their sound links them. || This is **top-down processing** at work, because knowledge of familiar words shapes how the heard sound is interpreted. >> The mistake happened during perception, so the odd phrase sounds like a slip but arises in a different way.
9|When **Fromkin** wrote this paper, researchers argued about whether the **phoneme**, or phone, a single speech sound, was a unit in speech planning. || If whole **consonant clusters** always moved together, “fish grotto” would have become “gish frotto” and “fresh clear water” would have become “clesh freer water.” || Instead, Fromkin presents evidence that clusters get **broken up all the time** in production, so single segments must be planning units. >> In “fish grotto” becoming “frish gotto,” only the r leaves the gr cluster and joins the f. >> In “fresh clear water” becoming “flesh queer water,” the r and l trade places while the f and k stay where they were.
10|**Fromkin (1971)** drew an early version of the **production model** that **Menn** describes, which traces how an intended meaning becomes speech. || The process starts with the **meaning to be conveyed**, builds **syntactic and semantic structure**, and adds **intonation** and information from the **lexicon** partway through. >> This produces strings of segments divided into syllables with their features, which **morphophonemic rules** and **phonetic rules** then specify fully. || Finally, **motor commands to the muscles** produce the **utterance**. >> Because the model has separate stages, an error can disturb one stage while the others stay intact, so different kinds of errors are evidence about different stages.
11|The basic model of **speech production** has five levels, the **message**, **functional**, **positional**, **phonological encoding** and **speech gesture** levels. || The **message level** is what the speaker intends to convey, and the **functional level** selects **lemmas** and links them to semantic roles. >> Later levels arrange the clause, build its sound form and prepare the movements that say it. || Following **Menn**, the focus for now is the message and functional levels, although the earlier sound slips were already **phonological encoding** errors. >> Separating the levels matters because different errors have different structures, so each kind of error points to a different level.
12|The **message level** is where the speaker decides what information to communicate, and the same event can become quite different messages. || You might tone down “I won a bunch of money on the game Sunday” for someone rooting for the losing team, because their feelings affect how directly you emphasize the win. >> For someone not interested in football you might explain why the game matters, and in a tweet you would choose only a few details. || The message level is **mysterious and hard to investigate experimentally**, because giving speakers the same event does not ensure they intend the same content.
13|Some speech production studies try to control the message level with an **elicitation paradigm**, a method in which every speaker describes the same material, such as the picture book “Frog, where are you?” >> The book shows a boy, a dog and frogs, so all speakers share the same characters and scenes. || It is **still hard to define the intended message** for a scene like this, because speakers can focus on different characters, actions or relations. >> How a speaker builds that message may also depend on **age**, **culture** and **audience**, so the method controls what people see more than what they mean to say.
14|Speakers cannot simply select finished “words” at the **functional level**, and a verb exchange error explains why. >> “Where’d I put it when I brought it in” came out as “Where’d I bring it when I put it in.” || The verbs switched places, but the moved verb appeared as “bring” rather than “brought,” so the tense must have been applied after the exchange. >> If the word “brought” itself had been selected, it would have moved as “brought.” || So the speaker selected the **lemma** BRING, a word’s identity before its final grammatical form is fixed, and it was expressed as present tense in its new position. >> The term **lemma** is borrowed from mathematics, where it refers to a proposition in an argument.
15|People often say the **opposite** of what they mean, and these slips are **errors in lemma selection**, where the wrong lemma wins the competition. >> One speaker said “I really like to” before correcting it to “hate to get up in the morning.” >> Others said “bottom” for “top” of the stack of books, “hot” for “cold,” and “the oral” for “written part of the exam.” || The wrong word comes from the same domain as the intended one, so the sentence stays grammatical while its meaning is reversed. || This happens because an antonym is related in meaning, strongly associated and able to fill the same roles, which makes it an unusually strong competitor.
16|Antonyms compete so well because they are **similar at a number of levels**, so it is easy for activation to spread from a **lemma** to its antonym. >> They are **semantically related** and **strongly associated**, which makes them a perfect storm in the spreading activation framework. || Antonyms can also take on the **same semantic roles** and belong to the **same word class**, so they can fill the same syntactic roles. >> Because of this, the rest of the speech production system can handle the wrong word, as when “hot” replaces “cold” in an otherwise normal sentence.
17|A **blend** combines parts of two competing words into a single form. >> **Lewis Carroll (1876)**, in his preface to The Hunting of the Snark, explained his **portmanteau** words this way, saying that someone who means to say both “fuming” and “furious” with a perfectly balanced mind will say “frumious.” || **Fromkin** quoted Carroll because he claimed such blends are possible in natural speech and proposed a hypothesis about how they occur. >> Fromkin added that such complete blends are seldom found in normal speech, so a blend of “furious” and “fuming” is more apt to come out as [fjumijəs] or [fjuriŋ]. >> This happens particularly because the **first syllables are identical**, so the words join where their sounds overlap.
18|Spontaneous **blends** show speakers competing between **alternative words** with similar meanings. >> Examples blend “mainly” with “mostly,” “switch” with “changed” to give “swindged,” “bother” with “trouble,” and “player” with “athlete.” || **Fromkin** proposed that a speaker matches **semantic features** while selecting words, and when there are several alternatives, both go into a **buffer** with their sound specifications. >> Either a selection happens there or the words are blended, so these errors seem to support Carroll’s assumptions. || There are almost always a number of alternative possibilities in speech production, so blends reveal competition that is an ordinary part of speaking.
19|**Semantic roles**, also called functional roles, describe how each participant relates to an event. >> In “The plums in the icebox were eaten by me,” the order is Undergoer, Location, Verb, Agent. >> In “I have eaten the plums in the icebox,” the order is Agent, Verb, Undergoer, Location. || With this simple event structure, lemmas can be slotted into roles, so the verb is TO EAT, the **Agent** or doer is ME, and PLUM is the **Undergoer** that is affected. || Arranging the roles in different orders has **syntactic consequences**, such as whether ME is said as “I” or “me,” and that is decided at the **Positional Level**.
20|By the end of the **functional level**, the speaker has decided on a **message**, selected appropriate **lemmas**, and associated those lemmas with **semantic roles**. || That is quite a bit, yet the speaker is **nowhere near putting a clause together**, much less saying it out loud. >> For an eating event the plan has EAT, the eater and the thing eaten, but not whether the sentence is active or passive, how pronouns are said, or any sounds. || The remaining work of ordering and pronouncing the clause comes at later levels, beginning with the **Positional Level**.

23|**Speech errors very often go undetected**, because **top-down effects** in speech perception let listeners recover the intended message despite odd wording. >> The process is similar to what must have happened for many **malapropisms** and **mondegreens**. || Some “errors” are **calculated for stylistic reasons**, as when **Paul Krugman** wrote “the upper hand is on the other foot,” quoting the film Hot Shots Part Deux. >> The line mixes two familiar idioms, “the upper hand” and the shoe being on the other foot. || A deliberate error can be **more memorable** than its correct counterpart because the mismatch draws attention to the wording itself, while an ordinary slip passes unnoticed as the listener focuses on the expected message.
