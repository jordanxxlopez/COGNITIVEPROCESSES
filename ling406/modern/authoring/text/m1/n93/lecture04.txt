2|**Association norms** summarize the words people produce in response to a cue word. >> A participant sees a word on a screen for a few seconds. >> The participant writes down the first few words that come to mind. || The task asks for **immediate associations**, not a dictionary definition. >> The same cue can bring up different associates in different people. >> Collecting responses across many people reveals which associations recur and which are unusual. || So a norm is a **pattern of responses** to each cue. It is measured through what people actually write.
3|LION is a **cue** in the association task. The participant writes the first words that LION brings to mind. || The responses reveal connections in the participant's **lexical knowledge**, which is what a person knows about words. >> A response such as TIGER would connect two animal concepts. >> TIGER is a possible associate, not a response that every participant must give. || So the cue only starts the response. The participant supplies the association.
4|TRIPE is a cue whose **form** looks like STRIPES. The two words share a sequence of letters. || Shared letters do not mean a shared **meaning**. >> Similarity in form is one relationship. >> A connection between meanings is a different relationship. >> Overlapping letters can occur in words with very different meanings. || So TRIPE and STRIPES make the **arbitrariness** of form and meaning concrete. The spelling of a word does not tell you what it means.
5|FREE is another cue in the association task. >> The participant supplies words that come to mind from this cue. >> The participant does not choose from a list of answers. || The response is **generated** by the participant. >> Recording it makes an internal association available to study. >> Responses can then be compared across people. || So the norm for FREE comes from what many participants produce on their own.
6|STRIPES is a cue that names a **visual property**. || An association can connect a **property** with an object or animal that has it. >> STRIPES and TIGER can be associated. >> One word names a pattern, and the other names an animal. >> This link differs from the **similarity** between two animal concepts such as LION and TIGER. || So associated words do not have to name the same kind of thing.
7|PEAR is a cue whose **form** overlaps with BEAR. || The two words look alike but have different meanings. >> PEAR names a fruit. >> BEAR names an animal. >> Their resemblance in form does not make them the same concept. || So PEAR and BEAR separate a **formal relationship** from a **semantic relationship**. A shared sound or spelling pattern is not evidence of similar meaning.
8|BEAR is another cue for a **freely generated association**. || Its form overlaps with PEAR. >> The two words resemble each other in form. >> They do not resemble each other in meaning. || **Association norms** record the responses people actually give. >> The experimenter does not decide in advance whether a response will be based on form or meaning. || So the norm captures whatever connection the cue really brings to mind.
9|TREE is a cue for the association task. || The participant's first responses give evidence about which words are **readily connected** with TREE. >> This evidence holds for that task context. >> Different people can supply different associates for the same cue. || The **norm** summarizes all those responses. >> It does not treat one response as the word's only association. || So a cue stays the same physical word, while its associates vary across people.
10|TIGER is a cue that connects to two other cues in different ways. || For **LION**, the link is **similar meaning**, because both are animals. >> For **STRIPES**, the link is a **characteristic pattern** that tigers have. || An animal can be associated with one of its properties. >> Two animal names can be related through shared category membership. >> Both kinds of link can be part of the structure of a **lexical network**. || So **association** and **similarity** are not the same relationship.

12|Three cues are tabulated as norms because they matter later. These are **LION**, **TIGER** and **STRIPES**. || The three cues connect meanings in different ways. >> LION and TIGER name **similar animals**. >> TIGER and STRIPES connect an animal with a **characteristic property**. >> Their associations can be measured by counting how often participants give one word in response to another. || The pairs TRIPE and STRIPES, and PEAR and BEAR, make a separate point. >> The two words in each pair overlap in form. >> Similar form does not guarantee similar meaning. >> The relationship between the sound or spelling of a word and its meaning is largely **arbitrary**. || So three relationships must be kept apart. These are similarity of form, similarity of meaning and the tendency of one word to bring another to mind.
13|Coming up with associates raises questions about how the norms were produced. || Participants may have used a deliberate **strategy** to come up with associates. >> A strategy can change which words a person writes. || The **list structure** could influence the answers. >> A list of only wild animals could encourage one set of responses. >> A list of only household appliances could encourage a different set. >> The surrounding list changes which ideas are easy to bring to mind. || **Associated** and **similar** are different. >> **Association** is a tendency for one item to bring another to mind. >> **Similarity** is about shared properties. >> TIGER and STRIPES are associated, but they are not two similar objects. || So a norm is evidence about responses under specific conditions. Interpreting it means asking how the cue was presented and what other cues were present.
14|Menn's chapter is titled "How Brains Work". A better title might be "Some basic metaphors from neuroscience that are useful in psycholinguistics". || These **metaphors from neuroscience** are the basis for understanding most theory and experimental design in the field. || Five ideas make up this vocabulary. >> **Activation** is the activity level of a representation. >> **Spreading activation** passes activity to connected representations over **weighted connections**. >> **Distributed representations** encode an item as a pattern across many units. >> **Associative learning**, such as **Hebbian learning**, strengthens links between units that are active together. >> **Bottom-up processing** runs from sensory features toward words. **Top-down processing** runs from word knowledge back to the features. || So these ideas connect how knowledge is organized with the time course of recognizing and producing language.
15|**Hubel and Wiesel (1968)** related neural activity to sensory input. Their work came at the same time as the **Cognitive Revolution**. || They recorded from **simple cells** in **primary visual cortex (V1)**. >> A simple cell responds to lines of particular orientations. >> It responds within a particular **receptive field**, which is the part of the sensory input the cell responds to. || They changed the orientation and movement of a line and compared the cell's activity. >> For upward movement of a line at about **315 degrees**, the cell fired **most strongly**. >> For other orientations and movements, the cell fired much less. || The **electrochemical activity** of a cell tells us about its representational and computational properties. >> This cell responds selectively. So it can be described as **representing** that combination of features. || So a neuron's activation can stand for a specific combination of stimulus features.
16|Cells further up the **visual processing stream** respond to more complex patterns. || In data adapted from **Gross and colleagues (1985)**, one cell in **inferotemporal cortex** responds to the profile of another monkey's face. >> For near-profile views, its firing rises strongly above baseline. >> For frontal and rear views, its response is much weaker. >> Its responses are highest around 80 to 100 degrees of face angle. || It seems natural to say this cell **represents** something complex, such as a face profile, rather than line segments. >> Its activation is **graded**. The cell responds a little to one view and much more to another. || In psycholinguistics, **activation** extends this idea metaphorically to **perceptual and conceptual representations**. So a word or a concept can also be more or less active.
17|Imagine an incredibly simple set of **lexical representations**. These are the stored representations of words. || Each word is a **node** in a network. >> A node is a unit with an activation value. >> A specific word can activate its node. >> This network has three nodes, for lion, tiger and stripes. || The printed input and the nodes are different levels. >> The visible word LION supplies perceptual evidence. >> The lion node represents the word the system could recognize from that evidence. || So a node lets a model state how much activity a word receives and how that activity affects recognition.
18|A word node must respond to many different-looking inputs. || The word can be printed as LION, lion or Lion. >> It can also change in typeface and size. >> Each change alters the physical display. >> All of these versions should activate the same lion node. || This is the **lack of invariance problem**. The same word has many different sensory forms. >> The same problem came up with **phonemes**. >> Eventually the **receptive fields** of word nodes would have to be very complicated to handle this. || For now, imagine there are nodes that can be activated by words. So the lion node maps many printed forms onto one word identity.
19|**Activation can be partial.** A node does not have to be fully on or fully off. || Here the word LION appears inside a **noise mask**, which is visual noise around the word. >> LION contrasts poorly with the mask. >> So the input gives only weak evidence for the word. >> So the lion node receives only **weak activation**. || The node can be partly active while recognition is still uncertain. >> Its activation can rise as more evidence comes through the noise. || So a **graded** activation value captures the stages before a word is identified.
20|As the stimulus becomes clearer, **activation increases**. || Two things change together. >> In the input, LION becomes easier to tell apart from the noise. >> In the model, the lion node becomes **more active**. || Stronger sensory evidence supports the same word more strongly. >> So recognition can be modeled as a response that grows with the strength of the input. || That is why changing the display can change how a word is processed.
21|At some point the **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)** becomes very favorable. SNR is the strength of the word signal relative to the noise around it. || At that point, activity for lion is strong enough to count as recognition. >> The simple network has **identified** the word lion. >> Tiger and stripes stay as other nodes, not the identified word. || This is how the **perceptual identification task** works. >> Participants push a button as soon as they can identify the item. >> The measure is how long it takes to identify it. >> A condition that makes identification easier moves the response earlier. || So the button press links an observable response to the rise of activation in the model.
22|Two kinds of factors can change how quickly a word is identified in this task. || **Features of the stimulus itself** concern the target word. >> How often the word is encountered is one such feature. >> How similar it is to other words is another. || **Features of the experimental environment** concern what surrounds the target. >> Having just seen the same word is one such feature. >> Having just seen a similar or related word is another. || So the same target can be tested in different contexts. Different targets can also be compared under the same task conditions.
23|Four factors can change how quickly a word is identified. || Two factors are **features of the stimulus**. >> **Word frequency** is how often a word is encountered. A frequent word can be easier to activate. >> Similarity to other words creates **neighborhood** relationships. || Two factors are **features of the experimental environment**. >> **Repetition priming** comes from having just seen that same word. >> Other priming comes from having just seen a similar or related word. || Frequency and repetition priming are different. >> Frequency reflects experience with a word built up over time. >> Repetition priming reflects a very recent presentation. >> A related prime changes the context without repeating the target. || So explaining an identification advantage means asking what differed between conditions.
24|Most models of this kind assume that activation **unfolds over time**. This change over time is called **activation dynamics**. || Activation dynamics can explain **frequency effects**. >> The dynamics are **tuned up by experience**. >> Words encountered more often become more sensitive. >> So frequent words are **easier to activate**. || The **Interactive Activation model** of **Rumelhart and McClelland (1986)** simulates this kind of process. >> For the word CAVE, activation rises above competing words while the input supports it. >> Then the activation gradually falls. || Different computational models simulate frequency effects in different ways. So frequency is tied to processing through experience.
25|Activation dynamics also explain **repetition priming**. Repetition priming is faster identification of a word seen again very shortly after the first time. || Activation does not rise and fall instantly. >> Activation may **fall off more slowly than it rises**. >> After the word is gone, some activation remains. || Now the word appears again while activation is still high. >> The **baseline** for its activation is already higher than usual. >> So the node can be activated more easily. || Repetition priming is a basic effect in psychology. That is why seeing a word again soon makes it faster to identify.
26|Two factors that influence a word's activity have been left out so far. || The first is **neighborhood effects**, which come from how similar a word is to other words. >> The second is priming from a similar or related word. >> This priming can be **associative**, **semantic** or **phonological**. || Both factors need something new in the network. >> Isolated word nodes can explain changes in one word's own activity. >> Repetition involves the same word twice. >> These effects involve one word changing a **different** word. || So the network needs links between word representations.
27|Many language phenomena require that nodes in a network are **connected**. After all, it is a network. || In a network with lion, tiger and stripes, the nodes are linked. >> Lion is connected to tiger. >> Tiger is connected to stripes. || The printed input activates lion directly. >> A connection can then carry activity from lion to tiger. >> This activity is **indirect**, because it arrives through the network rather than from the stimulus. || So a connection changes how one node responds when another is active.
28|**Spreading activation** explains how one word can prepare the system for a related word. || The association norms can be built into a network. >> The **connection strength** between two nodes is set by their association. >> When lion is activated, some activation travels along a **weighted connection** to tiger. >> Different connections can pass different amounts of activation. || This is a spreading activation account of **semantic priming**. >> Tiger is already partly active from the prime lion. >> So tiger is easier to recognize when it appears. || A semantic network can link many concepts. Vehicle links to car and bus. Fire engine links to the vehicles and to fire concepts. So one activated concept can spread to several related ones.

31|The contrast of STRIPES increases. More of its letter pattern stands out against the noise. || The letters are faint but easier to pick out from the mask. >> The input now gives **stronger support** for the same word. || The identity of the target does not change. >> Only the quality of the evidence changes. || So clarity changes the evidence while the word stays the same.
32|STRIPES becomes more **identifiable** as its contrast improves. || The letters now stand apart from the surrounding noise. >> The evidence for the word keeps growing. || In the perceptual identification task, the participant responds when the evidence is enough to recognize the word. >> The point of response depends on the quality of the stimulus. || So better stimulus quality brings the **identification response** closer.
33|The clearer STRIPES gives stronger word information than the heavily masked versions. || The noise field is the same kind as before. >> Only the letter pattern has become clearer. || A participant who recognizes the word can now make the **identification response**. >> The response reflects **recognizing the target word**. >> It is not a judgment about whether stripes go with a particular animal. || So the task measures recognition, not association.
34|STRIPES is now shown at high contrast. The dark letters stand out from the gray noise. || The target is easy to tell apart from the mask. >> Recognizing it activates the **stripes node** in the network. || This identified word can act as a **prime**. A prime is an earlier item that may change how a later item is processed. >> Its effect on another word depends on the **connections** between their nodes. || So identifying STRIPES prepares a test of what it does to a later word.
35|LION is the **target** in a second perceptual identification sequence. || It starts with weak contrast against the noise mask. >> So its letter pattern gives little usable information. >> The short word sits in the center of the noise and is hard to read. || The target can be separated from its recent context. >> A **priming** account asks whether earlier experience changes how quickly LION is recognized. || So the sequence tests whether the preceding word speeds up recognizing LION.
36|The contrast of LION increases. || LION is now faintly visible in the center of the mask. >> The letter pattern starts to separate from the background noise. >> So the **perceptual evidence** for the word gets stronger. || The same target can produce a **changing internal response**. >> This happens because the quality of its sensory evidence changes. || So recognition becomes more likely as the display gets clearer.
37|LION becomes clearer inside the mask. The four letters have more contrast than before. || A perceptual identification response can happen when the evidence supports recognizing the word. >> The **time** of that response is what gets measured. || A priming comparison asks whether recognition happens **sooner** after one preceding context than after another. >> LION is the common target in that comparison. || So response time for the same target is how priming is detected.
38|LION is now legible, while the noise mask is still present. || The stimulus gives substantial evidence for the word lion. >> In the network, that evidence raises the **activation of the lion node**. || Two sources can add to the lion node's state. >> One is the **clarity** of the target itself. >> The other is any **activation left by the context**. || So recognition happens from a state built by both the input and the context.
39|The clear LION display completes the identification sequence. The strong word signal is easy to tell apart from the noise. || The lion node can receive activation in two ways. >> It can be activated **directly** by the printed word. >> It can also be influenced **through connections** with other nodes. || The difference between direct input and activity arriving through the network is central to this experiment. So the question is whether STRIPES helped LION through the network.
40|Activity flows throughout the network. So activating stripes can **prime lion** even though the two words are **not directly associated**. || The path runs through a middle word. >> Stripes is connected to tiger. >> Tiger is connected to lion. >> So activation from stripes reaches lion by way of tiger. || This is called **mediated priming**. The priming passes through a middle word, here tiger. >> A model with only direct associations has no route from stripes to lion. >> So it could not explain the effect. || That is why this effect gives **compelling evidence for spreading activation**.
41|The stripes and lion experiment is very representative of psycholinguistics. || It has **careful study design**. >> It finds **small effects**. || The result is **counterintuitive**. Why would stripes prime lion? >> Stripes and lion are not obvious direct associates. >> Stripes primes lion because activity travels through the connections each word has with tiger. >> A model with only direct associations cannot explain this effect. >> So even a small effect can tell the two accounts apart. || Three broader issues remain. These are top-down and bottom-up processing, learning and distributed representations.
42|**Bottom-up processing** carries information from visual features up to letters and then to words. **Top-down processing** lets an active word send activation back to the letters that fit it. || The **Interactive Activation network** after **McClelland and Rumelhart (1981)** has three levels. >> Features are at the bottom. >> Letters are in the middle. >> Words such as WORK, WORD, WEAR and WEAK are at the top. || In the example, blots cover part of the last letter of WORK. >> The visible features fit more than one letter. >> WORK becomes active at the word level and sends activation back down to K. >> So the output for **K rises strongly** over time, while R and D stay low. || Top-down processing is one way of modeling **pattern completion**, which Menn discusses at length. So word knowledge helps complete a letter that is only partly visible.
43|**Learning** explains how all of this knowledge gets into the brain. The core idea is extremely simple but powerful. || **Hebbian learning** is summed up as "neurons that fire together, wire together." >> When two units are active together, the connection between them gets stronger. >> The real process is more complicated than this. >> But the neural learning mechanisms that are relatively well understood are **associative**. || In the bell and food example, a bell signal and a food signal both feed a salivation unit. >> The bell input and the food input are active together. >> So the **synapse** from the bell to the salivation unit is strengthened. >> An initially weak influence becomes more effective. || Historically, these mechanisms were considered **insufficient** to learn anything important about language. >> More recent arguments ask what role they play and how **innate knowledge** might constrain them. >> This matters because **large language models (LLMs)** learn many structural features of language with little more than this. || So associative learning changes the network that will process future input.
44|**Distributed representations** let associative learning pick up fairly sophisticated patterns. A distributed representation encodes an item as a pattern across many units. || **Generativity** is very important for language. >> To generalize, an associative model must be able to represent **similarity**. >> Similar items have overlapping patterns. >> So what is learned about one item can carry over to similar items. || Robins and canaries are the case here. >> What you know about robins and canaries can **generalize** to new birds as you learn about them. || The network takes an **item** input and a **relation** input. >> For canary with the relation can, the active outputs include grow, move, fly and sing. >> Hidden layers link the item and relation to that set of properties. || So shared internal patterns let a model respond to combinations it has not memorized one by one.
45|Five **neurally inspired metaphors** give a vocabulary for psycholinguistics. || **Activation** is how active a representation is, and it changes as input is processed. >> **Spreading activation** lets one representation influence another through connections. >> **Top-down and bottom-up processing** describe the direction of influence between levels. >> **Associative learning**, such as Hebbian learning, changes connection strengths. >> **Distributed representations** give a basis for similarity and generalization. || **Priming experiments** help reveal what people's **lexical networks** might look like. >> They measure how processing one item changes the processing of another. || So these ideas connect representations, mechanisms and observable behavior.
