2|**Speech errors** are slips in which a speaker produces something other than the intended utterance. They reveal how an utterance is planned. || People make speech errors because many items are active at once during planning. >> **Noise**, **frequency**, other active material and **similarity** can each help the wrong item win. || Speech errors interest psycholinguists because each error has a structure. >> An error can split a word into its parts. >> An error can move a sound into another position. >> An error can replace the intended word with a related word. >> The form of the error gives evidence about the units the production system uses. || **Fromkin** titled the article "Speech errors as linguistic evidence." >> Some linguists study **competence** (knowledge of the language) apart from **performance** (actual speaking). >> From that view, an error is a disruption of performance, so it can be set aside. >> Fromkin instead treats the structure of errors as evidence about the language system. That is why the title is gutsy. || So a useful error is one that is compared with the intended form. The parts that changed and the parts that stayed reveal what the system was doing.
3|**Spreading activation** models explain how an unintended item can become available during speech planning. Activation spreads from each word to related items. || Four key concepts explain how speech errors happen in these models. >> **Noise** is not only literal acoustic noise, although that can cause problems too. It is also fluctuation and interference in activation in different parts of the system. >> **Frequency** affects how easily an item becomes active. Common words are easier to activate than less common words. This effect seems to reach many levels of speech production. >> **Other sources of activation** add competitors. What a speaker just said stays active. What the speaker plans to say is already active. Things the speaker is thinking about but trying not to say may also be active. >> **Similarity** lets activation spread more easily between related items. The similarity can be semantic, syntactic or phonological. || So the intended item competes with alternatives that get activation from several sources at once. A speech error happens when one of those competitors wins.
4|A **tip-of-the-tongue (TOT) state** is partial activation of a word's meaning and sound form. That activation falls short of the **threshold** for producing the word. || The person knows the meaning but cannot retrieve enough of the sound form to say it. >> Less frequent words are more susceptible to TOT states. Their forms are harder to activate, so they reach the threshold less easily. || A **prime** (a related word read just before) can help. >> The prime adds activation to the missing sound form. >> That extra activation can push the form over the threshold. || **Lise Abrams** reviewed an example in which a definition asked for goods traded illegally. The target word was "contraband." >> People in a TOT state read a prime word and then tried the target again. >> **For first-syllable primes** such as "contact," "connotation" and "conscious," about **50 percent** of TOT states were resolved. >> **For middle-syllable primes**, resolution was intermediate. >> **For final-syllable primes**, resolution was close to the unrelated condition. >> **For unrelated words**, only about one quarter were resolved. || So priming helps most when the overlap is at the start of the word.
5|**Slips of the tongue** differ in the direction a sound moves. Spooner supposedly once said "Mardon me padam, this pie is occupewed. Can I sew you to another sheet?" || These examples come from Fromkin. Each type compares the intended form with the produced form. >> **In anticipation**, a sound planned for later appears too soon. "Week long race" became "reek long race" because the upcoming r replaced the start of "week." >> **In perseveration**, an earlier sound carries into a later position. "John gave the boy" became "John gave the goy" because the earlier g carried into "boy." >> **In metathesis**, also called an **exchange**, two elements trade positions. "Keep a tape" became "teep a cape" because the first k and t swapped. The rest of each word stayed in place. || The type depends on how the two forms relate. It does not depend on whether the result makes sense. || So the order of the intended sounds tells which type of slip occurred.
6|**Other sources of activation** are most obviously at work in these slips. What a speaker just said and plans to say stay active during speech. || Each direction of slip points to a different source of activation. >> **For anticipation**, material prepared for an upcoming position is already active. In "reek long race," the r is available before its own position. >> **For perseveration**, material just spoken is still active. In "John gave the goy," the g keeps its influence after its own position. >> **For an exchange**, two elements in the same planned utterance interfere with each other's positions. || The other concepts also play a part. >> **Similarity** and **frequency** affect which candidates compete well. >> **Noise** (fluctuation in activation) can affect which candidate gets selected. || Planning reaches beyond the sound being said right now. This overlap lets a speaker prepare what comes next. That is why the wrong active element can sometimes fill a position.
7|A **mondegreen** is a **slip of the ear**, in which a listener mishears what was said. The listener groups the sounds into different words than the speaker intended. || The term itself comes from a mishearing. >> "Laid him on the green" was heard as "Lady Mondegreen." >> In a song, "Hold me closer, tiny dancer" is misheard as "Hold me closer, Tony Danza." The real words are "Tiny Dancer," like the title of the song. >> **Andrew Nevins** is analyzing crowdsourced mondegreens from kissthisguy.com. || Speech is ambiguous because it arrives as a continuous stream. So listeners must infer the **word boundaries**. >> "The good candy came anyways" and "The good can decay many ways" sound very similar. >> A listener tells them apart mainly by **context**. || Context matters most in songs. In songs, **prosody** (the rhythm and melody of speech) is less helpful. So listeners lean on context and familiar words, and sometimes they pick the wrong ones.
8|A **malapropism** of this kind is a **perceptual error** more than a speech error. The speaker intends to say exactly what they said. || The error starts when the speaker hears an unfamiliar word. >> The speaker hears a word like "congenital," which means present from birth. >> The speaker decides that the intended word must have been a more familiar word, such as "congenial." >> Later the speaker describes "a congenial heart defect" and means it. || This is **top-down processing** (using known words and expectations to interpret what is heard). >> A familiar word helps organize the heard sound. >> The familiar word wins over the less familiar target. || Other collected examples include "lettuce days," "decaffeinated him" and "Utopian tubes tied." || So a production slip and a mislearned expression can sound equally odd. They arise through different processes.
9|**Fromkin** used speech errors as data about the units of **speech planning**. At the time, researchers argued about whether the **phoneme** (or phone, a single speech sound) was a unit in planning. || One possibility was that whole consonant **clusters** always move together. >> Then errors would have looked like "gish frotto" or "clesh freer water." || Instead, Fromkin presents evidence that clusters get broken up all the time in production. >> **For "fish grotto,"** the error was "frish gotto." The r left the gr cluster and joined the f in the first word. >> **For "fresh clear water,"** the error was "flesh queer water." The r and the l exchanged. The first consonants f and k stayed in place. >> **For "split pea soup,"** the error was "plit spea soup." Only the s moved out of the cluster. || So single segments can move on their own. That is why the phoneme counts as a unit in speech planning.
10|**Fromkin (1971)** proposed an early version of the production model that Menn describes. The model explains how an intended meaning becomes an utterance. || The model runs through a series of stages. >> It starts with the **meaning to be conveyed**. >> A syntactic structure generator and a semantic feature generator build **syntactic-semantic structures**. >> An intonation contour generator adds **primary stress** and **intonation**. >> The **lexicon** then supplies words specified for their features and the order of their segments. >> This gives strings of segments divided into syllables. >> **Morphophonemic rules** and **phonetic rules** then fully specify the sound sequence. >> **Motor commands** to the muscles finally produce the **utterance**. || Each stage is a separate representation. So an error can affect one stage while the other stages stay organized. That is why different kinds of errors give evidence about different stages.
11|The **basic model** of speech production has five levels. Each level solves a different problem in producing an utterance. || The levels come in a fixed order. >> The **message level** holds what the speaker intends to convey. >> The **functional level** selects **lemmas** (word identities before their final form) and links them to **semantic roles**. >> The **positional level** arranges them into a clause. >> The **phonological encoding level** specifies the sound structure. >> The **speech gesture level** prepares the movements used to say it. || Following Menn, the message and functional levels come first. The sound slips such as "reek long race" were already phonological encoding errors. || Separating the levels matters because the everyday word "word" blurs them together. Choosing a fitting word is different from choosing its grammatical form. It is also different from assembling its sounds. So different errors can be traced to different levels.
12|The **message level** decides what information a speaker intends to convey. The same event can become different messages. || Take the sentence "I won a bunch of money on the game Sunday." >> **For a fan of the losing team**, the listener's feelings may affect how directly the speaker stresses the win. >> **For someone not interested in football**, the speaker may need to explain why the event matters. >> **For a tweet**, the short public post changes which details are included. || The event stays the same in each case. The purpose and the chosen information change with the audience. || The message level is mysterious and hard to investigate experimentally. Giving speakers the same event does not mean they all intend the same content. So the message depends on the audience, the goals and what the listener already knows.
13|Some speech production studies try to control the message level with an **elicitation paradigm** (a task built to draw out speech). Speakers describe the same picture book, such as "Frog, where are you?" || The picture book gives every speaker the same scene. >> The scene contains a boy, a dog and frogs. >> Researchers can compare descriptions because the visible material is held constant. || The intended message is still hard to define. >> One speaker may focus on the boy, and another on the dog or the frogs. >> Speakers may assume different knowledge in the audience. >> Age, culture and audience may change how the message is built. || So the method controls what speakers see. It does not fully control what they intend to say.
14|A **lemma** is a word's identity before its final grammatical form is set. The term is borrowed from mathematics, where it refers to a proposition in an argument. || Selecting whole "words" at the functional level cannot be right. One error explains why. >> The intended sentence was "Where'd I put it when I brought it in." >> The produced sentence was "Where'd I bring it when I put it in." || The verbs switched places, but the moved verb changed tense. >> "Where'd" already contains the past-tense helper did. A verb after did takes the base form. >> So the moved verb came out as "bring," not "brought." >> If the word "brought" had been selected, it would have moved unchanged. >> PUT looks the same in both forms, so BRING gives the visible evidence. || Instead, the lemma BRING was selected and moved. Its tense was applied later, in its new position. That is why the functional level selects lemmas rather than finished words.
15|An **error in lemma selection** can replace the intended word with its **opposite**. People do this often because opposites are closely related in the lexical system. || Each example keeps the sentence and swaps one word. >> **For like and hate**, a speaker said "I really like to" and corrected it to hate. >> **For bottom and top**, a speaker said bottom and meant the top of the stack of books. >> **For hot and cold**, a speaker said "This room is too damn hot" and corrected it to cold. >> **For oral and written**, a speaker said oral and meant the written part of the exam. || Each pair shares a domain, such as temperature, position or exam format. The wrong word still fits the sentence. So the sentence stays well formed while its meaning is reversed.
16|**Antonyms** (words with opposite meanings) are actually quite similar at several levels. So activation spreads easily from a lemma to its antonym. || Two kinds of overlap give the antonym strong activation. >> Antonyms are **semantically related**. They share a domain, such as temperature for hot and cold. >> Antonyms are **strongly associated**. Thinking of one brings the other into the same comparison. >> In the spreading activation framework, these overlaps add up to a perfect storm. || Two more kinds of overlap let the antonym pass through. >> Antonyms can take on the **same semantic roles**. >> Antonyms belong to the **same word class**, so they can take on the same syntactic roles. >> So the rest of the speech production system can handle the wrong word. || That is why a big change in meaning can come from a small, grammatical substitution.
17|A **blend** combines parts of two competing words into one produced form. **Lewis Carroll** made blends on purpose and called them portmanteau words. || Carroll explained his word "frumious" in the preface to The Hunting of the Snark. >> A speaker means to say both "fuming" and "furious" but leaves the order unsettled. >> Leaning slightly toward fuming gives "fuming-furious." >> Leaning slightly toward furious gives "furious-fuming." >> A perfectly balanced mind gives "frumious." || **Fromkin** quoted Carroll because he said blends are possible in natural speech. He also proposed a hypothesis about how they occur. || Fromkin noted that such complete blends are seldom found in normal speech. >> A blend of [fjurijəs] and [fjumiŋ] is more likely to come out as [fjumijəs] or [fjuriŋ]. >> This happens because the first syllables are identical. || So spontaneous blends tend to join the two words where their sounds overlap.
18|**Spontaneous blends** reveal competition among alternative ways to express a message. Each produced form mixes two candidate words. || Each blend comes from a pair of competing words. >> "I swindged" blends **switch** and **changed**. >> Other blends mix **mainly** and **mostly**, or **bother** and **trouble**. >> Others mix **swinging** and **hip**, **spirant** and **fricative**, or **player** and **athlete**. || **Fromkin** proposed how blends happen. >> A speaker has a meaning to convey and selects words by matching **semantic features**. >> Where there are several alternatives, the speaker brings both into a **buffer** (a temporary storage compartment) with their sound specifications. >> Then either one word is selected, or the two words are blended. >> These errors seem to support Carroll's assumptions. That is why Carroll would have been an awesome linguist. || Speech production nearly always has a number of alternative possibilities. Choosing among them is an ordinary part of speaking. So blends make that everyday competition visible.
19|**Semantic roles** (also called functional roles) describe how each participant relates to an event. The roles stay the same when the sentence form changes. || The same event can be said two ways. >> **In the passive** "The plums in the icebox were eaten by me," the order is Undergoer, Location, Verb, Agent. >> **In the active** "I have eaten the plums in the icebox," the order is Agent, Verb, Undergoer, Location. || At the functional level, lemmas get slotted into these roles. >> The verb is TO EAT. >> The **agent** (the one doing the action) is ME. >> The **undergoer** (the thing the action happens to) is PLUM. >> The icebox is the **location**. || The order of the roles has syntactic consequences. >> The lemma ME becomes "I" in the active sentence. >> The lemma ME becomes "me" after "by" in the passive sentence. >> This choice of word form happens later, at the **positional level**. || So the event stays fixed while its sentence form changes.
20|At the **functional level**, a lot of planning is already done. Yet the speaker is nowhere near putting a clause together, much less saying it out loud. || Three things are done by the end of this level. >> The speaker has decided on a **message** to convey. >> The speaker has selected appropriate **lemmas**. >> The speaker has linked the lemmas with **semantic roles**. || Much is still undecided. >> For an eating event, the plan has EAT, the one eating and the thing eaten. >> It has not yet decided whether the sentence is active or passive. >> It has not yet set how the pronouns will be expressed. >> It has not yet set the full sound sequence. || So later levels still have to order the lemmas and turn them into speech.

23|Very often **speech errors go undetected**. **Top-down effects** in speech perception explain this. || Listeners use context and expectations to recover the intended message. >> So a speaker's real error can pass without notice. >> A similar process is assumed for many malapropisms and mondegreens. || Some "errors" are made on purpose for stylistic reasons. >> **Paul Krugman** wrote that "the upper hand is on the other foot," quoting Hot Shots Part Deux. >> The phrase combines "the upper hand" with the idea that the shoe is on the other foot. || A deliberate error can be **more memorable** than its correct counterpart. >> The odd mismatch draws attention to the wording itself. >> The listener recognizes both familiar phrases, so the mix is funny. || So whether an error was intentional matters when it is used as evidence about speech production.
