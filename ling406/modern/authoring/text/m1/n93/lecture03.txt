4|A **word form** depends on the job the word does in its sentence. || The argument is over a Scrabble game. One player says knifes is not a word because the plural of knife is knives. The other player answers that Tony knifes Bernardo in West Side Story. >> **For the noun** knife, the plural is **knives**. That is **inflectional morphology**, which changes a word's form for grammar, as in plural formation. >> **For the verb** knife, the form after he is **knifes**. The noun became a verb by **zero derivation**. Zero derivation is **derivational morphology** that changes the part of speech without adding an affix. >> **In "He knifes him"**, the pattern is a singular noun, then a verb, then a noun. The singular subject requires the **third person singular** ending on the verb. || So each player is right about a different word. Knives is the noun's plural, and knifes is the verb form that the sentence requires.
5|**Derivational morphology** is a process that derives new words from existing parts. The parts, or units, are called **morphemes**. >> A morpheme contributes meaning or grammatical information. It is not the same thing as a syllable or a single speech sound. || The word morphology has a fairly transparent derivation. >> **The part morph-** comes from Greek and means form. >> **The part -ology** also comes from Greek and here means something like the study of. >> Together they give "the study of the forms of words". || A similar process gives the word **morpheme**. >> Phone is another morpheme from Greek, probably meaning speech. It gives **phoneme**, the unit of speech sound. >> By the same pattern, a unit in text should get a name built the same way. >> The pattern does not always work. Graphology looks like it should be the study of those text units, but that is not what the word means. || So a complex word can be taken apart into morphemes that appear again in different words.
6|**Derivational morphology** is **productive**, which means it can make new words. It is productive only in limited ways. >> Made-up words are hard to guess, because the meaning of the final form is only **partially predictable**. || **Zero derivation** changes the part of speech of a word without adding anything. Even there, the new word usually works with a **narrow sense** of the base word. >> **For the noun** knife, many objects count. Image search results for knife include different cutting tools and tableware. A search for prison shiv gives an improvised weapon. >> **For the verb** knife, only one kind of action counts. To knife somebody is to attack that person with a knife. >> So the weapon-like objects, such as the prison shiv, fit the verb naturally. A table knife is still a knife, but it is not a natural fit for knifing somebody. || That is why changing a noun into a verb also takes knowledge of which sense the verb conventionally uses.
7|**Derivational morphology** is also limited in how widely and how regularly **affixes** are applied. An affix is a part added to a stem, such as -hood. || The pattern is a stem plus **-hood**. It gives a state of being that is associated with the stem noun. >> **For brotherhood and sisterhood**, the pattern works as expected. >> **For knighthood**, the result is a little odd. Two brothers share their brotherhood, but each knight has his own knighthood. >> **For neighborhood**, the word is perfectly good, but -hood does a different job entirely. It does not mean the state of being a neighbor. >> **For friendhood**, English says **friendship** instead, for no apparent reason. >> **For enemyhood**, there is no such word, although maybe there ought to be. The nearest words are less common ones such as enmity or antagonism. || So a productive affix does not attach equally well to every stem that would make sense. Knowing an affix includes knowing which words actually use it. >> Some of these words come from Liberman and Prince's introductory linguistics course at Penn.
8|**Zero derivation** changes a word's category without adding a visible affix. Even so, it does complicated things with **semantics**, which is meaning. || Dish, spoon, fork and table are strong associates of the noun knife. Their meanings as verbs are **not similar** to each other. >> **In "She can dish it out but she can’t take it"**, dish is a verb. Its meaning is learned and does not come from naming the object. >> **In "He was born with a silver spoon and a forked tongue in his mouth"**, forked takes the shape of a fork. Forked tongue also has an established figurative meaning. >> **In "They decided to table the issue after a day of inconclusive negotiations"**, table is a verb with a learned meaning. || Reading these as literal references to utensils or furniture would miss the intended meanings. || So words that are closely related as nouns can become verbs with unrelated meanings. That is why the meaning after zero derivation depends on **conventional usage**. >> The silver spoon expression is the kind of thing studied in **Cognitive Linguistics**.
9|**Derivational morphology** more commonly changes the pronunciation and spelling of a word. One way is by adding **affixes**. || Adding an affix can change the **phonology** of the rest of the word. Phonology is the sound pattern of the word. >> **For certificate**, the pronunciation is /sər.ˈtI.fɨ.kɨt/. >> **For certification**, it is /ˌsər.tɨ.fɨ.ˈkeI.ʃən/. >> **For recertification**, it is /ˈri.sər.tɨ.fɨ.ˌkeI.ʃən/. >> The **stress** falls on different syllables across the three words, and some vowels change too. || This is one reason for "The Chaos" of English spelling. Spelling that follows the spoken form more closely does not necessarily make words easier to read. || Some noun and verb pairs differ mainly in **stress**. >> **For record**, the noun and the verb have the same spelling but different stress. >> **For invite**, the same holds in "He did not receive an invite" and "We did not invite him". >> **For entrance**, the noun and the verb also differ in stress. Those two words are not actually related to each other. || So a derived word is not always the base pronunciation with an ending attached.
10|**Inflectional morphology** changes the meaning of words in a more **predictable** way than derivation does. It gives a different grammatical form of the same word. || **For plurals**, knife(n) becomes knives. One fork becomes two forks. One mouse becomes a lot of mice. || **For the third person singular present**, knife(v) becomes knifes. I walk becomes she walks. You run becomes he runs. || **For the past**, knife(v) becomes knifed. Today I walk becomes yesterday I walked. Today she sings becomes in the past, she sang. || Menn writes that “...we’d think it was silly to have separate dictionary entries for the singular and plural forms of nouns.” >> So inflected forms belong to one dictionary word and are not new words. || The phonological process of inflecting words often has some **irregularity**. >> Forks and walked use regular endings. Mice and sang change the sound of the word instead. >> The grammatical meaning is the same in both kinds. Mice is still plural and sang is still past. >> This irregularity matters for different language learning models and for kinds of developmental language impairment.
11|**Inflectional morphology** also depends on the **syntax** of the rest of the sentence. Syntax is how words are organized into phrases and sentences. || The phrase "two [noun]+pl" has a slot for a noun. Whatever noun goes there must be pluralized somehow. >> **For a regular noun**, the slot gives two forks. >> **For an irregular noun**, the slot gives two knives. >> The plural requirement stays the same whichever noun fills the slot. || **Derivational morphology** has no syntactic process like this, even for very productive forms. >> The closest thing is a formula such as “Once you [verb] it, you can un-[verb] it.” >> That formula is not syntax. No sentence requires every verb to have an un- form. || So syntax can require an inflection, but it does not require a derivation.
12|English is fairly limited in its use of **inflection**. Other languages mark much more grammatical information on their words. || English inflects for three things here. >> **For plurals**, there are knives, two forks and a lot of mice. >> **For the third person singular present**, there are knifes, she walks and he runs. >> **For the past**, there are knifed, walked and sang. || Many other languages do more. >> **For person**, many languages conjugate verbs much more elaborately than English does. The same is true for tense. >> **For number**, many languages have different ways of forming the plural for small and large numbers. >> **For nouns**, many languages have **case markings**. Case markings encode information about syntactic or semantic roles. || So English has inflection, but it does not have the full range found across languages.
13|**Inflectional** and **derivational morphology** share two properties and differ in the rest. || **Both kinds** create new phonological forms out of old parts. Both kinds change the meanings of base forms. || **Derivational morphology** creates **new words**. >> It is productive, but not always, and its productivity is typically limited. >> The verb knife made from the noun knife is derivational. || **Inflectional morphology** changes how words interact syntactically with other words. >> It is **highly productive**, but it is not always regular. >> It is **required by syntax**. >> The form knifes after he is inflectional. || So the test is what the change does. A change that makes a new word is derivation. A change that gives the form a sentence requires is inflection. >> Regular or irregular sound does not decide between them.
14|Sentences are built from more than single words. Words group into **phrases**, **clauses** and **sentences**. || **Content words** include nouns and verbs. Other content word classes are modifiers such as adjectives and adverbs. >> Parts of speech have come up only in a limited way before this point. That is because the cases were mostly single nouns and verbs. || **Function words** matter once more than a couple of words are put together. Determiners and prepositions are function words. >> They organize how the content words relate to each other. || The sentence “The video for “More than Words” came out before most of you were born” has two **noun phrases**. >> **The first noun phrase** is The video for “More than Words”. >> **The second noun phrase** is most of you. >> The two differ in length, so a noun phrase is not defined by a number of words. || So understanding a sentence takes more than knowing the meanings of its nouns and verbs.
15|The **noun phrase** The video for “More than Words” has three parts. Its structure is a determiner, then a noun, then a prepositional phrase. || Two **function words** organize it. >> **The** is a **determiner**. It begins the noun phrase. >> **For** is a **preposition**. It begins the prepositional phrase. >> The **prepositional phrase** lets the song title specify which video is meant. || **Video** is the head noun of the phrase. || The title “More than Words” is not really part of the sentence here. It is being used like a noun. >> With the quotes, the title names the song as one unit. >> Without the quotes, it is not a phrase. It is a bunch of words strung together in a row. || So this NP has the structure **Det + N + PP**. Det is the determiner, N is the noun, and PP is the prepositional phrase.
16|The noun phrase structure **Det + N + PP** is incredibly **abstract**. Phrases with different words and lengths can share it. || Three phrases have the same structure. >> **For "two guys from the early ‘nineties"**, two is the determiner and guys is the noun. From the early ‘nineties is the modifier. >> **For "a band with only one hit"**, a is the determiner and band is the noun. With only one hit is the modifier. >> **For "one and only one phrase unrelated to early ‘nineties one-hit wonder Extreme"**, one and only one is the determiner. Phrase is the noun, and the rest is the modifier. || The parts can differ inside. >> A determiner can be several words, as in one and only one. >> The first two modifiers are prepositional phrases that begin with from and with. >> The third modifier begins with the adjective unrelated, followed by a to phrase. It is an adjective phrase with a prepositional phrase inside it. || So the structure names positions and categories, not particular words.
17|An **abstract structure** such as noun phrase leaves out the individual words. This abstraction is needed for two reasons. || **Within one language**, it lets very different surface forms count as similar. Without it, there is no way to say that two phrases with different words are alike. || **Across languages**, it lets similarities and differences be stated coherently. All languages have **NPs**, but they can form them very differently. >> **For English**, the phrase is the cat in the coffee shop. The determiner comes first and the prepositional phrase follows the noun. >> **For Chinese**, the phrase is 咖啡店裡面的小貓, which is coffeeshop inside + cat. The order is PP then N. >> The Chinese NP has **no determiner**. || This helps explain why learning to use determiners in a second language (L2) is so difficult for speakers of Chinese languages. Their own NPs are formed without one, so the determiner has to be learned new.
18|A **prepositional phrase** is a preposition followed by a noun phrase. Its general structure is **P + NP**. || Breaking down three prepositional phrases gives the same structure each time. >> **For "from the early ‘nineties"**, from is the P. The NP is the early ‘nineties, which is Det + Adj + N. >> **For "with only one hit"**, with is the P. The NP is only one hit, which is given as P + Det + N. >> **For "unrelated to early ‘nineties one-hit wonder Extreme"**, unrelated to is treated as the P. The NP is early ‘nineties one-hit wonder Extreme, given as Adv + Adj + Adv + Adj + N. || The NP inside can be short or long. The preposition relates that whole NP to the rest of the expression. || So all these prepositional phrases are just **P + NP**.
19|Phrase structure is **hierarchical**, which means phrases sit inside other phrases. || Consider the phrase from the early ‘nineties. >> The **determiner**, the **adjective** and the **noun** are all inside the **noun phrase**. >> The noun phrase is inside the **prepositional phrase**. >> The prepositional phrase is inside a sentence, or inside yet another noun phrase. || A flat row of words with curly brackets and parentheses is a clumsy way to write this. The row has one level, but the structure has several. || **Trees** handle the levels better. A tree gives each phrase a node above the parts it contains. >> That is partly why people started using trees to diagram sentences. || So words that sit side by side can belong to different levels of structure. Hierarchy is also an important feature of languages.
20|A **verb phrase** is a verb together with the material that completes it. Verb phrases differ in what follows the verb. || In the sentence “The video for “More than Words” came out before most of you were born”, the verb phrase is V + PP. >> The verb is came out. >> The PP is before most of you were born. >> That PP is hard to unpack. Most of you were born looks like a verb phrase, but it specifies a point in time that complements before. || Many verb phrases also contain **noun phrases**. >> **For sang the song**, the structure is V + NP. >> **For worked himself to death**, the structure is V + NP + PP. Himself is the NP and to death is the PP. >> **For threw a party for him**, the structure is V + NP + PP. A party is the NP and for him is the PP. || So the verb is not the whole verb phrase. The phrases that follow the verb belong to it too.
21|A **clause** is a verb phrase and its subject. The **subject** is the noun phrase that controls the verb. || The sentence divides into two parts. >> **The NP** is The video for “More than Words”. Its structure is Det + N + PP. >> **The VP** is came out before most of you were born. Its structure is V + PP. || The clause is found by pairing the VP with its subject. >> The whole first NP is the **subject**. >> The **head noun** of that NP is video. >> The song title sits inside the PP, so it is not the subject. || So the whole sentence is **one clause**. It has one VP and one subject.
22|The subject **controls** the verb, which means the verb agrees with the **head** of the subject noun phrase. || “The plums that were in the icebox were delicious” is a simple sentence with one clause. >> Its subject NP is The plums that were in the icebox, given as Det + N + PP. Its VP is were delicious. >> The verb agrees with plums, not icebox, because plums is the head of the noun phrase. || “The plums in the icebox, which you were probably saving for breakfast, were delicious” has a **clause within a clause**. The inner clause is set off with commas. >> **For the inner clause**, you is the subject of were probably saving for breakfast. >> **For the outer clause**, plums is the subject of were delicious. >> Changing you to my roommate changes only the inner verb. It becomes was probably saving, and were delicious stays the same. || **In "Forgive me"**, the subject is **implicit**, which means it is not said. The speaker wants whoever shares his icebox to forgive him for eating the plums. || So each verb agrees with its own subject, not with the nearest noun.
23|Two sentences can mean the same thing and still be very different **syntactically**. || **In "I have eaten the plums in the icebox"**, the subject comes first. >> The **subject** is I. >> A verb phrase follows it. >> The verb phrase includes the NP the plums in the icebox. That NP is the **object** of the verb. || **In "The plums in the icebox were eaten by me"**, the plums in the icebox becomes the **subject**. >> The evidence is agreement. The verb were eaten agrees with the plural noun plums. >> The phrase by me is just a PP that fills out the VP. It names who did the eating, but it is not the subject. || The first sentence is **active** and the second is **passive**. || So the subject is the phrase that controls the verb. It is not always the one who did the action.
24|**Semantic roles** say what each participant does in the event. **Syntactic roles** say what each phrase does in the sentence, such as subject or object. || The roles in the plum sentences are **agent**, **undergoer** and **location**. The agent is the eater, the undergoer is the plums, and the location is the icebox. || **For the passive sentence**, the order is Undergoer + Location + Verb + Agent. >> The plums is the undergoer, in the icebox is the location, and by me is the agent. >> The subject is the undergoer. || **For the active sentence**, the order is Agent + Verb + Undergoer + Location. >> I is the agent, the plums is the undergoer, and in the icebox is the location. >> The subject is the agent. || So the **semantic roles do not change** between the two sentences. Only the syntactic roles change. >> English usually aligns the syntactic subject with the agent. That is why it seems counterintuitive that me is not the subject of the passive sentence. >> Grade school teaches that grammar tells "who did what to whom". Semantic roles do that job more than syntactic roles do. >> The relationship between syntactic and semantic roles also influences sentence processing.
25|Language is highly **generative**, which means familiar parts can be combined into new words and new sentences. || Three processes make it generative. >> **Derivational morphology** makes new words. >> **Syntax** organizes words into clauses, clauses into phrases, and phrases into sentences. >> **Inflectional morphology** modifies words for different syntactic roles. || People do this mostly **without thinking explicitly** about it. >> A speaker can pick a plural or build a phrase without naming any grammatical category. || One of the hardest things to understand about linguistics is what there is to explain. That is because some of the most complex phenomena are hiding in plain sight. || So ordinary speaking uses derivation, inflection and syntax together, even though it feels effortless.
