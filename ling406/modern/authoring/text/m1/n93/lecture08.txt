2|A **speech production model** describes how a speaker goes from a message to spoken words. The lower three levels finish the job after the functional level. || Each lower level has its own task. >> At the **positional level**, the chosen words are placed into a grammatical structure. >> At the **phonological encoding level**, the words become a string of **phonemes** (speech sounds that tell words apart). >> At the **speech gestural level**, the motor system turns that string into movements. || Speech errors give evidence for **abstract units**. >> Units such as **phonemes**, **lemmas** and **noun phrases** cannot be heard directly. >> Errors move these units or leave them in place in regular ways. That is why the units are useful in a theory of speaking. || Collected errors can be sorted by the level where they happened. Purely **observational research** also has weaknesses. So **experimental methods** are needed as well.
3|The sentence "Where'd I bring it when I put it in?" is an error with two verbs. It was earlier evidence that **lemmas** (words chosen for meaning and grammar, before their sounds) are a useful idea. || The **functional level** settles three facts for this sentence. >> There are two verbs, PUT and BRING. >> I/ME is the **Agent** (the doer) of both verbs. >> IT is the **Undergoer** (the thing acted on) of both verbs. || The functional level still leaves other facts open. >> It does not set the **temporal relationship** between the two verbs, which the word when expresses. >> It does not build the question or choose the tense of each verb. || The **positional level** expresses all of these facts in syntax. So word order, the question form and the verb forms are set there.
4|The **positional level** builds a syntactic structure with **slots** for the lemmas. Without the question-forming steps, the frame is roughly "Where'd I [verb, root form] it when I [verb, past] it in?" || Each slot asks for its own verb form. >> **For the first clause**, the slot needs the **base form** of the verb. Getting this clause right took real syntax, because it has **Wh- movement** and an **auxiliary verb**. >> **For the when clause**, the slot needs the **past form**. >> The frame also holds the other **particles** the grammar requires. || The lemmas from the functional level are then fed into these slots. The error arises during this filling. >> BRING went into the root form slot, so it came out as bring. >> PUT went into the past slot, so it came out as put. || So the verbs landed in the wrong slots, but each took the form its slot required.
5|The error "a language needer learns" is like the bring and put error in one way. It differs in another way. || The intended phrase is "a language learner needs." >> **The similarity** is that two lemmas, LEARN and NEED, got **switched** while being fed into the positional level. >> The positional frame stayed mostly the same. It is still a noun phrase followed by a verb. || **The difference** is in the noun. >> The noun is actually a **verb plus an affix**. >> In learner, the verb learn carries the ending -er. In the error, need carries that ending instead, giving needer. >> Adding -er to a verb is **derivational morphology** (making a new word from an existing one). || So this error swaps roots inside words, not just whole words.
6|In "a language needer learns," two lemmas switched while the **positional level** stayed mostly the same. The interesting part is the noun. || The noun slot is built as a **verb plus -er**. >> **-er** is a **derivational affix** (an ending that makes a new word). Here it turns a verb into a noun for the doer, as in learner. >> The verb slot asks for the **third person singular** form. Its ending -s is **inflectional morphology** (an ending set by grammar). || The tree has a noun phrase (NP) and a verb phrase (VP). >> Inside the NP, the noun position is a verb plus -er. >> Inside the VP, the verb position is a third person singular verb. || The roots NEED and LEARN swapped slots. The endings stayed with their slots. >> So need got -er and became needer. >> Learn got -s and became learns. || If the whole words learner and needs had swapped, the endings would have moved too. That is why the affixes count as **stranded** in their positions.
7|The error "He did that easy enoughly" should have been "He did that easily enough." It raises the same two questions as "a language needer learns." || In the intended phrase, the ending **-ly** attaches to easy. >> **In the error**, -ly attaches to enough instead. >> So easy loses its ending, and the odd form enoughly appears. || The two questions ask how the errors compare. >> One question is how this error is **similar** to "a language needer learns." >> The other is how it is **different**. || Both answers involve where the affix went and how the two words were arranged. So the intended and produced forms must be compared word by word.
8|"He did that easy enoughly" and "a language needer learns" share a **stranded affix** (an ending that stays in its slot while the root changes). || The shared part is the affix. >> **-ly** can make an **adjective** into an **adverb**. >> In both errors, an ending sits on the wrong root. || The difference is in the frame. >> In "needer," the **positional frame** stayed the same. >> In "enoughly," something also went wrong **while setting up the positional level**. || Two trees compare the frames. Each has an adjective phrase (AP) on top. >> **In the intended frame**, the adverb slot comes first. It is built as an adjective plus -ly. The plain adjective slot comes second. >> **In the starred error frame**, the adjective slot comes first and the adverb slot comes second. >> Easy filled the first slot, and enough filled the second slot. So enough got the -ly. || That is why this error points to a mistake in the frame itself, not only in filling it.
9|Errors like needer and enoughly are evidence that **derivational morphology** is real. **Affixes** seem able to float around at the **positional level**. || Another view says derived words are just separate stored words. >> In that view, they link to their roots in some rule-like way. >> The lexicon would hold sets such as needy, need and needless, or unlearn, learn and learner. >> Then affixes would need no separate representation. Speakers would not need quirky rules for building common words. || Errors argue against that view. Speakers make **non-words** like "needer" and "enoughly" on the fly. These words cannot come from a stored list. So the errors suggest three things. >> **Lemmas and affixes** are somehow represented **separately**. >> **Affixes have a slot** in the positional level. >> **Derived words** can be produced by a **grammatical process**. || So the speaker builds some words while speaking, not only retrieves them.
10|Adding an affix at the **positional level** still leaves **phonological work** to do. The sounds of the new word must still be worked out. || Simple cases add sounds to the end. >> Easy /’i.zi/ becomes easily /’i.zIl.i/. >> Enough /i.’nəf/ becomes enoughly /i.’nəf.li/. || Other cases change the base itself. >> The word certificate is pronounced /sər.ˈtI.fɨ.kɨt/. >> The word certification is pronounced /ˌsər.tɨ.fɨ.ˈkeI.ʃən/. >> The word recertification is pronounced /ˈri.sər.tɨ.fɨ.ˌkeI.ʃən/. >> Across these forms, **vowels change** and **stress moves**. || The positional level only states a process like adding -er or making an adjective into an adverb. That process does not give the final sounds. So a later level must still turn the plan into the right pronunciation.
11|**Phonological encoding** turns lemmas, affixes and syntactic particles into a **string of phonemes** to produce. || For "easy enoughly," this level worked fine. >> The speaker did not say "easy /inəvɪli/." >> The speaker made no other error in the sequence of phonemes. >> So the error happened earlier, at the positional level. || Other errors do happen at this level. >> For **fish grotto**, the speaker said gish frotto. >> For **Chomsky and Halle**, the speaker said Chomsky and Challe. >> For **with this ring I do wed**, the speaker said with this wing I do red. || In each case, the problem is the **ordering of phonemes**. >> Menn describes these errors as **"neat."** >> They follow the **phonotactic patterns** (rules for allowed sound sequences) of English. >> As far as anyone knows, they are also **clearly produced**. || So a phonological encoding error gives a clean wrong sound sequence.
12|The **speech gestural level** turns the planned phonemes into **gestures** carried out by the **motor system**. || Errors here differ from phonological encoding errors. >> They are **not necessarily "neat."** >> They result in **unclear or imprecise speech**. || **Many errors are ambiguous**. >> Ad hoc came out as odd hack. In sounds, /æd hɑk/ became /ɑd hæk/. >> /æ/ is a low front vowel, as in bat. /ɑ/ is a low back vowel, as in hot. || Two accounts fit this error. >> **At the phonological encoding level**, it could be **metathesis** (two sounds switching places in the plan). >> **At the gestural level**, it could be a **miscuing of tongue position** during speaking. || So the same error could come from the plan or from the movements.

14|Each collected error has to be placed at the level where it happened. Some are about word forms, and some are about sounds. || Two examples involve word forms. >> **For buyed in**, the speaker meant bought in. Buyed applies the regular past ending to a verb with an irregular past. This is **morphological regularization**. >> **For arising**, the speaker said "arriving." One consonant changed in a closely similar word. || Two examples involve sounds. >> **For pretty close to here**, close was said as [kloʊz]. The final consonant became voiced, unlike the voiceless one expected for this close. >> **For thoroughly**, the speaker said "throfly." Several parts of the sound sequence changed. || So each error is analyzed by comparing the intended sounds with the produced sounds. Then the changed unit points to a level.
15|More collected errors move different units. Some move whole words, and some move single sounds. || Some errors move words. >> In "Wash my teeth and brush my face," the **verbs** wash and brush switched. Their objects stayed in place. || Some errors move sounds. >> **For following vowel**, the speaker said "vollowing fowel." The first consonants **switched**. >> **For seat heaters**, the speaker said "heat seaters." Sounds moved, but the plural ending stayed on seaters. >> **For speech-language pathology**, the speaker said "speech plath... speech plang." The attempts and repairs are part of the record. || Some errors bring in a familiar sequence. >> **For Lana del Rey**, the speaker said "Marina del Rey." >> "For all intensive purposes" replaces part of a familiar phrase with different words. >> "It's in the stimulus" is another collected error. || So each error has to be checked for which unit moved and which stayed.

17|The speaker said "you enter a hundred-dollar room and you do yoga." The talk was about **hot yoga**. || The context is what makes it an error. >> In a talk about cost, a hundred-dollar room would be normal. >> In a talk about hot yoga, the hundred could describe the room's heat, not its price. >> So dollar is a real word, but it does not fit the meaning intended. || The sentence is otherwise fine. >> The clause is **grammatical**. >> The words are easy to pronounce. || So the error lies in **choosing the word** for the message, not in the sounds or movements.
18|The speaker said "Law and Order SUV." The intended title is Law and Order SVU. || The two forms use the same three letters. >> **SVU** belongs to the show's title. >> **SUV** is a familiar vehicle abbreviation. >> The last two letters **switched order**. || The produced form is a real, familiar sequence. >> So a competing familiar form may have been **selected**. >> The switch should not be assumed to be a simple motor slip. >> The result is still easy to say. || So the analysis starts with the exact change, SVU becoming SUV. It then asks which familiar forms were competing.
19|The speaker said "He cooked his kitchen." The intended word was chicken. || Kitchen and chicken are related in two ways. >> **In sound**, the words are very similar. Their first and middle consonants appear in opposite positions. >> **In meaning**, both relate to cooking. || The grammar stayed intact. >> Both words are nouns. >> Both fit the slot after his. >> So the sentence keeps its **grammatical frame**. || So this error mixes **sound similarity** with **meaning context**. The analysis has to look at both the word forms and the situation.
20|**Observational studies** of speech errors depend on what the observer notices and reports. || The observer's **expectations** matter. >> What the observer listens for decides which errors stand out. >> Learning about speech production can change those expectations. >> So expectations can affect both what is heard and what is reported. || Placing an error in the model also needs **context**. >> The analyst must reconstruct the speaker's intended message. >> The analyst must also know the situation in which the error happened. >> A form can be fine alone but wrong for what the speaker meant. || So a collection of errors reflects the observer as well as the speaker.
21|**Speech perception** biases which errors observers report. || Listeners have three biases. >> They are not very good at hearing sounds **"halfway" between two phonemes**, especially for **consonants**. >> They are **biased against** hearing **phonotactically illegal strings** (sound sequences the language does not allow). >> They are **biased toward** hearing ambiguous sounds **as words**. A sound partway between "giss" and "kiss" sounds more like "kiss." || Philosophers of science call this **theory-dependent observation**. >> Because of how perception works, observers might miss many errors. >> They might even miss **whole classes of errors**. || **Experimental methods** and careful measurement can fix some of these problems. They cannot fix all of them. So reported errors are not a full record of the errors people make.
