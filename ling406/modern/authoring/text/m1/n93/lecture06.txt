2|A **formal symbolic system** describes language with abstract symbols and rules. This way of describing language is very old. It uses terms similar to those used in **Artificial Intelligence**. || **Experimental psychology** in the 1950s and 1960s was actively hostile to talk of an **internal state**. >> Behaviorism explained learning through observable stimuli, responses and consequences. >> An internal state cannot be observed directly, so psychologists of that era avoided it. || Two works framed the debate. >> **Skinner's Verbal Behavior** tried to describe how language could be learned by **operant conditioning**. >> **Chomsky's (1959) review** of Verbal Behavior challenged that account. It is better remembered than the book itself. || Chomsky treated a **grammar** as an internal system. A grammar can characterize sentences a person has never encountered. || So Chomsky's review was an important moment in **the Cognitive Revolution**. That revolution brought internal mental representations back into explanations of language.
3|The formal tradition comes from interest in **Artificial Intelligence**. That interest dates back to the beginning of the **digital computer**. || **Alan Turing** thought about this question a lot. >> He developed decryption devices for the British Army during World War II. || Early computers were built as **universal function approximators**. >> A universal function approximator is a machine that can carry out many kinds of calculations and logical operations. >> No one had built a machine that did so many different kinds of operations before. >> Earlier machines did a single fixed task. || This flexibility raised a new question. >> Researchers asked how the computer differed from **human intelligence**, if at all. >> The difference could lie in the procedures, the representations, or something else. || So language research became tied to Artificial Intelligence. If language is symbols and procedures, a computer can test what those procedures accomplish.
4|The **Turing test** judges a machine by how it behaves in conversation. It grows out of a party game called the **imitation game**. || The imitation game has three players. >> **A and B** are a man and a woman. >> **C** is an examiner. C's job is to figure out the gender of A and B. >> **A** tries to fool C. >> **B** tries to help C guess the correct answer. || The test replaces either A or B with a **computer**. >> If C does not notice the swap, the computer has demonstrated **intelligence**. >> The examiner never inspects the program. The examiner only judges the conversation. || So the Turing test turns the broad question of intelligence into observable conversational performance. **Natural language** is the medium the examiner uses to decide.
5|The Turing test has several interesting features. Each one points toward ordinary language use and learning. || The first feature is the role of **natural language**. >> The machine must interpret questions and produce fitting answers. || The second feature is **deception**. >> One player tries to fool the examiner. >> So a respondent has to handle an examiner who may be trying to expose it. || The third feature is what Turing left out. >> Turing explicitly excluded **expert behaviors**. >> Calculating large sums is one excluded behavior. >> Composing poetry is another. >> Broad conversation is a different task from one expert skill. || The fourth feature is Turing's conclusion about **learning**. >> The best way to get an intelligent machine is to build something with the cognitive capabilities of a **child**. >> That machine then learns to be more like an **adult**. || So Turing treated intelligence as something that develops through experience. It is not supplied as a finished list of adult answers.
6|**Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver** is a later example of **GOFAI**. GOFAI is the approach that models intelligence as operations on explicit symbols. || The program receives an abstract description of a formal problem. >> It then applies procedures to symbols to find a solution. >> Each configuration of the problem is a **state**. A legal move changes one state into another state. >> A solution is a sequence of legal moves that reaches the **goal state**. || The program was very good at games and puzzles. >> The **Tower of Hanoi** is one example. Each arrangement of disks is a state, and each legal move links two states. >> **Chess** is another example. || It was also good at mathematics and logic. >> It handled **geometry proofs**. >> It handled **algebra word problems**. || So the General Problem Solver treats solving a problem as searching for a path through states. That search works only once the states, goal and legal moves are spelled out.
7|Early AI emphasized **formal problems** for two reasons. One reason was technology, and the other was theory. || The first reason was **technology**. >> Inputs to computers were still very limited. They came through punchcards and machine language. >> There was no way to input a high-resolution image and ask the computer to find the face. >> So explicit symbols were far easier to give a computer than rich perceptual information. || The second reason was **theoretical commitments**. >> Researchers believed **rational behavior** of this kind is what intelligence looks like. >> You can train a **pigeon** to guide a missile, but the pigeon does not know what it is doing. >> There is a deep argument about whether AI programs know what they are doing either. >> AI programs at least **represent goal states**. They also choose procedures for reaching them. || So formal puzzles fit both the machines and the theory of the time. That is why early programs modeled this kind of intelligence.
8|**Chomsky's critique** argued that knowing a language means having an internal **grammar**. A grammar is a system that enumerates sentences and gives each one a meaningful **structural description**. || A grammar captures several remarkable abilities. >> A speaker can tell **sentences from nonsentences**. >> A speaker can understand **new sentences**, in part. >> A speaker can notice **ambiguities**. || New sentences are not linked to familiar ones by any simple **similarity**. >> The similarity is not formal, semantic or statistical. >> It is not identity of grammatical frame either. >> So Chomsky called talk of **generalization** here pointless and empty. || A new item counts as a sentence because the internalized grammar **generates** it. >> We understand a new sentence partly because we can work out how the grammar **derives** it. || All normal children acquire comparable grammars of great complexity with remarkable speed. >> Chomsky took this as evidence that humans are **specially designed** to do it. >> He proposed a data-handling or **hypothesis-formulating** ability of unknown character. || So Chomsky said language needs an internal grammar, not conditioning.
9|Chomsky's first point is that **linguistic knowledge** is a **grammar**. A grammar is a set of abstract rules that produce grammatical sentences. || The number of grammatical sentences is **infinite**. >> A speaker has heard only a small part of them. >> Yet a speaker can make a **grammaticality judgment** for any new sentence. >> A grammaticality judgment is a decision about whether a sentence is well formed in the language. >> That ability is remarkable because the sentence was never heard before. || The grammar also explains ambiguity. >> One string of words can receive more than one structure. >> So knowing the words alone does not fix the meaning. || These judgments become central evidence. >> People's **intuitions** about whether sentences are grammatical are the key data for the study of language. >> The researcher asks which structures a person accepts, not only which sentences the person has said. || So the target of study is the rule system that makes all possible sentences available.
10|Chomsky's second point attacks **stimulus generalization**. Stimulus generalization is a behaviorist idea. A response trained to one stimulus spreads to similar stimuli. || Generalization needs similarity between the trained item and the new item. >> Grammatical sentences have no obvious similarities to one another. >> Their sounds can differ. Their meanings can differ. Their frames can differ. >> So similarity cannot link a new sentence to familiar ones. || A grammar can generate an **infinite number of sentences**. >> Stimulus generalization cannot cover that infinite set. || Chomsky offered a different explanation. >> We recognize a new sentence because the **internalized grammar** generates it. >> It does not need to match one remembered sentence. || So a theory of language has to explain how people recognize abstract grammatical relations. Spreading a trained response to a similar stimulus is a different process.
11|**Stimulus generalization** in behaviorism depends on concrete similarity between stimuli. || A **pigeon** can be trained to peck for a **green light**. >> Its pecking then generalizes to similar colors. >> Responding is strongest near green. It drops as the test color becomes less similar. >> This pattern is a **generalization gradient**. || Another pigeon does a **magnitude discrimination** task. >> It responds according to amounts, so number is another dimension of comparison. || The cases where operant conditioning generalizes share a feature. >> They depend on relatively concrete similarity, such as **color** or **number**. >> Each dimension can be varied step by step. || It is not clear how this extends to **abstract concepts**. >> Grammar is an abstract relation. >> No concrete dimension like color links one grammatical sentence to another. || So a conditioning account of language must say what plays the role of color for grammar. That is why generalization gradients do not explain sentences.
12|**Stimulus control** is Skinner's idea that some aspect of the environment controls a response. || Skinner's typical example is music. >> A person hears a piece of **music** and responds with an utterance. >> The music is treated as the stimulus that controls the utterance. || Chomsky said other examples only add to the **general mystification**. >> A **proper noun** is one such example. Skinner treated it as a response to a stimulus. || The trouble is identifying the stimulus. >> A piece of music has many properties. >> A speaker can respond to any of them. >> The stimulus has to be identified **independently** of the utterance. >> Otherwise the utterance itself decides what counts as its cause. || So stimulus control explains an utterance only if the stimulus can be named before the response.
13|Chomsky argued that **stimulus control** cannot predict speech. We do not know what the current stimuli are until the speaker responds. || Chomsky's reading is that Skinner posed the **wrong question**. >> Skinner does not only have a bad theory of language. >> His question itself cannot be answered. || What people say is decided by many things. >> **Social interactions** affect it. >> **Cognitive processes** affect it. >> **Previous experiences** affect it. >> Much of this has nothing to do with language. >> So naming the stimulus only after the response makes the prediction **circular**. || Chomsky proposed a **tractable problem** instead. >> Researchers study the **structure** of what people say. >> From that structure, they try to derive the set of **rules** that produces it. || So a grammar describes which sentences are possible. It does not explain why one person says one thing at one moment.
14|**Grammatical structure** and meaning can come apart. Chomsky (1955) gave a pair of sentences to make this point. || Neither sentence, nor any of their parts, had ever occurred in an English speaker's past experience. >> Sentence (a) is "colorless green ideas sleep furiously". >> Sentence (b) is "furiously sleep ideas green colorless". || For **sentence (a)**, the words follow English structure. Adjectives come before the noun, and a verb and adverb follow. So it is **grammatical**. >> For **sentence (b)**, the same words are reversed. The structure is gone, so it is **not grammatical**. || So a speaker's judgment cannot come from having heard the sentence before. It comes from the **structure** the grammar assigns. || A second example points the other way. >> The warning "Car! Getting towed! Yours!" is **ungrammatical**. >> Yet the stimulus that likely controlled it is perfectly clear. >> So communicating successfully and being grammatical are separate questions.
15|Chomsky describes language at the **computational level of analysis**. This term comes from **David Marr**, a visual neuroscientist. || Marr said cognitive systems have three **levels of analysis**. >> The **computational level** says what problem the system solves. >> The **algorithmic level** says what representations and procedures solve it. >> The **implementational level** says how those procedures are physically carried out. || The levels ask different questions about the same ability. >> A claim can be informative at one level without answering the others. >> Separating them makes clear whether a claim is about the task, the procedure or the physical machinery. || Chomsky's abstract grammar is a **computational** description. >> It states what language processing must achieve. || So Marr's levels place Chomsky's theory at the level of the task itself. An example makes the levels concrete.
16|A **cash register** makes Marr's three levels concrete. This example borrows heavily from the teaching of **Sharon Goldwater**. || Each level describes the cash register differently. >> At the **computational level**, it takes item prices and determines the amount due. Given the payment, it computes the change due. >> At the **algorithmic level**, it uses **addition** for the price. It uses **subtraction** for the change. >> At the **implementational level**, the machinery does not matter. || A credit card reader or a computer program counts as a cash register too. >> A mechanical register uses one kind of machinery. >> An electronic register or card reader uses another kind. >> Each one performs the same functions. || For the same prices and payment, the change due is fixed by the task. >> Changing the machinery does not change that answer. || So one function can have many physical forms. That is why the task can be described without knowing the hardware.
17|Marr called problems that break down cleanly into his levels **Type I problems**. || Viewing language as a Type I problem divides the work. >> Researchers can work at the **computational level**. For language, this is **Universal Grammar**. >> Researchers can also work at the **algorithmic level**. This covers specific processes and how they apply in different languages. >> They do not have to worry about the **implementational level**. || The implementational level includes several things. >> It includes the **errors** people make. >> It includes **memory limitations**. >> It includes **associative learning**. || The order of events matters. >> **Chomsky's critique** came about a decade before **Marr's** theoretical work. >> Both were influential in artificial intelligence and in theoretical work in the **GOFAI** tradition. || So the Type I view lets grammar be studied as a system of its own. Processing details are set aside until the computation is known.
18|Marr also described **Type 2** problems. These do not break down cleanly into levels. || Marr first linked his levels to Chomsky. >> Chomsky's **competence theory** for English syntax is exactly what Marr meant by a computational theory. >> Both care little about the details of the algorithms that run the computation. >> Algorithms are still hard to devise. But first one must know what they are supposed to do. >> A problem that divides this way has a **Type 1 theory**. || Many problems of biological information processing have a Type 1 theory. >> There is no reason they all should. || A **Type 2 theory** arises from many processes acting at once. >> The problem is solved by the simultaneous action of a considerable number of processes. >> Their interaction is its own **simplest description**. || So a Type 2 problem cannot be split into an abstract computation plus details. The interacting processes themselves are the explanation.
19|Marr suggested that the **grammar of natural language** may be a **Type 2** problem. || Marr described the purpose of language this way. >> A speaker holds a **data structure** that is not one-dimensional. >> Language turns it into a **one-dimensional** form. That form is sent as a **sequential utterance**. >> The listener then rebuilds a rough copy of the original in their head. || This raises a possibility about **English syntax**. >> There may be no Type 1 theory of the kind **transformational grammar** tries to define. >> The constraints of syntax may resemble **wired-in conventions**. Those conventions are useful ways of carrying out this tedious but vital operation. >> They may not be deep principles about the nature of intelligence. || An abstract theory of syntax may be an **illusion**. >> It may only approximate what the processes really do. >> Marr compared it to **Julesz's second order statistics theory**. That theory only approximates the processes that carry out texture vision. || So grammar may have a Type 2 theory rather than a Type 1 theory.
20|Details of how language is **implemented in the brain** might be unavoidable in a theory of language. || This follows from the Type 2 idea. >> Linguistic behavior may come from interacting processes. >> If it does, learning, memory, perception and action belong in the explanation. >> So the brain's organization may shape the organization of language itself. || A whole alternative approach to language research is based on this hypothesis about the **brain**. >> The first approach starts from an abstract grammar that the brain must carry out. >> The alternative starts from the learner and the processing system. >> It asks what language-like organization can develop from them. || So the two approaches differ in where an explanation starts. The alternative treats implementation as part of the theory of language.
21|The alternative approach goes by several names. These are **emergentism**, **statistical learning** and **parallel distributed processing**. || Its strong commitments are at the **implementational level**. || The first commitment is **distributed representations**. >> These representations are non-hierarchical. >> A representation is spread over a group of units. It is like activity patterns over groups of neurons. || The second commitment is **gradual learning**. >> Learning adjusts the strength of connections. It is like adjusting the strength of synaptic connections. >> Experience changes how an input pattern leads to an activity pattern. || The third commitment is a focus on **microstructure**. >> Many small interactions together produce a larger regularity. >> The division between **levels of analysis** is more graded. || So this approach expects the learning mechanism to shape the apparent rules in behavior.
22|**Elman (1990)** built **simple recurrent networks**. These networks capture different aspects of language performance in the emergentist framework. || The model has only one task. >> It tries to **predict the next item** it is going to see. >> It gradually learns to do this with **experience**. || The network passes information through several groups of units. >> The current **input** activates the **hidden units**. >> The hidden units produce an **output** prediction. >> A copy of the hidden activity goes into **context units**. >> When the next input arrives, the context units feed the hidden units too. || Some connections change and one does not. >> The connections that learn are adjusted to improve the prediction. >> The connection that copies hidden activity into context is fixed. || So the network's prediction depends on both the current item and what came before. Its context gives it a limited memory of the sequence.
23|A **letter-prediction model** built on these principles can find **words**. It was never given spaces. || The model receives a continuous string of letters. >> The string is "Manyyearsagoaboyandgirllivedbytheseatheyplayedhappily". >> It comes out organized as "many years ago a boy and girl lived by the sea they played happily". || The model's only job is to predict the next letter. >> **Prediction error** measures how wrong each guess is. || The error changes in a regular way. >> At the **start of a word**, the error is high. Many letters could come next. >> **Within a word**, the error **goes down**. Each letter limits what can follow. >> At the next word boundary, the error rises again. || So the peaks in error mark **word boundaries**. That is why word-like organization appears without any instruction to divide the input.
24|A similar model trained on **simple sentences** begins to identify **grammatical classes**. It even picks up some **semantic information**. || The model's task is still to predict the next word. >> The categories appear as a **side effect** of learning to predict sequences. >> No one told the model which words are nouns or verbs. || Elman examined the **hidden-unit activation patterns** for each word. >> Words used in similar contexts developed similar patterns. >> Similar patterns cluster together. || The clusters follow grammar and meaning. >> **Nouns** and **verbs** form separate broad groups. >> Nouns split into **animates** and **inanimates**. >> Animates split further into **humans** and **animals**. >> Inanimates include groups for **food** and **breakables**. >> Verbs split by how they take a **direct object**. || So knowing a word's class helps predict what can follow it. That is why prediction training produces grammatical and semantic grouping.
25|Many engineering solutions in **Natural Language Processing** work the same way. **Massive corpora** are analyzed statistically to find patterns. Corpora are collections of texts and transcriptions of spoken language. || **Peter Norvig** discussed Chomsky's critique. >> He agreed that neither sentence appeared in the published literature before 1955. >> But every two-word part had occurred before. >> "Colorless green" appeared in a 1942 book. >> "Green ideas" appeared in The New Republic in 1922. >> "Ideas sleep in books" appeared in Current Opinion in 1912. || **Pereira (2001)** tested a statistical model on the two sentences. >> The model was a **finite-state model** augmented with **word categories**. >> It was trained by **expectation maximization** on **newspaper text**. >> It computed that sentence (a) is **200,000 times more probable** than sentence (b). || Both sentences use the same words. >> So the difference comes from how the words and their categories are arranged. || So a statistical model can tell the two sentences apart. That challenges the claim that new sentences are beyond statistical learning.

27|Psycholinguistics has two important traditions. They differ in what linguistic knowledge is assumed to be. || The first tradition is **GOFAI**, **modular** or **Chomskyan**. >> It assumes linguistic knowledge is essentially **abstract and symbolic**. >> It assumes linguistic representations are **different in kind** from other knowledge. >> This knowledge still has to **interface** with messy perceptual, motor, memory and other cognitive capacities. >> The psycholinguist's job is to study this interface. || The second tradition is **emergentist** or **functionalist**. >> It assumes symbol and rule-like behavior are **emergent properties** of a **statistical learning** process. >> It assumes perceptual, motor and cognitive processes actually **shape** linguistic representations. >> The psycholinguist's job is to study **language use**. It relates language use to more general psychological phenomena. || So the traditions disagree about where regularities come from. One says a distinct abstract system. The other says the processes that learn and use language.

30|The **emergentist** or **statistical learning** approach starts from the messiness of language. The **"i before e" rule** has exceptions, and that messiness is all over every language. || Each level of language shows it. >> **Phonetic categories** are probabilistically defined over multiple dimensions. >> **Words** are ambiguous in every way imaginable. >> **Morphological paradigms** have exceptions. "Add -ed" forms the English past tense, but RUN-RAN and SLEEP-SLEPT break it. >> **Syntactic rules** are open to dialect and individual variation. They are also open to innovation and change over time. || A strong pattern and its exceptions exist side by side. >> Adding -ed works for many verbs. >> Irregular forms remain part of what speakers know. || So no single fixed rule covers each level. That is why this approach lets experience build graded expectations.
31|**Moravec's paradox** is a problem for the **GOFAI** approach. It says the tasks hard for computers are the opposite of those hard for humans. || For **computers**, proving theorems and solving geometry problems is comparatively easy. >> A supposedly simple task is extremely difficult. Recognizing a face is one such task. >> Crossing a room without bumping into anything is another. || For **humans**, the opposite is true. >> Recognizing faces and walking across rooms feel easy. >> Proving theorems feels hard. || The paradox raises a question about language. >> Characterizing language in formal terms, as for a computer program, may be a **false trail**. >> Perceptual and motor processing may matter more than formal rules suggest. || So formal problem solving may not be a complete model of intelligence or of language.
32|Fixed categories and rules are often **fuzzy** in practice. This has been recognized for a long time. || **Wittgenstein (1953)** made the point in **Philosophical Investigations**. >> His example is the category of **games**. >> Board games, card games, ball games and Olympic games are all called games. >> When you look at them, no single property belongs to every one. || These members are held together by **family resemblance**. >> One pair of games shares a feature. >> Another pair shares a different feature. >> The similarities overlap without one defining feature. || This challenges strict definitions. >> A useful category does not need one list of necessary properties. || So categories can stay coherent even when their members differ. That is why fixed rules often turn out to be fuzzy.
33|Many phenomena in language are **probabilistic**. Wittgenstein's games make the same point about categories. || **Wittgenstein (1953)** compared different games in **Philosophical Investigations**. >> Some games involve **amusement**. Some involve **winning and losing** or **competition** between players. Others involve **skill** or **luck**. >> These features do not occur in every game in the same combination. || The comparison changes from game to game. >> For **amusement**, chess is compared with noughts and crosses. >> For **competition**, patience is a game with no opponent. >> In **ball games** there is winning and losing. When a child throws a ball at the wall and catches it again, that feature disappears. >> For **skill**, skill in chess differs from skill in tennis. >> **Ring-a-ring-a-roses** keeps amusement, but many other features disappear. || Features that seemed central in one game disappear in another. >> Similarities crop up and disappear. >> The result is a complicated network of similarities **overlapping and criss-crossing**. >> Some are overall similarities, and some are similarities of detail. || So a category can rest on graded similarity rather than a fixed definition. That is why many phenomena in language are probabilistic.
34|**Family resemblances**, **similarity-based generalization** and **hunches** challenged formal accounts of the mind. Many psychologists produced results of this kind. || Each researcher worked in a different area. >> **Roger Shepard** studied **perception**. >> **Eleanor Rosch** studied **categorization**. >> **Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky** studied **decision-making**. >> Many others did similar work. || Their results made a lot of cognition seem less suited to **formal, rational interpretation**. >> People relied on similarity and graded structure. >> Their judgments did not follow a simple idealized rational procedure. || These areas point the same way. >> Language may also reflect similarity-based generalization. >> It may reflect properties of actual processing. || So psychology as a whole moved toward explanations based on real representations and processing.
