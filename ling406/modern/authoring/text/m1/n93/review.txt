4|The consonant chart organizes sounds by three **features**. Each feature describes one separate property of how a sound is made. >> **Place of articulation** is where the vocal tract closes or narrows. It runs along the horizontal axis, from the front of the vocal tract to the back. >> **Manner of articulation** is the degree of closure. It runs along the vertical axis, from fully closed to open. >> **Voicing** is the timing of vocal fold vibration. It separates the two sounds in each pair. || The two members of a voicing pair differ in when voicing starts. >> For the **voiced** member, voicing starts before or during the closure. >> For the **voiceless** member, voicing starts after the closure. || Bilabial sounds such as p and b sit toward the left, because the lips are at the front. Velar sounds sit farther right, because they are made farther back. || So every consonant gets three answers. These are where it is made, how closed it is, and when voicing starts.
5|**/p/** and **/b/** can each be described as a bundle of **features**. Features describe how sounds are alike and different in a precise, well-defined way. >> /p/ is plus **plosive**, plus **bilabial** and minus **voiced**. >> /b/ is plus plosive, plus bilabial and plus voiced. || The two sounds share the same **manner** and the same **place** of articulation. >> Both are plosives, so the airflow is fully stopped and then released. >> Both are bilabial, so the lips make the closure. || The two sounds differ only in **voicing**. >> Voicing here means the timing of **phonation** relative to the release of the closure. Phonation is vibration of the vocal folds. || The same difference separates **/t/** from **/d/**. That pair is made at a different place, but the voicing contrast is the same. >> So features make it possible to talk in general about **the voiced stops** /b/, /d/ and /g/. This general description is helpful for phonological descriptions.
6|A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in only one sound and also differ in meaning. Minimal pairs are important for showing **contrast**. || Two **phones** are **contrastive** when swapping them changes the meaning of a word. Contrastive phones are examples of different **phonemes**. || The test for two different **low back vowels** works this way. >> You must find two words whose difference depends only on those two vowels. >> For a speaker who distinguishes them, **caught** and **cot** do this job. The consonants stay the same, so the vowel carries the difference. >> For a speaker who merges these vowels, caught and cot do not show two separate phonemes. || So the evidence for a phoneme is a change in meaning. A sound difference alone is not enough.
7|Minimal pairs also show which **feature** a contrast depends on. || In English, three stop pairs all differ in **voicing**. >> **/b/ and /p/** differ in voicing. Both are made at the lips. >> **/t/ and /d/** differ in voicing. Both are made further back, at the alveolar place. >> **/k/ and /g/** differ in voicing. Both are made at the velar place. || Within each pair, place and manner stay the same. Across the pairs, the place changes. >> So the same voicing contrast repeats at three different places. || The evidence comes from comparing minimal pairs in each language. >> A contrast found in English does not automatically hold in another language. || So voicing is one general feature that separates many English words.
8|Phonemes are **not letters**. Literate people easily imagine speech as an orderly stream of letter-like units. Speech is nothing like that. || A **spectrogram** displays the sound of speech over time. >> Time runs from left to right. >> Frequency rises from bottom to top. >> Dark regions mean strong acoustic energy. || The spectrogram of "A sore back is a real pain" has no clean breaks between sounds. >> Neighboring sounds blend into each other through smooth transitions. >> The same phoneme does not have one fixed acoustic shape in every context. || So a transcription is a row of separate symbols, while the speech signal is **continuous**. That is why phonemes cannot be treated as letters lined up in the sound.
9|**Hockett (1955)** described speech with a homely analogy about **Easter eggs**. He called it the best analogy he could think of. >> A row of raw eggs of various sizes and colors rides along a moving belt. >> The belt carries the eggs through the two rollers of a **wringer**. The wringer smashes them and rubs them into each other. || Each part of the analogy stands for part of speech. >> The flow of eggs before the wringer is the series of impulses from the **phoneme source**. >> The mess that comes out is the output of the **speech transmitter**. >> An **inspector** examines the mess and decides which eggs went in. The inspector is the **hearer**. || The inspector does not have to put the eggs back together. That would be physically impossible. >> He only has to **identify** the eggs. He uses the broken yolks, the spread-out whites and the bits of colored shell. || So a listener identifies the intended phonemes from a smeared, overlapping signal. The listener never has to recover separate pieces.

11|**Derivational morphology** derives new words from existing parts. These parts are called **morphemes**. || The word **morphology** is itself built this way. >> **morph-** comes from Greek and means "form". >> **-ology** also comes from Greek and means something like "the study of". >> Together they make "the study of the forms of words". || A similar process gives the word **morpheme**. It also gives **phoneme**. >> **Phone** is another Greek morpheme, probably meaning "speech". || The pattern does not always work smoothly. >> Following it for units in text leads to **graphology**, which is an awkward result. || So a complex word is built from reusable parts, but the parts do not always predict the meaning.
12|**Derivational morphology** is **productive**, but only in limited ways. Affixes differ in how widely and how regularly they are applied. || The suffix **-hood** added to a noun stem means a state of being linked to that noun. >> For **brotherhood** and **sisterhood**, the pattern works as expected. >> For **knighthood**, the result is a bit odd. Two brothers share their brotherhood, but each knight has his own knighthood. >> For **neighborhood**, the word is perfectly good, but -hood does a different job entirely. >> For **friendhood**, there is no word. English says friendship instead, for no apparent reason. >> For **enemyhood**, there is no word either. The concept only has formal words like "enmity" or "antagonism". || Some of these examples come from **Liberman and Prince**. || So an affix does not apply to every stem it could fit. Knowing an affix means knowing where it applies and what job it does.
13|**Zero derivation** changes a word's category without adding any affix. Even so, it does complicated things with meaning. || The nouns **dish**, **spoon**, **fork** and **table** are strong associates of the noun **knife**. As verbs or in fixed phrases, their meanings are not similar. >> In "She can dish it out but she can't take it", dish is a verb that has nothing to do with plates. >> In "He was born with a silver spoon and a forked tongue in his mouth", spoon and fork appear in fixed figurative phrases. >> In "They decided to table the issue after a day of inconclusive negotiations", table is a verb about handling an issue. || So the answer is no. Each converted word has its own conventional meaning, which has to be learned. || Figurative phrases like silver spoon are studied in **Cognitive Linguistics**.
14|Derivational morphology more commonly **changes the pronunciation** and the spelling of a word. Adding an affix can change the sounds in the rest of the word. || The **certificate** family changes its **stress** with each affix. >> In certificate /sər.ˈtI.fɨ.kɨt/, the main stress falls on the second syllable. >> In certification /ˌsər.tɨ.fɨ.ˈkeI.ʃən/, the main stress moves to the fourth syllable. The second vowel also changes from I to ɨ. >> In recertification /ˈri.sər.tɨ.fɨ.ˌkeI.ʃən/, the main stress moves to the first syllable. || This is one reason for **"The Chaos"** of English spelling. >> It is also why spelling closer to the spoken form does not necessarily improve readability. || Some noun and verb pairs differ mainly in **stress**. >> **record** is one such pair. >> **invite** is another, as in "He did not receive an invite" and "We did not invite him". >> **entrance** also changes stress, but its noun and verb are not actually related to each other. || So morphology and pronunciation interact, and stress is often the main change.
15|**Inflectional morphology** changes the meaning of words in a more **predictable** way than derivation. It adds grammatical information to the same word. || For **plurals**, the noun marks number. >> knife becomes knives, one fork becomes two forks, and one mouse becomes a lot of mice. || For the **third person singular present**, the verb marks agreement. >> knife as a verb becomes knifes. I walk becomes she walks. You run becomes he runs. || For the **past**, the verb marks tense. >> knife as a verb becomes knifed. Today I walk becomes yesterday I walked. Today she sings becomes in the past, she sang. || **Menn** says we would think it silly to have separate dictionary entries for singular and plural nouns. || The sound changes of inflection often have some **irregularity**, as in mice and sang. >> This irregularity matters for **language learning models**. It also matters for kinds of **developmental language impairment**.
16|**Inflectional morphology** also depends on the **syntax** of the rest of the sentence. || The phrase "two [noun]+pl" has a slot for a noun. Whatever noun goes there must be pluralized somehow. >> For fork, the plural is regular, so it becomes two forks. >> For knife, the plural is irregular, so it becomes two knives. || Derivation has no syntactic process like this, even for very productive affixes. >> The closest case is a formula like "Once you [verb] it, you can un-[verb] it". >> That formula is not exactly syntax, so it does not force a derived form. || So syntax can require an inflected form. It does not require a derived form. This difference matters for models of **speech production**.

18|A simple model treats words as **nodes** in a **lexical network**. Each node is a lexical representation that a specific word can activate. || The example network has three nodes. >> The nodes are **lion**, **tiger** and **stripes**. >> The input is the printed word **LION**. || The input and the nodes are different levels. >> The printed word supplies perceptual evidence. >> The node stands for the word the system could recognize from that evidence. || So recognizing a word means activating its matching node.
19|**Activation** can be **partial**. A node can be partly active before the word is recognized. || Here, **LION** contrasts poorly with a **noise mask**. >> The word is hard to separate from the noise, so the input is weak evidence. >> The lion node therefore gets only **weak activation**. >> This activation is not yet enough for the word to be identified. || So the state of the network changes gradually as evidence comes in. It does not jump straight from off to recognized.
20|The **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)** compares the strength of the word with the strength of the background noise. || At some point, the SNR becomes very favorable. >> Activity in the lion node becomes strong. >> At that point the simple network has **"identified"** the word lion. >> The tiger and stripes nodes stay less active, so they are not identified. || This is a model of the **perceptual identification task**. >> Participants push a button as soon as they can identify the item. >> The measure is how long identification takes. || So a faster button press means the node reached strong activation sooner.
21|Most models of this kind assume that **activation unfolds over time**. A word's activation rises after the input arrives and then falls. || These models explain **frequency effects**, where frequent words are identified faster. One explanation is that experience tunes the **activation dynamics**. >> Each encounter might raise the word's **baseline** level of activation. The word then starts closer to recognition. >> Each encounter might strengthen its **incoming connections**. The word can then be activated more quickly. >> Each encounter might change a **threshold** parameter. The word then needs a different amount of activation to be recognized. || This is a very simple form of **learning**. || Different computational models simulate frequency effects in different ways. >> One example is **Rumelhart and McClelland's (1986) Interactive Activation model**. || So frequent words are faster because past experience has changed how their activation behaves.
22|Activation dynamics also explain **repetition priming**. Seeing a word again very soon after seeing it leads to faster identification. || Activation does not rise and fall instantly. >> One assumption is that activation **falls more slowly than it rises**. >> So some activation is still left over after the first exposure. || Suppose the word is encountered again while activation is still high. >> Its **baseline** is then already higher than usual. >> It needs less new activation to reach identification. || So the repeated word is identified faster because it starts from leftover activation.
23|The simple network leaves out **two factors**. >> **Neighborhood effects** depend on how similar a word is to other words. >> **Associative, semantic and phonological priming** depend on whether you just saw a similar or related word. || Both factors involve a second word. A network of separate nodes can only change each word's own activation. || So the network needs something added. It needs **connections** between nodes. Those connections let one word's activation affect another word.
24|**Spreading activation** is one way to represent word associations. The associations come from word association **norms**. || The model is a network of nodes. The **connection strengths**, or the distances, between nodes are set by their association. >> When you activate lion, some of that activation travels along a weighted connection to tiger. >> A strong association passes more activation than a weak one. || In the example network, vehicle connects to car and to bus. Fire engine connects to the vehicles and to fire-related concepts. >> So one active concept can partly activate several related concepts. || That is why a related word, already partly active, becomes easier to process. This benefit is **semantic priming**.
25|**Balota and Lorch** tested whether activation spreads beyond directly associated concepts. || They built prime and target pairs related only through a **mediating concept**. >> The prime **lion** and the target **stripes** are linked only through **tiger**. >> They compared directly related pairs, pairs linked through a mediating concept, a neutral prime and an unrelated prime. || They used two tasks. >> In **lexical decision**, people judged whether the target was a word. Directly related pairs such as lion and tiger sped up responses. Lion before stripes did not help compared with neutral or unrelated primes. >> In **pronunciation**, people read the target aloud. Both directly related primes and the lion before stripes primes sped up responses. || They concluded that activation **spreads beyond directly related concepts** in semantic memory. >> They suggested that features of the lexical decision task **masked** the indirect priming effect.
26|**Indirect priming** happens when a prime helps a target through a **mediating** word. || In the network, lion connects to tiger. Tiger connects to stripes. Lion and stripes are not directly associated. >> Activating lion sends activation to tiger. >> Tiger then passes some of that activation on to stripes. || The idea is that activity **flows throughout the network**. >> So activating lion can prime stripes, even with no direct link between them. || That is why indirect priming is evidence for spreading activation.
27|The indirect priming result is very representative of psycholinguistics. >> It needed **careful study design**. >> The effects are **small**. >> The result is **counterintuitive**. || In Experiment 2, **Balota and Lorch** measured **pronunciation latencies**. **Stimulus onset asynchrony (SOA)** is the time between the start of the prime and the start of the target. They used SOAs of 250 ms and 500 ms. >> Averaged over both SOAs, related primes gave 549 ms. >> Primes linked through tiger gave 558 ms. >> Neutral primes gave 577 ms, and unrelated primes gave 575 ms. || The indirect advantage is only 17 to 19 ms. It still appears at both SOAs. || Priming between stripes and lion seems strange because they have no direct link. >> The only route between them goes through tiger. >> So the effect only makes sense if activation spreads through the network.
28|**Belke and colleagues (2005)** used the **WEAVER++** model of word production. The model has a **conceptual stratum** and a **lemma stratum**. >> In the conceptual stratum, duck, fish, snake and mouse connect to shared nodes such as animal, bird, swim and scales. >> Each concept connects down to its **lemma**. A lemma is the word's entry in the syntactic stratum. || A naive view of spreading activation predicts **faster** naming. A block of similar pictures should prime one another. || The model explains how spreading activation can lead to **slower** naming instead. >> Naming requires **selecting** one lemma. >> Similar pictures activate many related lemmas at once, so the lemmas compete. || So more activation does not always help. Selection gets harder when competitors are active.

30|**Pierre Paul Broca** was a neurologist. In **1861** he presented the case of a patient called **"Tan"**. >> Tan had lost the ability to speak after a brain injury decades earlier. >> Broca presented the case to the newly founded **Society of Anthropology**. He was also its founding president. || Tan showed a split between speaking and understanding. >> For **speech**, Tan could only produce one jargon syllable. >> For his **other abilities**, he seemed to be "in possession of his senses". >> For **comprehension**, he seemed to understand what was being said. || Tan was not the first observed case of a specific loss like this. || So a brain injury can remove speech while leaving understanding in place. That made the case central to the **localization** of language.
31|Tan's brain is on display at the **Musee Dupuytren**. || The anatomical description reports a **large lesion**. >> The lesion definitely includes the third convolution of the frontal lobe. This is the **inferior frontal gyrus**. || This region became known as **Broca's area**. It is a portion of the left frontal lobe that is important for speech. || **Hughlings Jackson (1874)** criticized this reasoning at the time. >> He said that "to locate the damage which destroys speech and to locate speech are two different things". >> Damage in one place can stop speech without that place holding speech itself. || So a lesion shows where damage disrupts speech. It does not by itself show where speech is located.
32|In the **1870s**, German neurologists such as **Lichtheim** and **Wernicke** made more systematic observations of **aphasia**. Aphasia is a loss of language ability after brain damage. || They explained specific losses of function as **disconnections** between **centers**. >> In Lichtheim's house diagram, A is the center for sound images. B is the center for concepts. M is the center for movement images. >> A disconnection between A and B could cause a loss of understanding spoken words. >> A disconnection between A and M could cause a loss of verbatim repetition. || This approach of describing centers for specific functions is an ancestor of **modularity**. || Eling and Whitaker (2009) review this history. || So the explanation covers the pathways between centers, not only the centers themselves.
33|**Sigmund Freud** criticized the **"diagram-drawing" school** of aphasia research. He did this in his early neurological research. || Freud thought it naive to expect the brain to follow psychological categories. >> Examples of such categories are "word forms" and "concepts". >> He proposed instead that language emerged from **fields**. >> These fields connect perceptual and motor abilities to **drives**. || This is a **holist critique**. || It names a durable tension in **cognitive neuroscience**. >> One question is whether brain area functions can be directly related to cognitive functions. >> Another question is whether the brain is really organized by our descriptions of cognitive phenomena. || So a useful psychological category does not automatically match a brain area. The link has to be shown with evidence.
34|**Dronkers and colleagues** used **MRI** to assess Broca's landmark cases very precisely. One of these brains belonged to **Leborgne**, the patient known as Tan. || The MRI shows the real extent and location of the damage. >> The lesion is very **deep** and includes the **insula**. >> There is a lot of **white matter damage** to the **superior longitudinal fasciculus**. This pathway connects the front and back of the cerebral cortex. || Other work by this group links **speech apraxia** strongly to the insula, not to Broca's area. >> Speech apraxia is the condition Broca described in his patients. || So the damage was much larger than Broca's area alone. That is why the speech loss may come from the insula rather than Broca's area.

36|The **functional level** selects **lemmas**, not finished words. A lemma is a word's identity before its grammatical form is fixed. >> The term **lemma** is borrowed from mathematics. There it refers to a proposition in an argument. || One speech error explains why the functional level cannot just select words. >> The intended sentence was "Where'd I put it when I brought it in". >> The speaker said "Where'd I bring it when I put it in". || The verbs switched places, but the tense was applied after the switch. >> If the word "brought" had been selected, it would have moved as "brought". >> Instead it came out as "bring", the form its new position needed. || So the speaker selected the lemma **BRING**. It was expressed in its present form only later. That is why selection works on lemmas, not words.
37|People often say the **opposite** of what they mean because **antonyms** are similar at many levels. || Antonyms overlap in meaning and association. >> They are **semantically related**. >> They are **strongly associated**. >> In the **spreading activation** framework, these two links together make a perfect storm. || Antonyms also fit the same grammar. >> They can take on the same **semantic roles**. >> They belong to the **same word class**, so they can take on the same syntactic roles. >> So the rest of the speech production system can handle either one. || So activation spreads easily from a **lemma** to its antonym. That is why the wrong antonym gets said and still sounds grammatical.
38|**Semantic roles**, also called functional roles, describe how each participant relates to an event. They stay the same when the sentence form changes. || In "The plums in the icebox were eaten by me", the order is **Undergoer**, **Location**, Verb, **Agent**. >> The plums are the undergoer. The icebox is the location. The speaker is the agent. || In "I have eaten the plums in the icebox", the order is Agent, Verb, Undergoer, Location. >> The same participants keep the same roles. || This simple event structure lets lemmas be slotted into roles. >> The verb is TO EAT. The agent is ME. PLUM is the undergoer. || The order of the roles has **syntactic consequences**. >> The order decides how the lemma ME turns into a word, either I or me. >> That decision happens at the **positional level**. || So roles are set at the functional level, and word forms come later.
39|By the end of the **functional level**, three things are done. >> A **message** to convey has been decided. >> Appropriate **lemmas** have been selected. >> The lemmas have been associated with **semantic roles**. || This is quite a lot of work. Yet the speaker is nowhere near putting a clause together. >> The word order is not set yet. >> The word forms are not set yet. >> Nothing has been said out loud. || So the functional level produces a plan of meaning, not a sentence.
40|The **positional level** expresses the functional plan in syntax. The sentence "Where'd I bring it when I put it in?" works as the example. || After the functional level, the speaker knows three things. >> There are two verbs, **PUT** and **BRING**. >> I/ME is the **agent** of both verbs. >> IT is the **undergoer** of both verbs. || The speaker still needs more. >> The speaker needs the **temporal relationship** between the verbs. The putting in happened in the past, so its clause needs a past form. >> The speaker needs the question structure. || Expressing all of these things syntactically is the job of the positional level. || So knowing who did what is different from knowing where each word goes.
41|The **positional level** builds a **syntactic structure** with **slots**. The slots hold the lemmas from the functional level and the other particles the grammar requires. || Without the question and tense steps, the frame is "Where'd I [verb, root form] it when I [verb, past] it in?" >> Getting the first clause right takes a lot of syntactic work. It needs **Wh- movement** and an **auxiliary verb** to form the question. >> The first clause requires the **base form** of the lemma. >> The next clause requires the **past form**. || The error "Where'd I bring it when I put it in" arises while these slots are **filled with lemmas**. >> BRING went into the first slot, so it took the base form. >> PUT went into the past slot, and its past form is also put. || So the slot decides the form of the verb. The lemma only decides which verb it is.
42|"A language needer learns" is a similar speech error. || It is similar to the bring and put error. >> Two lemmas got switched when they were being fed into the positional level. >> The positional level itself stayed almost the same. || It is different in the **noun**. >> The noun is really a **verb plus an affix**. Here the verb need gets the affix -er. >> Adding -er is **derivational morphology**. >> The verb slot keeps its **inflectional** ending, so learn becomes learns. || The roots moved, but the affixes stayed in their slots. || So derivational affixes can belong to the positional frame too, not only to the lemma.
43|"He did that easy enoughly" is another similar error. || It is similar to "a language needer learns". >> Both have a **stranded affix**. A stranded affix stays in its slot while the root changes. >> Here the affix is **-ly**, which makes an adjective into an adverb. || It is different because something also happened when **setting up the positional level**. >> The intended adjective phrase has an adverb first. That adverb is built as an adjective plus -ly. An adjective comes second. >> The error structure reverses this order. The plain adjective comes first, and the adjective plus -ly comes second. >> So easy fills the plain adjective slot, and enough gets the -ly. || So this error involves the frame itself, not only the lemmas filling it.

45|**Oldfield and Wingfield (1965)** asked people to **name pictures** of objects. || They found a **log-linear relationship** between frequency and response time. >> More frequent names were produced faster. >> Response time fell in a straight line as the logarithm of frequency rose. || The line drawings used in picture naming come from **Snodgrass and Vanderwart (1980)**. || Frequency is related to **word length**, even in this tiny sample. >> Frequent words tend to be shorter. >> So length is a **confound**. A confound is a second variable that changes along with the one being studied. >> Either frequency or length could explain the naming times. || That is why this result is a classic cautionary tale.
46|English **word frequency** drops off very fast from the top words. This pattern is **Zipf's law** in action. || The 200 most frequent words in an old corpus were sorted into ten bins of twenty words each. >> For the **first bin**, mean frequency is above 15,000 per million. >> For the **second bin**, it drops below 4,000 per million. >> For the later bins, it falls close to zero. || **Length** goes the other way. >> Words in the first bin average under three letters. >> Words in the last bins average about five letters. >> So length is **inversely related** to frequency. || The top twenty words include the, of, and, to and a. Each has three letters or fewer. Later high-frequency words such as century and Washington are much longer. || So choosing frequent words also means choosing short words.
47|High-frequency (**HF**) words are generally **shorter** than low-frequency (**LF**) words. So a length difference always appears if LF words are selected at random. || The solution is **not to select LF words at random**. >> Choose long-ish HF words. >> Choose short-ish LF words. >> Then the two groups can be **matched on length** while frequency is manipulated. || So the groups differ in frequency but not in length. That separates the two explanations.
48|**Oldfield and Wingfield** eventually **matched** their high and low frequency words for **length**. || If length caused the effect, matching should have removed it. >> They still found a **robust frequency effect**. >> So they concluded that frequency effects are not merely length effects in disguise. || A lot of psycholinguistics tries to show that an observed effect is not just an effect of a **confounding variable**. || That is why careful stimulus control matters.

50|**Belke, Meyer and Damian (2005)** studied **cyclic semantic blocking** in **picture naming**. || In this paradigm, people repeatedly name **sets** of objects. >> In **homogeneous sets**, the names are semantically related. >> In **heterogeneous sets**, the names are unrelated. >> Naming latencies are usually longer in related sets. This cost is the **blocking effect**. || They ran three experiments. >> In **Experiment 1**, the effect only arose after all objects of a set had been shown and named once. >> In **Experiment 2**, the objects of a set were shown together. Blocking appeared in naming latencies and in **gaze durations**. >> The **gaze-to-speech lag** is the time from the eyes leaving an object to the start of saying its name. It showed repetition priming but no blocking. >> In **Experiment 3**, blocking generalized to new, previously unnamed items. || They proposed that the blocking effect comes from **refractory behaviour** in the semantic system.
51|Speech production must separate **activation** from **selection**. || Spreading activation from **duck** to **fish** means that activating one helps activate the other. >> This causes problems when the task is to **select** the correct **lemma** from many active ones. || The **WEAVER++** model sets **selection time** by a **ratio**. The ratio compares the activity of the most active lemma with the overall activation of all lemmas. >> When the target stands far above the rest, selection is fast. >> When many lemmas are active, the target stands out less, so selection is slower. || **Feedback from the category level** creates **collateral activation** for items from the same category. || So naming a duck among other animals slows down lemma selection. That is the **semantic blocking effect**.

54|In **Experiment 2**, Belke, Meyer and Damian measured three timings for each named object. Each timing is linked to one stage of production. || For **speech duration**, overall duration decreases over the experiment. It shows **no blocking effect**. >> Speech duration reflects the **gesture level**. || For **gaze duration**, there is a **blocking effect**, but only after the first cycle. Objects in homogeneous sets are looked at longer. >> Gaze duration reflects **lemma selection**. || For **lag**, there is **no effect**. Numerically, the blocking effect seems to go the opposite way, but this is not significant. >> Lag reflects **phonological encoding**. || So blocking appears only in the measure for lemma selection. That places the effect at **lemma selection**.
55|**Experiment 3** asked whether **blocking generalizes** to new items in the same category. The answer is yes. || New items were introduced in **Cycle 5**. >> Overall naming slows down dramatically for both kinds of sets. >> The **repetition priming** effect goes away in Cycle 5, because these items have not been named before. >> The **blocking effect** is still there. Homogeneous sets stay slower than heterogeneous sets. || The useful comparison is between new items from a blocked category and the items in **Cycle 1**. >> In Cycle 1, no category had been named yet, and there was no blocking. >> In Cycle 5, the items are new but their category has been named before, and blocking appears. || So blocking comes from the named **category**, not from repeating the same items.
56|Belke and colleagues isolated the blocking effect in the model by ruling out three other explanations. || The effect is **not visual recognition**. >> The stimuli were **normed and matched**. >> A **control experiment** checked the visual side. || The effect is probably **not phonological encoding**. This part is marked with a question mark, so it is less certain. >> The words in a set are **not phonologically similar**. >> **Gaze duration** shows the effect, but the **eye-speech lag** does not. || The effect is **not just a specific effect of repeating lists**. >> It **generalizes to new items**. || So the blocking effect is placed at **lemma selection**.
