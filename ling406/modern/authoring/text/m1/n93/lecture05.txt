2|**Phrenology** tried to relate brain structure to function by measuring the shape of the skull. A simplified phrenological chart assigns head regions to language, memory, thinking, love and other categories. || Its reasoning rested on three premises. >> The first premise says that **different parts of the brain** are responsible for different processes. >> The second premise says that **aptitude** is related to differences in brain region size. >> The third premise says that **brain region size influences skull shape**. || From these premises, careful skull measurements were supposed to reveal relationships between skull shape and brain functions. No one believes in phrenology anymore. || The premises are not equally wrong. >> The first premise stays a serious idea, because brain parts can contribute differently to behavior. >> The second premise needs a dependable link from an ability to the size of a region. >> The third premise fails, because skull shape is not a sensitive measure of brain shape. || So the idea of specialized brain parts survives, but reading the skull to measure them does not.
3|**Phrenology** had two important problems, one in its anatomy and one in its psychology. || The first problem was **inappropriate anatomical measurement**. >> Skull shape is not a sensitive measure of brain shape. >> So measuring the head cannot identify a brain mechanism. || The second problem was an **unconstrained definition of functions**. >> Broad social labels were treated as if each one named a single mental function. >> Studies of prisoners aimed to identify regions associated with "criminality". >> Studies of women with different numbers of children aimed to find the locus of "philopropogenitiveness". || A convincing structure and function study needs a valid anatomical measure. It also needs a clear account of the process being measured. || So phrenology failed on both sides of the relationship it tried to find.
4|**Flourens** was an early and vociferous critic of phrenology. He studied the brain with **experimental lesions**, which means damaging or removing brain tissue and then observing behavior. || He worked mainly with pigeons and rabbits. >> His experiments established **distinct functions** for the cerebral cortex, the cerebellum and mid-brain structures. >> So large parts of the brain did differ in what they do. || Within the cortex, he **failed to find any correspondence** between specific regions and specific senses. >> He insisted instead that the **size of the lesion** was critical. >> In his view, when one sense was lost, all were lost together. >> For example, removing most of the cortex makes sight, hearing and other senses disappear together. || So Flourens argued that loss depended on how much tissue was destroyed. He did not accept a precise map of sensory centers.
5|**Pierre Paul Broca** was a neurologist who studied **aphasia**, a loss of language ability after brain damage. || In **1861**, Broca presented the case of a patient called **"Tan"**. >> Tan had lost the ability to speak after a brain injury decades earlier. >> Broca gave the presentation to the newly established Society of Anthropology. >> He was also the founding president of that society. || Tan's abilities were split. >> For **speaking**, Tan could produce only **one jargon syllable**. >> For **everything else**, he seemed to be "in possession of his senses". >> For **understanding**, he seemed to follow what was being said. || This was not the first observation of a specific loss of speech. || So Tan's case suggested that producing speech and understanding speech can be damaged separately.
6|**Tan's brain** is on display at the Musee Dupuytren. Its anatomical description begins with a **large lesion**. || The lesion definitely included the **third convolution of the frontal lobe**. This region is also called the **inferior frontal gyrus**. || From this case comes **Broca's area**. >> Broca's area is a portion of the **left frontal lobe** that is important for speech. || **Hughlings Jackson (1874)** raised a contemporary criticism. >> He wrote that "to locate the damage which destroys speech and to locate speech are two different things". >> Damage in one place can stop speech. >> That does not mean speech itself lives in that place. || So a lesion links a region to a lost ability, but it does not show everything that region does.
7|In the **1870s**, German neurologists such as **Lichtheim** and **Wernicke** made more systematic observations of aphasia. || They explained specific losses as **"disconnections" between "centers"**. >> A center is a proposed brain location for one language function. >> A disconnection is damage to the pathway joining two centers. || Each loss came from a different broken pathway. >> For **understanding spoken words**, the loss might come from a disconnection between "A" and "B". >> For **verbatim repetition**, the loss might come from a disconnection between "A" and "M". >> In Lichtheim's house model, A stands for sound images, B for concepts and M for movement images. || This approach of describing centers for specific functions is an ancestor of **modularity**. || So the pathway between centers became part of the explanation, not only the centers themselves.
8|**Sigmund Freud** critiqued the **"diagram-drawing" school** of aphasia research in his early neurological work. || He felt it was naive to expect the brain to follow psychological categories. >> Categories like "word forms" and "concepts" describe behavior. >> Freud doubted that the brain is organized around those same categories. || He proposed instead that language emerges from **"fields"**. >> These fields connect perceptual and motor abilities to **"drives"**. >> This is a **holist critique**, because it treats language as spread across connected systems. || His critique raises a **durable tension** in cognitive neuroscience. >> One question asks whether brain area functions relate directly to cognitive functions. >> Another asks whether the brain is really organized according to our descriptions of cognition. || So a useful category for behavior is not automatically a separate part of the brain.
9|**Dronkers and colleagues (2007)** used **MRI** to reexamine Leborgne's brain, the patient known as Tan. MRI let them assess the extent and location of damage very precisely. || The scans revealed damage that surface inspection had missed. >> The lesion is **very deep** and includes the **insula**. >> There is also heavy **white matter damage** to the **superior longitudinal fasciculus**. >> This fiber tract connects the front and back of the cerebral cortex. || Other work by this group linked a speech disorder to a different region. >> The **insula**, not Broca's area, is strongly associated with **speech apraxia**. >> Speech apraxia is the condition Broca described in his patients. || So the lost speech in Broca's cases may come from deeper damage, not just from Broca's area.
10|Broca is still discussed 150 years later, even though his work was crude. || His **characterization of behavior** was crude. >> Linguistics then focused on historical issues, such as how languages were related. >> Linguists also worked on reconstructing ancient languages. >> Experimental psychology had not really been invented yet. >> So observations of behavior were necessarily informal. || His **anatomical localization** was also crude, and probably wrong. >> Fine-grained descriptions of the cerebral cortex were still in their infancy. >> **Brodmann's map** of cortical regions was not published until **1909**. || So Broca's limits came from the methods of his time.
11|**Broca's presentation of Tan** is seen as a **watershed moment** in neuropsychology. || Other researchers showed similar things around the same time. >> **The Daxes** reported related observations. >> **Wernicke** also reported a case. >> Wernicke's patient was **fluent** but produced **meaningless gibberish**. >> If anything, Wernicke's data are more interesting because of that contrast with Tan. || For a variety of reasons, Broca's case still became the turning point. >> It turned relating **specific functions to specific brain regions** into a **respectable pursuit**. || So Broca's lasting importance is that he made localization a serious research question.

13|**Cognitive neuropsychology** studies how brain damage affects mental functions. It depends on **cognitive psychology** for its definitions of those functions. || Much of what was learned about structure and function in the 20th century came from brain-damaged individuals. These were experimental observations of their behavior. || **Cognitive theory** provides hypotheses about which operations might be separable. >> One question asks if recognizing the **abstract forms of words** can be separated from computing their **meaning**. >> If damage harms one ability but spares the other, the two are **dissociated**. >> Tasks can separate these demands. >> **Physical identity** matching compares letters by appearance. >> **Name identity** matching compares letters by name. >> **Rule identity** matching sorts letters as vowels or consonants. || **Quantitative measurements** give a way to test such questions. >> They allow **statistical comparison** of how well each ability survives. >> This is critical because **deficits are rarely complete**. || So a clear theory and careful measurement together make a dissociation meaningful.
14|**Anatomical imaging** locates brain injuries at high resolution in living patients. || Before imaging, scientists had to wait for patients to "come to autopsy". >> Only then could they find the site of the lesion. >> Imaging removed that wait. || A scan of **H.M.'s** middle temporal lobes is one example. >> His damage was the result of surgery. || The limit from **Hughlings Jackson (1874)** still applies. >> Locating the damage that destroys speech differs from locating speech. >> Studies of brain-injured patients show what behavior looks like after a region is damaged. >> They tell only **indirectly** what the region does when intact. || So imaging sharpens the anatomy, but the explanation still needs a theory.
15|**Metabolic neuroimaging** lets researchers study brains in action. **Posner and colleagues (1988)** described **PET** studies that used radio-labeled oxygen as a marker for activity. || Metabolic methods like PET and **fMRI** have three key limits. >> The methods are **metabolic**, so they measure an index of **oxygen consumption**, not neural activity itself. >> The methods are **slow**, because metabolic processes unfold over **seconds**. Important neural events can occur in **milliseconds** or less. >> They have only **fair spatial resolution**. FMRI is better than PET. Resolution is still on the order of **millimeters**. Important events happen at the scale of **microns**. || So these images estimate activity indirectly, slowly and coarsely.
16|**Subtraction logic** applies cognitive psychology to metabolic imaging data. || A claim that "x part of the brain is active during y task" does not work. >> There is activity **all over the brain all of the time**. >> So active regions alone cannot identify a task. || Every study needs a **control condition**. >> The control shares most demands with the task condition. >> Subtracting the control image from the task image leaves the **difference in activity**. >> A motor image minus a rest image leaves a much smaller region. || **Posner and colleagues (1988)** used this logic to separate word form identification from meaning. >> Their language tasks shared visual input and responses. >> The tasks differed in whether **word meaning** had to be accessed. >> Subtracting the simpler task from the meaning task isolates **semantic processing**. || So the leftover activity belongs to the one operation the conditions differ in.
17|**Posner and colleagues (1988)** found one region active across several **semantic** contrasts. || Each contrast compared a meaning task with a simpler task. >> For **visual monitoring**, people watched words for dangerous animals, compared with passively viewing words. >> For **visual generating**, people produced uses for written words, compared with repeating them. >> For **auditory generating**, people produced uses for heard words, compared with repeating them. || All three contrasts activated the **left inferior frontal** region. >> The shared activity appears for both seen and heard words. >> So it links this region to **semantic processing** of words. || Interestingly, this region corresponds **roughly to Broca's area**. || So the region tied to lost speech in Broca's cases appears again in modern semantic tasks.
18|**A modern localization claim** must be compared with phrenology on how its evidence was obtained. || The question "Is this better than phrenology?" is rhetorical but worth exploring. || Phrenology failed for two reasons. >> It used an **invalid measure**, skull shape. >> It used **undefined functions**, such as "criminality". || Imaging studies answer both problems in part. >> They measure an **index of brain activity** instead of skull shape. >> They define the operation through a **controlled task contrast**. || An attractive image of an active region still settles nothing by itself. >> The contrast must truly isolate the proposed operation. >> The measurement must support linking that operation to the region. || So imaging is better than phrenology when its definitions and measurements are sound.
19|**Posner and colleagues (1988)** argued for localizing **component operations**, not whole tasks. || These operations act on **visual**, **phonological** and **semantic codes**. || Whole tasks involve many coordinated computations. >> Reading a word needs several of them. >> Forming a visual image needs several of them. >> Shifting attention needs several of them. >> So a complete task is not assigned to one brain area. || The PET evidence **complemented lesion evidence**. >> It related cognitive distinctions to activity during performance. >> Averaged blood-flow patterns suggested **consistency** across participants. >> The participants were right-handed and had good reading skills. || So their answer to "Is this better than phrenology?" is to localize defined operations, not broad traits or whole tasks.
20|**Language** has turned up at key moments in debates about **localization of function**. || It appeared in two major moments. >> **Broca's presentation of Tan** helped establish **lesion-behavior analysis** in neuropsychology. >> Some of the first **PET** and **fMRI** studies used activation of **Broca's area** in language tasks. >> Those studies took it as evidence that cognitive functions could be localized. || Language suits these investigations because it splits into **separable abilities**. >> Speaking can be lost while understanding seems spared, as in Tan. >> Understanding words, repeating words and judging meaning place different demands on processing. >> These differences give researchers clear contrasts for lesion studies and control conditions. || So language offers distinct, testable parts for relating function to structure.
