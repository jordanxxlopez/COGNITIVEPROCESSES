7|**Letters** belong to a writing system. **Speech sounds** belong to the spoken language. So letters are not **phonemes**. >> Each language has its own rules for turning spelling into sound. >> A familiar letter can lead an English reader to the wrong pronunciation in another language. || **English** spelling is especially ambiguous, or irregular. Even less irregular writing systems cause problems when languages are compared. || **Pinyin** is a relatively recently invented system for writing Mandarin. It is **perfectly regular**. >> For the Mandarin phrase "Wo qu tushuguan" (我去图书馆), the Pinyin letters look familiar to an English reader. English spelling rules still do not give the right pronunciation. >> The Chinese characters and the Pinyin write the same Mandarin phrase in two different writing systems. || **French** spelling is also very regular. >> For the French sentence "Comment tu t’appelles?", the letters are again familiar. The reader still has to know the French rules to say it. || A writing system can be regular inside one language without being a sound code for every language. That is why comparing speech sounds has to start from what speakers actually say, not from matching letters.
8|The **International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)** is a **standard system** for writing the sounds of the world's languages. It accurately captures how sounds are **similar** and how they **differ**. >> An IPA symbol stands for properties of pronunciation. It does not stand for the spelling habits of one language. >> Two sounds with different ordinary spellings can be compared directly. >> Two sounds that ordinary writing spells with the same letter can be told apart. || The **IPA consonant chart** arranges its symbols by how the sounds are produced. >> The rows include **plosive**, **nasal** and **fricative**. >> The columns include **bilabial**, **alveolar** and **velar**. >> Symbols in related positions stand for related ways of producing a sound. || So where a symbol sits tells how its sound relates to the sounds near it. That is why the consonant chart works as a system of comparisons and not only as a list of symbols to memorize.
9|The IPA consonant chart is organized by three **features**. They are **place**, **manner** and **voicing**. || **Place** of articulation is where the vocal tract is constricted. >> Place runs across the chart from the front of the vocal tract to the back. >> **Bilabial** sounds sit toward the left because their constriction is at the lips. >> **Velar** sounds and sounds made farther back sit farther to the right. || **Manner** of articulation is how the airflow is constrained. It is the degree of closure. >> Manner runs down the chart from closed to open. >> For a **plosive**, the closure is complete. >> For a **fricative**, the passage is narrower but not closed. || **Voicing** is about when the vocal folds vibrate relative to the closure. Voicing can begin before or during the closure, or after it. >> Voicing appears as pairs of symbols in one position. The right-hand symbol is the voiced one. >> The symbols p and b share the plosive row and the bilabial column. || So two consonants can match on two features and **differ on the third**.
10|**Features** describe how sounds are alike and different in a relatively precise and well-defined way. || **/p/** is plosive, bilabial and not voiced. **/b/** is plosive, bilabial and voiced. >> Both have the **same manner**, because both are plosives. >> Both have the **same place**, because both are bilabial. The lips close and then release. >> They **differ in voicing**. Voicing here is the timing of phonation relative to the release of the closure. || The difference between **/t/ and /d/** is the same kind of difference. >> That pair is made at a different place. >> It still pairs a voiceless stop with a voiced stop. || So features allow general statements about groups of sounds. One such group is the **voiced stops**, such as /b/, /d/ and /g/. That is why each consonant does not have to be described separately.
11|The contrast between **voiced** and **unvoiced** stops is produced differently across languages. It is also produced differently across contexts within one language. The IPA can write these differences compactly. || Three **phones** are told apart by their features. >> **[pʰ]** is plosive, bilabial, not voiced and **aspirated**. >> **[p]** is plosive, bilabial, not voiced and not aspirated. >> **[b]** is plosive, bilabial and voiced. >> **Aspiration** is the puff of air after the closure is released. The raised h in [pʰ] marks it. || **In English**, at the start of a word, the first sound of "peer" is often the unvoiced aspirated stop [pʰ]. The first sound of "beer" is often the unvoiced unaspirated stop [p]. >> A hand held in front of the mouth makes the difference in airflow easy to feel. || **In most varieties of French**, the contrast is really between [p] in "pou" and [b] in "boue". || So the labels voiced and unvoiced do not mean the same timing in every language. That is why the narrow IPA symbols keep these three pronunciations apart.
12|**Slashes** and **square brackets** mark two kinds of transcription. || Slashes mark **broad transcription**. Broad transcription writes the **phonemes** of an utterance. >> /p/ and /b/ are broad labels. >> /p/ is plosive, bilabial and not voiced. /b/ is plosive, bilabial and voiced. >> Broad transcription keeps the distinctions that organize the words of the language. || Square brackets mark **narrow transcription**. Narrow transcription includes **as much detail as possible**. >> [pʰ], [p] and [b] are narrow forms. >> [pʰ] adds that the stop is aspirated. [p] adds that it is not aspirated. >> Narrow transcription can keep a difference such as aspiration even when it does not distinguish words. || There are two broad labels here and three narrow forms. Writing down more phonetic detail does not create more phonemes. That is why a **phone** and a **phoneme** are not the same thing.
13|**Phonetic features**, especially for consonants, are anchored to **how speech is produced**. **Place of articulation** names a physical constriction in the vocal tract. >> The lips can close together. >> Different parts of the tongue can approach or touch different regions of the mouth. >> These movements change where the outgoing air is blocked or narrowed. || Three places differ in which articulators make the constriction. >> For a **bilabial** constriction, the upper and lower lips close at the front of the mouth. >> For an **alveolar** constriction, the tongue tip rises toward the ridge behind the upper teeth. >> For a **velar** constriction, the back of the tongue rises toward the soft palate. || A complete closure can happen at several different places. So place is a separate feature from manner. That is why finding the place means locating the main constriction, not reading the written letter.
14|Consonant features describe two things. One is the **route of the airflow**. The other is which **articulators** make the constriction. || A **nasal** consonant lets air out through the nose while the mouth is closed at some point. >> The passage between the mouth and the nose is open, so air keeps flowing despite the closure in the mouth. >> For a nasal made at the lips, the lips are closed and the route to the nasal cavity is open. >> So a nasal can share its place in the mouth with a stop and differ in the route of the air. || Two other places are at the front of the mouth, at the teeth. >> For a **labiodental** constriction, the lower lip meets the upper teeth. >> For a **dental** constriction, the tongue is at the front teeth. || So describing a consonant takes two separate steps. The first step follows the airflow. The second step locates the constriction.
15|**Vowels** are organized by **height**, by **front or back** tongue position and by **lip rounding**. || Height separates close vowels from open vowels. >> **Close** vowels tend to have a higher jaw position and a higher tongue than **open** vowels. >> The vowel chart runs from close at the top to open at the bottom. || Front or back is about where the tongue is. >> The tongue is generally farther forward in the mouth for **front** vowels than for **back** vowels. >> The vowel chart runs from front on the left to back on the right. || Rounding is the rounding of the lips. >> Where two symbols appear as a pair, the one on the right is the **rounded** vowel. || **/y/** is the vowel in the French word "tu" and the Mandarin word "qu" (去). >> /y/ can sound like /u/ to English speakers. >> /i/ and /y/ sit in the same close front region and differ in rounding. >> Making an /i/ and then rounding the lips changes the vowel toward /y/. The tongue does not move back as it would for /u/. || So a change in **lip shape** is separate from a change in **tongue position**.
16|A **vowel** category describes a pattern of articulation. It does not describe one fixed tongue shape. || The center of the **tongue body** gives one reference point for each vowel. >> The point captures one important difference among vowels. >> A point cannot give the whole outline of the tongue. >> Tongue outlines for different vowels can differ in more than the location of that point. >> The whole vocal tract contributes to the sound, not the tongue alone. || Some vowels are very **dynamic**. >> The **diphthongs** in "boat" and "bait" move through time. They are not one held configuration. >> A point also cannot give the path the tongue takes during such a vowel. || So a fixed tongue position describes only part of how a vowel is produced. That is why a dynamic vowel is understood by following how the configuration **changes over its duration**.
17|Speakers of the same language can differ in which **vowels** they distinguish. Even within American English, vowels are very salient and differ across **dialects**. || The two vowels in the lower back corner of the vowel chart are the test case. One is the rounded low back vowel /ɔ/. The other is the open back vowel /ɑ/. >> **For a speaker from the outer boroughs of New York City**, a mid-Atlantic dialect, the two vowels are different. So "caught" and "cot" are **not homophones**. >> **For most people from other parts of the country**, the two vowels are not distinguished. So "caught" and "cot" are **homophones**. || Both kinds of speaker are using English. The difference is a systematic dialect pattern and not a pronunciation mistake. || So the set of vowels has to be worked out for a particular speech variety. That is why finding out whether a speaker has the contrast means listening to that speaker.
18|The **lack of invariance problem** is that one speech category has different acoustic forms across speakers and contexts. Listeners understand speech anyway. || Dialect differences are a first case. >> Some people say "Aaron" and "Erin" with the same vowel. A listener who keeps the two vowels apart still understands them. >> A listener can hear a speaker produce different vowels for some time and only notice once attention is drawn to it. || Measured vowels are a second case. Each spoken vowel can be placed by its **first formant (F1)** and its **second formant (F2)**. Both are frequencies in cycles per second. >> Many productions of one vowel **spread** over a wide region. They do not land on one point. >> The regions for neighboring vowels **overlap**. || So a vowel cannot be identified by checking for one fixed pair of F1 and F2 values. That is why the problem asks how a listener gets a stable interpretation from a signal that varies. The problem comes up again and again.
19|A **minimal pair** is two words whose difference in meaning depends on one speech sound in the same context. Minimal pairs are important evidence of **contrast**. || Two phones are **contrastive** when a minimal pair exists in which the difference between them results in a difference in meaning. Contrastive phones are examples of **different phonemes**. || The two low back vowels were established this way. It took two different words whose distinction depended on the difference between those vowels. >> **For a speaker who distinguishes the vowels**, "caught" and "cot" are a minimal pair. The consonants around the vowel stay the same, so the vowel alone separates the words. >> **For a speaker who merges the vowels**, "caught" and "cot" sound the same. So that pair does not establish two vowel phonemes for that speaker. || So the evidence for a phoneme is a **difference in meaning**. Two pronunciations sounding different is not enough.
20|**Minimal pairs** also give evidence that phonemic contrasts depend on a particular **feature**. || In English, three pairs of stops all differ on **voicing**. >> **/b/ and /p/** are both bilabial stops, and voicing is what differs. >> **/t/ and /d/** are both alveolar stops, and voicing is what differs. >> **/k/ and /g/** are both velar stops, and voicing is what differs. || Inside each pair, place and stop manner stay the same and voicing changes. Across the pairs, the place changes and the voiced and voiceless relation repeats. So one feature describes all three contrasts. || The evidence comes from comparing minimal pairs in each language. >> Two phones existing physically does not make them a phonemic contrast in every language. >> A minimal pair ties a particular sound difference to a difference between words. || That is why voicing is known to matter for telling English words apart.
21|**Allophones** are different phones that are different realizations of the **same phoneme**. || There is a great deal of variability in how phonemes are realized. Some of this variability is very **predictable**. >> The variation depends on the position of the sound and on its neighboring sounds. >> So it is systematic and not random noise. >> It does not change which word is being said. || The phrases "pot that", "spot that" and "stop that" put **/p/** in three contexts. They can be recorded in **Praat** and compared. >> **In "pot"**, /p/ is at the start of the word, before the vowel. >> **In "spot"**, /p/ comes after /s/. >> **In "stop"**, /p/ is at the end of the word. || In all three, the consonant is still a realization of /p/. Its release and aspiration can differ from one context to the next. So the question is how pronunciation changes with context, not whether each difference makes a new word.
22|A recording of "pot that", "spot that" and "stop that" makes the **allophones of /p/** observable in time. || The recording is read in two forms together. >> The **waveform** is on top. It gives the **amplitude** of the sound signal over time. >> The **spectrogram** is below it. It gives the distribution of acoustic **energy** over time and frequency. >> Time runs left to right in both, so the same event lines up vertically. Frequency runs up the side of the spectrogram. || The three phrases put /p/ in different contexts. So the timing of the **closure**, the **release**, the **aspiration** and the following voiced sound can differ. >> Abrupt releases and quieter intervals can be matched to the speech around them. || These physical differences fit with all three words containing the **same phoneme /p/**. Whether two phones are different phonemes depends on whether the difference distinguishes words in that language.
23|An unreleased **[p̚]** and an aspirated **[pʰ]** can both end the English word "stop" without changing its meaning. || English speakers sometimes say [stɑpʰ]. >> This often happens when the word is stressed at the end of a sentence. >> It also happens when someone is trying to speak clearly, in **citation form**. || There is **no difference in meaning** between [stɑp̚] and [stɑpʰ]. >> No **minimal pair** can be found for these two phones in these phonological contexts in English. >> So the two phones are called **allophones**. Allo means different, so allophones are different phones for the same phoneme. || These forms also alternate across the **morphological forms** of a word. The forms "stop", "stops", "stopped" and "stopping" each put something different after the /p/. >> Adding material after the consonant changes its environment. >> So whether the /p/ is released and aspirated depends on which form the word is in. || That is why a difference that can be heard is not always a difference between phonemes.
24|The word **voicing** is used in **two different ways**. That is why it can be confusing. || **In narrow transcription**, the description is of specific **phones**. >> A phone is called **voiced** if the vocal folds vibrate during the whole gesture or sound. >> This is a claim about one actual production. || **In broad transcription**, the description is of **phonemes**. >> Voicing is **realized** in a number of different ways. >> One way is the presence or absence of **aspiration** at the beginnings of words. >> Another way is the relative duration of voicing to closure in "writer" and "rider". >> This is a claim about a contrast between categories. || So one voicing contrast between phonemes can have more than one physical cue. The cue changes with the context. That is why the first thing to ask about the word voiced is which level is being described.
25|**Phonotactics** is, roughly, which phonemes can occur together in syllable structures. || The rules are clearest with **clusters** that cannot occur in English words. >> The name Sbarro is written with sb at the start. >> English speakers say [spɑroʊ] or [zbɑroʊ]. >> They **never** say [sbɑroʊ], whatever they think they are saying. || Foreign names can force speakers to deal with clusters that English phonotactics does not allow. Speakers then tend to **insert vowels**. >> **For the Polish name Zbigniew**, they say [zɨ’bIg.nʲu]. >> **For the Kru name Gbagbo**, they say [gə.bɑ.gə.boʊ]. >> The inserted vowels break the cluster into extra syllables. || These changes follow the patterns of English syllables. They do not come from being unable to make each single consonant. || Rules about syllable structure turn out to be complex. They still seem to shape **speech production**. So the spelling alone does not predict what an English speaker says.
26|**Phonemes** are not letters. They are not even specific sounds or gestures. || A phoneme is "the smallest phonetic unit in a language that is capable of conveying a distinction in meaning." >> So a phoneme is defined by the **contrasts** it makes between words. This ties it to **minimal pairs**. >> One phoneme can have several **allophones** and still be one category. >> Applying the definition can be complicated in practice. || Three things belong to three different descriptions. >> A **letter** is part of a writing system. >> A **phone** is an actual speech realization. >> A **phoneme** organizes the contrasts that carry meaning. || The **International Phonetic Alphabet** gives speech scientists a way of communicating about similarities and differences between sounds across languages. >> So it is easy to think of the IPA as an alphabet of phonemes. >> That would be a mistake, because phonemes are not letters. || That is why the number of written symbols, phones and phonemes can all differ.
27|Speech is a **continuously changing signal**. It is not an orderly stream of letter-like units. || Literate people easily imagine speech as a row of separate units like letters. It is nothing like that. || A **spectrogram** of the sentence "A sore back is a real pain" makes the difference visible. >> Time runs from left to right, and frequency increases upward. >> Darker regions have stronger acoustic energy. >> Broad frequency patterns change over time, and transitions connect neighboring segments. >> The phonetic labels and boundary markers are added on top. They organize the reading of the signal and do not cut it into separate pieces. || A **phonemic transcription** gives useful categories. Those categories do not match isolated blocks that have the same acoustic shape in every context. || So recognizing the sentence means interpreting changing patterns. It does not mean finding a clean boundary around each letter. That is why **phonemes are not letters**.
28|Speech takes coordinated movements of the **articulators**. They are the lips, jaw, tongue and other structures of the vocal tract. || Each part changes the vocal tract in its own way. >> The **tongue** changes the internal shape of the oral cavity. >> The **lips and jaw** change its opening. >> Together, these positions shape the sound that comes out. || A side view of the vocal tract, from USC SPAN, makes the articulators visible in action. >> It locates the tongue inside the mouth and the passage extending toward the throat. >> The outline of the lips and the position of the tongue link the spoken output to the shape of the vocal tract. || The movements run as one **ongoing sequence**. So a speech **gesture** is part of a moving system. It is not one posture repeated unchanged for every occurrence of a phoneme. That is why the articulators do not stamp out one letter-like sound at a time.
29|**Hockett (1955)** gave an analogy for why recognizing speech is not recovering a row of separate pieces. || A row of Easter eggs is carried along a moving belt. The eggs have various sizes and colors and are not boiled. >> The flow of eggs before the wringer stands for the series of impulses from the **phoneme source**. || The belt carries the eggs between the two rollers of a wringer. The rollers smash the eggs and rub them more or less into each other. >> The mess that emerges stands for the output of the **speech transmitter**. >> So information about neighboring elements is spread together in the speech signal. || An **inspector** then examines the passing mess. He uses the broken and unbroken yolks, the spread-out albumen and the colored bits of shell. He decides what flow of eggs arrived at the wringer. >> The inspector stands for the **hearer**. >> He does not have to put the eggs together again, which is physically impossible. He only has to **identify** them. || So a hearer can identify the original sequence from a mixed signal without rebuilding each separate piece.
30|People have started to question whether **phonemes** are "real", either psychologically or linguistically. || A phonemic description is useful for describing contrasts. That does not settle how a speaker stores words or how a listener recognizes speech. >> A descriptive notation answers one question. >> A model of mental processing answers a related but different question. || Several papers raise the issue. >> **Lotto and Holt (2000)** wrote about the illusion of the phoneme. >> **Beckman and Edwards (2000)** wrote about how phonological categories develop and the primacy of lexical learning. >> **Port (2007)** asked how words are stored in memory, beyond phones and phonemes. >> **Goldinger and Azuma (2003)** wrote about puzzle-solving science in speech perception. || What these papers share is the relation between the categories used for analysis and actual **cognitive representation**. So the open question is whether phoneme-sized units are the right description of the mental processes themselves.
