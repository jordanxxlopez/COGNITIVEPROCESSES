2|**Oldfield and Wingfield (1965)** asked people to name pictures of objects. They measured the **response time** for each name. >> The example line drawings come from Snodgrass and Vanderwart (1980). That later set of drawings was not used in the original study. || They found a **log-linear relationship** between word frequency and response time. >> A log-linear relationship means the times fall on a straight line once frequency is put on a logarithmic scale. >> For **frequent names**, naming was faster. >> For **rare names**, naming was slower. || The problem is that frequency is related to **word length**, even in this tiny sample of objects. >> Frequent words tend to be **shorter**. >> So a frequent name and a rare name usually differ in length too. >> Either property could cause the difference in naming time. || This problem is called a **confound**. >> A confound is a second variable that changes along with the variable being tested. >> It gives another possible explanation for the same result. || So the frequency effect is a classic cautionary tale. A clear relationship is only the start of finding out which property causes it. The relationship can be checked with numbers using a web-based tool.
3|A **lexical database** collects information about many words in one place. Researchers can search it and compare words. || The **English Lexicon Project** is one such database. >> It gives the **lexical characteristics** of words, such as frequency and length. >> It also gives **behavioral data** from naming and lexical-decision tasks. >> Its homepage has separate links for generating word and nonword lists. >> Other links give trial-level data from lexical decision and naming. || The two kinds of links serve two jobs. >> One job is choosing words with certain properties. >> The other job is looking at how people actually responded to words. || A database lets researchers measure their candidate words instead of trusting intuition. >> A list that seems "frequent" may also differ in length. >> The database reveals that hidden difference before the materials are chosen. || So a lexical database ties the intended manipulation to real measurements of the words.
4|The word-query tools give **two ways** to get information about words. || The first way is to **generate a list** of items with specific lexical characteristics. >> The researcher sets the properties, and the database returns words that fit. >> This helps when a study needs a certain range of frequency or length. || The second way is to **submit a list** of items to retrieve their lexical characteristics. >> The researcher already has the words, and the database returns their properties. >> This helps when the words were chosen for another reason, such as belonging to a semantic category. || Both ways start from a different place but do the same job. >> Each one makes the properties of the stimuli explicit. >> Each one can reveal an unintended difference between conditions. || So stimulus design often goes back and forth between searching for words and checking them.
5|A database query separates **fields to retrieve** from **constraints** on which words get in. || A field is a property the database reports for each word. >> Checkboxes let the researcher request **length** and frequency measures. >> Requesting both lets the researcher see whether length changes along with frequency. || A constraint is a limit on a property. >> Minimum and maximum boxes set the range for the chosen frequency field. >> Only words inside that range enter the list. || For a frequency study, the goal is words that differ in frequency but not in a confound such as length. >> The frequency constraints pick out candidate words. >> The returned lengths tell the researcher whether more selection is needed. || The researcher turns a design goal into measurable selection criteria. The database does not decide the comparison by itself. So the choice of fields decides which confounds can be checked.
6|**Word frequency** in English is very uneven. The 200 most frequent words in an old corpus were put into ten "bins" of twenty words each. || The bins reveal two patterns. >> For **mean frequency**, the value drops off very dramatically from the most frequent bin to the next. >> For **mean length**, the value goes up as frequency goes down. || The steep drop in frequency is **Zipf's law** in action. >> Zipf's law describes how a small number of words make up a large share of all usage. >> The top twenty words are used far more often than the next twenty. || Length is **inversely related** to frequency. >> Inversely related means that as one goes up, the other goes down. >> The most frequent bins hold the shortest words on average. || That is why frequency and length are hard to manipulate separately. Picking very frequent words also picks many short words.
7|A **boxplot** summarizes a whole distribution, not just its average. The line inside the box is the **median**, which is the middle value. || The comparison used **467 high-frequency (HF) words** and **467 low-frequency (LF) words**. >> The HF words were the 467 most frequent words. >> The LF words were selected at random. || The two length distributions differ a lot. >> For **HF words**, the lengths center on a shorter value. >> For **LF words**, the box and the median sit higher. >> **Fewer than 25%** of HF words are as long as the median LF word. || The difference covers most of each sample. >> It does not come from one unusually short or long word. >> The two boxes barely overlap around their medians. || So random sampling does not solve the problem. Choosing LF words at random keeps the natural link between frequency and length in the materials.
8|The problem is that **HF words are generally shorter** than LF words. So this length difference will always happen if LF words are selected at random. || The solution is **not to select LF words at random**. >> The researcher picks **long-ish HF words**. >> The researcher picks **short-ish LF words**. >> The two groups then have similar lengths but still differ in frequency. || This is called **matching** on length while manipulating frequency. >> The chosen words come from the parts of the two distributions that overlap. >> These words are less typical of their groups, but they make the comparison possible. || Matching changes only the experimental sample. >> It does not claim that frequency and length are unrelated in the language. >> The final groups must be checked to confirm they differ in frequency and agree in length. || So matching lets the frequency effect be tested with length held similar.
9|**Oldfield and Wingfield** eventually addressed the length confound. They **matched their high- and low-frequency words for length**. || They still found a **robust frequency effect**. >> If length were the whole cause, matching length should have removed the naming difference. >> The difference stayed, so length cannot be the whole cause. || They concluded that **frequency effects are not merely length effects in disguise**. >> The matched design keeps frequency different and makes length similar. >> That separation gives the surviving effect its value as evidence. || This logic runs through much of the field. >> A great deal of psycholinguistics is an attempt to show that an observed effect is not just an effect of some **confounding variable**. >> A confounding variable is one that changes along with the variable of interest. || So a correlation among word properties becomes evidence only after the confound is controlled.
10|Careful **stimulus matching** checks many word properties at once. **Cutler (1981)** called this problem "Making up materials is a confounded nuisance." || **Cortese and Simpson (2000)** did a very good matching job. >> They matched regular and irregular words across many properties. >> The properties included frequency, familiarity, imageability, length and sound or spelling measures. >> Careful matching on a wide range of dimensions is an important part of good stimulus design. || Very fine-grained matching raises **two concerns**. >> The first concern is that the chosen items may be somehow "odd." Their statistics may also be inappropriate. >> The frequency-length confound is a fact about English and most languages. Picking items to avoid it creates a somewhat **artificial stimulus environment**, even when done well. >> The second concern is that **very few items** may be left. This study had only ten words per cell, which is small for a word naming experiment. || So matching trades a cleaner comparison for a stranger and smaller set of words.
11|Most interesting phenomena in psycholinguistics happen **below the level of conscious awareness**. || A person naming line drawings does not usually think about word frequency or length. >> The person just names the objects. >> The frequency and length effects still show up in the naming times. || So psycholinguists do not have to be as crafty about design as some other researchers. >> Researchers studying **racial stereotyping** need craftier designs. >> Researchers studying **altruism** also need craftier designs. >> In naming, people are not aware of the properties being tested. || In **norming studies**, researchers simply ask people to rate words on some dimension. >> They just hope for the best. >> Some studies still add **fillers**, which are extra items mixed in among the test items. || The question is what the participant is asked to do. It also matters whether that behavior gives the intended evidence. So a simple task can reveal a process people do not notice.
12|Many tasks measure things that participants do **not consciously control**. These measures serve as **dependent variables**, which are the outcomes a researcher measures. || Each measure tracks something different. >> **Eye-tracking** records where and when people look. It is very revealing in speech production studies. >> **EEG** and **fMRI** both measure brain activity. >> **Reaction time** and **accuracy** are also unlikely to be under direct control in most experiments. >> A **tachistoscope** from the 19th century controlled brief presentation of stimuli. || Brain activity is useful mainly because participants usually cannot control it directly. >> It matters less for what it says about the brain itself. >> Suppose two kinds of stimuli produce different brain responses with no overt task. >> Then those stimuli are different to the participant. || So a good design picks a measure that the participant cannot easily steer. That measure then reveals how the stimuli are processed.

14|**Belke, Meyer and Damian (2005)** studied picture naming with **cyclic semantic blocking**. Participants named sets of objects again and again. || The sets came in two kinds. >> In **homogeneous sets**, the object names were semantically related. >> In **heterogeneous sets**, the object names were unrelated. || Naming was slower in related sets once the objects had already been seen. >> **Experiment 1** found that the blocking effect appeared after the objects had been named once. >> **Experiment 2** showed objects at the same time. It found blocking in naming latency and gaze duration. It found no significant blocking in the gaze-to-speech lag. >> **Experiment 3** found that the cost spread to new names from the same categories. || The study has nice design features. >> It changes how the objects are presented. >> It adds eye tracking. >> It adds new items. || So the three experiments together locate where the naming cost arises.
15|**Roelofs' WEAVER++ model** describes how a single word is produced. It has **conceptual**, **syntactic** and **word-form** levels. || A general **sentence-production model** has more levels. >> It has a message level, a functional level and a positional level. >> Phonological encoding and articulation come after those. || The two models do not align exactly. >> WEAVER++ focuses on activation for single words. >> Its syntactic information is not "positional." >> Instead, it holds single-word features such as number and gender. || The conceptual level and the **lemma** level of WEAVER++ matter most for semantic blocking. >> A lemma is a word candidate with its syntactic information, such as number and gender. >> Activation of a concept spreads to its lemma. >> Word-form encoding comes later, after a lemma is selected. || So the comparison places the competition before the sounds and movements are planned.
16|A **spreading-activation model** links related concepts. When one concept is active, activation spreads to its neighbors. || Activating **duck** also activates shared features such as animal, bird and swim. >> Those features connect to other animals such as **fish**, **snake** and **mouse**. >> Each concept passes activation down to its own lemma. || A naive view of spreading activation makes one prediction. >> A whole block of similar pictures would make each name more active. >> So naming times should get faster. || The model reveals how naming can instead get **slower**. >> Naming requires choosing one candidate over the others. >> A related set makes the target more available. >> It also makes the competitors more available. || So naming time depends on the selection process, not only on how active the target is.
17|Speech production requires telling apart **activation** and **selection**. Activation is how active a word candidate is. Selection is choosing the one lemma to say. || Spreading activation from **duck** to **fish** means activating one helps activate the other. >> This helps make related words available. >> This causes problems when the task is to select the correct lemma from many active ones. || **WEAVER++** assumes that selection time depends on a **ratio**. >> The top of the ratio is the activity of the most active lemma. >> The bottom of the ratio is the overall activation of all lemmas. >> A larger total activation makes the ratio smaller, so selection takes longer. || Feedback from the **category level** adds collateral activation. >> Collateral activation is extra activation for other items in the same category. >> It raises the total activation of all lemmas. >> The target becomes less dominant over its competitors. || That is why semantic relatedness can **slow down lemma selection** instead of speeding it up.
18|Belke and colleagues ran the experiment for **8 cycles**. In each cycle, all of the stimuli were presented. || The objects appeared in two kinds of blocks. >> In **homogeneous (HOM) blocks**, the items were related, such as "duck", "mouse", "fish", "snake". >> In **heterogeneous (HET) blocks**, the items were unrelated, such as "duck", "saw", "chair", "bus". || The same stimulus appeared in both kinds of block. >> For **duck**, it was named in a HOM block in one cycle. >> In the next cycle, duck was named in a HET block. >> The blocks alternated across cycles. || This design allows two comparisons. >> Response times can change with repeated naming across cycles. >> Response times can differ between HOM and HET blocks. || So an item belongs to HOM or HET only because of the other objects in its set.
19|Two things happen in the naming times at once. || The first is a big **identity priming effect**. >> Identity priming is faster naming of an item because it was named before. >> Both HOM and HET times drop sharply after the first cycle. || The second is a **semantic blocking effect**. >> Semantic blocking is slower naming in related blocks than in unrelated blocks. >> In later cycles, HOM responses are slower than HET responses. || The two effects come from different comparisons. >> For **identity priming**, the comparison is across cycles for the same item. >> For **semantic blocking**, the comparison is HOM against HET within one cycle. >> A condition can get faster over time and still be slower than the other condition. || So the two effects do not contradict each other. Mixing up the comparisons would merge them into one misleading description.
20|A possible confound is **visual similarity**. Related objects might look alike, so the blocking effect could arise at the level of **visual object recognition**. || Belke and colleagues gave two responses. >> First, the stimuli were **normed**. Objects in the same category were not more visually similar on average than objects in different categories. >> Second, they ran a **control experiment** that blocked visual similarity. || The control experiment found a different result. >> For **visual similarity**, the blocking effect appeared in the first block of trials. >> For **semantic blocking** in Experiment 1, the effect appeared only after the first cycle. || The two responses do different jobs. >> Norming checks the chosen pictures. >> The control experiment tests what visual similarity itself does to naming. || So the effects in Experiment 1 are not likely to be due to visual similarity.
21|**Hermens and colleagues** showed people several objects at the same time. They did not find the blocking effect. || That raises a worry. >> Maybe something is odd about seeing objects one after another, in serial. >> If so, the blocking effect might come from the serial presentation. || Belke and colleagues addressed this with a new task. >> Participants viewed **displays of four items simultaneously**. >> They produced the names in serial, one after another. >> One homogeneous display held duck, snake, fish and mouse. || The task separates seeing from speaking. >> The participant can see the whole set at once. >> The participant still has to select and say each name in turn. || Blocking still appeared in naming latency and gaze duration with these displays. So the effect does not depend on seeing objects one at a time.
22|Belke and colleagues measured **eye movements** while participants named the four objects. **Eye tracking** records where the eyes are and for how long. || The eyes and the mouth work in a sequence. >> The participant looks at one object. >> The eyes then move to the next object. >> The name of the first object is spoken after the eyes have moved on. || The record splits each name into intervals. >> **Gaze duration (GD)** is the time spent looking at an object. >> **Articulation onset (Art)** is when the speaker starts saying its name. >> The **lag** is the time between the end of looking and the start of the name. || These intervals can be compared across HOM and HET displays. || So eye tracking reveals where in the sequence the semantic difference appears.
23|The experiment has **two key dependent variables**. Each one tracks a different stage of naming. || **Gaze duration** is the time spent looking at an object. >> There is evidence that **lemma selection** is coordinated with eye movements. >> So a change in gaze duration points to a change in lemma selection. || The **lag** is the time after the eyes leave an object and before its name starts. >> Later processes happen during the lag. >> **Phonological encoding** is building the sound form of the word. >> **Gesture planning** is planning the speech movements. >> These can happen without visual support once the eyes have moved on. || The two measures split apart stages that overall naming time combines. >> An effect on gaze duration points to selection. >> An effect on lag points to processing after the eyes move. || So the question is which interval the semantic manipulation changes.
24|**Griffin, Bock and colleagues** made demonstrations of **eye-mouth coordination**. They track where a speaker looks while describing an event. || One example is accurately labeling a patient in "The horse is kicking a cow." >> The **agent** is the one doing the action, which is the horse. >> The **patient** is the one affected by the action, which is the cow. || The demonstrations link three things. >> The first is the event being described. >> The second is the places the speaker looks. >> The third is the words the speaker produces. || The eye record makes sense when it is lined up with the spoken words. >> It reveals when each participant in the event matters for building the sentence. >> It extends object naming to a full sentence. || So eye-mouth coordination is a useful tool for studying speech production.
25|The eye-tracking experiment produced **three measures** with different results. || For **overall speech duration**, times decrease over the course of the experiment. >> Speech duration does not show a blocking effect. || For **gaze duration**, there is a **blocking effect**. >> People look longer at objects in HOM displays. >> The effect appears only after the first cycle. || For **lag**, there is **no effect**. >> Numerically, the blocking effect seems to go in the opposite direction. >> That difference is not significant. || Practice and semantic context must be kept apart again. >> A measure can get shorter across cycles without differing between HOM and HET. >> Only gaze duration differs reliably between HOM and HET. || So the semantic cost appears in the time spent looking at each object.
26|Each measure maps onto a stage of production. || **Speech duration** maps onto the **gesture level**. >> It drops over the experiment but has no blocking effect. || **Gaze duration** maps onto **lemma selection**. >> It has a blocking effect after the first cycle. >> Longer looking in HOM displays fits more competition among related lemmas. || **Lag** maps onto **phonological encoding**. >> It has no significant blocking effect. >> So there is no evidence of a blocking cost after the eyes leave the object. || The conclusion rests on the whole pattern. >> A positive effect on gaze duration points to selection. >> The lack of an effect elsewhere does not prove those stages are absent. || So the results place semantic blocking at **lemma selection**.
27|**Experiment 3** asked whether blocking **generalizes to new items** in the same category. **New items** were introduced in **Cycle 5**. || Two things happened at Cycle 5. >> The repetition priming effect went away for the new items. >> Overall performance slowed down dramatically. || The blocking effect was still there. >> HOM naming stayed slower than HET naming. >> The new items had never been named before. || The new items can be compared to the items in Cycle 1. >> In Cycle 1, no item had been named yet, and there was no blocking. >> In Cycle 5, the new items came from categories already named, and blocking appeared. || So yes, blocking generalizes to new items. It comes from the **semantic category**, not from repeating a particular list of names.
28|Belke and colleagues isolate the blocking effect in the model by ruling out **three other sources**. || The first source is **visual recognition**. >> Related objects might look alike. >> This source is ruled out. || The second source is **phonological encoding**. >> The words are not similar in sound. >> So sound overlap cannot cause the slowdown. || The third source is an **item-specific** effect. >> The effect might come only from repeating particular items. >> This is ruled out because blocking generalizes to new items. || Each control targets a different point between seeing an object and saying its name. >> Together the controls narrow the explanation. || So the remaining account is **lemma selection** among semantically activated competitors.
29|Each alternative explanation is paired with its own test. || For **visual recognition**, there are two tests. >> The stimuli were normed and matched for visual similarity. >> A control experiment tested visual similarity directly. || For **phonological encoding**, there are two tests, marked with a question mark. >> The words are not phonologically similar. >> Gaze duration is affected, but eye-speech lag is not. >> The question mark means the timing measures support an interpretation rather than directly observing the stage. || For **repeating specific lists**, there is one test. >> The effect generalizes to new items. >> The new items keep the category but change the actual names. || The tests give complementary evidence. >> Generalization changes item identity. >> Eye tracking splits naming into measured intervals. || So the effect is located around **lemma selection**.
30|An idea for an experiment starts with one question. The researcher asks "Couldn't it just be …?" || For **stimulus manipulations**, many features of words are correlated with **frequency**. >> One feature is **length**. >> Another feature is **age of acquisition**. >> Others are **concreteness** or **imageability**. >> Another is **contextual diversity**. || So frequency effects might be just a catch-all for these other properties. >> To test this, the researcher matches a suspected feature or manipulates it directly. >> Oldfield and Wingfield did this when they matched for length. || For **experimental manipulations**, even a simple blocking study involves many processes. >> One process is **object recognition**. >> Another process is **response selection**. >> Belke and colleagues tested such processes with controls, new items and eye tracking. || So a useful alternative must be specific enough to predict a different result.
