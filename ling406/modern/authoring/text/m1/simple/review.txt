4|Consonants are described by three **features**. A feature is one separate property of how a sound is made. || **Place of articulation** is where the vocal tract is constricted. The chart puts place on the horizontal axis, from the front to the back of the vocal tract. >> This is because bilabial sounds are made at the lips, so they sit toward the left. Velar and more back places sit farther right. || **Manner of articulation** is how the airflow is controlled. It runs from complete closure to open airflow. A **plosive** is a sound with a complete closure. || **Voicing** is the timing of **vocal fold vibration** (the buzzing of the vocal folds). Voicing can start before or during the closure, or after it. Within each pair, the right-hand symbol is the voiced member.
5|**/p/** and **/b/** have the same place and manner, but they contrast in **voicing**. Both are **bilabial** plosives, so the lips form a closure and then release it. This is because the place and manner stay the same, so only the voicing timing can differ. The two sounds differ in the timing of phonation relative to the release of the closure. The same kind of voicing comparison applies to /t/ and /d/, even though that pair is made at a different place. So we can talk about the **voiced stops**, /b/, /d/ and /g/, as one group.
6|A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in meaning because of a difference in one speech sound. This is because the rest of the word stays the same, so that one sound must carry the change in meaning. A minimal pair establishes that the two sounds are **contrastive**, meaning they belong to different **phonemes** (sound categories that can change word meaning). || Caught and cot serve this purpose for a speaker who keeps their vowels apart. The surrounding consonants stay the same, so the vowel difference carries the contrast. For a speaker who merges those vowels, that pair does not show two vowel phonemes. So the evidence is the contrast in meaning, not just two pronunciations that sound different.
7|In English, /b/ and /p/, /d/ and /t/, and /g/ and /k/ each differ in **voicing**. This is because each pair keeps the same place and stop manner, so only the voicing category changes. Comparing the pairs makes clear that voicing is the shared feature behind several contrasts. The bilabial, alveolar and velar stops occur at different places, yet the voiced and voiceless relation recurs at each place. || The evidence has to come from the language being studied. Two phones that exist in a language do not by themselves show a phonemic contrast there. Minimal pairs connect a sound distinction with a difference between words, so they are the test that matters.
8|Speech is a **continuous** acoustic signal, not a row of separate letters. This is because successive articulations overlap, so the sounds run into each other as the sentence unfolds. The **spectrogram** (a display of frequency over time, where darker areas show stronger energy) of "A sore back is a real pain" shows this. Time runs from left to right, and frequency runs up the display. || Broad frequency patterns change over time, and transitions connect neighboring sounds. A phonemic transcription gives useful categories. Those categories do not match isolated blocks with identical sound shapes in every context. So recognizing the sentence means reading changing patterns, not finding a clean boundary around each letter. The labels and boundary markers above the display only organize that reading.
9|Hockett's 1955 **analogy** describes the listener as an inspector who identifies eggs from a mess. This is because the eggs stand for the intended sequence of sounds, and the mess stands for the speech signal after production. The eggs are different sizes and colors, and the wringer breaks and mixes them. The inspector stands for the hearer. || The inspector does not have to put the eggs back together, which would be physically impossible. Instead the inspector identifies which eggs were there from broken yolks, spread whites and bits of shell. So recognizing speech does not need each original sound to be cleanly separated. Overlapping traces can still support identification.

11|**Derivational morphology** is the process that builds new words from existing parts. Those parts are called **morphemes** (the smallest meaningful units of words). This is because each part adds meaning, so combining parts makes a new word. The word **morphology** is built from **morph-**, which comes from Greek and means form, and **-ology**, which means the study of. || So morphology means the study of word forms. The word **phone** is another morpheme, and **morpheme** itself is built in a similar way. A morpheme is not the same thing as a syllable or a single speech sound. It contributes meaning or grammatical information.
12|**Derivational patterns** can be productive but still limited. The suffix **-hood** appears in brotherhood, sisterhood and knighthood. This is because each word names a state tied to the noun, but the meaning is not identical in each one. Brotherhood can describe a shared relation among brothers. Knighthood describes a status that each knight holds. || Some -hood words behave differently. Neighborhood is an established word, but -hood does a different job there. Friendhood is not used, because English says friendship instead. Enemyhood is only a proposed form, since words like enmity or antagonism cover that concept. So a recognizable affix does not combine equally with every base that could make sense.
13|**Zero derivation** changes a word's category without adding any visible affix. This is because the word form stays the same, so the new meaning has to come from learned uses. The verb in "She can dish it out but she can't take it" depends on a learned use of dish. "They decided to table the issue" depends on another learned use, of table. || Associations among knife, dish, spoon, fork and table do not make their verb uses interchangeable. "Forked tongue" combines an object-related shape with an established figurative meaning. Reading these as literal references to utensils or furniture would miss the intended meanings. So an unchanged word form does not guarantee an unchanged meaning.
14|Adding an affix can change the pronunciation of the word it attaches to. This is because the stress and vowels can shift when the new material is added. **Certificate**, **certification** and **recertification** differ in their stress patterns and in some vowels. This is one reason English spelling is hard, since a closer match to speech would not always make words easier to read. || The noun and verb forms of **record** and **invite** also differ in pronunciation. This is because the main change is stress, so the prominent syllable moves even though the spelling stays the same. The two uses of **entrance** are pronounced differently too. Their spelling is similar, but they are not related by derivation.
15|**Inflection** changes the grammatical form of a word while keeping the same word. This is because the grammatical job changes, but the word is still one lexical item. Plural nouns, third-person singular present verbs and past-tense verbs all show this. || Knife and knives are two forms of one noun. Knife, knifes and knifed are forms of one verb. Forks and mice both mark plural, and walked and sang both mark past tense. || The sound change can be irregular, but the grammatical job stays the same. Menn says it would be silly to have separate dictionary entries for the singular and plural forms of nouns. So inflection does not need a new dictionary meaning for each form.
16|Grammar can require a particular inflected form, but derivation does not have that requirement. This is because counting with **two** makes the following noun plural, whether the plural is regular, as in forks, or irregular, as in knives. The slot stays the same, and different nouns fill it. || Derivation does not work the same way. The pattern "Once you verb it, you can un-verb it" suggests a possible word, but it does not require every verb to have an un- form. This is because an un- form depends on whether a particular derived verb exists or makes sense. So inflection is a grammatical obligation, while a derived word is only a possible new word.

18|A **lexical node** is a unit in a simple network that stands for one word. The nodes LION, TIGER and STRIPES can each receive **activation** (a value that gives how strongly a node is active) from a presented word. This is because a presented word sends evidence to the node that represents it. || The input display and the lexical nodes are two different levels of description. A visible word supplies the evidence. The node is the word the system could recognize from that evidence. Treating each node as one unit makes it possible to describe how evidence for one word changes the whole system.
19|**Activation** can be partial before a word is identified. This is because LION contrasts poorly with the noise mask, so the input gives only weak evidence for LION. The LION node can receive some activation even while recognition is still uncertain. || A graded activation value makes these in-between states clear. The system does not stay inactive until recognition is complete. Its state changes as useful evidence comes out of the noise. Weak input raises a candidate's activity without yet giving enough support for a confident identification.
20|A **perceptual identification task** measures when a person can recognize an item. The participant presses a button as soon as the item can be identified. This is because the signal becomes strong enough for the network to identify LION. The **signal-to-noise ratio** (the strength of the useful word information compared with the background) decides when that point comes. || The response time connects the button press to the model's account of activation growing over time. TIGER and STRIPES stay active as alternative nodes, but LION is the one identified. A condition that makes identification easier moves the button press earlier. So researchers can compare the factors that help word recognition.
21|**Frequent words** are identified faster because experience changes how activation unfolds. This is because each encounter can change the system in a simple way. A word might start with a higher **baseline activation**. It might also have stronger incoming connections, or a lower **recognition threshold** (the amount of activation needed for a response). || Each mechanism links past experience to faster identification. A higher baseline gives a head start before input arrives. Stronger connections make the same input raise activation more. A lower threshold means less activation is needed for a response. || Rumelhart and McClelland's 1986 Interactive Activation model simulates these effects. In its activation curves, time runs along the horizontal axis and activation along the vertical. The curve for CAVE rises as input is processed and then falls, while other words stay much less active. These curves describe states inside a model, not measured firing rates.
22|Seeing a word again soon after seeing it makes it faster to identify. This is because activation falls off more slowly than it rises. If the word is encountered again while activation is still high, its baseline is already higher than usual. || The first presentation changes the system that receives the second. The falling side of the CAVE curve stays above its earlier baseline. So a repeat during that decline starts from a raised level instead of the resting level. A shorter gap leaves more **residual activation** (activation that remains after the input ends), so the repeat is identified sooner.
23|Two more effects need relationships among word nodes. These are **neighborhood effects** (how similar a word is to other words) and **related-word priming** (seeing a similar or related word first). This is because the first effect depends on similar words influencing one another. The second depends on one word changing how another word is processed. || A model of isolated word nodes can only change the activity of one word. So to explain an effect from a different word, the model must specify how the representations interact. Related-word priming can be associative, semantic or phonological. A connected network provides the mechanism for that influence.
24|**Spreading activation** means that activating one word sends some activity to related words. This is because the network has weighted connections, so activating LION sends activity along the connection to TIGER. Each connection strength gives how strongly two words are associated. || This network can explain **semantic priming**, where a target is easier to access after a related word. The target is partly activated by the prime, so it is ready sooner when it appears. Association norms show which words are related. A prime can change the target's starting state without being the target. || A semantic network can link vehicle, car and bus, and it can link fire engine to both vehicles and fire-related concepts. This is because multiple connections let one activated concept influence several related words.
25|Balota and Lorch tested whether activation can travel through a connecting concept that is never presented. LION and STRIPES are not directly associated, but each is linked to TIGER. This is because the indirect route goes from LION to TIGER and then to STRIPES. || The comparisons included direct primes, primes that reach the target through a connecting word, neutral primes and unrelated primes. **Mediated priming** is the name for that indirect route. In **lexical decision**, people judge whether the target is a word. Direct primes helped, but mediated priming did not beat the neutral or unrelated primes. || In **pronunciation**, people read the target aloud. Both direct and mediated priming helped responses. Balota and Lorch proposed that lexical decision hid the indirect benefit. Since mediated priming helped in pronunciation, activation does travel through the connecting concept.
26|**Mediated priming** happens when a prime helps a target through an intervening association. This is because LION is connected to TIGER, and TIGER is connected to STRIPES, so activating LION can raise activity at STRIPES. LION and STRIPES are not directly associated, so the route through TIGER is the only link. || TIGER supplies this route without being presented as an extra stimulus. A direct association links a prime to its target in one step. The indirect route needs activity to pass through the network. So facilitation between indirectly related words is evidence about how the network is organized. The same path can be followed from the other end, with STRIPES as the input and TIGER as the connecting node.
27|Pronunciation was faster after mediated priming than after either control prime. Averaged across the two timing conditions, mediated priming gave a mean of 558 ms. Neutral primes gave 577 ms, and unrelated primes gave 575 ms. Latency here is the time before a participant starts saying the target, so a lower number means an earlier start. || Direct related primes were fastest, at 549 ms on average. The benefit of mediated priming was 19 ms against neutral primes and 17 ms against unrelated primes. Small differences like these matter because careful control conditions can pick out influences that are hard to notice in everyday speech. || **Stimulus onset asynchrony** (SOA, the time from the start of the prime to the start of the target) was 250 ms or 500 ms. At 250 ms, mediated priming averaged 558 ms, compared with 570 ms for neutral and 574 ms for unrelated primes. At 500 ms, mediated priming again averaged 558 ms, compared with 583 ms and 576 ms. Since mediated priming was faster at both SOAs, the benefit did not depend on one timing.
28|**Spreading activation** can also slow naming. This is because activating several related words can make it harder to choose one of them. Activating duck can spread activation to fish, snake and mouse through shared properties and categories. || One might expect a block of similar items to make naming faster. But a naming task requires selecting one candidate over the others. A semantically homogeneous set activates several related candidates at once. That activity makes the target available, but it also makes its competitors available. So the effect on naming time depends on the selection process, not only on extra activation for the target.

30|In 1861, Pierre Paul Broca presented the case of **Tan**, a patient who had lost the ability to speak after a brain injury decades earlier. This is because Tan could produce only one jargon syllable, yet seemed otherwise to be in possession of his senses. Tan also seemed to understand what was said to him. || The contrast between speaking and understanding made the case important. Producing speech and understanding speech can be affected differently by brain damage. A serious speaking problem could exist alongside other abilities. So the case pointed toward a more specific explanation than a complete loss of intelligence or all language ability. Broca presented it to the newly established Society of Anthropology, where he was also the founding president.
31|Tan's brain had a **lesion** (a region of damage) that was large and definitely included the third frontal convolution. That convolution is also called the **inferior frontal gyrus**. This is because the anatomical description places the damage in the left frontal lobe. This observation helped establish **Broca's area**, a part of the left frontal lobe that is important for speech. || Hughlings Jackson made a criticism in 1874. Finding the damage that destroys speech is not the same as finding where speech is. This is because damage can interrupt a process without showing every operation of the intact system. The preserved brain also shows the injury was extensive, not a sharply isolated speech component.
32|In the 1870s, German neurologists Lichtheim and Wernicke studied aphasia more systematically. They explained losses of function as **disconnections** between centers. This is because a damaged pathway between two centers can cause one kind of loss without destroying both centers. In Lichtheim's house model, A stands for sound images, B for concepts, and M for movement images. || A disconnection between A and B would interfere with understanding spoken words. This is because the heard form could not reach its meaning. A disconnection between A and M would interfere with verbatim repetition. This is because the heard form could not reach the speech movements. So the pathway matters as much as the center. || The triangular diagram joins A, B and M. Lowercase a marks the acoustic input pathway, and lowercase m marks the motor output pathway. Numbered interruptions mark sites whose damage is proposed to cause different impairments. This approach of describing centers for specific functions is an ancestor of **modularity**.
33|Freud criticized the diagram-drawing approach to aphasia research. This is because he thought it was naive to expect the brain to be organized by psychological categories such as word forms or concepts. He proposed instead that language emerged from fields connecting perceptual and motor abilities to drives. || This raises a lasting question in cognitive neuroscience. Can brain area functions be related directly to cognitive functions? This is called a **holist critique**. A psychological distinction can be useful even when the biology involves interacting systems rather than one isolated center. So a mental category has to be connected to brain mechanisms through evidence.
34|Dronkers and colleagues used MRI to measure the damage in Broca's landmark cases more precisely. This is because MRI can show the extent and location of damage. The lesion goes deep and includes the **insula**. It also has much white matter damage in the **superior longitudinal fasciculus**, a pathway that connects the front and back of the cerebral cortex. || Other work from the same group suggests that the insula, not Broca's area, is strongly associated with **speech apraxia**. Speech apraxia is the production problem Broca described in his patients. So the anatomical evidence changes how the historical case should be read. The case moves from a small surface speech center toward a wider pattern of injury that includes disrupted connections.

36|A **lemma** (the abstract identity of a word before its final grammatical form is set) is selected before the form is fixed. This is because the error "Where'd I bring it when I put it in" swaps the two verbs, but the swapped verb takes a new form. The intended sentence was "Where'd I put it when I brought it in." BRING appears as "bring" after "Where'd I", not as "brought". So the lemma BRING was selected, and then expressed in present form. || An exchange of two fully formed words would carry "brought" along unchanged. This is why the error supports separating lemma selection from the processes that set the final form. The first clause already has "did", so the verb after it takes the base form. PUT looks the same in its base and past forms, so BRING gives the clear evidence.
37|People often say the opposite of what they mean because antonyms are strong competitors during **lemma selection**. This is because antonyms are semantically related and strongly associated, so activation spreads easily from a lemma to its antonym. Antonyms also belong to the same word class and can take the same semantic and syntactic roles. || So the rest of the speech production system can handle the wrong word. "Hot" and "cold" both describe temperature and can complete the same sentence frame. A wrong candidate can still form an ordinary sentence. Semantic similarity here does not mean the same meaning. It means sharing a domain and a close relation within that domain. So a big change in meaning can follow from a small, grammatically smooth substitution.
38|**Semantic roles** stay the same across sentences that describe the same event. This is because each participant keeps its relation to the event even when its grammatical position changes. "The plums in the icebox were eaten by me" and "I have eaten the plums in the icebox" describe the same basic event. || In the first sentence, the plums are the undergoer, the icebox is the location, and the speaker is the agent. The passive places undergoer and location before the verb and agent. The active starts with the agent, then the verb, undergoer and location. So the agent is the one doing the eating, no matter where that participant appears.
39|**Functional planning** has done three things. It has decided on a message to convey, selected the appropriate lemmas, and linked each lemma to a semantic role. This is because the speaker needs the word choices and their roles before a sentence can be built. For an eating event, the system can identify EAT, the one acting, and the thing eaten. || These choices do not yet decide whether the sentence is active or passive, how the pronouns come out, or the full sound sequence. So the plan is organized but is not yet a spoken clause. Separating these steps explains how a lexical choice can move while later processes still arrange the sentence.
40|In "Where'd I bring it when I put it in?", the **functional level** supplies two verb lemmas, BRING and PUT, with their participants. The speaker is the agent of both verbs, and IT is the undergoer of both. This is because those role assignments identify who takes part in each event. || A grammatical sentence also has to express how the two events are related. The positional level sets the clause pattern, the question structure, and the verb forms that tense requires. So knowing which participant performs an action is different from knowing where each word goes in the spoken sequence. A speaker can keep the right participants while putting a verb in the wrong place.
41|The question frame is "Where'd I [verb, root form] it when I [verb, past] it in?" This is because the first clause has the auxiliary did, so its main verb takes the base form. The clause after **when** needs a past-tense form. The positional level supplies both requirements as well as the slots for the selected lemmas. || If BRING and PUT enter the wrong slots, their forms can still fit the slots they occupy. The verb in the first slot is a base form, and the verb in the second slot is a past form. So the identity of a lemma separates from the inflection the frame requires. The error can be thought of as happening while the positional slots are being filled with the activated lemmas.
42|In "a language needer learns", two lemmas switched places, but the endings stayed with their grammatical jobs. This is because the noun is built from a verb root plus the derivational affix **-er**, and the final verb gets third-person singular **-s**. The structure has a noun phrase, NP, and a verb phrase, VP. || Derivational morphology changes the kind of word, as -er turns a verb into an agent noun, such as learner. Inflectional morphology expresses a grammatical requirement, as -s does in learns. Both affixes are still readable in the error. So the roots changed places, but each affix stayed attached to the job it does.
43|"He did that easy enoughly" also has a **stranded affix** (an affix left without its expected root). This is because -ly is the derivational ending that turns an adjective into an adverb. But this error also changed how the positional structure was set up. Two trees contrast an adverb followed by an adjective with an adjective followed by an adverb. || Changing which root fills the adverb position produces "enoughly", while "easy" is left without its expected ending. This is because the -ly ending goes with the adverb position. The starred structure puts the adverb position after the adjective position. So this error changes the frame itself, not only two lemmas inside an unchanged frame.

45|Oldfield and Wingfield asked people to name pictures of objects. They found a **log-linear relation** between frequency and response time. This means the points fall roughly on a straight line when frequency is put on a log scale. The relation means more frequent names were named faster. The line drawings come from Snodgrass and Vanderwart (1980). || Frequency is confounded with length here. This is because frequent words tend to be shorter, so a frequent name is usually also a short one. Either property could explain the faster naming. A **confound** (a second variable that changes along with the one being tested) makes the result ambiguous. So length has to be controlled before frequency can be called the cause.
46|English **word frequency** is highly uneven. The 200 most frequent words in an old corpus (a large collection of text) were sorted into ten groups of twenty. The mean frequency falls sharply from the most frequent group to the next. This is because a small number of words account for a large share of usage. The steep drop fits **Zipf's law** in the ranked vocabulary. || Length moves in the opposite direction. The highest-frequency groups contain shorter words on average, so length is inversely related to frequency. Selecting very frequent words therefore selects many short words. So a random low-frequency comparison will usually differ in more than frequency.
47|Researchers can avoid this **confound** by choosing the words deliberately instead of at random. This is because high-frequency words are generally shorter than low-frequency words, so random low-frequency words would almost always be longer. Researchers pick relatively long high-frequency words and relatively short low-frequency words. || These choices come from the overlapping parts of the two length distributions. The chosen words are less typical of their frequency groups, but they make the contrast possible. The check is whether the final groups still differ on frequency while matching on length. So matching keeps the frequency difference and holds length similar. Matching applies only to the chosen words. Across English as a whole, frequent words are still generally shorter.
48|Oldfield and Wingfield later matched their high- and low-frequency words for length. They still found a robust frequency effect. This is because if length alone explained the result, matching length should have removed the naming difference. Since the difference survived, frequency has an effect beyond length. || A **confound** is handled by changing the materials so that it predicts something different from the proposed explanation. Here frequency stays different while length becomes similar. That separation gives the surviving frequency effect its value. So much of psycholinguistics tries to show that an effect is not just another confounding variable.

50|Belke, Meyer and Damian (2005) used **cyclic semantic blocking** to study picture naming. Participants repeatedly named sets of objects whose names were either semantically related or unrelated. This is because the design compares naming in related sets with naming in unrelated sets. Naming was slower in the related sets once the objects had already been encountered. Repetition gives its own speed up, so the cost of related context still stands out. || The experiments used changes in presentation, eye tracking and new items to find where the cost arises. Experiment 1 showed that the blocking effect appeared after the objects had been named once. Experiment 2 presented the objects all at once. It found blocking in naming latency and gaze duration, but no significant blocking in the gaze-to-speech lag. Experiment 3 showed that the cost generalized to new names from the same categories.
51|In speech production, activation and selection must be kept apart. This is because spreading activation from duck to fish means activating one can help activate the other. That becomes a problem when the task is to select the correct **lemma** (the word identity chosen for speech) from many active ones. || The WEAVER++ model, a model of word production, says selection time depends on the most active lemma compared with the overall activation of all lemmas. So when feedback from the category level adds collateral activation to other members of the category, the target is less dominant. The speaker then takes longer to select the correct lemma.
52|Belke, Meyer and Damian's 2005 study used **cyclic semantic blocking** to examine picture naming. Participants named sets of objects over and over, with names that were either semantically related or unrelated. This is because the repeating cycles let the study compare the same objects in the two kinds of set. Naming slowed in the related sets once the objects had been encountered. || Experiment 1 found that the blocking effect appeared after the objects had been named once. Experiment 2 presented the objects simultaneously, and it found blocking in naming latency and gaze duration. It found no significant blocking in the gaze-to-speech lag. Experiment 3 found the cost also generalized to new names from the same categories. The paper calls related sets homogeneous and unrelated sets heterogeneous.
53|Speech production requires keeping activation and selection apart. This is because a related word can become active without being the word that gets chosen. Spreading activation from duck to fish means activating one can help activate the other. That causes trouble when the task is to choose the correct lemma from many active ones. || The **WEAVER++** model, a model of word production, assumes that selection time depends on the most active lemma compared with all lemmas together. Category feedback adds collateral activation to items from the same category. Then the target is less dominant, so lemma selection slows.
54|Gaze duration shows a blocking effect, but only after the first cycle. This is because looking at an object is coordinated with selecting its name. So a longer gaze in homogeneous sets fits more competition among related lemmas. So the selective gaze effect supports a **lemma selection** account of semantic blocking. || The gaze-to-speech lag shows no effect. This is because the numbers go in the opposite direction, but the difference is not significant. Overall speech duration decreases over the experiment, but it shows no blocking effect. A missing lag effect gives no parallel evidence for a cost after the gaze leaves the object. || The stages match up this way. Speech duration matches the gesture level, gaze duration matches lemma selection, and the lag matches later phonological encoding (building the sound form of the word). The drops across the experiment are related to repetition, not to blocking. The important comparison is whether homogeneous and heterogeneous sets differ within each measure.
55|Blocking still appears when new items from the same categories are introduced. This is because the effect does not depend on repeatedly naming one particular list of names. In cycle five, new items were introduced, and naming became much slower. The repetition advantage for the old items was lost, so both curves rose sharply. || Even so, the homogeneous sets stayed slower than the heterogeneous sets. This is because the process is tied to the semantic context, which survived the change in items. The effect therefore **generalized beyond the particular names** that had been practiced. Comparing cycle five with the first cycle helps separate category experience from simply having named a particular object before.
56|Belke, Meyer and Damian paired each alternative explanation with a **control test**. This is because a test that rules out an alternative makes the blocking effect easier to attribute to lemma selection. Visual recognition is addressed by norming and matching the pictures and by a separate visual-similarity control. || The phonological alternative is examined by using words that are not phonologically similar and by separating gaze duration from the eye-to-speech lag. The repeated-list alternative is tested by introducing new items from the same categories. || The generalization result is especially important because it changes the names while keeping the category history. The eye-tracking result divides the naming sequence into measured intervals. So these designs give complementary evidence that the effect is located around lemma selection. The phonological stage stays a question, because the timing measures support an interpretation rather than observing a hidden stage directly.
