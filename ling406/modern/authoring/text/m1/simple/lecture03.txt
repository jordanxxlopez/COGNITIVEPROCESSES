4|Knifes can be a word, because it is a form of the verb knife. This is because the form of a word depends on the job it does in the sentence. As a noun, knife has the plural **knives**. As a verb, knife has the form **knifes** after he. In the Scrabble argument, one player says the plural of knife is knives. The other player says Tony knifes Bernardo in West Side Story. Since each player is using a different word, both are right. || Three ideas are in this argument. >> **Inflectional morphology** (changing a word's form for grammar) is what makes the plural knives. >> **Derivational morphology** (making a new word out of an existing one) is what makes the verb knife from the noun. This kind is **zero derivation** (the part of speech changes and no affix is added). >> **Phrases and clauses** decide the ending. "He knifes him" has a singular noun, then a verb, then a noun. The subject is singular, so the verb has to take the **third person singular** ending.
5|**Derivational morphology** is how new words get made out of existing parts. The parts are called **morphemes** (units that add meaning or grammatical information). A morpheme is not the same as a syllable or a single speech sound. || The word morphology can be taken apart into two morphemes. This is because it was built from two Greek parts. The first part is morph-, which means form. The second part is -ology, which here means something like the study of. Since the two parts are put together, morphology means "the study of the forms of words". || A unit in text should get a name built the same way as morpheme and phoneme. This is because these unit names are built from Greek parts. The part morph- gives **morpheme**. Phone, another Greek morpheme that probably means speech, gives **phoneme**. || Graphology is where the pattern goes wrong. It looks like it should mean the study of units in text, but it does not mean that.
6|The meaning of a new derived word can only be partly guessed from the word it came from. This is because **derivational morphology** is **productive** (it can make new words) only in limited ways. People who hear a made-up word often cannot guess what it means. So the meaning of the final form is only **partially predictable**. || To knife somebody, a person would use a weapon-like knife such as the prison shiv. This is because of how **zero derivation** (changing the part of speech without adding anything) works. It usually uses only a **narrow sense** of the base word. The noun knife covers many objects. An image search for knife gives different cutting tools and tableware. A search for prison shiv gives an improvised weapon. The verb knife covers only one kind of action, which is attacking somebody with a knife. Since the verb is that narrow, a table knife is a knife but does not fit the verb well.
7|The suffix **-hood** can make new words, but it does not work on every word. This is because **derivational morphology** is limited in how widely and how regularly **affixes** (parts added to a stem) get used. || The -hood pattern takes a stem and adds -hood. The new word means a state of being that goes with the stem noun. >> Brotherhood and sisterhood follow the pattern. >> Knighthood follows it in an odd way. Two brothers share their brotherhood, but each knight has his own knighthood. || Three words do not follow the pattern. >> Neighborhood is a perfectly good word, but -hood does a different job in it entirely. It does not mean the state of being a neighbor. >> Friendhood is not what English says. English says friendship instead, for no apparent reason. >> Enemyhood is not a word, though maybe it ought to be. The only words for the idea are less common ones such as enmity or antagonism. || Since -hood works for some stems and not others, each word has to be learned. >> Some of these words come from Liberman and Prince's introductory linguistics course at Penn.
8|No, the meanings of these words are not similar as verbs. The words are dish, spoon, fork and table, which are strong associates of the noun knife. This is because **zero derivation** (changing a word's category without adding a visible affix) does complicated things with **semantics** (meaning). The word looks the same, but its meaning as a verb is learned separately. || Each sentence uses a meaning that people have to learn. >> In "She can dish it out but she can’t take it", dish is a verb. Its meaning does not come from naming the object. >> In "He was born with a silver spoon and a forked tongue in his mouth", forked uses the shape of a fork. Forked tongue also has an established figurative meaning. >> In "They decided to table the issue after a day of inconclusive negotiations", table is a verb. Its meaning does not come from the furniture. || Since the meanings come from **conventional usage**, reading the words as utensils or furniture gets the sentences wrong. >> The silver spoon expression is the kind of thing that **Cognitive Linguistics** studies.
9|Derivation usually changes how a word sounds and how it is spelled. This is because **derivational morphology** often adds **affixes**. An affix can change the **phonology** (the sound pattern) of the rest of the word. || The certificate words make this concrete. >> The word certificate is pronounced /sər.ˈtI.fɨ.kɨt/. >> The word certification is pronounced /ˌsər.tɨ.fɨ.ˈkeI.ʃən/. >> The word recertification is pronounced /ˈri.sər.tɨ.fɨ.ˌkeI.ʃən/. >> The **stress** lands on different syllables in the three words, and some vowels change too. So the base word does not keep its old pronunciation when an affix is added. || This is one reason for "The Chaos" of English spelling. Spelling that stays closer to the spoken form does not necessarily make words easier to read. || Some nouns and verbs are told apart mostly by **stress**. >> Record has one stress as a noun and another as a verb. >> Invite does the same in "He did not receive an invite" and "We did not invite him". >> Entrance also has two stress patterns, but those two words are not actually related to each other.
10|**Inflectional morphology** changes a word's meaning in a more predictable way than derivation does. This is because inflection only gives a different grammatical form of the same word. It does not make a new word. Menn puts it succinctly when she says “...we’d think it was silly to have separate dictionary entries for the singular and plural forms of nouns.” || English has three inflections here. >> The **plural** turns knife(n) into knives, one fork into two forks, and one mouse into a lot of mice. >> The **third person singular present** turns knife(v) into knifes, I walk into she walks, and you run into he runs. >> The **past** turns knife(v) into knifed. Today I walk becomes yesterday I walked. Today she sings becomes in the past, she sang. || The meaning is predictable, but the sound is not always predictable. This is because the phonological process of inflecting words often has some **irregularity**. Forks and walked just add an ending. Mice and sang change the word itself. Since mice is still plural and sang is still past, the grammar is the same either way. >> This irregularity comes up again with language learning models and kinds of developmental language impairment.
11|The rest of the sentence can force a word to be inflected. This is because **inflectional morphology** depends on **syntax** (how words are put together into phrases and sentences). In the phrase "two [noun]+pl", whatever noun goes in the slot must be made plural somehow. A regular noun gives two forks. An irregular noun gives two knives. Since the word two is there, the noun cannot stay singular. || Nothing in a sentence forces a word to be derived. This is because there are no syntactic processes like this for **derivational morphology**, even for very productive forms. The closest thing is a formula like “Once you [verb] it, you can un-[verb] it.” That formula is not syntax. No sentence makes a verb take an un- form. || Since only inflection is forced by the sentence, this is one way to tell inflection and derivation apart.
12|English does not use **inflection** (changing a word's form for grammar) very much. This is because English only inflects for a few things. >> It marks **plurals**, as in knives, two forks and a lot of mice. >> It marks the **third person singular present**, as in knifes, she walks and he runs. >> It marks the **past**, as in knifed, walked and sang. || Many other languages do much more with inflection. >> Many languages conjugate verbs much more elaborately than English for **person**. They also do more for **tense**. >> Many languages have different ways of making the **plural** for small numbers and for large numbers. >> Many languages have **case markings** on nouns (markings that tell the noun's syntactic or semantic role). || Since English leaves most of this out, English is a small case of what inflection can do.
13|Derivation makes new words, and inflection changes a word to fit its sentence. || The two kinds of morphology are alike in two ways. >> Both make new phonological forms out of old parts. >> Both change the meanings of base forms. || **Derivational morphology** creates **new words**. It is productive, but not always, and it is typically limited. This is because a pattern such as -hood works on some words and not on others. || **Inflectional morphology** changes how words interact syntactically with other words. It is **highly productive**, but it is not always regular. It is also **required by syntax**. This is because a phrase such as "two [noun]+pl" forces the plural, and some plurals such as mice are irregular. || To figure out which kind a change is, ask what the change does. The verb knife made from the noun knife is a new word, so it is derivation. The form knifes after he is the form the sentence needs, so it is inflection.
14|A sentence is more than a list of single words. This is because words are grouped into **phrases**, **clauses** and **sentences**. || There are two kinds of words in these groups. >> **Content words** include nouns and verbs. They also include modifiers such as adjectives and adverbs. >> **Function words** come in as soon as more than a couple of words are put together. Determiners and prepositions are function words. They tell how the content words go together. || The sentence “The video for “More than Words” came out before most of you were born” has two **noun phrases**. >> The first noun phrase is The video for “More than Words”. >> The second noun phrase is most of you. || Since one noun phrase is long and the other is short, a noun phrase is not a set number of words. It is a group of words that works as one unit.
15|The video for “More than Words” is a **noun phrase** with the structure **Det + N + PP**. This is because it has a determiner, then a noun, then a prepositional phrase. || Each part does one job. >> The is the **determiner** (Det). It is a function word, and it begins the noun phrase. >> Video is the noun (N). It is the head of the phrase. >> For is a **preposition**. It is a function word, and it begins the **prepositional phrase** (PP). >> The prepositional phrase is what lets the song title tell which video is meant. || The title “More than Words” is not really part of the sentence. This is because it is being used like a noun, as the name of a song. If the quotes are taken away, it is not a phrase. It is just a bunch of words strung together in a row.
16|Phrases with very different words can have the same structure. This is because the structure **Det + N + PP** is incredibly **abstract**. It names the kinds of parts and their order, not the words. || Three phrases have this structure. >> In "two guys from the early ‘nineties", two comes first, then guys, then from the early ‘nineties. >> In "a band with only one hit", a comes first, then band, then with only one hit. >> In "one and only one phrase unrelated to early ‘nineties one-hit wonder Extreme", one and only one comes first. Then comes phrase, then everything after it. || The parts are not the same size in each phrase. >> The **determiner** can be one word or several, as in one and only one. >> The **head nouns** are guys, band and phrase. >> The first two modifiers are **prepositional phrases** that start with from and with. >> The third modifier starts with the adjective unrelated and then has a to phrase. So it is an adjective phrase with a prepositional phrase inside it.
17|This level of **abstraction** is needed to say that different phrases are the same kind of thing. This is because phrases can look very different on the surface. Abstraction leaves out the individual words. Without it, there is no way to talk about very different surface forms as similar. || The abstraction is also needed to compare languages. This is because all languages have **NPs** (noun phrases), but they can build them very differently. >> English says the cat in the coffee shop. The determiner comes first, and the PP comes after the noun. >> Chinese says 咖啡店裡面的小貓, which is coffeeshop inside + cat. The order is **PP + N**, so the PP comes before the noun. >> The Chinese NP has **no determiner**. || Since Chinese NPs have no determiner, this helps explain why speakers of Chinese languages find determiners very hard to learn in a second language (L2).
18|All of these **prepositional phrases** have the same structure, which is **P + NP**. This is because each one is a preposition (P) followed by a noun phrase (NP). || Taking three of them apart makes this clear. >> From the early ‘nineties is from and then the NP the early ‘nineties. That NP is Det + Adj + N. >> With only one hit is with and then the NP only one hit. That NP is given as P + Det + N. >> Unrelated to early ‘nineties one-hit wonder Extreme is unrelated to and then an NP. That NP is given as Adv + Adj + Adv + Adj + N. || The NP after the preposition can be short or long. Since the preposition connects that whole NP to the rest, the NP counts as one unit.
19|Phrases are **hierarchical** (smaller phrases are inside bigger phrases). This is why a flat row of words with curly brackets and parentheses is a clumsy way to write them. || The phrase from the early ‘nineties has levels. This is because its parts are inside each other. >> The determiner, the adjective and the noun are all inside the **noun phrase**. >> The noun phrase is inside the **prepositional phrase**. >> The prepositional phrase is going to be inside a sentence, or inside yet another noun phrase. || Since phrases are inside other phrases, people started using **trees** to diagram sentences. A tree puts each phrase above the parts that are inside it. So a tree can keep every level apart, and a flat row cannot. || Hierarchy is also an important feature of languages.
20|A **verb phrase** is a verb plus the phrases that go with it. Different verbs have different phrases after them. || Came out before most of you were born is **V + PP**. >> The verb is came out. >> The PP is before most of you were born. >> This PP is hard to take apart. This is because most of you were born looks like a verb phrase. It is there to give a point in time that complements before. || Many verb phrases also have **noun phrases** in them. >> Sang the song is **V + NP**. The NP is the song. >> Worked himself to death is **V + NP + PP**. The NP is himself, and the PP is to death. >> Threw a party for him is **V + NP + PP**. The NP is a party, and the PP is for him. || Since these phrases all belong with the verb, the verb alone is not the whole verb phrase.
21|The whole sentence “The video for “More than Words” came out before most of you were born” is one **clause**. This is because a clause is a verb phrase (VP) and its **subject**. The subject is the noun phrase that controls the verb. This sentence has one VP and one subject, so it is one clause. || The sentence has two parts. >> The NP is The video for “More than Words”. It goes Det + N + PP. >> The VP is came out before most of you were born. It goes V + PP. || The subject is the whole first NP. The **head noun** of that NP is video. The song title is inside the PP, so the title is not the subject.
22|A verb agrees with the head noun of its own subject. That is what it means for the subject to **control** the verb. || In “The plums that were in the icebox were delicious”, the verb agrees with plums, not icebox. This is because plums is the **head** of the noun phrase. This sentence is one simple clause. Its parts are given as Det + N + PP and then VP. || If you changes to my roommate, the verb were probably saving changes to was probably saving. This is because “The plums in the icebox, which you were probably saving for breakfast, were delicious” has a **clause within a clause**. The inner clause is set off with commas. >> You is the subject of were probably saving for breakfast. >> Plums is the subject of were delicious. >> Since my roommate would only replace you, only the inner verb changes. Were delicious stays the same because its subject is still plums. || "Forgive me" has an **implicit subject** (a subject that is not said out loud). The speaker wants whoever shares his icebox to forgive him for eating the plums.
23|Two sentences can mean the same thing and still have different subjects. This is because the **subject** is decided by the syntax, not by who did the action. || In "I have eaten the plums in the icebox", the subject is I. This is the **active** sentence. >> The subject comes first, and a verb phrase comes after it. >> The verb phrase includes the NP the plums in the icebox. That NP is the **object** of the verb. || In "The plums in the icebox were eaten by me", the subject is the plums in the icebox. This is the **passive** sentence. >> The way to tell is agreement. The verb were eaten agrees with the plural noun plums. >> By me is just a PP that fills out the VP. It tells who did the eating, but it is not the subject. || Since the same plums are the object in one sentence and the subject in the other, the two sentences are very different syntactically.
24|The **semantic roles** do not change between the active and the passive sentence. This is because semantic roles are about what each participant does in the event. The event is the same in both sentences. The eater is the **agent**, the plums are the **undergoer**, and the icebox is the **location**. || Only the order and the **syntactic roles** (such as subject and object) change. >> The passive sentence goes Undergoer + Location + Verb + Agent. The plums is the undergoer, in the icebox is the location, and by me is the agent. Its subject is the undergoer. >> The active sentence goes Agent + Verb + Undergoer + Location. I is the agent, the plums is the undergoer, and in the icebox is the location. Its subject is the agent. || It can seem counterintuitive that me is not the subject of the passive sentence. This is because English usually lines up the syntactic subject with the agent. Grade school teaches that grammar tells "who did what to whom". Semantic roles do that job more than syntactic roles do. || How the two kinds of roles match up also affects sentence processing.
25|Language is highly **generative** (people can keep making new words and sentences out of familiar parts). This is because three things work together. >> **Derivational morphology** lets people make new words. >> **Syntax** lets people organize words into clauses, clauses into phrases, and phrases into sentences. >> **Inflectional morphology** lets people modify words for different syntactic roles. || People do this mostly without thinking explicitly about it. A speaker can make a plural or build a phrase without naming any grammar category. || This makes linguistics hard in one way. One of the hardest things to understand is what there is to explain. This is because some of the most complex phenomena are hiding in plain sight.
