2|After the functional level, three more levels turn the chosen words into speech. This is because the words still need a grammatical order, a sound plan and mouth movements. The **positional level** puts the words into a grammatical structure. The **phonological encoding level** turns them into a string of **phonemes** (speech sounds that tell words apart). The **speech gestural level** has the motor system carry out that string as movements. || Speech errors are evidence that **abstract units** are real. This is because errors move units like **phonemes**, **lemmas** and **noun phrases** in regular ways. Nobody can hear these units directly. Since errors treat them as units anyway, the units are useful in the theory. || Collected errors can be sorted by the level where they went wrong. Purely **observational research** also has weak spots, so **experimental methods** are needed too.
3|In "Where'd I bring it when I put it in?", the functional level already knows the verbs and who does what. This sentence was earlier evidence that **lemmas** (words chosen for meaning and grammar, before their sounds) are useful. || The functional level knows three things. There are two verbs, PUT and BRING. I/ME is the **Agent** (the doer) of both verbs. IT is the **Undergoer** (the thing acted on) of both verbs. || The functional level does not yet know everything else. It does not set the **temporal relationship** between the verbs, which the word when expresses. It does not set the question form or the verb tenses. || The **positional level** is where all of this gets expressed in syntax. This is because the positional level builds the actual sentence structure. Since the roles alone give no word order or tense, a separate level has to add them.
4|The positional level builds a sentence frame with **slots**, and each slot decides the verb's form. Without the question steps, the frame is roughly "Where'd I [verb, root form] it when I [verb, past] it in?" || This is because English grammar asks for different forms in each clause. The first clause needs the **base form** of the verb. That clause took real syntax, with **Wh- movement** and an **auxiliary verb**. The when clause needs the **past form**. The frame also holds the other **particles** the grammar needs. || The error happens while the lemmas from the functional level are being put into the slots. BRING went into the root form slot, so it came out as bring. PUT went into the past slot, so it came out as put. Since each verb takes the form of its slot, the sentence still sounds grammatical.
5|"A language needer learns" is similar to the bring and put error because two lemmas got switched. The speaker meant "a language learner needs." This is because LEARN and NEED got swapped while being put into the positional level. The frame stayed mostly the same, a noun phrase and then a verb. || It is different because of the noun. The noun is actually a **verb plus an affix**. Learner is the verb learn plus -er. In the error, need gets the -er instead, so it becomes needer. Adding -er to a verb is **derivational morphology** (making a new word out of an existing one). || Since only the roots moved, this error happened inside words, not between whole words.
6|In "a language needer learns," the roots NEED and LEARN switched slots, but the endings stayed put. This is because the endings belong to the slots, not to the words. || The noun slot is built as a **verb plus -er**. That -er is a **derivational affix** (an ending that makes a new word). It turns a verb into a noun for the person doing it. The verb slot asks for the **third person singular** form, which adds -s. That -s is **inflectional morphology** (an ending the grammar requires). || The tree has a noun phrase (NP) and a verb phrase (VP). Need landed in the noun slot, so it got -er and became needer. Learn landed in the verb slot, so it got -s and became learns. || If the whole words learner and needs had swapped, their endings would have moved with them. Since the endings stayed, the affixes are called **stranded**.
7|"He did that easy enoughly" puts the ending **-ly** on the wrong word. The speaker meant "He did that easily enough." || This is because -ly should have gone on easy, making easily. Instead it went on enough, making the odd form enoughly. Easy was left without any ending. || The questions are how this error is similar to "a language needer learns," and how it is different. To figure that out, compare the intended words with the produced words one at a time.
8|"Easy enoughly" is similar to "a language needer learns" because both have a **stranded affix** (an ending that stays in its slot while a different root lands there). Here the affix is **-ly**, which makes an **adjective** into an **adverb**. || It is different because something also went wrong while setting up the **positional level** itself. This is because the frame's order changed. In the intended frame, the adverb slot comes first, built as adjective plus -ly. The plain adjective slot comes second. In the starred error frame, the adjective slot comes first and the adverb slot comes second. || Easy filled the first slot and enough filled the second slot. So enough got the -ly. Since the frame itself flipped, this error is not only about filling slots, unlike "needer."
9|Errors like needer and enoughly are evidence that **derivational morphology** is real. This is because **affixes** seem to float around at the positional level. || Another view says derived words are just separate stored words. In that view, they are linked to their roots in some rule-like way. The lexicon would hold sets like needy, need and needless, or unlearn, learn and learner. Then affixes would not need their own representation. Speakers would not need quirky rules for building common words either. || But speakers make **non-words** like "needer" and "enoughly" on the fly. These could not come from a stored list. So the errors suggest three things. **Lemmas and affixes** are stored **separately**. **Affixes have a slot** in the positional level. **Derived words** can be built by a **grammatical process**.
10|Adding an affix at the positional level does not finish the job. This is because the sounds of the new word still have to be worked out. || Sometimes the sounds are just added on. Easy /’i.zi/ becomes easily /’i.zIl.i/. Enough /i.’nəf/ becomes enoughly /i.’nəf.li/. || Sometimes the base word changes too. The word certificate is /sər.ˈtI.fɨ.kɨt/. The word certification is /ˌsər.tɨ.fɨ.ˈkeI.ʃən/. The word recertification is /ˈri.sər.tɨ.fɨ.ˌkeI.ʃən/. Across these words, the **vowels change** and the **stress moves**. || The positional level only says something like "add -er" or "make this adjective into an adverb". Since that instruction does not give the sounds, a later level still has to work out how to say the word.
11|**Phonological encoding** (turning lemmas, affixes and particles into a string of phonemes) worked fine for "easy enoughly." This is because the speaker did not say "easy /inəvɪli/" or mess up the sounds in some other way. So that error happened earlier. || Errors at this level mix up the **order of phonemes**. Fish grotto came out as gish frotto. Chomsky and Halle came out as Chomsky and Challe. With this ring I do wed came out as with this wing I do red. || Menn calls these errors **"neat."** This is because they follow English **phonotactic patterns** (rules for which sound sequences are allowed). As far as anyone knows, they are also **clearly produced**. Since only the order went wrong, the wrong sounds still come out clean.
12|At the **speech gestural level**, the motor system carries out the planned phonemes as **gestures** (movements). Errors here make speech **unclear or imprecise**. This is because the mistake is in the movement, not the plan. So these errors are **not necessarily "neat,"** unlike phonological encoding errors. || **Many errors are ambiguous.** Ad hoc came out as odd hack. In sounds, /æd hɑk/ became /ɑd hæk/. /æ/ is a low front vowel, as in bat. /ɑ/ is a low back vowel, as in hot. || This error could come from two levels. At the phonological encoding level, it could be **metathesis** (two sounds switching places in the plan). At the gestural level, it could be a **miscuing of tongue position**. Since both give the same result, the error alone cannot tell which level failed.

14|These errors split into word form errors and sound errors. This is because each one changed a different kind of unit. || Buyed in, for bought in, is a word form error. The speaker put the regular past ending on a verb whose past is irregular. This is **morphological regularization** (using the regular pattern where an irregular form belongs). Arising came out as "arriving." One consonant changed in a word that sounds very similar. || In "It's pretty close to here," close was said as [kloʊz]. The last consonant became voiced, but this close normally ends in a voiceless one. Thoroughly came out as "throfly," with several sounds changed. || Since each error changes a different unit, compare the intended and produced sounds first. Then the unit that changed tells which level went wrong.
15|Some of these errors move whole words, and others move single sounds. This is because different units can get switched. || In "Wash my teeth and brush my face," the **verbs** wash and brush switched. The objects teeth and face stayed in place. || Following vowel came out as "vollowing fowel." The first consonants **switched**. Seat heaters came out as "heat seaters." Sounds moved, but the plural ending stayed on seaters. Speech-language pathology came out as "speech plath... speech plang." The speaker made attempts and repairs, and those count as evidence too. || Some errors bring in a familiar sequence. Lana del Rey came out as "Marina del Rey." "For all intensive purposes" puts different words into a familiar phrase. "It's in the stimulus" is another collected error.

17|Saying "a hundred-dollar room" in a talk about hot yoga is an error in picking the word for the meaning. The speaker said "you enter a hundred-dollar room and you do yoga." || This is because the context changes what fits. In a talk about cost, a hundred-dollar room would be normal. In a talk about **hot yoga**, the hundred could be about the room's heat, not its price. So dollar is a real word, but it does not fit what was meant. || The rest of the sentence is fine. The clause is **grammatical**, and the words are easy to say. Since the grammar and sounds are fine, the error is in **choosing the word**.
18|"Law and Order SUV" switched the last two letters of the title SVU. This is because **SUV** is a familiar vehicle abbreviation with the same three letters. **SVU** is part of the show's title. || Since the produced form is a real, familiar sequence, a competing familiar form may have been **selected**. So the error should not be assumed to be a simple motor slip. The result is still easy to say. || To analyze it, start with the exact change, SVU becoming SUV. Then ask which familiar forms were competing.
19|"He cooked his kitchen," said for chicken, mixes up sound and meaning. This is because kitchen and chicken are close in both ways. || **In sound**, the words are very similar. Their first and middle consonants show up in opposite positions. **In meaning**, both have to do with cooking. || The grammar did not break. Both words are nouns, and both fit the slot after his. So the sentence keeps its **grammatical frame**. || Since sound and meaning both play a part, the analysis has to look at the word forms and the situation.
20|Yes, what observers **expect** can change which errors they **hear and report** in **observational studies**. This is because people listen for the kinds of errors they expect. Learning about speech production changes what they expect. So it can change what they notice and what they write down. || To figure out where an error happened in the model, the **situation** matters. This is because the analyst has to rebuild what the speaker meant to say. A form can be fine by itself but wrong for what the speaker meant. || Since both the observer and the context shape the record, a collection of errors is not a neutral sample.
21|Observers miss many speech errors, sometimes whole classes of them, because of how **speech perception** works. Philosophers of science call this **theory-dependent observation**. || This is because listeners have three biases. They are bad at hearing sounds **"halfway" between two phonemes**, especially **consonants**. They are **biased against** hearing **phonotactically illegal strings** (sound sequences the language does not allow). They are **biased toward** hearing unclear sounds **as words**. A sound between "giss" and "kiss" sounds more like "kiss." || **Experimental methods** and careful measurement can fix some of these problems. They cannot fix all of them. Since perception filters what gets reported, the reported errors are not the full set.
