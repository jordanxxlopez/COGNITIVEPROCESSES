2|People name pictures of objects faster when the object's name is a frequent word. **Oldfield and Wingfield (1965)** found this when they asked people to name pictures of objects. The example line drawings come from a later set of drawings by Snodgrass and Vanderwart (1980), not from the original study. || They found a **log-linear relationship** (the times fall on a straight line once frequency is put on a logarithmic scale) between frequency and response time. So the more frequent the name, the shorter the response time. || There is a catch, because frequency is also related to **word length**, even in this tiny sample. This is because frequent words tend to be shorter. So a frequent name is usually also a short name. That means either frequency or length could be making naming faster. || This kind of problem is called a **confound** (a second variable that changes along with the one being tested). Since length changes along with frequency, length has to be controlled before frequency can be called the cause. A web-based tool lets researchers check this relationship with numbers.
3|A **lexical database** lets researchers look up the real properties of words instead of guessing. This is because it collects information about many words in one place. The **English Lexicon Project** is one such database. || It gives two kinds of information. The first is **lexical characteristics** (properties of words, such as frequency and length). The second is **behavioral data** from naming and lexical-decision tasks. The homepage has separate links for making word and nonword lists. It also has links to trial-level lexical-decision and naming data. || This matters because a list that seems "frequent" might also differ in length. A researcher might miss that by intuition. The database makes that hidden difference show up before the words are chosen. Since the properties are measured, the researcher can fix the difference while designing the stimuli.
4|The query tools can be used in two ways, depending on where the researcher starts. || The first way is to **generate a list** of items with specific lexical characteristics. This is because sometimes a study needs words in a certain range of frequency or length. The researcher sets the range, and the database finds words that fit. || The second way is to **submit a list** of items to retrieve their lexical characteristics. This is because sometimes the words were already chosen for another reason, such as belonging to a semantic category. The researcher gives the words, and the database returns their properties. || Both ways do the same job. They make the properties of the stimuli clear. Since the properties are clear, an unintended difference between conditions can be caught. So stimulus design often goes back and forth between searching and checking.
5|A database query has two parts, the **fields** to report and the **constraints** on which words get in. || Fields are the properties the database reports for each word. The researcher ticks checkboxes for **length** and for frequency measures. This is because getting both lets the researcher see whether length changes along with frequency. || Constraints are limits on a property. Minimum and maximum boxes set the range for the chosen frequency field. Only words inside that range make it into the list. || For a frequency study, the goal is words that differ in frequency but not in length. The frequency limits find candidate words. Then the returned lengths tell the researcher whether more picking is needed. Since the database only follows the criteria, the researcher still decides the comparison.
6|Word frequency in English drops off very sharply, and length goes up as frequency goes down. This is because a few words are used far more than all the rest. || To see this, the 200 most frequent words in an old corpus were put into ten "bins" of twenty words each. The mean frequency drops very dramatically from the most frequent bin to the next. It keeps falling across the later bins. This steep drop is **Zipf's law** (a small number of words make up a large share of all usage). || The second pattern is that length is **inversely related** to frequency (as one goes up, the other goes down). The most frequent bins have the shortest words on average. || Since the most frequent words are also the shortest, picking frequent words means picking short words. That is what makes frequency and length hard to separate.
7|High-frequency words are much shorter than low-frequency words picked at random. **Fewer than 25%** of high-frequency words are as long as the middle low-frequency word. || This comes from comparing **467 high-frequency (HF) words** with **467 low-frequency (LF) words**. The HF words were the 467 most frequent words. The LF words were picked at random. || A **boxplot** (a summary of a whole set of values) shows each group's word lengths. The **median** (the middle value) of the LF group sits higher than the median of the HF group. The difference covers most of each group, not just one odd word. || This means picking LF words at random does not get rid of the length difference. This is because random picking keeps the natural link between frequency and length. Since the link stays, any naming difference could still come from length.
8|The way to fix the length problem is to stop picking low-frequency words at random. || This is because HF words are generally shorter than LF words. So random LF words will always come out longer. || Instead, the researcher picks **long-ish HF words** and **short-ish LF words**. This brings the two groups to about the same length. The groups still differ in frequency. This is called **matching** on length while manipulating frequency. || Matching only changes the words in the experiment. It does not say that frequency and length are unrelated in the language. The researcher still has to check that the final groups differ in frequency and agree in length. Since length is now the same in both groups, any naming difference can be tied more directly to frequency.
9|Frequency still affects naming even when length is held the same. **Oldfield and Wingfield** found a **robust frequency effect** after matching their high- and low-frequency words for length. || This is because matching took length out of the comparison. If length were the real cause, the naming difference should have disappeared. The naming difference stayed. So they concluded that **frequency effects are not merely length effects in disguise**. || Much of psycholinguistics works this way. Researchers try to show that an effect is not just an effect of some **confounding variable**. A confounding variable is one that changes along with the variable of interest. Since controlling the confound did not remove the effect, frequency matters beyond length.
10|Careful matching on many dimensions is good design, but it has costs. **Cutler (1981)** called this "Making up materials is a confounded nuisance." || **Cortese and Simpson (2000)** did a very good matching job. They matched regular and irregular words on many properties. These included frequency, familiarity, imageability, length and sound and spelling measures. || The first cost is that the chosen items may be somehow "odd." Their statistics may also be inappropriate. This is because the frequency-length confound is a fact about English and most languages. So picking words to avoid it creates a somewhat **artificial stimulus environment**, even when done well. || The second cost is that very few items may be left. This study had ten words per cell. That is small for a word naming experiment. Since every extra constraint removes words, heavy matching can leave strange and tiny word sets.
11|Psycholinguists do not have to be as crafty about design as some other researchers. This is because most interesting effects in psycholinguistics happen **below the level of conscious awareness**. || For example, a person naming line drawings does not usually think about word frequency or length. The person just names the objects. The frequency and length effects still show up in the naming times. || Researchers studying **racial stereotyping** or **altruism** have to be craftier about experimental design. || So in **norming studies** (studies that collect ratings of words), researchers simply ask people to rate words on some dimension. They just hope for the best. Some studies still add **fillers** (extra items mixed in with the test items). Since people are not aware of these processes, a simple task can still measure them.
12|Many tasks measure things that participants do not consciously control. These measures are **dependent variables** (the outcomes the researcher measures). || **Eye-tracking** records where and when people look. It is very revealing in speech production studies. **EEG** and **fMRI** both measure brain activity. A **tachistoscope** from the 19th century presented stimuli briefly. || Brain activity is useful mainly because participants usually cannot control it directly. This is because people usually cannot choose how their brain responds. So suppose two kinds of stimuli produce different brain responses, even with no overt task. That suggests the stimuli are different to the participant. || **Reaction time** and **accuracy** are also unlikely to be under direct control in most experiments. Since participants cannot steer these measures, they reveal how the stimuli are really processed.

14|Naming is slower when the objects in a set are related in meaning. **Belke, Meyer and Damian (2005)** found this using **cyclic semantic blocking** (naming the same sets of objects over and over). || In **homogeneous** sets, the names were related. In **heterogeneous** sets, the names were unrelated. Naming was slower in related sets once the objects had already been seen. || They ran three experiments to find where this slowdown comes from. **Experiment 1** found the blocking effect after the objects had been named once. **Experiment 2** showed the objects at the same time. It found blocking in naming latency and gaze duration, but not significantly in the gaze-to-speech lag. **Experiment 3** found the slowdown also hit new names from the same categories. || The study has nice design features. It changes how objects are presented, adds eye tracking and adds new items.
15|The **WEAVER++** model by Roelofs only roughly lines up with the general sentence-production model. This is because WEAVER++ focuses on activation for single words, not whole sentences. || WEAVER++ has **conceptual**, **syntactic** and **word-form** levels. The general model has a message level, a functional level and a positional level. Then come phonological encoding and articulation. || The syntactic level is where they differ most. In WEAVER++, syntactic information is not "positional." Instead, it is single-word information such as number and gender. || The conceptual level and the **lemma** level matter most for blocking. A lemma is the word candidate with its syntactic information, such as number and gender. Word-form encoding happens later, after a lemma is chosen. Since blocking is about choosing words, it fits the lemma level.
16|Spreading activation can make naming slower, even though it sounds like it should make naming faster. || In a **spreading-activation model**, related concepts are linked. Activating **duck** also activates features such as animal, bird and swim. Those features link to other animals, such as **fish**, **snake** and **mouse**. Each concept then passes activation to its lemma. || A naive view says a whole block of similar pictures should speed up naming. This is because every name in the block gets extra activation. || The model explains why naming can get slower instead. This is because naming means choosing one name over the others. A related set makes the target more active. It also makes the competing names more active. Since the competitors are active too, choosing the right one takes longer.
17|Activating related words makes it harder to pick the right word. This is because speech production has two separate parts, **activation** (how active a word is) and **selection** (choosing the one lemma to say). || Spreading activation from **duck** to **fish** means activating one helps activate the other. That causes a problem when the task is to select the correct lemma from many active ones. || **WEAVER++** says selection time depends on a **ratio**. The top of the ratio is the activity of the most active lemma. The bottom is the overall activation of all lemmas. When the total goes up, the ratio goes down, so selection takes longer. || Feedback from the **category level** adds **collateral activation** (extra activation for other items in the same category). Since that raises the total, lemma selection slows down.
18|The same pictures were named in both related and unrelated blocks. This is because the researchers wanted to compare the same item in two contexts. || The experiment ran for **8 cycles**. In each cycle, all of the stimuli were presented. || **Homogeneous (HOM)** blocks had related items, such as "duck", "mouse", "fish", "snake". **Heterogeneous (HET)** blocks had unrelated items, such as "duck", "saw", "chair", "bus". || The same stimulus, such as "duck", appeared in HOM and HET blocks in alternating cycles. So duck is not a HOM item or a HET item by itself. It counts as HOM or HET because of the other items in its block. || This lets researchers compare two things. They can see how naming times change across cycles. They can also see how HOM and HET times differ.
19|There are two effects happening in the naming times at the same time. || The first is a big **identity priming effect** (faster naming of an item that was named before). This is because naming the same pictures again makes them quicker. Both HOM and HET times drop sharply after the first cycle. || The second is a **semantic blocking effect** (slower naming in related blocks). In later cycles, HOM responses are slower than HET responses. || The two effects do not contradict each other. This is because they come from different comparisons. Identity priming compares one item across cycles. Semantic blocking compares HOM and HET within the same cycle. Since the comparisons are different, a condition can get faster over time and still be slower than the other condition.
20|The blocking effect is not likely to come from visual similarity. The worry was that related objects might look alike, so blocking could happen during **visual object recognition**. || Belke and colleagues gave two responses. First, the stimuli were **normed** so that objects in the same category were not more visually similar on average than objects in different categories. || Second, they ran a **control experiment** that blocked visual similarity. It found different results. Blocking by visual similarity showed up in the first block of trials. Semantic blocking showed up only after the first cycle. || This is because the two kinds of blocking had different timing. Semantic blocking did not appear in the first cycle. Since the timing was different, the effects in Experiment 1 are not likely due to visual similarity.
21|Blocking still happens when objects are seen all at once. Belke and colleagues tested this because of a study by **Hermens and colleagues**. That study showed several objects simultaneously and found no blocking effect. || The worry was that maybe something is odd about seeing objects one after another, in serial. If so, blocking might only come from serial viewing. || To check, participants viewed **displays of four items simultaneously**. They then said the names in serial, one after another. One display had duck, snake, fish and mouse. || This is because the task separates seeing from speaking. The participant sees the whole set at once. The participant still has to pick and say each name in turn. Since blocking appeared in naming latency and gaze duration, it does not depend on serial viewing.
22|Belke and colleagues measured **eye movements** while people named the four objects. This is because eye movements break naming into smaller pieces of time. || The participant looks at one object. Then the eyes move to the next object. The name of the first object is said after the eyes have already moved on. || The pieces have labels. **GD** is **gaze duration** (the time spent looking at an object). **Art** is when the speaker starts saying the name. The **lag** is the time between the end of looking and the start of the name. || Since each piece is measured separately, researchers can see which piece differs between HOM and HET displays.
23|There are two key dependent variables, **gaze duration** and **lag**. Each one tracks a different stage of naming. || Gaze duration is the time spent looking at an object. It matters because there is evidence that **lemma selection** (choosing the word) is coordinated with eye movements. So a change in gaze duration suggests a change in lemma selection. || Lag is the time after the eyes leave an object and before its name starts. Later processes happen here, such as **phonological encoding** (building the sound form) and **gesture planning** (planning the speech movements). This is because these processes can happen without visual support once the eyes have moved on. || Since the two measures track different stages, they can show which stage the semantic effect hits.
24|Where a speaker looks lines up with what the speaker says. **Griffin, Bock and colleagues** made demonstrations of this **eye-mouth coordination**. || One example is accurately labeling a patient in "The horse is kicking a cow." The **agent** (the one doing the action) is the horse. The **patient** (the one affected by the action) is the cow. || This is because eye tracking records where the speaker looks while the sentence is being said. So the eye record can be lined up with the spoken words. || Since looking and speaking line up, eye tracking can reveal when each part of an event matters for building a sentence.
25|Of the three eye-tracking measures, only gaze duration shows a blocking effect. || **Overall speech duration** gets shorter over the course of the experiment. It does not show a blocking effect. || **Gaze duration** shows a blocking effect, but only after the first cycle. People look longer at objects in HOM displays. || **Lag** shows no effect. Numerically, the blocking effect in lag seems to go in the opposite direction. This difference is not significant. || This is confusing at first because the measures can change over the cycles. That change comes from practice, not from semantic context. A measure can get shorter across cycles without differing between HOM and HET. Since only gaze duration differs between HOM and HET, the semantic cost shows up in looking time.
26|The gaze-duration result places semantic blocking at **lemma selection**. This is because each measure maps onto a different stage of production. || **Speech duration** maps onto the **gesture level**. It drops over the experiment but shows no blocking. || **Gaze duration** maps onto **lemma selection**. It shows blocking after the first cycle. Longer looking in HOM displays fits more competition among related lemmas. || **Lag** maps onto **phonological encoding**. It shows no significant blocking. So there is no evidence of a cost after the eyes leave the object. || Since only the lemma-selection measure shows blocking, lemma selection is where the cost is clearest.
27|Yes, blocking generalizes to new items in the same category. **Experiment 3** tested this by introducing **new items** in **Cycle 5**. || In Cycle 5, the repetition priming effect went away. This is because the new items had never been named before. So overall performance slowed down dramatically. || Even so, the blocking effect was still there. HOM naming stayed slower than HET naming. || The new items can be compared to the items in Cycle 1. In Cycle 1, nothing had been named yet, and there was no blocking. In Cycle 5, the new items came from categories that had already been named. Since blocking appeared for these new items, it comes from the **semantic category**, not from repeating a particular list.
28|Belke and colleagues isolate the blocking effect by ruling out three other explanations. || It is not **visual recognition**. This is because the stimuli were matched for visual similarity, and a control experiment gave a different result. || It is not **phonological encoding** (building the sound form). This is because the words are not similar in sound. So sound overlap cannot slow them down. || It is not **item-specific**. This is because it generalizes to new items from the same category. || Each explanation sits at a different point between seeing an object and saying its name. Since all three are ruled out, what is left is **lemma selection** among related words that are all active.
29|Each rival explanation for blocking has its own test, and the tests point to **lemma selection**. || **Visual recognition** is ruled out in two ways. The stimuli were normed and matched. A control experiment also tested visual similarity directly. || **Phonological encoding** is ruled out, but with a question mark. The words are not phonologically similar. Also, gaze duration is affected, but eye-speech lag is not. The question mark is there because timing measures only support an interpretation. They do not directly observe the stage. || A **specific effect of repeating lists** is ruled out because the effect generalizes to new items. Since each rival explanation fails its test, the effect is placed at lemma selection.
30|A good experiment idea starts with the question "Couldn’t it just be …?" This is because most effects could have another cause. || For **stimulus manipulations**, many word features are correlated with frequency. These include **length**, **age of acquisition**, **concreteness** or **imageability** and **contextual diversity**. So frequency effects might just be a catch-all for these other properties. Researchers can test this by matching a feature, as Oldfield and Wingfield did with length. || For **experimental manipulations**, even a simple blocking study involves many processes. Two of them are **object recognition** and **response selection**. Belke and colleagues tested such processes with controls, new items and eye tracking. || Since each alternative predicts a different result, asking the question leads straight to a new comparison.
