2|**Phrenology** tried to figure out what parts of the brain do by measuring the shape of people's skulls. A simplified phrenological chart marks head regions for language, memory, thinking, love and other things. || It rested on three premises. >> Different parts of the brain are responsible for different processes. >> **Aptitude** (how good someone is at something) is related to the size of a brain region. >> The size of a brain region changes the shape of the skull. >> If all three were true, careful skull measurements would reveal brain functions. || No one believes in phrenology anymore, but not every premise is completely wrong. This is because the first premise is still a serious idea, since brain parts can do different jobs. The third premise is the clearly wrong one, because skull shape is not a sensitive measure of brain shape. The second premise would need a dependable link from an ability to region size before skulls could tell anything.
3|Phrenology failed for two reasons, one about anatomy and one about psychology. || The first reason is **inappropriate anatomical measurement**. This is because skull shape is not a sensitive measure of brain shape. So measuring the head cannot tell you what the brain underneath looks like. || The second reason is an **unconstrained definition of functions** (no limits on what counted as a mental function). This is because researchers picked broad social labels and treated each one as a single brain function. >> Studies of prisoners tried to find the region for "criminality". >> Studies of women with different numbers of children tried to find the spot for "philopropogenitiveness". || Since both the measure and the functions were bad, phrenology could not connect a real brain part to a real mental process.
4|**Flourens** was an early and vociferous critic of phrenology, and his lesion experiments argued against its map. He used **experimental lesions** (damaging or removing brain tissue and then watching how behavior changes). He worked mainly with pigeons and rabbits. || He did find that big brain parts do different things. This is because his experiments showed **distinct functions** for the cerebral cortex, the cerebellum and mid-brain structures. || But he **failed to find** specific cortical regions for specific senses. This is because senses did not drop out one at a time. When one sense was lost, all were lost together. For example, when most of the cortex is removed, sight, hearing and other senses disappear together. || Since the senses went together, he insisted that the **size of the lesion** was what mattered, not its location.
5|In **1861**, the neurologist **Pierre Paul Broca** presented a patient called **"Tan"** who could not speak but seemed to understand. Tan had lost the ability to speak after a brain injury decades earlier. Broca gave this talk to the newly established Society of Anthropology, where he was also the founding president. || Tan's case mattered because his abilities came apart. This is because one ability was badly damaged while others looked fine. >> Tan could only produce **one jargon syllable**. >> Otherwise he seemed to be "in possession of his senses". >> He even seemed to **understand** what was being said. || This was not the first case of a specific loss of speech. Since Tan lost speaking but kept understanding, the case suggested that **producing speech** and **understanding speech** can be damaged separately.
6|Tan's brain gave its name to **Broca's area**, a part of the **left frontal lobe** important for speech. Tan's brain is now on display at the Musee Dupuytren. || The name comes from where his damage was. This is because Tan had a **large lesion** (an area of damaged tissue). The lesion definitely included the **third convolution of the frontal lobe**, also called the **inferior frontal gyrus**. || **Hughlings Jackson (1874)** criticized this at the time. He wrote that "to locate the damage which destroys speech and to locate speech are two different things". This is because breaking one spot can stop speech even if speech depends on much more. Since damage only shows what stops working, it does not show where speech itself happens.
7|In the **1870s**, German neurologists **Lichtheim** and **Wernicke** explained different language losses as broken links between brain **"centers"**. They made more systematic observations of **aphasia** (language loss after brain damage). A center is a proposed brain spot for one function. || They said a specific loss comes from a **"disconnection"** between two centers. In Lichtheim's house model, A is sound images, B is concepts and M is movement images. >> Losing the ability to understand spoken words might come from a break between "A" and "B". This is because understanding needs the heard sound to reach its meaning. >> Losing verbatim repetition might come from a break between "A" and "M". This is because repeating needs the heard sound to reach the speech movements. || Describing centers for specific functions like this is an early form of **modularity**.
8|**Sigmund Freud** argued that the brain is probably not organized around our psychological categories. He critiqued the **"diagram-drawing" school** of aphasia research in his early neurological work. || He felt it was naive to expect brain areas for things like "word forms" or "concepts". This is because those are labels we use to describe behavior. Nothing guarantees the brain is built around those same labels. || He proposed instead that language comes from **"fields"** that connect perceptual and motor abilities to **"drives"**. This is a **holist critique** (the view that language is spread across connected systems instead of sitting in separate centers). || Freud's point leaves a **durable tension** in cognitive neuroscience. Can brain area functions be matched directly to cognitive functions? Is the brain really organized the way we describe cognition?
9|MRI showed that the damage in Broca's famous cases was much deeper than Broca's area. **Dronkers and colleagues** used **MRI** to measure the size and location of damage in Broca's landmark cases very precisely, including Leborgne's brain (Tan). || This is because the scans could see inside the brain, not just its surface. >> The lesion goes very deep and includes the **insula**. >> There is also a lot of **white matter** damage to the **superior longitudinal fasciculus** (a tract that connects the front and back of the cortex). || Other work by this group found that the **insula**, not Broca's area, is strongly tied to **speech apraxia**. Speech apraxia is the speaking problem Broca described in his patients. Since the deeper damage fits that problem, Broca's area may not be the main cause of Tan's lost speech.
10|Broca is still talked about 150 years later, even though both his behavior description and his anatomy were crude. || His description of behavior was crude because of the time. This is because linguistics then focused on history, like how languages were related and reconstructing ancient languages. Experimental psychology had not really been invented yet. So observations of behavior had to be informal. || His anatomical localization was also crude, and probably wrong. This is because detailed descriptions of the cerebral cortex were still in their infancy. For example, **Brodmann's map** of cortical regions was not published until **1909**. || Since these tools did not exist yet, Broca's limits came from his time, not just from him.
11|Broca's presentation of Tan is still discussed because it made linking **specific functions** to **specific brain regions** a **respectable pursuit**. || Other people found similar things around the same time. **The Daxes** reported related cases. **Wernicke** reported a patient who was **fluent** but produced **meaningless gibberish**. If anything, Wernicke's data are more interesting, because his patient talked easily but made no sense. || Still, for a variety of reasons, Broca's case is seen as a **watershed moment** in neuropsychology. This is because it convinced researchers that this kind of question was worth serious study.

13|**Cognitive neuropsychology** (studying how brain damage changes mental abilities) needs **cognitive psychology** to say what the abilities are. Much of what was learned about brain structure and function in the 20th century came from testing people with brain damage. || This is because a theory says which mental operations might be separate. For example, is recognizing the **abstract form** of a word separate from figuring out its **meaning**? If damage hurts one but not the other, the two can be **dissociated** (pulled apart). || Tasks can target different levels. >> **Physical identity** matching asks if two letters look the same. >> **Name identity** matching asks if two letters have the same name. >> **Rule identity** matching asks if two letters are both vowels or both consonants. || **Quantitative measurements** let researchers compare abilities statistically. This matters because **deficits are rarely complete**, so a patient is usually worse at something, not totally unable.
14|**Anatomical imaging** lets researchers see where a brain injury is while the patient is still alive. Before imaging, scientists had to wait for patients to "come to autopsy" to find the lesion. A scan of **H.M.'s** middle temporal lobes is one example, and his damage came from surgery. || But imaging the damage still does not tell what the healthy region does. This is because of the point from **Hughlings Jackson (1874)**. Locating the damage that destroys speech is different from locating speech. || Patient studies show what behavior looks like **after** a region is damaged. Since they only show the damaged brain, they tell only **indirectly** what the region does when intact.
15|**PET** and **fMRI** let researchers watch brains in action, but only in an indirect, slow and blurry way. **Posner and colleagues** described PET studies that used radio-labeled oxygen to mark activity. || They are indirect because they are **metabolic**. This is because they measure an index of **oxygen consumption**, not the neurons' activity itself. || They are slow because metabolic processes take **seconds**, while important neural events can happen in **milliseconds** or less. || They are blurry because of their **fair spatial resolution**. FMRI is better than PET, but both see detail only down to **millimeters**. Important things happen at the scale of **microns**, which is much smaller.
16|You cannot just say "x part of the brain is active during y task", so imaging studies need a **control condition**. This is because there is activity all over the brain all of the time. So seeing activity during a task tells you nothing on its own. || **Subtraction logic** fixes this. The researcher takes the task image and subtracts the control image. What is left is the activity for the part that differs between the two. For example, a motor image minus a rest image leaves a much smaller region. || **Posner and colleagues** used this to separate recognizing word and letter forms from processing word meaning. Their tasks shared the same visual input and responses. The key difference was whether **word meaning** was needed. Since meaning was the key difference, the leftover activity reflects **semantic processing**.
17|The region **Posner and colleagues** found for word meaning roughly matches **Broca's area**. || This is because three meaning tasks all lit up the same **left inferior frontal** region. >> Watching words for dangerous animals was compared with just viewing words. >> Thinking of uses for written words was compared with repeating them. >> Thinking of uses for heard words was compared with repeating them. || The same region appeared whether words were seen or heard. Since it appeared across all three meaning contrasts, it is linked to **semantic processing** (working with word meaning).
18|Is this better than phrenology? It can be, but only if the measurement and the definitions are good. The question is rhetorical but worth exploring. || This is because phrenology failed in two ways. It used a bad measure, skull shape. It used vague functions, like "criminality". || Imaging fixes both problems in part. It measures an index of **brain activity** instead of skull shape. It defines the operation with a **controlled task contrast** instead of a broad label. || A colorful image of an active region still proves nothing by itself. The contrast has to really isolate the operation. Since the image only shows what differs between tasks, the task design decides what it means.
19|**Posner and colleagues** answered by localizing small **component operations**, not whole tasks like reading. These operations work on **visual**, **phonological** (sound) and **semantic** (meaning) codes. || This is because a whole task needs many computations working together. Reading a word, forming a visual image and shifting attention each use several of them. So no whole task belongs to one brain area. || Their PET evidence **complemented lesion evidence**, because it showed activity while people performed tasks. Averaged blood-flow patterns looked **consistent** across participants. The participants were right-handed and good readers.
20|Language is good for studying how brain parts relate to functions because it splits into separate, testable abilities. Language has shown up at key moments in debates about **localization of function**. >> **Broca's presentation of Tan** helped start **lesion-behavior analysis** (comparing damaged brain areas with lost abilities). >> Some of the first **PET** and **fMRI** studies used activation of **Broca's area** in language tasks as evidence that functions can be localized. || This is because language abilities can come apart. Tan lost speaking but seemed to keep understanding. Understanding words, repeating them and judging their meaning each need different processing. Since these abilities differ, researchers can build clear contrasts for patient studies and control conditions.
