2|Language can be described as a **formal symbolic system** (abstract symbols plus rules for combining them). This way of describing language is very old, and it uses terms like those in **Artificial Intelligence**. || In the 1950s and 1960s, experimental psychology was actively hostile to this idea. This is because it talks about an **internal state** inside the mind. Behaviorists only wanted to explain learning with things they could observe. Those things were stimuli, responses and consequences. || **Skinner's Verbal Behavior** tried to explain how language is learned by **operant conditioning** (learning because a response gets reinforced). **Chomsky's (1959) review** of that book argued it could not explain what speakers know. The review is better remembered than the book itself. || The review was an important moment in **the Cognitive Revolution**. This is because Chomsky treated a **grammar** as something inside the speaker. A grammar can handle sentences the person has never heard. Since that internal system had to be taken seriously, internal mental states came back into psychology.
3|The formal tradition comes from early computers. Interest in **Artificial Intelligence** goes back to the beginning of the digital computer. **Alan Turing** thought about this a lot. He built decryption devices for the British Army during World War II. || Computers made people ask how a machine differs from **human intelligence**, if at all. This is because computers were invented as **universal function approximators** (machines that can do many kinds of calculations and logical operations). No one had built a machine that did so many different kinds of operations before. Earlier machines did just one fixed job. || Since one machine could run many different procedures, people could ask where the real difference with a human lies. It might be in the procedures, in the representations, or in something else. So language research got tied to AI.
4|The **Turing test** says a computer is intelligent if it can take a player's place in a conversation game without anyone noticing. || The test is built on the **imitation game**, which has three players. A and B are a man and a woman. C is an examiner, and C's job is to figure out which is the man and which is the woman. A tries to fool C. B tries to help C guess the right answer. || In the test, a **computer** replaces either A or B. If C does not notice the swap, the computer has demonstrated **intelligence**. This is because C can only judge from the conversation. C never gets to look at the machine's program. || Since the whole judgment happens through talking, **natural language** is what the whole test runs on.
5|The Turing test is about everyday language and learning, not about expert skills. || This is because the test runs on **natural language**. The machine has to understand questions and give answers that fit. || The test also involves **deception**. One player is trying to fool the examiner. So the machine has to cope with an examiner who may be trying to catch it. || Turing explicitly left out **expert behaviors**. Calculating large sums does not count. Composing poetry does not count either. Holding a normal conversation is a different job from one expert skill. || Turing concluded that the best way to get an intelligent machine is to start with a **child**. The machine would have the cognitive abilities of a child. It would then learn to be more like an **adult**. Since it learns, its intelligence comes from experience, not from a finished list of adult answers.
6|**Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver** solved problems by moving symbols around with procedures. It is a later example of **GOFAI** (the approach that treats intelligence as working with explicit symbols). || This is because the program was given an abstract description of a formal problem. Each setup of the problem is a **state**. A legal move turns one state into another state. The program keeps applying procedures until it reaches the **goal state**. || It was very good at games and puzzles. In the **Tower of Hanoi**, each arrangement of disks is a state. Each legal move connects two arrangements. It also handled **chess**. || It was good at mathematics and logic too. It did **geometry proofs**. It did **algebra word problems**. Since all of these have clear states and rules, they suited the program well.
7|Early AI focused on **formal problems** for two reasons. One reason was the technology, and the other was a theory about intelligence. || The first reason was **technology**. Inputs to computers were still very limited. People fed them punchcards and machine language. There was no way to give a computer a high-resolution image and say "find the face". So symbols were much easier to give a computer than images. || The second reason was a **theoretical commitment**. Researchers thought **rational behavior** like puzzle solving is what intelligence looks like. You can train a **pigeon** to guide a missile, but the pigeon does not know what it is doing. Whether AI programs know what they are doing is a deep argument too. Still, AI programs at least **represent goal states**. They also choose procedures for reaching them. Since programs do this, they looked closer to real intelligence than a trained animal.
8|Chomsky said that knowing a language means having a **grammar** inside your head. Conditioning cannot explain it. || This is because a grammar lists sentences and gives each one a meaningful **structural description**. That lets a speaker do remarkable things. A speaker can tell **sentences from nonsentences**. A speaker can partly understand **new sentences**. A speaker can notice **ambiguities**. || New sentences are not linked to familiar ones by any simple **similarity**. They do not look alike in form, meaning or statistics. They do not share the same grammatical frame either. So Chomsky called talk of **generalization** here pointless and empty. || We recognize a new sentence because our internalized grammar **generates** it. We understand it partly by figuring out how the grammar **derives** it. || All normal children learn comparable, very complex grammars remarkably fast. Chomsky took this to mean humans are **specially designed** for language. He proposed a **hypothesis-formulating** ability of unknown character.
9|Knowing a language means knowing a **grammar** (a set of abstract rules that produce grammatical sentences). || This matters because there are an **infinite** number of grammatical sentences. No one could ever hear them all. Yet people can make a **grammaticality judgment** (deciding whether a sentence is well formed) for any new sentence. That is remarkable, since the sentence was never heard before. So the judgment must come from the rules, not from memory. || The grammar also explains ambiguity. One string of words can get more than one structure. So knowing the words alone does not settle the meaning. || Because of this, people's **intuitions** about whether sentences are grammatical are the key data for studying language. Researchers ask which sentences people accept, not just which ones they say.
10|Grammatical sentences do not look like each other in any obvious way. So the behaviorist idea of **stimulus generalization** cannot handle new sentences. || This is because stimulus generalization (a trained response spreading to things that look similar) needs similarity. A new item gets the response only if it resembles the trained item. Grammatical sentences have no obvious similarity to one another. A grammar can also generate an **infinite** number of them. Since there is no shared similarity, generalization has nothing to spread along. || Chomsky gave a different account. We recognize a new sentence because our **internalized grammar** generates it. The sentence does not need to match one we already know. Spreading a trained response to similar things is a different process from recognizing grammar.
11|Conditioning spreads to new things only along concrete similarities like color. It is unclear how that could work for abstract ideas like grammar. || This is because of how **stimulus generalization** works in behaviorism. A **pigeon** is trained to peck for a **green light**. It then also pecks at similar colors. It pecks most near green and less as the color gets more different. This pattern is a **generalization gradient** (responding drops as the stimulus gets less similar). || Another pigeon does a **magnitude discrimination** task. It responds based on amounts, so number works like color here. || In both cases the similarity is concrete, such as **color** or **number**. Grammar has no concrete scale like that. Since there is no such scale, it is not clear how conditioning extends to **abstract concepts**.
12|**Stimulus control** is Skinner's idea that something in the environment controls what a person says. Chomsky argued it only works if you can name the stimulus apart from the response. || Skinner's typical example is someone responding to a piece of **music** with an utterance. The music is treated as the stimulus. The utterance is treated as the response. || Chomsky said other examples only add to the **general mystification**. Skinner even treated a **proper noun** as a response to a stimulus. || This is a problem because a piece of music has many properties. A speaker could react to any one of them. If we use the utterance to decide which property was the stimulus, the utterance picks its own cause. Since nothing is named ahead of time, nothing is really explained.
13|Skinner was asking the **wrong question**, not just giving a bad answer. || This is because we cannot predict what someone will say from the stimuli around them. We do not know what the current stimuli are until the person responds. So naming the stimulus afterward makes the account **circular** (it uses the answer to explain itself). || What people say, and when, depends on a whole mix of things. It depends on **social interactions**. It depends on **cognitive processes**. It depends on **previous experiences**. Much of this has nothing to do with language. || So Chomsky proposed a **tractable problem** (one that can actually be solved). Researchers study the **structure** of what people say. From that structure, they try to work out the **rules** that produce it. Since the rules describe possible sentences, they do not have to explain why one person says one thing.
14|A speaker can tell that a sentence is grammatical without ever having heard it, even when its meaning makes little literal sense. || Chomsky (1955) compared "colorless green ideas sleep furiously" with "furiously sleep ideas green colorless". Neither sentence, nor any of their parts, had ever come up in an English speaker's past experience. Still, the first is **grammatical**, while the second is **not grammatical**. || This is because the first one follows English structure. Adjectives come before the noun, and a verb and an adverb follow it. The second one uses the same words in reverse. That breaks the structure. Since the words are the same, the difference must be the **structure**. || The opposite case also happens. The warning "Car! Getting towed! Yours!" is **ungrammatical**. Yet the stimulus that probably controlled it is perfectly clear. So getting a message across and being grammatical are separate things.
15|Chomsky describes language at the **computational level of analysis**. The term comes from **David Marr**, a visual neuroscientist. || Marr said every cognitive system can be described at three **levels of analysis**. || The **computational level** describes what problem the system is solving. || The **algorithmic level** describes the steps and representations used to solve it. || The **implementational level** describes the physical machinery that carries out those steps. || These levels ask different questions about the same ability. This is because knowing what counts as the right answer is different from knowing the steps. Both are different from knowing the hardware. Since Chomsky's grammar says what language must achieve, it sits at the computational level. An example makes this clearer.
16|A **cash register** does the same job no matter what machine does it. This example borrows heavily from **Sharon Goldwater**. || At the **computational level**, the register takes item prices and figures out the amount due. Given the payment, it figures out the change. || At the **algorithmic level**, it uses **addition** for the price. It uses **subtraction** for the change. || At the **implementational level**, the machinery does not matter. This is because a credit card reader or a computer program also counts as a cash register. Each one does the same adding and subtracting. For the same prices and payment, each gives the same change. Since the answer never depends on the machine, the job can be described without the hardware.
17|Language can be treated as a **Type I problem** (Marr's term for a problem that splits cleanly into his levels). Then researchers can study grammar without worrying about how it is implemented. || This is because the work divides into levels. The computational level for language is **Universal Grammar**. The algorithmic level is the specific processes and how they apply in different languages. || The implementational level gets set aside. That level includes the **errors** people make. It includes **memory limitations**. It includes **associative learning**. Since these are set aside, grammar can be studied as a system of its own. || The timing is easy to mix up. Chomsky's critique came about a decade before Marr's theoretical work. Both were influential in AI and in theoretical work in the **GOFAI** tradition.
18|A **Type 2** problem is one where many processes work at the same time. Their interaction is its own simplest description, so it cannot be split into clean levels. || Marr first connected his idea to Chomsky. Chomsky's **competence theory** for English syntax is exactly what Marr meant by a computational theory. Both care little about the messy details of the algorithms. Finding good algorithms is still hard. But first you have to know what they are supposed to do. A problem that splits this way has a **Type 1 theory**. || Many problems in biological information processing have a Type 1 theory. There is no reason they all should, though. This is because some problems are solved by many processes acting at once. When that happens, describing the interaction is the simplest description there is. Since nothing simpler exists, the problem has a **Type 2 theory**.
19|Marr suggested that the grammar of natural language may have a **Type 2** theory rather than a Type 1 theory. || This is because of what language does. A speaker has a **data structure** in mind that is not one-dimensional. Language turns it into a one-dimensional **sequential utterance**. The listener then rebuilds a rough copy of the original in their head. || Seen this way, English syntax may have no Type 1 theory of the kind **transformational grammar** tries to define. The rules of syntax may just be **wired-in conventions** for doing this tedious but vital job. They may not be deep principles about intelligence. || So an abstract theory of syntax may be an **illusion**. It would only roughly match what the processes really do. Marr compared it to **Julesz's second order statistics theory**. That theory only roughly matches the processes behind texture vision.
20|How language is implemented in the **brain** might be impossible to leave out of a theory of language. || This is because language may come from many processes working together. If it does, then learning, memory, perception and action shape the result. So the way the brain is organized may shape the way language is organized. || A whole alternative approach to language research is built on this hypothesis. The usual approach starts from an abstract grammar that the brain must carry out. The alternative starts from the learner and how the brain processes things. It asks what language-like patterns can grow out of that. Since the starting point differs, the two approaches explain language differently.
21|The alternative approach is called **emergentism**, **statistical learning** or **parallel distributed processing**. Its strongest claims are about the **implementational level**. || The first claim is **distributed representations** (non-hierarchical patterns spread over many units). This is like activity patterns over groups of neurons. || The second claim is **gradual learning**. Learning slowly adjusts the strength of connections. This is like changing the strength of synaptic connections. || The third claim is a focus on **microstructure** (the many small interactions that add up to a bigger pattern). It also draws a more graded line between **levels of analysis**. || Since the learning system itself builds the patterns, the apparent rules of language are expected to come from it.
22|**Elman (1990)** built **simple recurrent networks** to capture different parts of language performance. The model only tries to predict the next item it will see. || This is because it learns by guessing and correcting. The current **input** goes to the **hidden units**. The hidden units make an **output** guess about the next item. A copy of the hidden activity is saved in the **context units**. When the next input comes in, the context units feed back into the hidden units. || The learning connections slowly adjust to make better guesses. The copying connection stays fixed. || Since the context units hold on to what came before, the guess depends on the current item and the earlier ones. The model gradually gets better with experience.
23|A **letter-prediction model** can find words even though the text has no spaces. || The model reads "Manyyearsagoaboyandgirllivedbytheseatheyplayedhappily". It ends up splitting it into "many years ago a boy and girl lived by the sea they played happily". || This is because the model only tries to guess the next letter. **Prediction error** (how wrong each guess is) changes as it reads. At the start of a word, many letters could come next, so the error is high. Inside a word, each letter narrows down what comes next. So the error goes down within words. At the next word, the error jumps back up. || Since the error jumps at each new word, the jumps mark the **word boundaries**. Nobody told the model where words were.
24|A similar model trained on **simple sentences** starts to sort words into **grammatical classes**. It even picks up some **semantic information** (meaning). || This is because the categories come as a **side effect** of learning to predict. Knowing what kind of word just came helps guess what comes next. Words used in similar spots end up with similar **hidden-unit activation patterns**. Elman looked at these patterns and grouped the similar ones. || The groups follow grammar. **Nouns** end up in one group and **verbs** in another. || The groups also follow meaning. Nouns split into **animates** and **inanimates**. Animates split into humans and animals. Inanimates include **food** and **breakables**. Verbs split by how they take a **direct object**. Since nobody labeled the words, the model figured out these groups on its own.
25|A statistical model can tell "colorless green ideas sleep furiously" apart from the reversed version. Many engineering tools in **Natural Language Processing** work this way. || This is because they analyze **massive corpora** (collections of texts and transcriptions of spoken language). They pick up statistical patterns from them. || **Peter Norvig** discussed Chomsky's critique. He agreed that neither sentence appeared in published writing before 1955. But every two-word part had appeared. "Colorless green" was in a 1942 book. "Green ideas" was in The New Republic in 1922. "Ideas sleep in books" was in Current Opinion in 1912. || **Pereira (2001)** used a **finite-state model** with word categories added. It was trained by **expectation maximization** on newspaper text. It figured that sentence (a) is **200,000 times more probable** than sentence (b). Since both use the same words, the model is responding to word order.

27|Psycholinguistics has two main traditions. They disagree about what linguistic knowledge is. || The **GOFAI, modular or Chomskyan** tradition says linguistic knowledge is abstract and symbolic. It says linguistic representations are different in kind from other knowledge. This knowledge still has to **interface** (connect) with messy perception, movement, memory and other mental abilities. So the psycholinguist's job is to study that interface. || The **emergentist or functionalist** tradition says symbol and rule-like behavior are **emergent properties** of a statistical learning process. Emergent properties are patterns that come out of a process instead of being built in. It says perception, movement and thinking actually shape linguistic representations. So the psycholinguist's job is to study **language use**. That means connecting language use to more general psychological patterns.

30|Language is messy at every level, just like the exceptions to the **"i before e" rule**. This messiness is why the **emergentist or statistical learning** approach exists. || **Phonetic categories** (speech sound categories) are defined by probabilities over several dimensions. || **Words** are ambiguous in every way imaginable. || **Morphological paradigms** have exceptions. English forms the past tense by adding -ed. But RUN-RAN and SLEEP-SLEPT break that pattern. || **Syntactic rules** change between dialects and individuals. They also change with innovation and over time. || Since no level follows one clean rule, a learning system has to handle both the main pattern and its exceptions.
31|**Moravec's paradox** is a problem for the **GOFAI** approach. Tasks that are easy for computers are hard for humans, and the other way around. || For computers, proving theorems and solving geometry problems is fairly easy. Recognizing a face is extremely hard for them. Crossing a room without bumping into anything is extremely hard too. || For humans, it is the opposite. Faces and walking feel easy, and theorems feel hard. || This raises a question about language. This is because GOFAI describes language in formal terms, like a computer program. But computers and humans find different things hard. Since they differ, describing language like a computer program may be a **false trail**.
32|Fixed categories and rules are often **fuzzy** in real life. People have known this for a long time. || **Wittgenstein (1953)** made this point in **Philosophical Investigations**. He used the category of **games**. Board games, card games, ball games and Olympic games are all games. But when you look closely, no single feature is shared by every game. || This is because games are linked by **family resemblance** (overlapping similarities with no one shared feature). One pair of games shares one feature. Another pair shares a different feature. Since the features overlap like this, the category holds together without a strict definition.
33|Many things in language are **probabilistic**. Wittgenstein's games show why a category can work without one fixed definition. || **Wittgenstein (1953)** compared games feature by feature. Chess and noughts and crosses are not amusing in the same way. Patience has no competition between players. Ball games have winning and losing, but a child throwing a ball at a wall and catching it does not. Skill in chess is different from skill in tennis. Ring-a-ring-a-roses is amusing, but many other features are gone. || This is because similarities crop up and disappear from one game to the next. They make a complicated network that is **overlapping and criss-crossing**. Some are overall similarities, and some are similarities of detail. Since membership comes from overlap, it can be graded rather than all or nothing.
34|Many psychologists produced results about **family resemblances**, **similarity-based generalization** and **hunches**. Those results made a lot of cognition seem less suited to formal, rational rule following. || **Roger Shepard** produced results like this in **perception**. **Eleanor Rosch** did it in **categorization**. **Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky** did it in **decision-making**. Many others did too. || This is because their results showed people relying on similarity and gut judgments. Those judgments did not follow a simple ideal rational procedure. Since so much cognition works this way, language might too.
