2|People make **speech errors** because many things are active in the system at once while they plan what to say. This is because **noise**, **frequency**, other active material and **similarity** can all give the wrong item a boost. When the wrong item gets enough of a boost, it comes out instead of the intended one. || Speech errors are interesting to psycholinguists because they reveal how speech is planned. This is because an error can split a word into parts, move a sound, or swap in a related word. The way it goes wrong tells you which units the system works with. || **Fromkin**'s title "Speech errors as linguistic evidence" is gutsy. This is because linguists can study **competence** (knowledge of the language) apart from **performance** (actual speaking). From that view, an error is just a performance mistake to throw away. Fromkin says the opposite. Since the structure of an error compares directly with the intended form, errors count as real evidence about language.
3|Speech errors happen when the wrong item gets more activation than the intended one. This is because in **spreading activation** models, activation spreads from each word to related items. There are four key concepts. || **Noise** is not just literal acoustic noise, although that causes problems too. It also means activation going up and down in different parts of the system. || **Frequency** matters because common words are easier to activate than rare ones. This seems to affect many levels of speech production. || **Other sources of activation** also count. What you just said stays active for a while. What you are about to say is already active. Things you are thinking about but trying not to say may be active too. || **Similarity** matters because activation spreads more easily between related items. Items can be similar in meaning (semantic), in grammar (syntactic) or in sound (phonological). || Since all four can add up on one competitor, that competitor can win and come out by mistake.
4|A **tip-of-the-tongue (TOT) state** is when you know the meaning of a word but cannot say it. This is because the word's meaning and sound are only partly activated. That activation stays below the **threshold** (the level needed to say the word). || Less frequent words are the ones most likely to get stuck. This is because their sound forms are harder to activate. || Priming helps because a **prime** (a related word you read first) adds activation to the missing sound form. That extra push can get the form over the threshold. || **Lise Abrams** reviewed an example where a definition asked for goods traded illegally. The answer was "contraband." People stuck in a TOT state read a prime and then tried again. Primes sharing the first syllable, like "contact," "connotation" and "conscious," fixed about **50 percent** of TOT states. Primes sharing the middle syllable helped a medium amount. Primes sharing the final syllable helped about as little as unrelated words. Unrelated words fixed only about one quarter. Since the first syllable helped most, overlap at the start of the word matters most.
5|**Slips of the tongue** are sorted by which way a sound moves. Spooner supposedly once said "Mardon me padam, this pie is occupewed. Can I sew you to another sheet?" These examples come from Fromkin. || **Anticipation** is when a sound comes too early. This is because a sound planned for later jumps ahead. "Week long race" came out as "reek long race," since the r from "race" showed up early. || **Perseveration** is when a sound sticks around too long. This is because an earlier sound carries into a later spot. "John gave the boy" came out as "John gave the goy," since the g from "gave" carried forward. || **Metathesis**, or an **exchange**, is when two sounds trade places. "Keep a tape" came out as "teep a cape." The k and the t swapped, and the rest of each word stayed put. || The type depends only on how the produced form relates to the intended form. It does not matter whether the result makes sense.
6|The spreading activation idea most clearly at work in these slips is **other sources of activation**. This is because what you just said and what you plan to say are both active while you talk. || In anticipation, the planned sound is active too early. So the r in "reek long race" fills a spot before its own. || In perseveration, the spoken sound is still active. So the g in "John gave the goy" fills a spot after its own. || In an exchange, two sounds in the same plan get in each other's way. So each one lands in the other's spot. || The other ideas still help decide what happens. **Similarity** and **frequency** affect which candidates compete well. **Noise** (activation going up and down) affects which one gets picked. || Since planning always runs ahead of the sound you are saying, the wrong active sound can sometimes slip in.
7|A **mondegreen** is a **slip of the ear**, where a listener hears different words than the ones said. This is because the listener splits the sounds into words in the wrong places. || The name itself comes from a mishearing. "Laid him on the green" was heard as "Lady Mondegreen." In a song, "Hold me closer, tiny dancer" gets heard as "Hold me closer, Tony Danza." The real words are "Tiny Dancer," like the song title. **Andrew Nevins** is analyzing crowdsourced mondegreens from kissthisguy.com. || This happens because speech comes as one continuous stream. So the listener has to figure out the **word boundaries** (where one word ends and the next begins). "The good candy came anyways" and "The good can decay many ways" sound almost the same. The listener uses **context** to tell them apart. || Mondegreens are common in songs. This is because songs make **prosody** (the rhythm and melody of speech) less helpful. Since prosody helps less, context and familiar words take over, and sometimes they are wrong.
8|A **malapropism** like this is a hearing mistake more than a speaking mistake. This is because the speaker says exactly what they mean to say. || First, the speaker hears a word like "congenital" (present from birth). Then the speaker decides the intended word must be a more familiar one, like "congenial." After that, the speaker says "a congenial heart defect" and fully means it. || This is **top-down processing** (using the words you already know to make sense of what you hear). This is because a familiar word helps organize the sound. The familiar word beats the less familiar one. Other examples include "lettuce days," "decaffeinated him" and "Utopian tubes tied." || So two errors can sound equally strange but come from different places. A slip happens while speaking. A malapropism comes from hearing the word wrong earlier.
9|Speech errors show that single sounds are units in **speech planning**. When **Fromkin** wrote the paper, people argued about whether the **phoneme** (or phone, a single speech sound) was a planning unit. || Whole consonant **clusters** might have always moved together. Then errors would look like "gish frotto" or "clesh freer water." Fromkin found that clusters get broken up all the time instead. || "Fish grotto" came out as "frish gotto." This is because the r left the gr cluster. The r then joined the f in the first word. || "Fresh clear water" came out as "flesh queer water." This is because the r and the l swapped. The f and the k stayed where they were. || "Split pea soup" came out as "plit spea soup." Only the s moved. || Since one sound can move out of a cluster by itself, the single sound must be a unit the planner works with.
10|**Fromkin (1971)** made an early version of the model Menn describes. It shows each step from a meaning to a spoken **utterance**. || It starts with the **meaning to be conveyed**. Then a syntactic structure generator and a semantic feature generator build **syntactic-semantic structures**. Then an intonation contour generator adds **primary stress** and **intonation**. || Next the **lexicon** gives words with their features and the order of their segments. This makes strings of segments divided into syllables. || Then **morphophonemic rules** and **phonetic rules** fill in the exact sounds. Last, **motor commands** to the muscles produce the utterance. || The steps matter because each one is a separate stage. So an error can mess up one stage while the others stay fine. Since each stage can fail on its own, different errors tell you about different stages.
11|The **basic model** of speech production has five levels. This is because making an utterance involves several different jobs. || The **message level** is what you want to get across. The **functional level** picks **lemmas** (word identities before their final form) and gives them **semantic roles**. The **positional level** puts them in order in a clause. The **phonological encoding level** fills in the sounds. The **speech gesture level** gets the speech movements ready. || Following Menn, the first two levels come first. The sound slips like "reek long race" were already phonological encoding errors. || The levels are kept apart because the everyday word "word" mixes them up. Picking a fitting word is one job. Picking its grammatical form is another. Putting its sounds together is a third. Since these are separate jobs, different errors can come from different levels.
12|The **message level** is where you decide what you want to get across. The same event can turn into different messages. This is because the audience changes what you choose to say. || Take "I won a bunch of money on the game Sunday." With a fan of the losing team, their feelings may change how much you play up the win. With someone not interested in football, you may need to explain why it matters. In a tweet, the short public post changes which details you keep. || The event is the same each time. Only the purpose and the chosen details change. || The message level is mysterious and hard to study experimentally. This is because giving everyone the same event does not make them all mean the same thing.
13|A picture book like "Frog, where are you?" helps control what speakers see, but not what they mean to say. This is because it is an **elicitation paradigm** (a task made to draw out speech) that gives everyone the same scene. || The scene shows a boy, a dog and frogs. Since everyone sees the same thing, researchers can compare the descriptions. || The intended message is still hard to pin down. This is because one speaker may focus on the boy while another focuses on the dog or the frogs. Speakers may also guess differently about what the listener knows. Age, culture and audience may change how the message gets built. || Since the scene is fixed but the message is not, the method controls the input more than the message.
14|We cannot say the functional level picks finished "words." This is because one error reveals that the tense gets added later. || The speaker meant "Where'd I put it when I brought it in." The speaker said "Where'd I bring it when I put it in." The verbs switched places. || But the moved verb came out as "bring," not "brought." This is because "Where'd" already has the past-tense helper did in it. A verb after did takes the plain form. So the tense was fitted to the verb's new spot. || If "brought" had been picked as a finished word, it would have moved unchanged. PUT looks the same in both forms, so BRING is the verb that gives this away. || Since the tense came later, the speaker picked the **lemma** BRING (the word's identity before its final form). The term lemma is borrowed from mathematics, where it means a proposition in an argument.
15|People so often say the opposite of what they mean because opposites are very closely linked. This is an **error in lemma selection**, where the wrong word gets picked. || One speaker said "I really like to" and then fixed it to hate. One said bottom but meant the top of the stack of books. One said "This room is too damn hot" and fixed it to cold. One said oral but meant the written part of the exam. || Each pair is about the same thing. Hot and cold are both about temperature. Top and bottom are both about position. Oral and written are both kinds of exam. || The wrong word still fits the sentence. This is because it is the same kind of word as the right one. Since it fits, the sentence sounds normal even though the meaning is flipped.
16|People say the opposite because activation spreads easily from a lemma to its antonym. This is because **antonyms** (words with opposite meanings) are actually alike in several ways. || Antonyms are **semantically related**, so they are about the same thing. They are also **strongly associated**, so thinking of one brings up the other. In the spreading activation framework, this is like a perfect storm. || Antonyms can also take on the **same semantic roles**. They are in the **same word class**, so they can take on the same syntactic roles. This means the rest of the speech production system can handle the wrong word. || Since the antonym gets lots of activation and still fits, saying "hot" for "cold" makes a normal sentence with the opposite meaning.
17|A **blend** is one word made by mixing parts of two competing words. **Lewis Carroll** made blends on purpose and called them portmanteau words. || In The Hunting of the Snark, Carroll explained "frumious." You plan to say both "fuming" and "furious" without deciding which comes first. If you lean a little toward fuming, you say "fuming-furious." If you lean toward furious, you say "furious-fuming." With a perfectly balanced mind, you say "frumious." || **Fromkin** quoted Carroll because he said blends can happen in natural speech. Carroll also proposed an idea about how they happen. || Fromkin found that complete blends like "frumious" are rare in normal speech. A blend of [fjurijəs] and [fjumiŋ] is more likely to come out as [fjumijəs] or [fjuriŋ]. This is because the first syllables are identical. Since the words already share a start, they join where they overlap.
18|**Spontaneous blends** happen when two words that mean nearly the same thing are both active. This is because neither word wins outright, so parts of both come out. "I swindged" mixes **switch** and **changed**. Other blends mix **mainly** and **mostly**, **bother** and **trouble**, **swinging** and **hip**, **spirant** and **fricative**, and **player** and **athlete**. || **Fromkin** explained how this happens. The speaker picks words by matching **semantic features** to the meaning. When there are several options, both go into a **buffer** (a temporary storage compartment) along with their sounds. Then either one gets picked or the two get blended. These errors seem to support Carroll's assumptions. || There are almost always several possible words in speech production. This is because choosing among options is a normal part of speaking. Since options are almost always there, blends show competition that happens all the time.
19|**Semantic roles** (also called functional roles) say who did what in an event. They stay the same even when the sentence order changes. || "The plums in the icebox were eaten by me" has the order Undergoer, Location, Verb, Agent. "I have eaten the plums in the icebox" has the order Agent, Verb, Undergoer, Location. Both describe the same event. || At the functional level, lemmas get slotted into the roles. The verb is TO EAT. The **agent** (the one doing it) is ME. The **undergoer** (the thing it happens to) is PLUM. The icebox is the **location**. || The order still matters for the grammar. This is because the lemma ME becomes "I" in the active sentence. It becomes "me" after "by" in the passive one. That choice is made later, at the **positional level**.
20|By the end of the **functional level**, a lot is already done. The speaker has decided on a **message**. The speaker has picked the right **lemmas**. The speaker has linked those lemmas to **semantic roles**. || But the speaker is still nowhere near putting a clause together, much less saying it out loud. This is because none of these steps sets the word order or the sounds. Whether the sentence is active or passive is still open. How the pronouns come out is still open. The full sound sequence is still open. || Since only the plan is ready, later levels still have to order the lemmas and turn them into speech.

23|Very often **speech errors go unnoticed**. This is because of **top-down effects** in speech perception. Listeners use context and expectations to figure out the intended message. So a real error can slip by. The same kind of process is assumed for many malapropisms and mondegreens. || Some "errors" are made on purpose for **style**. **Paul Krugman** wrote "the upper hand is on the other foot," quoting Hot Shots Part Deux. It mixes "the upper hand" with the shoe being on the other foot. || A deliberate error can be **more memorable** than the correct version. This is because the odd mix grabs attention to the wording itself. The listener spots both familiar phrases, which makes it funny. || Since some errors are made on purpose, it matters whether an error was intentional before using it as evidence about speech.
