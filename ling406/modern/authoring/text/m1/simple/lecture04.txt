2|**Association norms** (lists of the words people give for a cue word) come from asking people for the first words they think of. A person sees a word on a screen for a few seconds. Then the person writes down the first few words they think of. || This works because it asks for **immediate associations**, not a dictionary definition. Different people can write different words for the same cue. When many people do the task, you can count which answers come up again and again. You can also see which answers are unusual. Since the answers are collected from many people, the norm is a pattern of responses for each cue.
3|LION is a **cue** (the word a participant responds to), and the participant comes up with whatever words it brings to mind. This is because the task records the person's own answer. The cue does not give the answer. || A person might write TIGER. That answer would connect two animals. But TIGER is just one possible answer, and nobody has to give it. || The answers tell you about the person's **lexical knowledge** (what they know about words and how words connect). Since everyone's answers can differ, you need many people to build a norm.
4|TRIPE looks a lot like STRIPES, but the two words do not mean anything alike. This is because looking alike and meaning something alike are two different relationships. || TRIPE and STRIPES share a run of letters. So their **form** (spelling and sound) overlaps. But their **meanings** are not connected. Words with very different meanings can still share letters. || This makes the **arbitrariness** of form and meaning easy to see (the spelling of a word does not tell you what it means). Since form and meaning are separate, a shared spelling is not evidence of a shared meaning.
5|FREE is a cue, and the participant comes up with their own words for it. This is because the task does not offer a list to choose from. The person just writes the words that come to mind. || The response is **generated** (made up by the person, not picked from options). Writing it down turns a hidden association into something you can look at. Then you can compare the answers across people. || Since each person makes up their own answer, the norm for FREE shows what people really connect with that word.
6|STRIPES names a **visual property** (a feature you can see, here a pattern), so its associates can be a different kind of thing. This is because an association can link a property to an animal that has it. || STRIPES and TIGER can be associated. STRIPES is a pattern. TIGER is an animal. So the two words name different kinds of things but still go together. || This is not the same as **similarity** (sharing properties). LION and TIGER are similar because both are animals. STRIPES and TIGER are associated but not similar. Since these are different links, associated words do not have to name the same kind of thing.
7|PEAR and BEAR look alike, but they name totally different things. This is because looking alike is a **formal relationship** (shared sound or spelling). Meaning alike is a **semantic relationship** (shared meaning). || PEAR is a fruit. BEAR is an animal. Their forms overlap, but that does not make them the same concept. || Since the two relationships are separate, a shared sound or spelling pattern is not evidence that two words mean similar things.
8|BEAR is another cue that looks like PEAR but does not mean anything like it. This is because BEAR and PEAR overlap in form, not in meaning. || **Association norms** record the answers people actually give. The experimenter does not decide ahead of time whether an answer will come from form or from meaning. So whatever link the cue brings to mind gets recorded. || Since the norm just records real answers, it can catch links based on meaning and links based on form.
9|TREE is a cue, and its norm adds up the different words that different people give for it. This is because the same word can bring different associates to mind for different people. || The first answers people give show which words are easily connected with TREE. That evidence comes from this one task. || The **norm** (the summary of everyone's answers) does not pick one answer as the only association. Since the cue stays the same word while the answers vary, the norm keeps all of them.
10|TIGER connects to LION in one way and to STRIPES in a different way. This is because TIGER and LION are both animals, so they have **similar meanings**. TIGER and STRIPES go together because stripes are a **characteristic pattern** of tigers. || So an animal can be associated with one of its own properties. Two animal names can also be related because they are in the same category. Both kinds of link can be part of a **lexical network** (a network of connected words). || Since these two links are different, **association** and **similarity** are not the same thing.

12|Only LION, TIGER and STRIPES are tabulated, because these three matter later. This is because they connect meanings in two different ways. LION and TIGER are **similar animals**. TIGER and STRIPES link an animal to a **characteristic property**. You can measure each association by counting how often people give one word when they see the other. || The pairs TRIPE and STRIPES, and PEAR and BEAR, make a different point. The words in each pair overlap in form. But similar form does not mean similar meaning. The link between how a word sounds or is spelled and what it means is largely **arbitrary** (not predictable). || Since these links can differ, keep three things apart. These are similar form, similar meaning and one word bringing another to mind.
13|"Associated" is different from "similar." **Association** is when one item tends to bring another to mind. **Similarity** is when two things share properties. TIGER and STRIPES are associated, but they are not two similar objects. || Did people have a particular strategy? They may have used a deliberate **strategy** to come up with words. That strategy can change which words they write. || Could the **list structure** have influenced the answers? Yes, the list structure could change them. This is because the other words on the list change which ideas are easy to think of. A list of only wild animals could push people toward one set of answers. A list of only household appliances could push them toward a different set. || Since the answers depend on the task, a norm only tells you about responses under those conditions.
14|Menn's chapter is called "How Brains Work," but a better title would be "Some basic metaphors from neuroscience that are useful in psycholinguistics". This is because the chapter gives a few ideas borrowed from neuroscience. These ideas are the basis for most theory and experimental design in the field. || **Activation** is how active a representation is. || **Spreading activation** is activity passing to connected representations over **weighted connections** (links of different strengths). || **Distributed representations** store an item as a pattern spread over many units. || **Associative learning**, such as **Hebbian learning**, makes links stronger between units that are active together. || **Bottom-up processing** goes from sensory features up to words. **Top-down processing** goes from word knowledge back down to the features.
15|One brain cell can fire hardest for one exact combination of line angle and movement. **Hubel and Wiesel (1968)** found this when they related cell activity to sensory input. Their work happened at the same time as the **Cognitive Revolution**. || They recorded from **simple cells** in **primary visual cortex (V1)**. A simple cell responds to lines at certain angles inside its **receptive field** (the part of the visual input that the cell responds to). || They moved a line at different angles and compared how much the cell fired. The cell fired the most when a line at about **315 degrees** moved upward. It fired much less for other angles and movements. || This matters because a cell's **electrochemical activity** tells you what it represents and computes. Since this cell picks out one combination of features, you can say it **represents** that combination.
16|Cells further along the **visual processing stream** respond to more complex things, like another monkey's face in profile. This is because, in data adapted from **Gross and colleagues (1985)**, one cell in **inferotemporal cortex** fires strongly for near-profile views of a monkey's face. It fires much less for front and back views. Its firing is highest around 80 to 100 degrees of face angle. || So it makes sense to say this cell **represents** something complex, not just line segments. Its activation is **graded** (it can be a little active or very active). It goes up a little for one view and a lot for another. || In psycholinguistics, **activation** borrows this idea as a metaphor. It applies it to **perceptual and conceptual representations**. Since activation is graded, a word or concept can also be more or less active.
17|A very simple model treats each word as a **node** (a unit with an activation level) that the word can switch on. This is because imagining simple **lexical representations** (stored representations of words) makes the network easy to follow. || The network here has three nodes, for lion, tiger and stripes. A specific word activates its own node. || The printed word and the node are two different levels. The printed LION is the evidence you see. The lion node is the word the system could recognize from that evidence. Since the node has an activation level, the model can say how much activity a word gets.
18|One word node has to respond to many different-looking versions of the same word. This is because LION can be printed as LION, lion or Lion. It can also come in different typefaces and sizes. Each version looks different on the screen. But all of them should switch on the same lion node. || This is the **lack of invariance problem** (one identity shows up in many different physical forms). The same problem came up with **phonemes**. To handle it, the **receptive fields** of word nodes would eventually have to be very complicated. || For now, just imagine there are nodes that words can activate. Since every version should reach the same node, the node maps many printed forms onto one word.
19|A node can be only partly switched on. This is because the word LION here is inside a **noise mask** (visual noise around the word). LION does not stand out much from the noise. So the input gives only weak evidence for the word. So the lion node gets only **weak activation**. || The node can be partly active even while you still cannot tell what the word is. As more of the word comes through the noise, the activation can go up. || Since activation can be partial, a model can describe the stages before a word is identified.
20|As the word gets clearer, the activation of its node goes up. This is because two things change together. On the screen, LION gets easier to tell apart from the noise. In the model, the lion node becomes **more active**. || Stronger evidence supports the same word more strongly. So recognizing a word can be modeled as a response that grows as the input gets stronger. || Since clarity and activation go up together, changing the display can change how a word is processed.
21|When the word is clear enough, the network counts it as **identified**. This is because the **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)** (how strong the word is compared with the noise) becomes very good. Then the lion node is active enough to say the network recognized lion. Tiger and stripes are not identified. || This is how the **perceptual identification task** works. Participants push a button as soon as they can tell what the word is. The time it takes is what gets measured. Anything that makes the word easier to identify makes the button press come earlier. || Since the press happens when activation is high enough, the reaction time tells you about activation in the model.
22|What factors might change how quickly you identify a word? There are two kinds. This is because the speed can depend on the word itself and on the setting. || **Features of the stimulus itself** are about the target word. One is how often you run into the word. Another is how similar it is to other words. || **Features of the experimental environment** are about what came before the word. One is whether you just saw that same word. Another is whether you just saw a similar or related word. || Since the two kinds are separate, you can test the same word in different settings. You can also compare different words in the same setting.
23|Four things can change how quickly you identify a word. Two are about the word itself, and two are about the experiment. || About the word, **word frequency** (how often you run into a word) matters. A common word can be easier to activate. How similar a word is to other words also matters, through **neighborhood** relationships. || About the experiment, **repetition priming** (being faster after just seeing the same word) matters. Just seeing a similar or related word can also speed things up. || Frequency and repetition priming are easy to mix up. Frequency is about experience with a word built up over time. Repetition priming is about seeing the word a moment ago. A related word changes the setting without repeating the target. Since these differ, you have to ask what changed between conditions.
24|Words you see more often are easier to activate, because experience tunes up their **activation dynamics** (how activation rises and falls over time). This is because most models of this kind assume activation **unfolds over time**. Experience **tunes up** that process. Words you see more often become more sensitive. So they get activated more easily. That is one way to explain **frequency effects**. || The **Interactive Activation model** of **Rumelhart and McClelland (1986)** simulates this kind of process. For the word CAVE, activation climbs above the competing words while the input supports it. Then it slowly drops. || Different computational models simulate frequency effects in different ways. Since experience changes the dynamics, frequency is tied to how a word gets processed.
25|Seeing a word again very soon makes it faster to identify. This is because activation does not go up and down instantly. It may **drop more slowly than it rises**. So some activation is still there after the word is gone. || If the word shows up again while activation is still high, its **baseline** (starting activation level) is already higher than usual. So it takes less to activate it. || This is **repetition priming**, a basic effect in psychology. Since the leftover activation gives the word a head start, the second identification is faster.
26|Two factors still need explaining. These are **neighborhood effects** (effects of how similar a word is to other words) and priming from a similar or related word. That priming can be **associative**, **semantic** or **phonological**. || They need something new in the network. This is because a single word node can only change its own activity. Repetition priming involves the same word twice. But these two factors involve one word changing a **different** word. || Since one word has to affect another, the nodes need to be linked.
27|To explain many things about language, the nodes in a network have to be **connected**. After all, it is a network. || This is because activity needs a way to get from one word to another. Lion is connected to tiger. Tiger is connected to stripes. || The printed word LION activates lion **directly**. Then the connection carries some activity from lion to tiger. That activity on tiger is **indirect**, since it came through the network and not from the screen. || Since nodes are connected, one active node can change how another node responds.
28|Activating one word sends some activation to words it is associated with. This is called **spreading activation**. || This is because the connections can be set by the association norms. The **connection strength** between two nodes depends on how associated they are. When lion is activated, some activation travels along a **weighted connection** (a link with a strength) to tiger. Different links can pass different amounts. || This explains **semantic priming** (faster recognition of a word after a related word). Tiger is already partly active from lion. So tiger is easier to recognize when it shows up. || A semantic network can link many ideas. Vehicle links to car and bus. Fire engine links to the vehicles and to fire ideas. Since links spread activity, one active idea can boost several related ones.

30|STRIPES starts out almost invisible inside a strong **noise mask**. This is the first step of a **perceptual identification** sequence. The contrast is very low. So the letters are hard to pick out from the background. || The word can already start to get some support from the input. But the evidence is too weak to say for sure what the word is. Since the evidence is so weak, nobody can identify it yet.
31|As STRIPES gets more contrast, more of its letters stand out from the noise. This is because the letters, though still faint, are easier to pick out from the mask. So the input gives **stronger support** for the word. || The word itself did not change. Only the quality of the evidence changed. Since the word is the same, clarity changes how much evidence there is, not which word it is.
32|STRIPES gets easier to identify as its contrast improves. This is because the letters now stand apart from the noise. So the evidence for the word keeps growing. || In the perceptual identification task, the person responds once there is enough evidence to recognize the word. Since better quality gives more evidence sooner, stimulus quality decides when the **identification response** can happen.
33|The clearer STRIPES gives enough information for the participant to respond. This is because the letters are now clearer than in the heavily masked versions. The noise is the same kind as before. || A person who recognizes the word can now press the button. That press means they **recognized the word**. It is not a judgment about whether stripes go with some animal. Since the task only asks for recognition, it measures recognizing, not associating.
34|At high contrast, STRIPES is easy to identify, and that switches on the **stripes node**. This is because the dark letters stand out clearly from the gray noise. Recognizing the word activates its node in the network. || Now STRIPES can act as a **prime** (an earlier word that may change how a later word is processed). Whether it affects another word depends on the **connections** between their nodes. Since the stripes node is now active, its activity can travel along those connections.
35|LION is the **target** (the word being identified) in a second sequence, and it starts out hard to read. This is because LION has weak contrast against the noise mask. So its letters give little useful information. || The target can be kept separate from what came just before it. A **priming** account asks whether that earlier experience changes how fast LION is recognized. Since STRIPES came first, the question is whether it speeds up LION.
36|As LION gets more contrast, the evidence for the word gets stronger. This is because LION is now faintly visible in the center of the mask. The letters start to separate from the noise. || So the same word causes a **changing internal response**. The word stays the same, but the quality of the evidence changes. Since the evidence keeps improving, recognizing LION gets more likely.
37|What gets measured is **when** LION is identified. This is because LION is now clearer, and the four letters have more contrast than before. A response can happen once the evidence is enough to recognize it. || A priming comparison asks whether recognition happens **sooner** after one earlier word than after another. LION is the same target in both cases. Since only the earlier word differs, a faster time means priming.
38|LION is now readable, and two things help its node get active. This is because the clear stimulus gives strong evidence for lion. That raises the activation of the lion node. || The first thing is the **clarity** of the word itself. The second thing is any **leftover activation from the context**. Since both add up, recognition starts from a state built by the input and the context together.
39|The clear LION finishes the sequence, and its node can be activated in two ways. This is because the printed word activates the lion node **directly**. Other nodes can also affect it **through connections**. || The strong word signal is easy to tell apart from the noise. Since the lion node can get help through connections, the experiment asks whether STRIPES helped LION that way.
40|Activating stripes can **prime lion**, even though the two words are **not directly associated**. This is because activity flows throughout the network. Stripes is connected to tiger. Tiger is connected to lion. So activation goes from stripes to tiger and then from tiger to lion. || This is called **mediated priming** (priming that passes through a middle word, here tiger). A model with only direct links has no path from stripes to lion. So that model could not explain the effect. || Since the effect needs activation to travel through tiger, it is **compelling evidence for spreading activation**.
41|The stripes and lion study is very typical of psycholinguistics. This is because it has **careful study design** and it finds **small effects**. || Why would stripes prime lion? Stripes primes lion because activity travels from stripes through tiger to lion. This is **counterintuitive** because stripes and lion are not obvious associates. A model with only direct links cannot explain the effect. || Since the two models predict different things, even a small effect can tell them apart. || Three broader issues remain. These are top-down and bottom-up processing, learning and distributed representations.
42|Knowing a word can help you see a letter that is only partly visible. This is called **top-down processing** (an active word sends activation back down to its letters). It works together with **bottom-up processing** (information goes from visual features up to letters and then words). || The **Interactive Activation network** after **McClelland and Rumelhart (1981)** has three levels. Features are at the bottom. Letters are in the middle. Words such as WORK, WORD, WEAR and WEAK are at the top. || In the example, blots cover part of the last letter of WORK. The visible features fit more than one letter. But WORK becomes active and sends activation back down to K. So the output for **K goes up a lot** over time, while R and D stay low. || This is one way of modeling **pattern completion**, which Menn discusses at length.
43|Knowledge gets into the brain through **associative learning**. This is because connections get stronger when units are active together. **Hebbian learning** sums this up as "neurons that fire together, wire together." The real process is more complicated. But the neural learning mechanisms that are fairly well understood are **associative**. || In the bell and food example, a bell signal and a food signal both feed into a salivation unit. The bell and the food are active at the same time. So the **synapse** (connection) from the bell gets stronger. Then the bell has more effect. || People used to think these mechanisms were **not enough** to learn anything important about language. Now the debate is about what role they play and how **innate knowledge** might limit them. **Large language models (LLMs)** learn many structural features of language with little more than this.
44|A model can generalize what it learns to new, similar things by using **distributed representations** (each item stored as a pattern spread over many units). This is because **generativity** is very important for language. To generalize, an associative model has to represent **similarity**. Similar items get overlapping patterns. So learning about one item also changes the patterns used for similar items. || So what you know about robins and canaries can carry over to new birds as you learn about them. || The network takes in an **item** and a **relation**. For canary with the relation can, the outputs include grow, move, fly and sing. Hidden layers connect the item and relation to those properties.
45|Five ideas borrowed from neuroscience give psycholinguistics a way to talk about word knowledge. || **Activation** is how active a representation is, and it changes as input comes in. || **Spreading activation** lets one representation affect another through connections. || **Top-down and bottom-up processing** tell you which direction influence goes between levels. || **Associative learning**, such as Hebbian learning, changes how strong connections are. **Distributed representations** let a model handle similarity and generalize. || **Priming experiments** help figure out what people's **lexical networks** look like. This is because they measure how processing one word changes processing of another.
