7|Letters are not **phonemes** (the sound units that tell words apart). This is because each language has its own rules for turning spelling into sound. So one letter can stand for different sounds in different languages. English spelling is especially irregular. || Even regular spelling systems cause trouble when you compare languages. **Pinyin** is a fairly new system for writing Mandarin, and it is perfectly regular. The Mandarin phrase "Wo qu tushuguan" (我去图书馆) uses letters an English reader knows. But reading it with English rules gives the wrong sounds. The Chinese characters and the Pinyin write the same phrase in two different ways. || French spelling is also very regular. The French sentence "Comment tu t’appelles?" still needs the French rules to say it right. || Since a spelling system only works for its own language, comparing sounds has to start from what people actually say.
8|The **International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)** is one standard way to write the sounds of every language. This is because its symbols stand for how a sound is made, not for one language's spelling. So you can compare two sounds even when their normal spellings differ. You can also tell apart two sounds that normal spelling writes with the same letter. || The **IPA consonant chart** puts symbols in order by how the sounds are made. The rows include **plosive**, **nasal** and **fricative**. The columns include **bilabial**, **alveolar** and **velar**. Symbols near each other are made in related ways. || Since a symbol's position tells you how its sound relates to its neighbors, the chart is a set of comparisons. It is not just a list to memorize.
9|The consonant chart is sorted by three **features** (properties of how a sound is made). They are place, manner and voicing. || **Place of articulation** (where the vocal tract is narrowed or closed) goes across the chart from front to back. Bilabial sounds are on the left, because they are made at the lips. Velar sounds and sounds made farther back are on the right. || **Manner of articulation** (how much the air is blocked) goes down the chart from closed to open. A **plosive** closes the air off completely. A **fricative** makes a narrow passage instead. || **Voicing** is about when the vocal folds start vibrating compared with the closure. They can start before or during the closure, or after it. Voicing shows up as pairs of symbols, and the right-hand one is voiced. So p and b share the bilabial plosive spot. || Since these are three separate features, two consonants can match on two and differ on the third.
10|/p/ and /b/ are made in the same place and the same way, and differ only in voicing. This is because both are **bilabial plosives**, so the lips close and then let go. /p/ is not voiced, and /b/ is voiced. **Voicing** here is the timing of the vocal fold vibration compared with when the lips let go. || /t/ and /d/ differ in the same way. They are made at a different place than /p/ and /b/. But they still pair a voiceless stop with a voiced stop. || So features describe how sounds are alike and different in a fairly exact way. Since the same feature repeats across pairs, you can talk about **voiced stops** in general, like /b/, /d/ and /g/.
11|Voiced and unvoiced stops are made differently in different languages, and in different spots within one language. The IPA writes this with three symbols. The first symbol, **[pʰ]**, is a bilabial plosive that is not voiced and is **aspirated** (a puff of air comes out after the lips open). The second symbol, **[p]**, is the same but with no puff. The third symbol, **[b]**, is a bilabial plosive that is voiced. || In English, at the start of a word, "peer" often starts with [pʰ]. "Beer" often starts with [p], which has no puff and no voicing. You can feel this by holding your hand in front of your mouth and saying both words. || In most kinds of French, the contrast is really [p] in "pou" against [b] in "boue". || Since English and French build the contrast differently, voiced and unvoiced do not mean the same timing in every language.
12|Slashes and square brackets mean two different kinds of transcription. This is because they answer different questions about the same speech. || Slashes mark **broad transcription** (writing just the phonemes). /p/ and /b/ are broad. They keep only the differences that tell words apart in the language. || Square brackets mark **narrow transcription** (writing as much detail as possible). The forms [pʰ], [p] and [b] are narrow. The form [pʰ] adds that the sound has a puff of air. The form [p] adds that it has none. Narrow transcription can keep a puff even when it does not change the word. || There are two broad labels here but three narrow forms. Writing more detail does not make more phonemes. Since detail and word differences are separate, a **phone** (an actual sound) and a **phoneme** (a sound category) are not the same thing.
13|Consonant features come from how your body makes the sound. This is because **place of articulation** names where the air gets blocked or narrowed in your vocal tract. Your lips can close together. Different parts of your tongue can touch different parts of your mouth. Each movement changes where the air gets stopped. || There are three places to know here. For a **bilabial** sound, the top and bottom lips close at the front of the mouth. For an **alveolar** sound, the tip of the tongue goes up to the ridge behind the upper teeth. For a **velar** sound, the back of the tongue goes up to the soft palate. || A full closure can happen at any of these places. So place is a different feature from manner. Since place is about the body, you find it by locating the main closure, not by looking at the letter.
14|Consonant features describe where the air goes and what body parts block it. || A **nasal** sound sends air out through the nose while the mouth is closed somewhere. This is because the opening between the mouth and the nose stays open. So air keeps flowing even though the mouth is blocked. For a nasal made at the lips, the lips are closed and the path to the nose is open. So a nasal can be made at the same place as a stop and still send the air a different way. || Two other places use the teeth at the front of the mouth. For a **labiodental** sound, the lower lip touches the upper teeth. For a **dental** sound, the tongue is at the front teeth. || Since airflow and place are separate, you check them as two separate steps.
15|Vowels are sorted three ways, by height, by front or back, and by lip rounding. || Height is about how close or open the vowel is. **Close** vowels usually have a higher jaw and tongue than **open** vowels. The vowel chart goes from close at the top to open at the bottom. || Front or back is about where the tongue sits. The tongue is usually farther forward for **front** vowels than for **back** vowels. The chart goes from front on the left to back on the right. || **Rounding** is whether the lips are rounded. When two symbols sit as a pair, the right one is rounded. || **/y/** is the vowel in French "tu" and Mandarin "qu" (去). It can sound like /u/ to English speakers, but it is not /u/. This is because /y/ is close and front like /i/. If you say /i/ and then round your lips, you get closer to /y/. Your tongue does not move back the way it does for /u/. Since only the lips changed, lip shape is a separate thing from tongue position.
16|A vowel is a pattern of movement, not one fixed tongue shape. This is because the whole vocal tract shapes the sound, not just the tongue. || Each vowel can be marked by the center of the **tongue body**. That point captures one important way vowels differ. But the whole outline of the tongue can differ in more ways than that one point. || Some vowels keep moving while you say them. These are **diphthongs** (vowels that change during the sound), like the vowels in "boat" and "bait". A single point cannot tell you the path the tongue takes. || Since some vowels change while they are being made, you have to follow the change over the whole vowel.
17|People who speak the same language can differ in which vowels they keep apart. This is because vowels change a lot across **dialects**, even within American English. || The example is the two vowels in the lower back corner of the vowel chart. One is the rounded /ɔ/, and the other is the open back /ɑ/. A speaker from the outer boroughs of New York City, a mid-Atlantic dialect, keeps these two vowels apart. So for that speaker, "caught" and "cot" are different words. Most people from other parts of the country do not keep them apart. So for them, "caught" and "cot" are **homophones** (words that sound exactly the same). || Both groups are speaking normal English. So this is a regular dialect pattern, not a mistake. Since dialects differ, you can only tell whether someone has the contrast by listening to that person.
18|The **lack of invariance problem** is that one speech sound comes out physically different across speakers and situations, but listeners still understand it. || This is because people vary a lot. Some people say "Aaron" and "Erin" with the same vowel. A listener who keeps those vowels apart still understands them. A listener can hear a speaker's different vowels for some time and only notice when someone points it out. || Measured vowels show the same thing. Each vowel can be plotted by its **first formant (F1)** and **second formant (F2)** (two frequencies in the vowel, in cycles per second). Many tokens of one vowel spread over a wide area instead of one spot. The areas for neighboring vowels overlap. || Since no single pair of F1 and F2 values marks a vowel, listeners have to figure out the vowel from a signal that keeps changing. This problem comes up again and again.
19|A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in meaning because of just one sound. Minimal pairs are how you prove two sounds are different phonemes. This is because two phones are **contrastive** when swapping one for the other changes the meaning. Contrastive phones are different phonemes. || The two low back vowels were proved this way. It took two words whose difference depended only on those vowels. For a speaker who keeps the vowels apart, "caught" and "cot" are a minimal pair. The consonants around the vowel stay the same, so only the vowel tells the words apart. || For a speaker who merges the vowels, "caught" and "cot" sound the same. So that pair does not prove two vowel phonemes for that speaker. Since the test is about meaning, two sounds being different is not enough on its own.
20|Minimal pairs also show that a contrast depends on one particular feature. In English, /b/ and /p/, /t/ and /d/, and /k/ and /g/ all differ on **voicing**. This is because in each pair the place and the stop manner stay the same. Only voicing changes inside a pair. || Across the pairs, the place changes. /b/ and /p/ are bilabial, /t/ and /d/ are alveolar, and /k/ and /g/ are velar. The voiced and voiceless difference repeats at each place. So one feature describes all three contrasts. || We know this from comparing minimal pairs in each language. Just because two sounds exist does not make them a contrast in every language. Since minimal pairs link a sound difference to a word difference, they show that voicing matters for English words.
21|**Allophones** are different phones that are different ways of saying the same phoneme. This is because how a phoneme is said varies a lot. Some of that variation is very predictable. It depends on where the sound is and what sounds are next to it. So it follows a pattern and is not random. It also does not change which word you are saying. || You can record three phrases in **Praat** (a program for recording and analyzing speech) to compare /p/. In "pot that", /p/ starts the word before the vowel. In "spot that", /p/ comes right after /s/. In "stop that", /p/ ends the word. || The puff of air and the release of /p/ can differ across the three. Since all three are still /p/, the question is how the sound changes with its spot, not whether it becomes a new word.
22|The recording of "pot that", "spot that" and "stop that" lets you see the **allophones of /p/** over time. This is because /p/ sits in a different spot in each phrase. So the timing of the closure, the release, the puff of air and the next voiced sound can differ. || The recording is read in two forms. The **waveform** is on top. It gives the loudness of the signal over time. The **spectrogram** is below. It gives the energy at each frequency over time. Time runs left to right in both, so the same moment lines up top and bottom. || These differences still fit with all three words having the same phoneme /p/. Since a new phoneme needs a difference that changes words, these differences alone do not make new phonemes.
23|An unreleased **[p̚]** and an aspirated **[pʰ]** at the end of "stop" do not change the meaning. So they are allophones of /p/. || This is because English speakers sometimes say [stɑpʰ]. They often do it when stressing the word at the end of a sentence. They also do it when trying to speak clearly, in **citation form**. But [stɑp̚] and [stɑpʰ] mean the same thing. No minimal pair can be found for these two phones here in English. **Allo** means different, so allophones are different phones for the same phoneme. || These forms also switch across **morphological forms** (different forms of one word). The forms are "stop", "stops", "stopped" and "stopping". Each one puts something different after the /p/. That changes whether the /p/ is released and aspirated. || Since hearing a difference does not mean the meaning changed, an audible difference is not always a new phoneme.
24|The word voicing can be confusing because it is used in two different ways. || In **narrow transcription** (describing specific phones), a sound is called voiced if the vocal folds vibrate during the whole sound. This is about one real sound that someone made. || In **broad transcription** (describing phonemes), voicing can be **realized** (actually produced) in several different ways. One way is whether there is **aspiration** (a puff of air) at the start of a word. Another way is how long voicing lasts compared with the closure in "writer" and "rider". This is about a contrast between categories. || Since one phoneme contrast can show up through different physical cues, you first have to ask which level someone means by voiced.
25|English speakers change sound clusters that English does not allow. This is because of **phonotactics** (roughly, which phonemes can go together in a syllable). || The name Sbarro is spelled with sb at the start. English speakers say [spɑroʊ] or [zbɑroʊ]. They never say [sbɑroʊ], whatever they think they are saying. || Foreign names can force speakers to deal with these clusters. Speakers then usually add vowels. The Polish name Zbigniew comes out as [zɨ’bIg.nʲu]. The Kru name Gbagbo comes out as [gə.bɑ.gə.boʊ]. The added vowels split the cluster into extra syllables. || These changes follow English syllable patterns. They do not happen because speakers cannot say each consonant. Since syllable rules are complex but still shape how we speak, spelling alone does not predict what an English speaker says.
26|Phonemes are not letters, and they are not even specific sounds or movements. This is because a **phoneme** is "the smallest phonetic unit in a language that is capable of conveying a distinction in meaning." So a phoneme is defined by the meaning differences it makes. That links it to minimal pairs. One phoneme can also have several allophones. Using this definition can be complicated in practice. || Three things are easy to mix up. A **letter** is part of a writing system. A **phone** is a sound someone actually makes. A **phoneme** is a category that tells words apart. || The **International Phonetic Alphabet** lets speech scientists talk about how sounds are alike and different across languages. So it is easy to think of the IPA as an alphabet of phonemes. That would be a mistake, since phonemes are not letters.
27|Speech is one sound that keeps changing, not a neat row of letter-like pieces. This is because literate people get used to letters, so they imagine speech that way. But speech is nothing like that. || A **spectrogram** (a display of sound energy over time and frequency) of "A sore back is a real pain" makes this clear. Time runs left to right, and frequency goes up. Darker areas have more energy. The patterns change over time, and neighboring sounds blend into each other. The labels and boundary lines are added on top, and they do not cut the sound into pieces. || A phoneme category does not match one block of sound that looks the same every time. Since the sounds blend together, recognizing the sentence means making sense of changing patterns, not finding a clean edge around each letter.
28|Speech is made by the lips, jaw, tongue and other parts of the vocal tract moving together. This is because their changing shape is what shapes the sound that comes out. || A side view of the vocal tract from USC SPAN lets you see these **articulators** (the moving parts that make speech) in action. It shows where the tongue is inside the mouth and the passage down toward the throat. || Each part does its own job. The **tongue** changes the shape inside the mouth. The **lips and jaw** change how open the mouth is. || The movements happen as one continuous sequence. So a speech **gesture** (a movement that makes a sound) is part of a moving system. Since the parts keep moving, they do not make one letter-like sound at a time.
29|**Hockett (1955)** said hearing speech is like an inspector identifying eggs after they have been smashed together. This is because speech mixes its units together, but the listener only has to identify them. || First, a row of unboiled Easter eggs of different sizes and colors rides along a moving belt. These eggs stand for the signals from the **phoneme source**. || Next, the belt carries the eggs through a wringer. The rollers smash the eggs and rub them into each other. That mess stands for the output of the **speech transmitter**. || Last, an inspector looks at the yolks, the spread-out egg whites and the bits of shell. He figures out which eggs went into the wringer. The inspector stands for the **hearer**. He does not have to put the eggs back together, which is impossible. Since he only has to identify them, a hearer can recognize the original sounds from a mixed signal.
30|Some researchers now question whether phonemes are "real", either psychologically or linguistically. This is because phonemes are useful for describing contrasts. But that does not tell you how people store words or recognize speech. || Four papers raise this question. **Lotto and Holt (2000)** wrote about the illusion of the phoneme. **Beckman and Edwards (2000)** wrote about how phonological categories develop and the primacy of lexical learning. **Port (2007)** asked how words are stored in memory, beyond phones and phonemes. **Goldinger and Azuma (2003)** wrote about puzzle-solving science in speech perception. || A way of describing sounds is not the same as a model of the mind. So the open question is whether the mind really uses phoneme-sized units.
