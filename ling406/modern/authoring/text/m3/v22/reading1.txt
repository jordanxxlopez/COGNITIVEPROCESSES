2|The first thing to remember about **phonemes** is that **phonemes are not letters**. A phoneme is a category of speech sound, while a letter is a written symbol, so the two do not have to match one to one. || A single letter can take part in different pronunciations, and a single phoneme can be spelled in different ways. >> So identifying the letters in a word is a different task from identifying its phonemes, because one concerns writing and the other concerns sound.
3|A **mispronouncing contest**, in which people willfully mispronounced words like COLONEL and PARADIGM, made it clear that phonemes are not letters and that **letters are not phonemes** either. >> Following the usual letter-to-sound patterns gives a plausible pronunciation that is wrong for the real word. || Scripts differ in how regularly their spelling matches sound, and this difference is called **orthographic depth**. >> Pinyin for Mandarin and French are relatively regular scripts. || **English is a relatively deep script** because of its irregularities, while **Finnish and Korean are very shallow scripts**. >> A deep script is less predictable than a shallow one, but it is not completely random.
4|**Finnish** is a very **transparent** writing system, which is called **shallow** in the terms of the **Orthographic Depth Hypothesis**. >> After about a minute of following a beginning Finnish lesson along with its text, the pattern becomes easy to grasp. || Each letter corresponds more or less unambiguously to one phoneme, and **doubled letters** stand for **geminated consonants** (consonants held longer) or **long vowels**. || Because spelling matches sound so closely, **Finnish children learn to decode very quickly**. >> **Decoding** means producing a pronunciation from a written form, so a child who learns the letter-sound matches can read many new words.
5|**English is an outlier** among languages that use the Roman alphabet, and one result is that **children learn to decode more slowly in English**. || **Aro and Wimmer** compared first-grade children learning English with children learning French, German, Dutch, Spanish, Swedish and Finnish. >> The children read **pseudowords** aloud, which are made-up words that follow the spelling and sound patterns of their language. || Children learning the other languages produced plausible pronunciations **at a much higher rate** than English learners. >> A pseudoword cannot be remembered as a stored word, so success depends on decoding, and the less predictable English spelling makes decoding harder to learn.
6|**Korean** writing is even more regular than Finnish, and in Hangul the **symbols for similar phonemes look similar** to one another. || The letters are arranged into **syllable blocks** with an initial, a medial and sometimes a final position. || The feature that roughly matches **velar place of articulation** (sounds made with the back of the tongue at the soft palate) is the basic angular **ㄱ shape**. >> The velar consonants g, kk and k are written ㄱ, ㄲ and ㅋ, so they share one shape family, with doubling or an extra stroke telling the related sounds apart.
7|Pseudowords such as GLORP, SPLING, JONK, FEESH and QUISP can be read aloud even though no reader has them stored as words. || A reader builds a plausible pronunciation from familiar **English spelling patterns**, so reading them is **decoding in action**. >> Success cannot come from retrieving a stored pronunciation, because each item is new, so the reader is using knowledge that **generalizes across words**. || These items test decoding rather than vocabulary, and on their own they do not decide whether that knowledge is made of rules or of learned statistical patterns.
8|**Lupker, Brown and Colombo (1997)** had people read real words and **pseudowords**, here called **nonwords**, aloud and compared how fast they began naming each kind. >> Pseudowords were read **nearly as quickly and accurately as real words**, and naming them was **only slightly slower** than naming **low-frequency words**. || The small difference suggests that people decode **fairly naturally, automatically and implicitly**, so an unfamiliar spelling does not force a slow, deliberate effort. || Computational models of word reading ask how decoding should be characterized. >> The central question is whether decoding is best captured by **categorical rules** or by **probabilistic constraints**.
9|Pseudowords such as VONTH, PHANGE, STREAD and SOUGH involve **decoding with ambiguity**, because their spellings support **more than one plausible pronunciation** in English. || A reader's answer depends on the spelling-to-sound patterns learned from earlier words, and different familiar words can favor different answers to the same new form. >> These items probe how readers settle uncertainty in spelling-to-sound mappings, so there is no single correct pronunciation that every reader must agree on.
10|**Generalization** to new items is a good way to test what kinds of **regularities** people have learned about a system, because a new item cannot have been memorized. >> The **Wug Test** is another example of this kind of generalization test. || For STREAD, both /strɛd/ and /strid/ feel acceptable, and it is hard to imagine a **rule set** that covers that intuition. >> A categorical rule would pick one output, while a **statistical account** can give different amounts of support to each pronunciation. || So a model of reading has to explain why both answers seem fine, not just produce one of them.
11|It is uncontroversial that some **decoding** is part of normal reading, and that **irregular** or **inconsistent** words are **harder to recognize** than regular words, which is the **regularity effect**. || The effect is straightforward for words like PINT, ACHE and CHOIR, because the usual spelling-to-sound patterns would give the wrong pronunciation. || It is less clear-cut for **neighborhoods**, which are sets of words sharing a spelling pattern, such as ROLL, POLL, TOLL and DOLL or COOK, TOOK, BOOK and SPOOK. >> In each set most members share one pronunciation while DOLL or SPOOK differs, so the question becomes how much support a pronunciation gets from similar words.
12|There is a lot of evidence that readers use **sublexical information**, meaning units smaller than a whole word, especially when turning spelling into sound. >> People read nonwords quickly and accurately, and words that follow typical spelling-to-sound patterns are easier to read than words that do not. || That leaves the question of **lexical processes**, which use knowledge of **particular words**. >> Nonwords test generalization, while differences among familiar words can reveal what readers know about specific words, so a full account needs both kinds of information.
13|The **Interactive Activation Model** of **McClelland and Rumelhart (1981)** links visual **features**, **letters** and **words**, so features activate letters and letters activate candidate words such as WORK, WORD, WEAR and WEAK. || When the last letter of WORK is partly covered, knowledge of the word helps identify it. >> Active word candidates send **feedback** to their letters, so K gains activation over time while R rises a little and fades and D stays low. || So letter recognition can depend on the surrounding word, and the network settles on an answer gradually rather than all at once.
14|In the **Interactive Activation Model**, **feedback** from active **known words** increases the activation of the **letters that occur in those words**. || This makes recognition **interactive**, because information does not only flow upward from features to a finished word. >> Letter-to-word activation gives evidence for a word candidate, and word-to-letter feedback, called **top-down feedback**, supports that candidate's letters. || So an **ambiguous letter** becomes easier to recognize when it appears inside a familiar word.
15|Two **lexical effects** have already come up, the overall effect of **lexicality** and the effect of **word frequency**. >> Lexicality is whether an item is a real word, and real words are named faster than nonwords. || **Word frequency** is how often a word has been encountered, and just as in **picture naming**, more past encounters with a word lead to **faster naming**. || **Balota and colleagues (2004)** found that this effect is **robust over a very large number of observations**, using data from the **English Lexicon Project**. >> Naming time drops steadily as frequency rises across a very large set of words, so the effect does not depend on a few easy examples.
16|One worry about **frequency effects** was that they might be **confounds** of word length and other variables, meaning that frequent words might be fast only because they are shorter. || A **multiple regression analysis** of about three thousand words read by thirty young and older adults tested this by asking whether frequency still predicts response time once the other word properties are taken into account. >> **Objective and subjective frequency** both still predicted response time in **lexical decision** (deciding whether a letter string is a real word) and **naming** for both age groups. >> **Higher frequency** went with **faster responses**, so the frequency effect **is not just a confound** of length.
17|The **frequency by regularity interaction** is a landmark finding in psycholinguistics, and it has shaped other areas, most immediately **inflectional morphology**. || The **frequency effect is much larger for exception words than for regular words**. >> Among **high-frequency** words, exception and regular words are named about equally fast, but among **low-frequency** words, exception words are named much more slowly. || An **interaction** means the effect of one factor depends on the other, so regularity costs a lot for rare words and very little for common ones. >> One interpretation is that common words are read through word knowledge so quickly that decoding barely matters.
18|One interpretation of the interaction is that word recognition uses **two separate processes**. >> The **sublexical process** is decoding, and the **lexical process** is knowledge of a particular word, including its learned pronunciation. || For **high-frequency words**, the lexical process is so fast and efficient that the sublexical process has **little influence**. || The sublexical process only shows up when people read **nonwords** or **low-frequency words**. >> Nonwords have no stored word to retrieve, and for a rare exception word the slower lexical process gives the regularized decoding output time to interfere.
19|**Acquired phonological dyslexia** is reading difficulty caused by **brain injury**, which is why it is called acquired rather than **developmental dyslexia**. >> These patients have difficulty **specifically with pseudoword naming**. || **Cacciopo van Vliet and colleagues (2003)** compared real-word reading with several nonword reading tests in two patients, MO and IB. >> Both patients read real words **almost perfectly** across concreteness, word class and frequency, but gave plausible pronunciations for **far fewer nonwords**. || So familiar-word reading can stay intact while **decoding new letter strings** is badly damaged, although the deficit is relative because some nonwords were still read well.
20|**Acquired surface dyslexia** is also reading difficulty that follows **brain injury**, but here the difficulty is **specifically with irregular words**. || The mispronunciations tend to be **regularizations**, meaning the word is read with the common spelling-to-sound pattern instead of its learned pronunciation. >> DEAF is read as “deef”, SHOE as “show”, ONE as “own” and STEAK as “steek”. || The patients MP and KT read regular words and nonwords well and did worst on **low-frequency exception words**. >> Most of their errors on exceptions were regularizations, so the errors are systematic rather than random failures to see the letters.
21|Patients with **acquired surface dyslexia** struggle with **irregular words**, make **regularization** errors and have **relatively spared pseudoword reading**. || Patients with **acquired phonological dyslexia** struggle with **pseudoword naming** but have **relatively spared exception word reading**. || These opposite patterns form a classic **double dissociation**, in which one group is impaired on one task but not the other while another group shows the reverse. >> Relatively spared does not mean perfect, because the evidence lies in the contrast between the two patterns of performance.
22|**Double dissociations** are so powerful because they are hard to explain by saying that one task is **simply harder** than the other. >> If pseudowords were just harder than exception words, brain damage should keep hurting pseudoword reading more, and no patient would show the reverse pattern. || A reversal across patients suggests that the two tasks rely on **separable processes**, so pseudowords and exception words place different demands on the reading system. >> The evidence still needs a theory of what those processes are, and two low scores without an opposite pattern are not a double dissociation.
23|Double dissociations give evidence that the processes behind **pseudoword reading** and **irregular word reading** are **separable**. || In **Morton's logogen model**, as presented by **Morton and Patterson (1980)**, **phonological dyslexia** can come from specific damage to the **grapheme-phoneme conversion** module, which turns letters into sounds. >> Pseudowords suffer because they have no stored word pronunciation to fall back on. || **Surface dyslexia** can come from damage to the input or output **logogens**, which are stored word representations, or to the connections between them. >> Exception words then depend on conversion, which gives the wrong, regularized pronunciation, and these boxes are parts of a theory rather than a map of brain regions.
24|**Patterson (1981)** gave early signs that the dual-route account is **not so simple**. || The dual-route view held that surface dyslexia errors come from the **non-lexical** grapheme-phoneme route, so they should show **no lexical influences**. >> Yet **Marcel (1980)** looked carefully at the errors and found lexical influences, and **neologisms**, which are made-up nonword responses, were rarer than a purely non-lexical process predicts. >> A patient read “incense” as “increase”, a real word, when a purely letter-to-sound error should have produced the neologism “inkense”. || Patterson saw two options, either surface dyslexia reflects a **more complicated combination of the two routes** or non-lexical reading is itself a **more complicated route**. >> Patterson suggested that combining normal and patient evidence holds the best promise for progress.
