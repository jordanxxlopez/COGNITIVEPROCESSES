2|**Linguistic relativity**, also called the **Sapir-Whorf hypothesis**, is the idea that differences between languages influence how their speakers organize and think about the world. || **Sapir (1929)** argued that speakers of different languages live in **distinct worlds**, not the same world with different labels attached. **Whorf (1956)** argued that we **dissect nature along lines laid down by our native language**, because the flux of impressions from the world has to be organized largely by the linguistic systems of our minds. >> The **strong** reading says language **determines** what a person can think, while a **weaker** reading says habitual language use only **influences** attention, memory or preferred ways of representing a situation.
3|Even **Sapir and Whorf** did not necessarily hold the **strong relativist position** that their famous quotations suggest. || In the first half of the twentieth century, linguistics was really part of **anthropology**, so treating language as part of **culture** seemed more natural than it does to linguists today. >> Because language and culture travel together, a difference between two language communities may come from culture or experience rather than from the language itself, so a good study has to separate them. || The studies that follow test relativity mainly in **space** and in the relationship between **space and time**, because these domains allow narrow claims that can be tested.
4|Saying **where the ball is** requires a **frame of reference**, which is the system that decides what counts as front, left or a fixed direction. || The same ball can be located relative to the viewer, relative to the chair's own front, or relative to a direction that depends on neither, so the answer depends on which frame the speaker picks. >> The ball does not move at all, yet its description changes, because changing the frame changes the relation being described.
5|**Levinson and colleagues (2002)** distinguish three frames of reference for the same scene of a ball beside a chair. || The **intrinsic frame** uses the object's own orientation, as in “The ball is at the chair's front.” The **absolute frame** uses a fixed direction, as in “The ball is north of the chair.” >> The **relative frame** uses the observer's viewpoint, as in “The ball is to the left of the chair (from my viewpoint).” || If the observer turns around, relative left and right swap sides, but **north stays north**, so the absolute description does not change.
6|Most languages describe nearby things **egocentrically**, meaning relative to the speaker's own body, such as to your left or right, in front of you or behind you. || **Tzeltal**, a Western Mayan language spoken in Chiapas, does not, because it uses **absolute directions** even for nearby things. >> A small number of other languages, mostly unrelated to one another and spread widely, in places such as Bali and Queensland, also use absolute directions. || For most English speakers, a request to raise your east hand does not feel intuitive, because their language names body sides as left and right rather than by compass direction. >> This contrast raises the question of whether the way a language habitually describes space relates to how its speakers remember space without words.
7|Tzeltal has a historically **landmark-based** system in which things are **uphill** or **downhill** from one another, not to the left or right of the speaker or listener. || In fact, uphill and downhill are used as **cardinal directions**, the fixed directions such as north and south, so Tzeltal is an **absolute system**. >> The terms come historically from the slope of the land, but speakers use them as fixed directions rather than pointing to the nearest hill each time. || Because the direction stays fixed when the speaker turns, as **Levinson and colleagues (2002)** describe, a position is described the same way from any orientation.
8|New Yorkers use **uptown** and **downtown** as if they were cardinal directions, but these terms do not work outside Manhattan, so they form a truly **landmark-based system**. || Manhattan also sits at an angle to **magnetic north**, so the subway map leaves New Yorkers confused about real north and south. >> A local direction system is useful only where its landmark exists, so it is not an absolute frame that works everywhere. || The Manhattan case is an aside about reference systems, not an experiment about language and thought.
9|**Majid and colleagues (2004)** describe the **chips task**, a **nonlinguistic test** of whether people remember space in **relative** or **absolute** terms, in which the response itself uses no words. || A participant sees a card with a large dot and a small dot on one table, waits, is turned around to face the opposite way, and then picks the matching card out of four cards on a second table. >> Because the person has turned around, the card that keeps the dots the same relative to the body differs from the card that keeps them the same relative to north. || **Dutch speakers** gave **overwhelmingly relative** responses and **Tzeltal speakers** gave **mostly absolute** responses, matching each language's preferred frame. >> Language matches the pattern here, but the comparison does not separate language from all other experience.
10|**Majid and colleagues (2004)** also used a harder **transitive inference** task, in which a person combines two learned relations to work out a third one. || At the first table a person sees a cube and a cone, then turns around and sees the cube and a cylinder at a second table. After turning back to the first table, where only the cone remains, the person must place the cylinder. >> A relative coder and an absolute coder put the cylinder on opposite sides of the cone, because the person has turned between the two displays. || **Dutch speakers** again gave **mostly relative** answers, and **Tzeltal speakers** gave **mostly absolute** answers.
11|**Li and Gleitman (2002)** pointed out that the Tzeltal speakers were tested **outside**, with **visible, familiar landmarks**, while typical psychology studies are run in **windowless rooms**. || A setting full of landmarks makes landmark-based coding easy, and a windowless room makes viewer-based coding more convenient. >> So the language difference could be **confounded** with the testing setting, meaning the two change together and either one could explain the result. || They tried to **induce landmark-based reasoning in English speakers**, because changing the setting while keeping the language the same tests whether the setting alone can shift the response.
12|**Li and Gleitman (2002)** tested English speakers in three settings, a room with the **blinds down**, a room with the **blinds up** and a view of the street, and **outdoors**. || With the blinds down, English speakers **overwhelmingly used relative position**, like the Dutch speakers in earlier studies. With the blinds up or outdoors, their relative preference was **less pronounced**. >> In those landmark-rich settings, participants tended to split into people giving all absolute or all relative answers, and they asked far more often what the task wanted. || Because only the setting changed, the **environment can shift** the frame people use, although the change is a weaker preference rather than a full switch to absolute coding.

14|If the language groups were tested in different conditions, the difference between them may be **confounded** with those conditions, meaning the setting changed along with the language and could explain the result. || The bias toward **relative spatial reasoning** might then be an **artifact**, a result produced by the testing method, of the way psychology tests are usually run in universities. >> So an uncontrolled comparison between groups cannot tell a language effect apart from an effect of the testing conditions.
15|**Levinson and colleagues (2002)** were not impressed by the result from **Li and Gleitman (2002)**. || They argued that Li and Gleitman made it **too transparent** that rotation was being tested, because they **reduced memory load** and shortened the **time and distance between stimulus and test**. >> When a task is easy and obvious, people can choose a response on purpose instead of using their habitual spatial coding. || They also argued that the participants were **Ivy League students**, who excel at working out what the experimenter wants and then making it happen. >> This is a **demand characteristics** concern, meaning that participants' guesses about the purpose of a study can change their answers.
16|**Levinson and colleagues (2002)** re-ran the **animals in a row** experiment with **Dutch college students**, but they changed three things. || They added a **fourth animal** and used a **longer delay**, both to **increase memory load**, and they put the stimulus and response tables **farther apart** to make the rotation less noticeable. || Under these harder and less transparent conditions, a **bias toward relative framing remained**. >> So the landmark effect depends on how the task is set up, and when memory demands are real the relative preference holds.
17|For speakers of languages that use **relative frames**, the bias toward relative over absolute frames is **robust to the environment** under two conditions. || The task must have a **significant memory component**, and the experimenter's interest in rotation and frames of reference must **not be obvious**. || The importance of memory muddies the waters, because a task that looks spatial may be solved partly with **verbal strategies** while the person holds the information in mind. >> So the same nonverbal response could come from a verbal memory as well as a spatial one.
18|Memory muddies the waters because participants may **recode the animals** in **working memory**, the short-term store that holds information while it is used. They may store the animals as a spoken list such as “lion, tiger, bear,” and then naturally lay the list out **from left to right**. || A list keeps the order of the animals but not their compass direction, so rebuilding it left to right produces a **relative-looking** answer without any relative spatial memory. || Left to right seems a natural way to lay out a sequence in time because English is **read and written from left to right**, a possibility that later studies of reading direction test.
19|The lyric by **Brown and Byrd (1970)**, “You're like a dull knife, just ain't cuttin',” uses **metaphorical language**, which describes one kind of experience with words from another. || Failing to make a compelling argument is “not cutting” because a dull knife fails at its job, and “talking loud” but “saying nothing” needs no literally loud voice, since it means talking a lot without making a point. >> Metaphors like these let physical words express **abstract concepts**, which raises the question of how much of ordinary language is truly non-metaphorical. || Arguments about language and thought can generate more heat than light, so the studies that follow ask a narrow question, whether language affects how people think about **time in terms of space**.
20|**Boroditsky and colleagues (2010)** point out that both English and Mandarin use **horizontal front and back terms** for time, as when English speakers look forward to good times ahead or think back to troubles behind them. || Mandarin speakers also **systematically and frequently use vertical terms**, so earlier events are **shàng** (“up”) and later events are **xià** (“down”). >> The phrase “shàng ge yuè” means last month and “xià ge yuè” means next month, while the same words also mean climbing and descending in space. >> In one corpus of written Mandarin counted by Chen (2007), **more than a third** of the spatial metaphors for time were vertical. || This difference in talk motivates testing whether Mandarin speakers also **think about time vertically**.
21|**Boroditsky (2001)** ran a **priming** experiment, in which a first task makes a related later task faster or slower. || Participants first read a **horizontal** spatial prime such as “The black worm is ahead of the white worm” or a **vertical** one such as “The black ball is above the white ball,” and then answered **purely temporal earlier or later questions**. >> **English speakers** answered **faster after horizontal primes**, while **Mandarin speakers** answered **faster after vertical primes**. || English speakers given **short training on vertical metaphors** for time, such as “Cars were invented above fax machines,” then answered **faster after vertical primes**, so the effect can shift with brief experience.
22|**Casasanto and Bottini (2005)** offered an alternative explanation for the vertical time effect in Mandarin, the **direction of text**. || English is written in left-to-right lines, Chinese is **mostly left-first** but is sometimes written in vertical columns, and Taiwanese text is **mostly right-first** in vertical columns. >> So a vertical time effect could come from **reading habits** rather than from vertical metaphors in the language. || When people arranged items in order, every English speaker arranged them **left to right**, most Chinese speakers did too, and Taiwanese speakers were split across **left to right, right to left and top to bottom**.
23|**Boroditsky and colleagues (2010)** controlled for reading direction by **excluding Chinese readers who reported reading top to bottom**. || **English speakers** were faster with the **left-is-earlier** key mapping than with the right-is-earlier mapping, but they showed **no clear advantage** for either vertical mapping. >> **Mandarin speakers** were faster with the **left-is-earlier** mapping and also with the **top-is-earlier** mapping. || Because the vertical effect remained without the top-to-bottom readers, that reading habit **cannot fully explain** it.
24|**Casasanto and Bottini (2010)** asked people to push a **blue button for past events** and a **red button for future events**, or the reverse in different blocks. || Because the colors switched sides, the **past** button was **on the left half the time** and **on the right** the other half, so color cannot explain a difference between mappings. >> With normal text, people were **faster when past was on the left and future was on the right**, in both blocks. || This advantage fits an implicit **mental timeline** that runs from earlier on the left to later on the right, and it appears even though the judgment is about time rather than space.
25|**Casasanto and Bottini (2010)** then had people do a few minutes of **mirror reading**, which means reading text printed backward, before doing the same past and future button task. || In the **first block** the usual advantage for the left-earlier mapping stayed about the same. >> By the **second block** the effect **reversed**, so people were **faster when earlier was on the right and later was on the left**. || Because brief reading experience flipped the mapping, **reading direction contributes** to how people lay out time in space, and the mapping is **flexible** rather than fixed by native language alone.
26|The passage about snow words comes from a classic rant by **Pullum (1989)** called “The Great Eskimo Vocabulary Hoax,” and he comes down **against** the exaggerated relativist story. >> The word Eskimo is not an acceptable term for the Inuktitut people, whose language is the most likely source of the examples, and it appears only because it is the title. || **Boas (1911)** originally noted that English uses separate roots for forms of water, such as lake, river, rain and foam, just as Eskimo uses separate roots such as aput ‘snow on the ground’ and qana ‘falling snow.’ >> Boas's point was simply that English expresses these ideas with phrases built on the root snow, but things could have been otherwise. || **Whorf (1940)** later picked up the example of Boas and used it vaguely in an amateur linguistics article in Technology Review, and it grew into an **exaggerated story** about vocabulary and thought.
