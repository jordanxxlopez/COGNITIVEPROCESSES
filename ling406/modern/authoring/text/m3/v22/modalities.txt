2|**Signed languages** and **Braille** are really different cases, so they should not be treated as the same kind of thing. || A signed language is a **natural language** with its own **linguistic structure**, while Braille is a **system for reading by touch** that lets blind people read a spoken language they share with sighted people. >> So changing the modality of a whole language is not the same as changing how a written language is read. || They are grouped together because both reveal the **plasticity of sensory systems**, which is how the brain gets organized differently when language input comes from a different modality.
3|**Emmorey and colleagues (2004)** list the obvious ways **sign languages** differ from spoken languages, which are **modality**, **articulators**, **use of space** and **iconicity**. >> The modality is visual, the articulators are the hands, face and upper body working together, and positions in space can carry relations between words. || **Iconicity** means that the form of a sign resembles its meaning, but it is **easy to exaggerate** when individual words are viewed with their **glosses**. >> A gloss is the written translation given for a sign, so it makes the resemblance seem more obvious than it is, and a learner still has to learn the forms and structure of the language.
4|Fluent signing **coordinates the hands, face and upper body**, and signers keep **eye contact** with each other while they sign. || Signing is **fluid** and **fast**, so it is hard for a learner to **segment words**, meaning to find where one sign ends and the next begins. >> The same is true of fluent speech, where word boundaries are also not obvious to a learner. || So the thing to study is coordinated language production across all of these articulators, not one isolated hand shape.
5|As with speech, sign has **featural descriptions**, so the **phonology of sign language** can be described with features, as **Emmorey and colleagues (2003)** did. || Signs can **differ contrastively** in **hand configuration**, **location** of the gesture and **movement**, which means that changing just one of these features changes which sign it is. >> “please” and “sorry” form one such pair of signs, and “onion” and “apple” form another pair. || So phonology is about the organization of contrastive form, and it is not limited to sound.
6|The research question is whether there is **categorical perception** for these sign dimensions, meaning whether people sort a smooth physical change into sharp categories and tell apart differences best at the category boundary. || To test it, hand configuration was changed in small steps along a **continuum** of images. >> The **phonemic** continuum ran from the sign PLEASE to the sign SORRY, so it crossed a change in which sign it is. >> The **allophonic** continuum ran from “open-N” to “closed N” in the sign SAY-NO-TO, so the hand changed but the sign stayed the same. || Signers identified each image and discriminated pairs of images, and the answer was **yes for the phonemic continuum only**.
7|For **deaf signers**, the **phonemic** continuum produced a **fairly sharp categorical boundary** between the fourth and sixth stimuli, and this boundary lines up with the **peak in the discrimination function**. >> So hand shapes that crossed the line between PLEASE and SORRY were the easiest to tell apart, which is the familiar pattern from speech sounds. || In the **allophonic** condition, the **identification function** was **much shallower**, so many more stimuli were ambiguous, and there was **no peak in discrimination**. || Since only the contrast that changes sign identity is perceived this way, signers show **categorical perception** for some contrastive features of sign.
8|Sign language uses **space** to show the **relationships between words** in a sentence. || In a very transparent case, the **relative position of two objects** is given by **where the classifier sign is made**. >> A **classifier sign** is a sign that represents an object, so making it at a certain spot in signing space places that object at that spot in the scene. || So understanding sentences of any complexity requires **keeping track of where signs were made**, and spatial processing becomes part of understanding the language.
9|**Campbell, MacSweeney and Waters (2008)** found that brain activity during **sign language comprehension** has important similarities with spoken language and important differences from it. || Like speech, sign shows **left lateralization** and activates the **classical language areas**, which are **Broca's area** in the inferior frontal region and **Wernicke's area** in the posterior superior temporal region. || Unlike speech, sign also activates **visual areas** and **superior parietal** areas, which are classic **spatial areas**. >> They compared deaf signers, hearing signers and hearing people perceiving audio-visual speech, so the extra visual and spatial activity reflects the demands of a seen, spatial signal.
10|**Bavelier and colleagues (2001)** asked whether **deafness**, **experience with sign**, or both change **spatial attention**. || They compared **hearing controls**, **deaf signers** and **hearing signers**, who watched dots and responded to brief changes in **luminance** (brightness) or **velocity** (speed of motion). >> Attention was directed either to the **center** of the screen or to the **periphery**, which is the outer edge of the visual field. || **Deaf participants** had an **advantage** in this task only when attention was directed to the **periphery**. >> Including hearing signers separates signing experience from the lack of hearing, because both groups know sign but only one group is deaf.
11|**Learning sign language** changes the **laterality** of the motion perception areas, labeled **MT/MST**, from the right side of the brain to the left side. >> This happens in both deaf and hearing signers, perhaps because motion perception needs to “talk to” the **left-lateralized language networks** in people who learn sign. || **Enhanced peripheral visual attention**, however, is found **only in deaf individuals** and not in hearing signers. >> Perhaps this is because hearing people depend more on **auditory cues** for spatial attention when things are at the far periphery. || So signing experience and deafness have **two different effects** on visual processing.
12|To separate **language processing** from the visual, spatial and motor processing of **hand movements**, sign comprehension was compared directly with **tic-tac**. || Tic-tac is a sophisticated and outmoded **gestural system** that was used to communicate about odds at horse races in the UK. >> Like sign, it uses organized, sequenced hand movements that carry meaning, but it is not a natural language. || So any difference in brain activity between the two reflects **language** rather than simply watching hands move.
13|**Campbell, MacSweeney and Waters (2008)** found **stronger activity around Wernicke's area**, in the **posterior superior temporal** region, for sign language than for tic-tac. >> Since both involve organized, sequenced hand movements, the extra activity reflects processing a **natural language** rather than processing moving hands. || The frontal activation was **superior to Broca's area**, meaning it lies above that area, so it should not be described as a Broca's area effect.
14|**Sign language has phonological structure**, and native signers show **categorical perception** for some contrastive features. || **Learning a signed language** leads to some **brain reorganization**, particularly in the **lateralization of motion processing**. >> Attention to the visual periphery is critical for both hearing and deaf signers, but only **deaf signers** show **enhanced visual attention in the periphery**. || Compared with non-language stimuli that also involve organized, sequenced hand movements, signed sentences activate the **posterior STG** (superior temporal gyrus), which is **Wernicke's area**.
15|**Emmorey (2002)** showed with American Sign Language that understanding a **signed spatial description** can require a **perspective transformation**, which is mentally turning the scene around. || The location of a table within a room can be described in sign from the **speaker's perspective** or from the **addressee's perspective**. >> The speaker's version uses the signs I-ENTER, TABLE and THERE, and the addressee's version uses YOU-ENTER, TABLE and THERE, while the table stays in the same spot by the entrance. || Signers show **better comprehension** for room descriptions from the **speaker's perspective**, even though that description requires the mental transformation. >> So the best understood description is not the one that avoids every spatial transformation.
16|**Braille reading** is in many ways **equivalent to reading print**, because the reader is still reading a written form of a spoken language. || One difference is that Braille symbols are **optimized for tactile discrimination**, meaning telling shapes apart by touch, so reading Braille **by sight is really hard**. || Another difference is that the **fingers take in letters one at a time**, so the letters arrive in a sequence, which is a different demand from viewing printed text.
17|**Reading Braille activates visual cortex** in blind individuals, even though the input comes through touch. || When **early blind** participants are compared with **late blind** participants, the **earlier sight is lost**, the **more extensive** the visual activity to tactile stimuli. || The **visual word form area**, a region tied to processing written words, is **strongly activated in early blind** participants, more so than in late blind participants. >> So the usual sense of a brain region does not fully decide what it processes, and the timing of sight loss shapes how the brain is organized.
18|Earlier, the question came up whether **competition** from hearing could be preventing hearing individuals from developing enhanced **peripheral visual attention**, and this study tests competition directly. || **Merabet and colleagues (2008)** had **sighted** people wear **blindfolds for five days** and trained them to **read Braille**, while a control group was trained without a blindfold. || Relative to the control group, the **blindfolded group learned more quickly**. >> So taking away competing visual input sped up tactile learning, even over a short time in sighted adults.
19|Over the course of the experiment, **Merabet and colleagues (2008)** found that sighted, blindfolded participants began to show **some activity in primary visual cortex** when **reading Braille**. || When the **blindfold was removed** on the sixth day, this effect was **completely reversed**. >> So the change in visual cortex was **rapid and reversible**, which fits uncovering inputs that already existed rather than building a new pathway in a few days.
20|**Merabet and colleagues (2008)** used **transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)**, which disrupts activity in a targeted brain area, on **visual cortex**, and it **disrupted Braille reading in the blindfolded group only**. >> On the fifth day, the blindfolded group's **error rate rose** under TMS compared with baseline and sham, but after the blindfold came off this effect was gone. || This is tentative evidence that blindfolded participants were somehow **using visual cortex for tactile processing**. || One explanation is that visual cortex **gets tactile input all the time**, even in sighted people, but this input is **too weak to compete** with the stronger, more consistent visual input. >> So even **short-term removal of visual input** can **unmask** tactile responses in visual cortex.
21|**Language circuitry is resilient** across modalities, because **classical language areas** respond to **sign language** despite its very different surface form, and **Braille reading** engages networks important for **processing text**. || **Cross-modal plasticity**, in which brain areas for one sense take on input from another, suggests that brain organization is shaped over development by **competition**. >> When auditory input is unavailable, areas thought to be auditory are used to process sign, and visual attention is enhanced at the periphery. || The same idea of competing cognitive and perceptual processes appeared in the **Competition Model** of second language learning.
