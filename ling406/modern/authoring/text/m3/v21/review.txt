2|**Phonemes** are not the same thing as letters, and letters are not the same thing as phonemes. A **mispronouncing contest**, in which people willfully mispronounced words such as COLONEL and PARADIGM, helped make this point. >> These words are hard because their spelling does not reliably tell a reader how they sound. || Scripts differ in how regular their spelling is, and this difference is called **orthographic depth**. Pinyin for Mandarin and French are relatively regular, but English is a relatively **deep** script because of its many irregularities. >> Finnish and Korean are very **shallow** scripts, so their spelling predicts sound closely.
3|Finnish is a very **transparent** writing system, which is also called **shallow** under the **orthographic depth hypothesis**. Each letter corresponds more or less unambiguously to one **phoneme**, so a reader can work out the pronunciation from the spelling. >> Doubled letters mark **geminated** consonants, which are held for longer, or **long vowels**. || Because the spelling is so regular, **Finnish children** learn to decode pronunciations for written forms very quickly. >> A short Finnish lesson was played as an example, and the speaker suggests that listeners pick up the idea within about a minute. The lesson is a demonstration, so it supports the spelling claims rather than any recovered recording.
4|English is a bit of an **outlier** among languages that use the Roman alphabet. As a result, kids learn to **decode** more slowly in English than they do in most other languages. || In first grade, children learning a wide variety of other languages generate plausible pronunciations for **pseudowords** at a much higher rate than English learners. A pseudoword is a made-up word that follows the spelling and sound patterns of its language. >> Because a pseudoword is not a familiar stored word, the child has to generalize from learned spelling and sound patterns. The lower English performance fits with English being a deep script, and the test is about decoding skill, not about the child's whole language ability.
5|Korean is even more regular than Finnish, and similar **phonemes** are written with similar symbols. The letter shapes therefore carry information about sound. >> The basic velar consonant letters share an angular shape, and extra strokes or doubling mark related sounds. || The question asks which orthographic feature matches **velar place of articulation**, which means the place where the back of the tongue touches the soft palate. The answer is the angular shape that the velar consonants share. >> Korean letters are grouped into syllable blocks with **initial**, **medial** and sometimes **final** positions, so the blocks are not unrelated pictures of whole words.
6|This is a **landmark finding** in psycholinguistics, and it has shaped later work on areas such as inflectional morphology. The **frequency effect** is much larger for **exception words** than for regular words. >> An exception word has a pronunciation that breaks the usual spelling pattern. || **Naming latency**, the time needed to begin saying a word, is much longer for low-frequency exception words than for low-frequency regular words. High-frequency words show a much smaller regularity difference, so the two kinds of word come close together. >> An **interaction** means the effect of one factor depends on the other, so the regularity cost is not added equally to high-frequency and low-frequency words.
7|One interpretation says there are **two separate processes** in word recognition. The first is a **sublexical process**, which is decoding, and the second is a **lexical process**, which is knowledge of a particular word. || For **high-frequency words**, the lexical process is so fast and efficient that the sublexical process has little influence. The sublexical process appears mainly when a reader sees **nonwords** or **low-frequency words**. >> A low-frequency exception is slower to retrieve, so the regularized output from decoding can interfere with it. This is an interpretation of the interaction, not the interaction itself, so other architectures must be tested against the same pattern.
8|**Seidenberg and McClelland** built **PDP models** of visual word recognition, and these models make different assumptions from dual-route models. In these models, **rules** are generalizations learned from many words, not a separate process. || The **lexicon** is also **distributed**, so some whole-word information is stored in the spelling to sound mappings and some is stored in other parts of the system. Their research strategy differs as well. They explain reading with **domain-general learning mechanisms** instead of reverse-engineering the system from adult and neuropsychological data. >> Distributed knowledge does not mean the model has no memory for individual words, because distributed refers to how knowledge is spread across the network.
9|The model captured the **frequency by regularity interaction** without assuming that frequency and regularity result from different processes. || **Regular** words are consistent with statistical patterns that many other words share, while **exception** words are inconsistent with those patterns. Frequency also affects how well words are learned, so highly frequent words are less affected by inconsistency with other words. >> A low-frequency exception has both weak item-specific learning and poor support from the dominant pattern. That combination creates a larger disadvantage than either factor alone.
10|In principle, a PDP model can read **nonwords** with the same set of weights it uses for words. Generalization takes advantage of the same statistical patterns that give rise to regularity effects. || A new spelling activates patterns that overlap with familiar spellings, so the network can generalize to it. No stored entry for that nonword is required, so this is proposed as a way to decode without a separate **grapheme-to-phoneme rule** module. >> Novel pronunciation comes from applying learned statistical structure, so nonword reading does not automatically require a separate symbolic route.
11|In practice, though, the model was not very good at **nonwords**, so its nonword accuracy was weak. This leaves an **ambiguity in model evaluation**. The failure could mean the theoretical claim was wrong, or it could mean that something specific to the way the model was built was wrong. || The theoretical claim is that nonwords and exceptions can be read by the same associative mechanism. **Poor nonword performance** can challenge either that claim or a particular implementation. One possibility is that a single associative mechanism cannot explain the task. Another possibility is that the chosen representations fail to capture the similarities needed for generalization. >> Improving the implementation while keeping the mechanism helps separate these two possibilities. A better representation is a different move from adding a separate nonword route.
12|Many studies find a relationship between **decoding**, which is nonword reading, and **phonological processing**. The phonological representations used by **Seidenberg and McClelland** were unusual, and they were called **Wickelfeatures**. || **Plaut and colleagues** improved the phonological representations, and their model produced better performance on nonwords. This supports a representational explanation for at least part of the earlier failure. >> Changing the representation can improve decoding without adding a symbolic rule system, which matters when judging the original theoretical claim.
13|**Generalization depends on similarity**, and this principle was inherited from behaviorism. Poor phonological representations, such as Wickelfeatures, fail to capture the similarity space of words in the language. || **Word-specific information** holds up better under poor representations, because it does not depend entirely on generalization. A familiar word keeps support from its own training history, while a nonword lacks that item-specific experience and depends more heavily on generalization. >> Unfamiliar forms are therefore especially vulnerable to poor representations, and no dedicated nonword mechanism is needed to explain this. A weaker shared representation and damage to a separate route both create a task difference, but they point to different explanations.
14|**Harm and Seidenberg** studied this more systematically by manipulating the amount of **noise** in the phonological representations. Increasing noise makes the sound representations less reliable, while the general learning framework stays the same. || The manipulation reproduced several findings from research on developmental dyslexia. It produced poorer nonword decoding, a relative sparing of word recognition, and a link between the severity of the phonological deficit and the degree of decoding difficulty. >> Nonwords are especially dependent on reliable generalization, so degraded shared representations can create a reading pattern that looks selective. Noise is the manipulated property, and word and nonword performance are the outcomes, so the simulation tests a directional mechanism rather than only observing a correlation.
15|The **Pinker and Ullman** account is a **dual route** model for the past tense, and it raises the question of how it is similar to and different from the dual-route model for reading. The lexicon supplies irregular forms such as HELD, while **grammatical combination** can produce WALK plus -ed. || Like dual-route reading, this account separates word-specific knowledge from a productive operation. Unlike reading aloud, its task is to transform a stem according to a grammatical feature, not to convert spelling into sound. >> The input includes a stem and a past-tense feature, and a **blocking** connection keeps a retrieved irregular from competing unchecked with a regular form.
16|The **Words and Rules** proposal was motivated partly by a theory of grammar. Dual-route theories of reading were strongly motivated by **neuropsychological double dissociations**, while **Pinker and colleagues** were concerned with categorical, **symbolic** grammars. || Those grammars describe productive patterns, but these rules often have exceptions. The morphology proposal keeps the grammatical rule system distinct from exceptional lexical information. >> So the two architectures look similar, but the reading model came from patient dissociations and the past-tense model came from an argument about grammar.
17|Rather than make the grammar inelegant by adding machinery for exceptions, linguists often left the exceptions out of the grammar. The **dual-process** model instead handles exceptions with a second process, which keeps the rule grammar elegant, symbolic and categorical. || This second process had all the properties that the rule route did not have. It was **word-specific**, so it is lexical, and it was **sensitive to frequency** of occurrence. It was also **sensitive to phonological similarity**, as in KEEP and KEPT, WEEP and WEPT, and LEAP and LEAPT. >> An exception can belong to a small family of similar words rather than being unrelated to other words. Assigning these properties to lexical memory lets the rule system stay categorical and general.
18|**Rumelhart and McClelland** offered an alternative in which all of the learning is associative. They modeled the past tense as a learned association between present and past sound patterns, so a single trainable network learns across verbs instead of applying a separately specified -ed rule. || One learning mechanism is proposed for both regularities and exceptions. Regularity is a pattern learned from many examples, and irregular forms are learned within the same system. >> The model encodes the phonology of a root, transforms that representation through modifiable connections, and decodes an output pattern, so the fixed encoding and decoding stages surround a modifiable pattern associator.
19|**U-shaped learning** describes an early success, then a decline, and then a later recovery. Children often get worse before they get better, so their performance goes down before it goes back up. >> A child may correctly say RAN, later produce RUNNED, and eventually return to RAN. || The middle error is a **regularization**, because the child applies the common past-tense pattern to an irregular verb. >> The U shape describes accuracy over the course of development, not the shape of a word, and development is what the model was built to capture.
20|The **Words and Rules** account explains U-shaped learning through changes in the use of rules and lexical exceptions. Early correct forms are imitations that do not depend on the same mechanisms as adult processing. || When a rule is first learned, it is applied to all cases, so it works as the best hypothesis for items in general. Over time, exceptions are learned one by one and become part of lexical knowledge, and then a **blocking process** develops. >> Blocking means that retrieving the right irregular prevents the otherwise available regular form, so overregularization is productive rule use that exceptions have not yet constrained.
21|**Rumelhart and McClelland's** model can also produce **U-shaped learning**, because its training set is based on data about the input children receive, and that input changes over time. Early on, irregular verbs predominate, since the highest-frequency verbs tend to have irregular past-tense forms such as GIVE, TAKE, HAVE and GO. || As the vocabulary grows to include more regular forms, the model reorganizes to capture those regularities, and this interferes with some of the early-learned forms. Later, the network recovers the irregular responses while keeping broad regularity. >> No new symbolic rule component is inserted at the point of decline, so the drop comes from changing weights and changing input.
22|The model was trained on a small number of verbs, so the researchers could run a **wug test** with real words. A real English verb can be novel to the model if it was left out of training, so the test uses the logic of a wug task even when the items are real words to people. || Testing untrained verbs asks whether the network learned **transferable structure**. Generalization is defined relative to the learner's experience, so a familiar human word still counts as a novel test for the model when it was withheld from training. >> The model generates many appropriate past-tense features, but getting features right is different from producing whole words perfectly. A high proportion of correct **Wickelfeatures** does not mean the same proportion of whole words was pronounced perfectly.
23|Some model outputs contain changes that are unlike ordinary human **regularizations**. Examples include a MADE(D)-like change to MATE, and the unusual outputs MEMBLED for MAIL and TOUREDER for TOUR. || The model authors acknowledge that people are unlikely to make some of these errors. They suggest that additional output constraints could reduce these bizarre responses. >> Capturing an overall learning pattern is different from matching the detailed form of human errors, so a plausible learning curve is not enough if the model produces implausible words.
24|**Joanisse and Seidenberg** proposed that phonology supports **rule-like generalization**, while irregular forms require greater support from **semantics**. In this proposal, the same system links speech input, meaning and speech output. || Reliable sound representations support extension from familiar present and past pairs to new forms. Irregular mappings receive additional support from the particular word's semantic representation. >> The proposal concerns different degrees of dependence on shared systems, not a separate symbolic rule route for every regular response.
25|**Joanisse and Seidenberg** aimed to explain a **dissociation** in neuropsychological data, in which irregular and nonword past-tense performance come apart. These tasks differ in how much they depend on learned word-specific information and on phonological generalization. || A nonword has no familiar item-specific semantic history to support its inflection, so it depends on generalization. A known irregular can need semantic support because its output is not well predicted by the dominant pattern. >> Damaging phonological or semantic integrity tests whether these different dependencies can produce opposite impairment profiles, and the same architecture has to explain both sides of the pattern.
26|Patients with **Broca's aphasia** have injury to left anterior brain regions and poor speech production. Their **regular morphology** is especially poor for nonwords, so they struggle to say that wug becomes wugged and blick becomes blicked. || A novel BLICK must be transformed without relying on a familiar stored past tense, which makes nonword inflection especially demanding. Regular forms are also vulnerable, but irregular forms are relatively better in this comparison. >> This difficulty is connected to sound processing, so it motivates looking at phonological contributions to the apparent morphological deficit. Nonword accuracy falls below both regular and irregular accuracy.
27|**Wernicke's** and **Alzheimer's** patients, and **anomic** patients with injury to inferior or middle temporal regions, have a larger problem with **irregular** past-tense forms. Their irregular performance is lower than their regular and nonword performance. || This creates the other side of the dissociation, and it motivates examining the information that especially supports known irregular forms. >> The irregular condition is the lowest of the three, so relatively preserved nonword inflection can exist alongside poor irregular inflection. The diagnostic labels do not make every individual's performance identical.
28|The classical contrast says that **Wernicke's aphasia** involves fluent but often poorly interpretable speech, sometimes described as **word salad**, with neologisms, while syntax stays roughly intact. **Broca's aphasia** involves **telegraphic speech**, with difficulty with word order and grammatical particles, although content words are relatively spared. || This contrast is attractive because it seems to separate word problems from grammar problems. Fluency, grammatical organization and meaningful word selection are different dimensions of speech, and each example profile emphasizes a different disruption.
29|Wernicke's aphasia is described as a problem with activation in the **lexicon**, possibly with inhibition, and Broca's aphasia is described as a problem with **grammar**. This is fairly distant from how Broca described his original cases. || Broca's actual patients would not necessarily be classified as having Broca's aphasia today. They were arguably unable to produce speech at all, apparently because of motor control problems, so they would be classified as having **apraxia of speech**, which concerns planning or coordinating speech movements. >> So the modern label names a grammar problem, while Broca's own patients had a problem with the motor control of speech.
30|The Wernicke and Broca dichotomy lines up with a sharp distinction from late twentieth century formal linguistics between the **grammar** and the **lexicon**. This goes back to Chomsky's formulation of the computational problem of language learning and to demonstrations of the independence of syntax and semantics. || A grammar combines forms, while lexical knowledge concerns words and their properties. **Colorless green ideas sleep furiously** is grammatical but semantically unusual, so syntactic well-formedness cannot be reduced to whether a sentence describes a sensible event. >> That example contrasts with a word order that breaks English syntax, which isolates grammatical organization from familiarity or plausibility.
31|The **functional level** selects word meanings and semantic roles, while the **positional level** organizes word forms into a grammatical sequence. Phonological encoding and articulation come later in producing an utterance. || A **lemma** is a representation used in lexical selection, which is different from simply executing its sound pattern. Lemmas have an arbitrary relationship to their referents, while the syntactic trees and affixes at the positional level are products of abstract syntactic processes. >> Words such as HOUSEBOAT and DOGHOUSE have meanings that are clearly not arbitrary, and speech errors suggest that derivational morphology is done at the positional level. || Word selection, ordering, sound encoding and articulation are different levels of explanation, so an apparent lexical and grammatical contrast should be linked to those operations carefully. The proposal runs from conceptual preparation through the functional and positional levels to phonological encoding and speech gestures.
32|In most languages, people describe nearby space **egocentrically**, using the speaker's own position and orientation. Left, right, in front of you and behind you are familiar examples. || **Tzeltal**, a Western Mayan language spoken in Chiapas, does not do this. Tzeltal and a small number of other languages, which are mostly unrelated to one another and broadly distributed, use **absolute directions** instead. >> Tzeltal speakers commonly use absolute directions where English speakers might use these relative terms, so the contrast raises the question of whether habitual language use relates to nonverbal spatial coding.
33|**Tzeltal** has a historically **landmark-based** system, in which things are either uphill or downhill from one another rather than to the left or right of the speaker or listener. The terms **uphill** and **downhill** work as cardinal directions, which makes this an **absolute system**. || A landmark origin and an absolute use are different properties. A historical connection to a slope does not mean that each use points to the nearest visible hill. A direction is kept as a stable reference rather than recalculated from the speaker's current left and right. >> This lets positions be described consistently as the speaker changes orientation, so the contrast is with a description that depends entirely on the viewpoint.
34|**Majid and colleagues** used a non-linguistic test to tell **relative** spatial memory from **absolute** spatial memory. A participant sees a configuration, waits, and then chooses its match at a second table after being turned completely around. || A **relative** response keeps the arrangement in line with the person's viewpoint, while an **absolute** response keeps its orientation in the environment. The two responses diverge after rotation, because the north direction stays fixed while the person turns. >> **Dutch speakers** predominantly gave relative responses, while **Tzeltal speakers** gave more absolute responses. The pattern fits their preferred linguistic frames, but it does not isolate language from all other experience.
35|**Majid and colleagues** made the task harder by testing **spatial inference across displays**. Participants learn the relation between two objects, rotate to another display, and then learn a second relation that involves a shared object. || A cube appears in both displays, so it links the cone and cylinder relations. Participants then place one object relative to another using the combined information. Relative and absolute coding lead to different placements because the person's orientation has changed. >> **Dutch** and **Tzeltal** response patterns again differ in the direction that matches their preferred spatial frames, and the final placement tells a viewer-relative inference apart from one that keeps environmental direction.
36|**Li and Gleitman** argued that landmark-based similarity can be induced in **English speakers**. The Tzeltal speakers were tested outside, with visible and familiar landmarks, whereas typical psychology lab studies are done in windowless rooms. || **Testing conditions** could contribute to the apparent language-group difference. An environment with useful landmarks can encourage landmark-based coding, while a windowless laboratory can make viewer-relative descriptions more convenient. >> Manipulating landmark access within English speakers tests whether the behavior changes without changing the native language. || A **language comparison** can be confounded with a **setting comparison**, so the environment has to be considered before the whole difference is assigned to linguistic structure. The critical contrast is access to spatial cues, because windows, outdoor views and familiar surroundings change which references are useful.
37|In the **blinds down** condition, **English speakers** overwhelmingly used relative position in this task, just like the Dutch speakers in earlier studies. With the blinds up and a view of the street, or outdoors, the preference for relative position was less pronounced. || **Li and Gleitman** found that English speakers' preference for relative coding weakened when external landmarks were available. The manipulation changed the spatial information while keeping the language group the same, so task context can influence the response. >> This challenges an explanation in which language alone fixes the frame that people choose. || The relative preference became weaker, which is not a complete switch to absolute coding.
38|**Levinson and colleagues** were not impressed. They argued that **Li and Gleitman** made it too transparent that they were testing something about rotation, because the task reduced memory load and the time and distance between stimulus and test. || Li and Gleitman's participants were Ivy League students, and students like these are very good at working out what the experimenter wants and then giving it. >> Participants who infer what is being tested can change their response for reasons other than habitual spatial coding. || This is a **demand characteristic** explanation, in which the participants' reading of the experiment influences the response apart from their normal coding preference. The response focuses on memory load and task transparency, rather than denying that landmarks can ever matter.
39|The research question is whether there is **categorical perception** for these dimensions in sign language. Categorical perception can be tested by varying a sign feature in small steps, while the observer's identification and discrimination responses are measured. || The **hand-configuration continuum** moves between forms. One continuum crosses a **phonemic** distinction between the signs PLEASE and SORRY, while the **allophonic** comparison changes hand configuration without the same change in sign identity. >> The test separates a physical change from a linguistically meaningful category change, because both continua vary visually but only one crosses the relevant contrast. || A sharp **identification boundary**, with better discrimination near that boundary, would indicate category-sensitive perception. Comparing equal-sized steps within and across a linguistic boundary is more informative than comparing only the two endpoints.
40|**Deaf signers** show a sharper categorical boundary for phonemic changes than for allophonic changes. In the phonemic condition, identification shifts steeply between the fourth and sixth stimuli, and discrimination is strongest at that same boundary. || In the allophonic condition, the identification function is much shallower, so many more stimuli are ambiguous, and there is no peak in the discrimination function. Comparable physical steps are easier to tell apart when they cross the category boundary. >> The steep identification shift combined with the discrimination peak supports **categorical perception**, because identification alone would give a less complete test.
41|**Campbell and colleagues** compared brain activity during sign language comprehension with activity during spoken language comprehension. Deaf signers, hearing signers and hearing participants who watched audiovisual speech were included, and the comparison found both shared features and differences. || Sign comprehension and speech comprehension both show **left lateralization** and recruit the classical language areas, namely the inferior frontal region known as Broca's area and the posterior superior temporal region known as Wernicke's area. >> Similarity does not require identical activation everywhere. || Signing also recruits more **visual areas** and draws less on auditory areas, and it engages **superior parietal** regions that are linked to spatial processing. The extra visual and spatial activity reflects the demands of processing a signed signal, and hearing signers are useful because signing experience is not limited to people who are deaf.
42|Sign language has **phonological structure**, and native signers show **categorical perception** for some contrastive features. Learning a signed language also causes some **brain reorganization**, particularly in the **lateralization of motion processing**. || Attention to the visual periphery matters for both hearing and deaf signers, but only deaf signers show enhanced processing of visual attention in the periphery. Compared with non-language stimuli that also involve organized, sequenced hand movements, signed sentences activate posterior superior temporal cortex, which is Wernicke's area. >> Signed languages therefore have linguistic form, grammar and language-related neural organization. || A coherent account has to separate shared language organization from **sensory adaptations**. Neither the view that sign is just gesture nor the view that modality makes no difference captures this evidence, and the findings jointly show that linguistic experience and sensory history contribute in different ways.
43|**Johnson and Newport** asked which of two versions of the **critical period** hypothesis their data fit better. The **exercise hypothesis** says that early language use preserves language-learning capacity, while the **maturational-state hypothesis** allows capacity to decline despite early use. || Both versions assume an early advantage, but they differ in what early successful experience protects. Later second-language difficulty in people who already acquired a first language is more directly compatible with maturation than with complete preservation through exercise. >> A late learner has already exercised language capacity, so the difficulty that late learners show favors the maturational-state version. || Does this change the interpretation of the first-language data? It does not make early first-language experience unimportant. Early access and age-related change address related but distinct questions, and the exercise account allows preservation through use while the maturational-state account predicts decline with maturation itself.
44|**Mayberry and colleagues** compared later language learning after different early language histories. Many deaf children do not get sign language input but have an otherwise typical upbringing. || Children who first encountered American Sign Language between the ages of nine and fifteen had real difficulty learning it when it was their first language. A comparison group that went deaf after first learning a spoken language did much better, and these differences persisted into adulthood. >> Deaf individuals without accessible early language performed worse than those who had acquired an early spoken or signed language. || Age of starting ASL is not the same as age of first language access. The comparison separates late first-language learning from late acquisition of an additional language, and earlier linguistic experience can support learning a new language later.
45|A possible confound is whether the **blocking effect** arises at the level of **visual object recognition**. Two responses address this question. First, the objects were **normed**, which means rated so that objects in the same category were not more visually similar to one another than objects in different categories on average. || Second, a control experiment blocked visual similarity along, and the blocking effect was observed in the first block of trials. The visual manipulation therefore affected early trials, while the semantic pattern of interest developed differently across repetitions. >> So the effects in the first experiment are unlikely to be due to visual similarity. || A control is informative when it tests the alternative mechanism directly. Matching appearance and manipulating visual similarity provide complementary evidence, and the difference in time course helps tell visual recognition apart from the proposed lexical contribution.
46|**Hermens and colleagues** found no effect when people saw several objects at the same time, so perhaps something is different about seeing objects in serial. **Belke and colleagues** addressed this by having participants view displays of four items at once while producing their names in serial order. || Showing all the objects at once reduces dependence on a succession of isolated pictures, but serial naming still requires selecting and producing one label after another. This separates display timing from naming order, which helps locate the source of interference. >> Simultaneous visibility does not remove the need for sequential lexical selection. **Eye tracking** adds timing information about how each object was processed.
47|Participants' eye movements were measured while they named the objects. **Gaze duration** records how long the eyes stay on an object, and **eye-speech lag** is the interval between leaving an object and beginning its spoken name. || A participant can move the eyes toward the next object before saying the current one, so these two measures capture overlapping parts of preparation. Different blocking effects on the two measures can help constrain which processing stage is affected. >> One total naming time can hide several processes. Coordinating eye and speech measures reveals where a condition begins to change performance, because looking at an object and speaking its name are not the same event.
48|**Semantic blocking** increases gaze duration only after the first presentation cycle. Overall speech duration decreases over the course of the experiment, but it shows no blocking effect. || There is no effect on **eye-speech lag**. The blocking effect numerically seems to go in the opposite direction, but this is not significant, so it should not be reported as evidence for a reversed effect. || Gaze duration is linked to **lemma selection**, while the lag is linked to **phonological encoding**. The location of the effect across measures matters more than a general statement that naming is slower, because the time course and selectivity constrain the proposed mechanism. >> The gaze pattern supports a contribution at lemma selection rather than at articulation.
49|The question is whether phoneme identification for word recognition works more like **thresholded** processing, labeled lumber mill, or **cascaded** processing, labeled food web. **McMurray and colleagues** tested identification of a /b/ and /p/ continuum in a visual world display, with eye movements recorded. || Participants heard the continuum while viewing pictures including BEAR and PEAR. **Voice onset time**, or VOT, is the delay between releasing the consonant and the start of voicing, and it can change how strongly the sound supports /b/ or /p/. >> A thresholded account would pass a completed categorical decision onward. || A **cascaded** account allows graded information to influence word candidates before uncertainty is fully resolved. The critical test is whether within-category differences influence lexical responses, because a binary final choice alone may hide that information. The display includes a target, a phonetic competitor and unrelated fillers, and looking at the competitor is informative because its name overlaps with the heard word.
50|Participants used the mouse to select the image most consistent with what they heard, such as BEAR or PEAR, and their choices produced a typical step-like **identification function**. Shorter VOTs favored the /b/ response and longer VOTs favored the /p/ response. || Near the boundary, the preferred category changes rapidly, so the steep transition marks where the choice flips. >> A forced choice can end in one category even when processing used graded evidence, which is why eye movements provide a separate measure.
51|Eye movements reveal that ambiguity is **gradient**. Looks to the competitor are higher for sounds close to the boundary between /b/ and /p/, so there are more looks to the competitor close to the boundary. || A participant can ultimately select BEAR while spending more time looking at PEAR when the sound is close to the boundary. This supports the claim that fine phonetic information stays relevant to lexical activation, instead of disappearing after a categorical phoneme decision. >> Final identification and moment-by-moment competition reveal different aspects of processing, so a sharp choice boundary can coexist with graded lexical uncertainty. || The gaze curves group trials by VOT and final response, and the effect concerns looks to the unchosen competitor, not merely how often participants click the wrong image.
52|**Competitor fixation** increases as a token approaches the category boundary from either side. For BEAR responses, a more /p/-like VOT increases looks to PEAR, and for PEAR responses, a more /b/-like VOT increases looks to BEAR. || The two line types separate early and late observation windows, so they distinguish time windows rather than two groups of participants. The gradient remains visible in the later competition measure, which supports continued sensitivity to within-category phonetic detail. >> The result favors **cascaded** use of graded evidence over a model that passes on only a completed phoneme label. || The relevant predictor is the **distance from the boundary**, not only which side of the boundary a token occupies, so similar final choices can still have different lexical-competition profiles.
