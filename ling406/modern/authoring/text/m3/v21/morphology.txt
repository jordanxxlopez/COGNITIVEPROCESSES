2|**Morphology**, the way words are built from meaningful parts, is **generative**, because English speakers can form new words they never memorized one by one. || At one point there was no noun "generative," just the verb "to generate," but English has a process for turning verbs into nouns, and that process is **derivational morphology**. >> **Inflectional morphology** adds grammatical information to an existing word, and in English it pretty much means the **plural** and the **past tense**. || Extending a pattern can even produce playful forms, as in "She found me crying and she crew too and we both crode." >> Crew and crode are not standard past tenses of cry, but they come from extending irregular-sounding patterns to a new verb, so they reveal **productive use of patterns** rather than recall of forms someone heard.
3|**Jean Berko Gleason** ran the classic **wug test**, which measures how readily people extend an inflection to a word they have never heard. || A child sees a drawing of one creature labeled "This is a WUG," then sees two of them and has to finish the sentence "There are two" with the right word. >> Saying **wugs** means the child applied plural knowledge to a brand-new noun, and because the word is new, the answer cannot be a memorized form. || The study reveals how readily people generalize the **past tense** to new words, and children do this too, which is why the test uses cute drawings. >> Success reveals **productive knowledge**, which is the ability to apply a learned pattern to new items.
4|The English **past tense** has **regular** forms, which add -ed, and **irregular** forms, which change in their own way. || Regular verbs include show and showed, bake and baked, and wade and waded, and new verbs get the same ending, so blick becomes blicked, trog becomes trogged and wumble becomes wumbled. >> Because those nonwords were never heard before, they test whether a speaker can **extend** the regular pattern. || Irregular verbs include go and went, take and took, eat and ate, and sleep and slept. >> Any theory of morphology has to explain both these **exceptions** and the **productive extension** of the pattern to novel verbs.
5|**Pinker and colleagues** proposed the **Words and Rules** account, in which words and rules are **separate processes** with separate representational formats. || **Regular** past tenses come from a **symbolic rule** such as "add -ed," so the rule can apply to any new verb without a stored entry for its past tense. || **Irregular** past tenses are **lexicalized**, meaning stored as individual words, inside an **associative network** that links each verb to its own past form. >> So remembered exceptions and productive rule use count as two different kinds of knowledge, not as one system.
6|**Pinker and Ullman** proposed a **dual route** model of the past tense, in which a word stem such as walk or hold, plus a grammatical feature such as past tense, feeds two routes. || The **lexicon** route uses lookup and association in **declarative memory** to retrieve an irregular such as held, and it is tied to temporo-parietal cortex. >> The **grammar** route uses combination in the **procedural system** to build walk plus -ed, and it is tied to frontal cortex and basal ganglia, while a **blocking** link lets a retrieved irregular stop the regular form. || It is similar to the dual-route model of reading because both split **word-specific knowledge** from a **productive general operation**, but it differs because reading converts spelling into sound, while this model changes a stem to mark a grammatical feature.
7|The past-tense model and the dual-route model of reading have a **similar architecture** but **different origins**. || The dual-route model of reading was developed from **neuropsychological double dissociations**, in which one patient loses one ability while another patient loses the opposite one. >> Pinker and colleagues were motivated by other concerns. || **Generative linguistics** of the time was mostly about **categorical, symbolic, rule-based grammars**, and these rules often have **exceptions**. >> So the Words and Rules model came from an argument about keeping grammar rules apart from exceptions, rather than from patient dissociations.
8|Rather than make the grammar **inelegant** by adding machinery to handle exceptions, linguists would often just leave the exceptions out of the grammar. || The **dual-process model** offers a way to handle the exceptions while keeping the grammar elegant, symbolic and categorical. >> Its second process has all the properties that the rule route does not have. || That process is **word-specific**, or lexical, it is **sensitive to frequency of occurrence**, and it is **sensitive to phonological similarity**, as in keep and kept, weep and wept, and leap and leapt. >> Because similar-sounding irregulars share a pattern, an exception can belong to a small family instead of standing completely alone.
9|**Rumelhart and McClelland** offered an alternative in which the past tense is **all associative learning**, with no separate rule. || Their network encodes the sound of the **root form** as **Wickelfeatures**, the model's features for sound patterns, and passes them through a **pattern associator** with **modifiable connections** to Wickelfeatures of the past tense, which are decoded back into sounds. >> The encoding and decoding networks are fixed, so all of the learning happens in the modifiable connections. || One mechanism learns both **regular** and **irregular** verbs, so regularity is a pattern picked up from many examples rather than a stored rule.
10|Rumelhart and McClelland built one of the first learning models of a language process, so they focused on capturing **developmental phenomena**, especially **U-shaped learning**. || Some frequent irregular verbs are learned quite early, so a young child correctly says ran. >> Then, spontaneously, the child starts producing **regularizations** such as runned, which apply the regular pattern to an irregular verb. >> Eventually the child starts producing the correct form ran again. || Learning is called U-shaped because accuracy goes down before it goes back up, so children often get worse before they get better.
11|U-shaped learning matters to the **Words and Rules** approach because that approach explains the dip as a series of **distinct stages and processes**. || Early correct forms are just **imitations**, so they do not depend on the same mechanisms as adult processing. >> When the rule is first learned, it is applied to all cases, because the rule is the **best hypothesis** for verbs in general, and this produces runned. || Over time, exceptions are learned **associatively**, one by one, and added to lexical knowledge, while the **blocking process** develops. >> Blocking lets the retrieved irregular stop the regular form, so the child returns to saying ran.
12|**Rumelhart and McClelland's** model also produces U-shaped learning, because its **training set** follows data about the input to children, which changes over time. || Early on, irregular verbs predominate, because the highest-frequency verbs tend to have irregular past tenses, such as GIVE, TAKE, HAVE and GO. >> As the vocabulary expands to include more regular forms, the model **reorganizes** to capture those regularities. || This reorganization **interferes** with some of the early-learned irregulars, so their correct responses weaken while regularized alternatives briefly grow stronger. >> With more training the correct irregular responses recover, and no rule component is ever added.
13|The model was trained on a **small number of verbs**, so its makers could run a **wug test with real words** that the model had never seen. || They gave it **unfamiliar low-frequency regular verbs** such as guard, kid, carp, shape and mail and recorded the past tenses it produced. >> A real English verb counts as novel for the model when it was left out of training. || The model generated most **Wickelfeatures** correctly for both regular and irregular verbs, with regulars somewhat higher. >> Getting most features right is not the same as producing a whole correct word, and some responses were odd forms such as membled for mail.
14|The model also made a few responses that **added or changed consonants**, which people rarely do. || These were maded as the past tense of mate, squawked for squat, membled for mail, and toureder for tour. >> The model's authors judged it unlikely that humans would make these errors, especially the last two, but noted that the responses were mostly **near threshold**. || They suggested that an **auto-associative network** among the output units could **clean up** the output pattern and filter out these **bizarre responses**. >> They had not yet implemented this suggestion, so the fix remained an untested proposal.
15|**Prince and Pinker** argued that a model built only from features cannot keep competing outputs from **blending** together. || In Rumelhart and McClelland's model, various subregularities and the regular rule all compete to activate **Wickelfeatures**, so the input sing can activate features for sing, sang, sung, singed and sanged all at once. >> The model never puts out any single word, only an unordered collection of features, many of them contradictory. || So it cannot avoid blending distinct competing outputs into **bizarre hybrids**, such as toureder as the past of tour or membled for mail.
16|**Joanisse and Seidenberg** built a newer model in which speech input, **semantics** and speech output are linked through learned connections. || Their premise is that **rule-like generalization** is supported by **phonological representations**, the sound patterns of words. >> So regular past tenses and new forms depend mainly on reliable sound knowledge. || **Irregular** forms require **greater support from semantics**, the meaning of the specific word, because their past tense does not follow the common sound pattern.
17|The **Joanisse and Seidenberg** model targets a **dissociation** between **irregular** and **nonword** past tense in **neuropsychological data**, where some patients do worse on one and other patients do worse on the other. || A nonword has no stored meaning, so its past tense depends on **phonological generalization**. >> A known irregular does not follow the common pattern, so it leans on **semantic** support. || So if damaging phonology produces one patient pattern and damaging semantics produces the other, a single system can explain both sides without separate rule and word routes.
18|**Broca's aphasia** follows injury to **left anterior** brain regions and brings **poor speech production**. || These patients have problems with **regular morphology**, especially **nonwords** such as wug and wugged or blick and blicked. >> Their nonword accuracy is clearly the lowest, while regulars and irregulars are both much higher, with irregulars slightly ahead. || A nonword has no stored past tense, so the patient must generate it from the sound pattern alone, which makes this condition the hardest.
19|When **phonology** is damaged in the model, its ability to compute past tense forms is **reduced**. || **Nonwords** become worse than both regulars and irregulars, and regulars are numerically worse than irregulars. >> This profile closely matches the Broca's patients across all three conditions. || So a broad **phonological impairment** can produce what looks like a selective loss of regular morphology, without removing any symbolic rule.
20|Other patients have **problems with irregulars**, including **Wernicke's** and **Alzheimer's** patients and **anomic** patients with injury to **inferior and middle temporal** brain regions. || These patients are poor on **irregular** past tenses, while their regular and nonword past tenses stay much higher. >> This is the reverse of the Broca's pattern, so together the two groups form the dissociation the model is aiming at. >> A theory then has to explain why known irregulars suffer in these patients when new forms do not.
21|When **semantics** is damaged in the model, **irregulars** become worse than regulars and nonwords, and nonwords are a bit worse than regulars. || The same profile appears in **Alzheimer's patients**, whose irregulars are also the lowest of the three types. >> Regular and new forms can still rely on the common sound pattern, so losing meaning hurts them less. || So **semantic impairment** produces the reverse of the phonological-damage profile, because irregulars depend more on meaning.
22|**Bird, Patterson, Lambon-Ralph, McClelland and Seidenberg** found that in **Broca's patients**, the difference between regulars and irregulars is **reduced** when items are adequately **controlled for phonology**. >> So part of the apparent regular deficit may come from sound differences between the items rather than from a lost rule. || **Patterson, Lambon-Ralph, Hodges and McClelland** found that in **semantic dementia** patients, the degree of impairment with irregulars **correlates strongly** with the severity of the **semantic deficit**. >> This link is what the phonology and semantics account predicts, because irregulars lean on meaning. || Both results raise **other issues with the double dissociation data**, which had been read as evidence for separate rule and word systems.
23|The past-tense account divides the work between phonology and semantics in the same way as the **triangle model** of dyslexia. || **Phonological representations** support **generalization**, which is a result of **statistical learning** about patterns in the mapping from present to past. >> So impaired phonological representations damage these generalizations. || **Semantic representations** support **idiosyncratic forms**, the ones that deviate from the overwhelmingly regular "add -ed" pattern and need support from meaning. >> Some irregular forms can take advantage of **sub-patterns** shared with similar verbs, so they are likely to be **spared**.
24|**Specific Language Impairment**, or SLI, is **impaired language development** in people whose nonverbal IQ and performance are in the normal range. || It affects grammar, including both **morphology** and **syntax**. >> It also affects **phonology**, **reading**, **working memory** and **speech perception**, which reach beyond grammar. || Because the problems are this broad, a theory that places the deficit only in grammatical rules has to explain these other weaknesses as well.
25|**Past tense deficits** are a **sine qua non** of SLI, meaning a feature the disorder is never without. || Earlier claims by **Gopnik, van der Lely, Pinker, Ullman and Clahsen** suggested that the deficit was **specific to rules**. >> On this view SLI could be evidence that language acquisition requires **domain-specific mechanisms**, ones used only for language. || A deficit in learning rules predicts **problems with regulars and nonwords, but not irregulars**, because irregulars are stored as words.
26|**Van der Lely and Ullman** compared past tense accuracy in an **SLI** group and **same-age controls** on regular verbs, irregular verbs and nonwords. || The SLI group was **low on all three**, with regulars slightly above irregulars and nonwords the lowest, while the controls scored far higher on every type. >> So irregulars were impaired too, rather than spared as a pure rule deficit predicts. || The result is a **broad deficit** with nonwords hardest, and an account has to explain that whole pattern.
27|A **developmental model** with **weakened phonology** learned past tenses less well across all three item types. || With a **severe phonological deficit** it was very poor, and with a **weaker phonological deficit** it did better, but in both cases regulars were highest, irregulars next and **nonwords lowest**. >> The **intact** model did much better, both at the end of training and at an earlier point in training. || So a phonological learning weakness reproduces the main SLI pattern, with no missing grammatical rule.
28|In the model, **phonology** affects the development of **all forms**, because every past tense, whether regular, irregular or new, has to be produced as sounds. || So weakening phonology lowers performance on regulars, irregulars and nonwords alike. >> Phonology is especially important to **nonwords**, because a new word has no item-specific learning and depends entirely on generalizing the sound pattern. || Familiar words have their own training history to partly support them, so they lose less when phonology is weak.
29|Like the **triangle model** of reading, this account has **no special rules** for the past tense, just **mappings between phonology and semantics**. || There is **no separate lexicon** that stores the exceptions, so the ideas of **rule** and **exception** only approximately describe the system. || The account rests on an **association between irregular forms and semantic processing** and an **association between wug test performance and phonological processing**. >> Each association links a type of past tense to the system the model says it depends on.
