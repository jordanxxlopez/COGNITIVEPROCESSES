2|**Dual-route models** of visual word recognition propose two reading routes because several reading findings seem to need two separate processes. **Pseudoword reading** (reading made-up letter strings) is fast and automatic, real **word reading** is faster, and **high-frequency words** are read fastest. >> Accuracy and response time also depend on **regularity**, and **frequency and regularity interact**. >> In **acquired dyslexia**, pseudoword reading and irregular word reading can be impaired separately, so the two abilities are **dissociable**. || The models therefore hypothesize a **sublexical route** that turns spelling into sound through **grapheme-to-phoneme conversion**, also called **decoding**, and a **lexical route** that recognizes real words. >> A **grapheme** is a written unit that is linked to a sound.
3|**Coltheart and colleagues** built the **Dual Route Cascaded model**, or **DRC**, which joins an interactive lexical route to a separate rule route. Its lexical portion is just the **Interactive Activation model**, with an **orthographic lexicon** for written word forms and a **phonological lexicon** for spoken word forms. || The DRC has **no implementation of semantics**, because both lexicons are independent of meaning. || The **Grapheme-Phoneme Rule System** runs on completely different processing assumptions from the interactive portion, so rule-based conversion and word-form activation are separate mechanisms. >> The rule path joins the output system instead of becoming another interactive layer of the lexicon.
4|The **GPC rules** (the grapheme-phoneme conversion rules) are **activated serially**, letter by letter, and they are **not affected by word frequency** in any way. They also receive **no feedback**, so nothing in the lexicon changes how a rule applies. || The GPC route does **not interact with the lexical route** except in the **phoneme response buffer**, where both routes connect to the same output units. >> Because they share those units, the two routes can **compete** to activate different pronunciations. >> For an exception word, the lexical route can push the correct pronunciation while the rules push a **regularized** one.
5|The GPC rules map written units onto phonemes, and the full rule list includes **multiletter rules** such as eigh and ough. It also includes **context-sensitive rules** that apply only next to certain letters, such as c before e or i. >> Each rule also states whether it applies at the beginning, middle, end or all positions of a word. || A rule list alone is not enough, because several rules can match the same letters. The model also needs **meta-rules** that decide when to apply rules at different levels of analysis. >> For instance, **larger-unit rules supersede smaller-unit rules**, so a multiletter rule wins over the rules for its single letters.
6|The GPC rules are **not totally independent of statistics**, because they are often based on the **most frequent pronunciation** for a letter or combination of letters. || Once chosen, though, the rules are **fixed and categorical**, so every rule behaves the same way during processing no matter how strong its support was. >> EIGH is pronounced /eI/ about two thirds of the time and /aI/ the rest, and the separate EIGH rule for spellings without a final T always gives /eI/. >> OUGH is spread over five pronunciations, so its rule has **weaker statistical support** than the EIGH rule, yet both work the same way as rules.
7|The DRC's rules are **kind of silly**, in the sense that they **miss a lot of cases**, but this was seen as a **feature, not a bug**. Better rules could be written by taking statistics into account more flexibly, and the DRC's rules did go through several revisions to improve its **nonword reading**. || The DRC argues that people **do not use finely tuned rules** for the GPC route. If they did, there would be no **regularization errors** in **surface dyslexia** and no **slow-down for low-frequency exception words**. >> So the simple rules are meant to reproduce the errors people really make, not to pronounce as many words correctly as possible.
8|The DRC borrows the idea of **informational encapsulation**, in which a process does not use every source of relevant knowledge, from models of syntax. There, parsing strategies such as **late closure** and **minimal attachment** were motivated by syntactic complexity only, so parsing was kept separate from world knowledge and lexical co-occurrence statistics. || The dual-route models are designed to be **similarly modular**, so the GPC route chugs along without consulting word knowledge. It produces correct pronunciations for many words and **incorrect responses to some exceptions**. >> Its operation becomes visible when people make **regularization errors** and when people are made to **name pseudowords**, because a pseudoword has no stored entry for the lexical route to supply.
9|The DRC can **simulate a lot of phenomena** in visual word recognition, across **reading aloud**, **lexical decision** and the **acquired dyslexias**. >> Reading aloud simulations include frequency, lexicality, regularity and consistency effects and the interaction of regularity with frequency. >> Acquired dyslexia simulations include exception, regular and nonword reading in surface dyslexia and pseudohomophone effects in phonological dyslexia. || **Coltheart and colleagues** built the architecture from a quarter century of reading research rather than from backpropagation, because they held that **human behavioral data** are the major source of constraint on such models. >> Covering many effects with one architecture is strong support, but it also matters which assumptions change between tasks.
10|Modeling **lexical decision**, which is deciding whether a letter string is a word, sometimes means going to **extremes to make contact with data**. The DRC first **reduces letter-word inhibition**, a change that makes the task harder but without which the results make no sense. || The model answers **yes** if any single word passes an activity threshold, or if **total activity across the orthographic input lexicon** passes a second threshold that drops when the lexicon is active early. >> It answers **no** if neither criterion is met by a **deadline** counted in processing cycles, and that deadline is **nearly twice as fast** as the model normally reads aloud. >> These thresholds and deadlines are extra decision assumptions added on top of the basic two-route model.
11|The **key feature of the DRC is modularity**, and the GPC route and lexical route are modular in three ways. Each route has its **own processing assumptions**, and the two routes are **informationally encapsulated** from one another, so neither uses the information inside the other. || The routes are also **domain-specific**, which means each one accepts only its own kind of input. >> The GPC rules can take only **letters as inputs**, and they can pass **phoneme activations** only to the phoneme system.
12|**Seidenberg and McClelland** took an **emergentist approach** with a **PDP model** of visual word recognition, a network that learns and stores knowledge spread across its connections. They treated **reading skill** as emerging from **mappings among spelling, pronunciation and meaning**, which are **orthography**, **phonology** and **semantics**. || They modeled how these mappings are learned by training a **simple associative network**. >> They studied only the mapping from **orthography to phonology**, called **O to P**, in a **single route** used for every kind of word. || So reading skill comes from learning, rather than from a hand-built list of rules and word entries.
13|The PDP model of **Seidenberg and McClelland** makes **different assumptions** from dual-route models. **Rules** are just **generalizations from lots of words**, not a separate process, and the **lexicon is distributed**, so some whole-word information is stored in the O to P mappings and some in other parts of the system. >> The model still holds knowledge of individual words, but that knowledge is spread across the network. || It also follows a **different research strategy**, because it explains reading through **domain-general learning mechanisms** instead of **reverse-engineering** the system from adult and neuropsychological data.
14|The PDP model captured the **frequency by regularity interaction** without assuming that frequency effects and regularity effects come from **different processes**. || This works because **regular words** are consistent with statistical patterns from many other words, while **exception words** are inconsistent with those patterns. >> Frequency also affects **how well each word is learned**, so **highly frequent words** are less affected by their inconsistency with other words. >> A low-frequency exception gets weak learning of its own and no help from the shared patterns, so it is the hardest case to read.
15|The PDP account redefines **regularity** in terms of **consistency**, which depends on a word's **friends**, similar spellings with the same pronunciation, and its **enemies**, similar spellings with a competing pronunciation. || **Regular consistent** words such as LATE and DUST have many friends and no enemies. **Regular inconsistent** words such as GAVE and MINT have many friends but at least one enemy, such as HAVE for GAVE and PINT for MINT. >> **Ambiguous** words such as DOWN and KNOWN, or POUR and SOUR, have about equal numbers of friends and enemies. >> **Exception** words such as HAVE and PINT have few if any friends and many enemies.
16|In principle, a PDP model reads **words and nonwords in the same route**. **Nonwords** are named through the **same set of weights** used for words, so no separate rule module and no stored entry for the nonword is needed. || **Generalization** to a new spelling takes advantage of the **same statistical patterns** that give rise to **regularity effects**. >> A new spelling overlaps with familiar spellings, so the learned spelling-to-sound patterns carry over to it.
17|In practice, the early PDP model was **not so great**, because its **nonword accuracy was not very good**. This is a **classic case of ambiguity in model evaluation**, because a failed simulation does not say what caused the failure. || The failure could mean the **theoretical claim** was wrong, the claim that nonwords and exceptions can be read by the **same associative mechanism**. >> Or the problem could be **specific to the way the model was made**. || Later work pointed to the second answer, because the model's **phonological representations** were poor and better ones improved nonword reading.
18|Many studies find a **relationship between decoding**, meaning nonword reading, **and phonological processing**. || The phonological representations in the model of **Seidenberg and McClelland** were sort of weird, because they used **Wickelfeatures**. >> A model by **Plaut and colleagues** **improved the phonological representations** and produced **better performance on nonwords**. || So at least part of the earlier failure came from how the model was made, and decoding improved without adding a rule system.
19|Phonological representations matter for **nonword generalization** because **generalization depends on similarity**, a principle inherited from **behaviorism**. **Poor phonological representations**, such as Wickelfeatures, fail to capture the **similarity space** of words in the language, so learning cannot transfer well to new forms. || **Word-specific information** is **more robust** to poor representations, because a familiar word does not depend entirely on generalization. >> A nonword has no training history of its own, so it suffers most when similarity is captured badly.
20|**Harm and Seidenberg** tested the link between phonology and nonword generalization more systematically by **manipulating the amount of noise** in the model's phonological representations. More noise made the sound representations less reliable. || The simulations **replicated key findings on developmental dyslexia**. >> The **degree of phonological deficit** was related to the degree of **phonological dyslexia**, which is poor nonword decoding. >> **Word recognition was relatively spared**, because familiar words lean less on generalization. || So a selective reading deficit can emerge from degraded phonology, without any separate nonword mechanism.
21|The PDP account also has to explain the **double dissociations** in acquired dyslexia. Nonword reading can already be **specifically impaired** in these models, even though they have **no nonword reading mechanism**. || **Plaut and colleagues** explored the other end of the dissociation, **surface dyslexia**. They implemented a **simplified semantic system** and then **lesioned** it, meaning they damaged that part of the model. >> The lesions to semantics produced a **specific impairment on exception words**, because exceptions get little support from spelling-sound patterns and lean on meaning.
22|The triangle model is **not just the dual-route model renamed**. **Semantic representations are directly involved**, whereas the DRC is committed to a lexicon independent of semantics. >> A later model by **Harm and Seidenberg** implemented featural semantics and simulated the mapping of spelling to meaning. || The **O to P** and **S to P** mappings (spelling to sound and meaning to sound) share the **same processing assumptions and learning algorithm**, and both are **probabilistic**. || The pathways **interact over the course of learning**, so the O to P pathway develops differently alongside an O to S to P pathway. >> This **interactivity and gradedness** let the models capture that double dissociations are **never entirely clean**. Phonological dyslexics are not perfect on exception words, and surface dyslexics are not perfect on nonwords.
23|**Woollams and colleagues** tested the model's prediction of a **strong association** between **semantic abilities** and **exception word reading**. They compared simulations that varied the strength of semantic activation with **semantic dementia** patients who varied in a composite semantic score. || In both the models and the patients, **weaker semantics went with poorer low-frequency exception reading**. >> The relation was tighter in the models than in the patients, whose scores were more spread out. || So exception word reading tracks semantic ability, as the triangle account predicts.
24|The association still **misses a few data points**. A few patients are **statistical outliers**, because their low-frequency exception reading stays very high, in some cases despite reduced semantic scores. || **Woollams and colleagues** would argue that these points should be treated the way **outliers are normally treated** when evaluating the fit of a model to data. >> A graded association does not predict zero variability, so a few unusual patients do not by themselves overturn it.
25|Because **semantic dementia** is a **progressive condition**, Woollams and colleagues could follow how exception word reading changed **as semantic abilities declined**. || **In most cases**, when a patient's semantic abilities declined over time, **her reading ability also declined**. >> Patients with multiple observations also showed **regression to the mean**, moving back toward the overall relation on retesting. >> If the dissociation between semantics and exception word reading came from **idiosyncratic outliers**, it would show just this pattern, so the unusual scores look like noise rather than a separate pattern.
26|**Coltheart and colleagues** simulated the same surface dyslexia data with the DRC. They generated a population of **forty thousand model patients** varying on two parameters, the **integrity of the lexical route** and the **integrity of the GPC rules**. || For each patient in the Woollams data, they **selected the model run that best fit** that patient's data. >> In this way they produced an **excellent fit to the data**, but the fit came from choosing among many possible settings after seeing each patient.
27|Someone could argue that this **better fit is actually a bad thing** because it relies on **much greater flexibility**. Picking the best match from forty thousand model patients can reproduce each patient without the model **predicting** the relation beforehand. || The triangle model predicted the link between semantics and exception reading from its own assumptions. >> So **goodness of fit** is only one part of evaluating a model, and it matters how much freedom produced the fit.
28|**Dual-route** and **triangle models** both cover a range of reading data, including some basic facts. **Decoding is fast and automatic**, as in pseudoword reading, and words that **diverge from typical spelling-to-sound mappings** are harder to read. >> This **regularity** or **consistency effect** interacts with **word frequency**. >> **Pseudoword reading** and **exception or inconsistent word reading** are dissociable in neuropsychological patients. || The models agree on these findings but differ on the mechanisms that produce them.
29|The two models give different explanations for each phenomenon. For **pseudoword reading and regularity effects**, the dual-route model uses a dedicated **GPC rule system**, while the triangle model uses **statistical regularities** learned over many words. >> For **frequency effects**, the dual-route model uses **distinct input and output lexicons** separate from the GPC rules, while in the triangle model **more exposure to a word** gives better learning for that word. || For **phonological dyslexia**, the dual-route model points to damage in the **GPC route**, while the triangle model points to damage in **phonological processing generally**. >> For **surface dyslexia**, the dual-route model points to damage in the **lexical route**, while the triangle model points to damage in **semantic processes generally**.
30|The **key contrast** is dissociations against associations. **Dual-route models** are generally motivated by **double dissociations**, so because pseudowords and exception words can be impaired independently, they are modeled as **independent processes**. || **Statistical learning models** instead reveal **associations** between reading and broader language abilities. >> **Phonology** is important for learning and using O to P generalizations, which links it to phonological dyslexia. >> **Semantics** is important for words where O to P generalization is **ambiguous**, which links it to surface dyslexia.
