2|**Phonemes** are speech-sound categories, not letters. A writing system represents a language, but its symbols do not have to match those categories one to one. >> A single letter can take part in different pronunciations. >> A single phoneme can be written with different spellings. || So the first thing to remember about phonemes is that **phonemes are not letters**. >> Identifying a letter is a different task from identifying a phoneme. >> The question is about how sound relates to writing. It is not about how many letters a word has. || That is why spoken categories and written symbols are kept separate.
3|A **mispronouncing contest** tested whether letters and phonemes line up. In it, people willfully mispronounced words such as COLONEL and PARADIGM. >> Reading COLONEL by its usual letter patterns gives a wrong pronunciation. >> Reading PARADIGM the same way also gives a wrong pronunciation. >> So phonemes are not letters, and **letters are not phonemes** either. || **Orthographic depth** is how consistently a script's spelling matches its pronunciation. >> For **English**, many spellings do not clearly specify their sounds. So English is a relatively **deep** script, because of its irregularities. >> For **Finnish** and **Korean**, spelling matches sound very predictably. So they are very **shallow** scripts. >> **Pinyin** for Mandarin and **French** are also relatively regular. || A deep script is less predictable, but it is not random. The difference concerns the writing system, not the ability of its readers. That is why an unfamiliar English spelling cannot always be decoded from its letters alone.
4|**Finnish** has a very **transparent** writing system. The **Orthographic Depth Hypothesis** calls this a **shallow** system. >> Each letter matches one phoneme more or less unambiguously. >> Learning one letter-sound match helps with many different words. >> Listening to a beginning Finnish lesson while reading its text makes this clear within about a minute. || **Doubled letters** mark a difference in length. >> A doubled consonant stands for a **geminated** consonant, which is a consonant held longer. >> A doubled vowel stands for a **long vowel**. || **Finnish children** learn to **decode** very quickly. Decoding means producing a pronunciation from a written form. >> The matches are consistent, so children can apply them to written forms they have never seen. || So a transparent script makes decoding fast to learn.
5|**English** is an **outlier** among languages that use the Roman alphabet. One result is that children learn to **decode** more slowly in English. || **Aro and Wimmer** compared children learning to read in several languages. The children read **pseudowords** aloud. Pseudowords are made-up words that follow the spelling and sound patterns of their language. >> A pseudoword is not a stored familiar word. So reading it tests decoding. >> In **Grade 1**, children learning French, German, Dutch, Spanish, Swedish or Finnish read about 85 to 93 percent accurately. >> In Grade 1, **English** learners read only about **half** of the pseudowords accurately. >> English learners improve more slowly and reach about 88 percent by Grade 4. || So English decoding develops more slowly. That is because English spelling-sound matches are less predictable. The result does not mean that English children cannot decode.
6|**Korean** writing, called **Hangul**, is even more regular than Finnish. In Hangul, the symbols for similar phonemes look similar to one another. >> Letters are grouped into **syllable blocks**. >> Each block has an **initial** position, a **medial** position and sometimes a **final** position. || The written shapes reflect how the sounds are organized. >> For **velar** consonants, the shared feature is the basic angular shape ㄱ. Velar consonants are made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate. >> The letter ㄱ is g, the letter ㄲ is kk and the letter ㅋ is k. >> Doubling the shape gives kk. Adding a stroke gives k. || So the **angular ㄱ shape** corresponds roughly to velar place of articulation. That is why Hangul shows sound structure in the letter shapes themselves.
7|Reading **pseudowords** aloud puts **decoding** into action. GLORP, SPLING, JONK, FEESH and QUISP are not English words. || A reader can still pronounce each one. >> Each item follows enough English spelling patterns to build a **plausible pronunciation**. >> The reader has no stored pronunciation for these items. So success cannot come from memory of each word. >> The reader uses knowledge that **generalizes** across words. || The items test decoding, not vocabulary or word meanings. On its own, this does not decide between categorical rules and learned statistical patterns. So reading a new letter string reveals knowledge beyond memorized words.
8|**Lupker, Brown and Colombo (1997)** had people read items aloud and measured **naming time**. Naming time is how quickly a pronunciation becomes available. >> They compared pseudowords, here called **nonwords**, with real words. || Nonwords were read **nearly as quickly and accurately** as real words. >> In this example, nonword naming times were only **5 to 12 percent slower** than for low-frequency words. >> The comparison is with low-frequency words, not with all real words. || So people do decoding fairly **naturally**, **automatically** and **implicitly**. >> One open question is how decoding should be characterized. >> Computational models of word reading ask whether it is best captured by **categorical rules** or **probabilistic constraints**. || The speed result alone does not pick between rules and probabilistic constraints. That is why both kinds of model must explain fast nonword naming.
9|Some pseudowords have **more than one plausible pronunciation**. VONTH, PHANGE, STREAD and SOUGH are examples. || Each item contains a spelling pattern that English pronounces in more than one way. >> A reader answers using spelling-sound patterns learned from familiar words. >> Different familiar words can favor different answers for the same new item. >> So these items have no single agreed pronunciation. || These items test how readers handle **ambiguity** in the mapping from spelling to sound. So generalization can be **graded** rather than all-or-none.
10|**Generalization** is a good way to test what regularities people have learned about a system. A new item cannot have been memorized, so the answer reveals the learned pattern. >> The **Wug Test** is another example of testing generalization. || STREAD can be read as /strɛd/ or as /strid/. >> Readers accept **both** pronunciations. >> The two readings differ only in the vowel. The rest of STREAD stays the same. || It is hard to imagine a **rule set** that covers this intuition. >> A **categorical rule** gives one output. So it must explain why two outputs feel acceptable. >> A **statistical account** can give each pronunciation a different amount of support. || So accepting both readings reveals the kind of knowledge being generalized.
11|The **regularity effect** is that **irregular** words are harder to recognize than **regular** words. Irregular words are also called **inconsistent** words. >> It is uncontroversial that some form of decoding is part of normal reading. || For some words the case is straightforward. >> PINT, ACHE and CHOIR do not follow the usual spelling-sound patterns. So decoding them by those patterns gives the wrong pronunciation. || For **neighborhoods** of words the case is less clear-cut. A neighborhood is a set of words that share a spelling pattern. >> In ROLL, POLL, TOLL and DOLL, three words share one pronunciation and DOLL differs. >> In COOK, TOOK, BOOK and SPOOK, three words share one pronunciation and SPOOK differs. || So a word's difficulty depends partly on how its spelling neighbors are pronounced. That is why regularity is better treated as how much support a pronunciation gets from similar words.
12|**Sublexical information** is information about units smaller than a whole word. Readers use it heavily, especially to translate spelling into sound. >> People read **nonwords** quickly and accurately. >> Words that follow typical spelling-to-sound patterns are easier to read than words that do not. || **Lexical information** is knowledge of particular words. >> Nonwords test generalization from spelling patterns. >> Differences among familiar words can reveal word-specific knowledge. || So general decoding explains only part of reading. That is why **lexical processes** also need an account.
13|The **Interactive Activation Model** of **McClelland and Rumelhart (1981)** links features, letters and words. Visual features activate letters. Letters activate candidate words such as WEAR, WORK, WORD and WEAK. >> Active word candidates send **feedback** to the letters they contain. || In a word perception example, blots cover parts of the letters in WORK. >> The visible features of the last letter fit K and R, but not D. >> Of those letters, only K makes a known word, WORK. So WORK sends feedback to K. || Over time, activation for **K** rises close to the maximum. >> Activation for **R** rises a little and then falls back. >> Activation for **D** stays low. || So **word knowledge helps identify an ambiguous letter**. Recognition builds up over time rather than happening at once.
14|In the **Interactive Activation Model**, **feedback** from known words raises activation for the letters in those words. >> A partly active word candidate sends activation back down to its letters. >> Letter-to-word activation gives evidence for a word. >> Word-to-letter feedback supports that word's letters. || So recognition is **interactive**. Information does not flow only upward from features to a finished word. >> Recognizing a word and recognizing its letters support each other. || That is why an ambiguous letter is **easier to recognize inside a familiar word**.
15|Two **lexical effects** have already come up. Both reflect experience with particular words. >> The **lexicality effect** is that real words are named faster than nonwords. >> The **word frequency effect** is that words met more often are named faster. >> In one naming study, high-frequency words took about 442 ms and low-frequency words about 482 ms. Nonwords took about 541 to 552 ms. || As with **picture naming**, the number of past encounters with a word affects how fast it can be named. || **Balota and colleagues (2004)** used data from the **English Lexicon Project**. >> Naming time drops steadily as word frequency rises. >> The effect is **robust** over a very large number of observations. || So familiarity with a particular word speeds naming, beyond what general spelling patterns give.
16|Frequency effects might be **confounds** of word length and other variables. A confound is another variable that changes along with frequency and could cause the same effect. >> Frequent words could, for example, also be shorter. || A **multiple regression analysis** tested this. Multiple regression measures how much each predictor explains reaction time while the other predictors are included. >> The analysis used about 3000 words read by 30 young and older adults. >> The tasks were **lexical decision** and **naming**. Lexical decision is judging whether a letter string is a real word. >> Predictors included sound features, length, frequency, neighborhood size and consistency. || **Objective** and **subjective frequency** still predicted reaction time with the other variables included. >> Their coefficients were **negative** for both age groups and both tasks. So higher frequency went with **faster** responses. || So the frequency effect is **not just a confound** of length.
17|The **frequency by regularity interaction** is a landmark finding in psycholinguistics. The **frequency effect** is much larger for **exception words** than for **regular words**. >> Exception words have a pronunciation that breaks the usual spelling pattern. >> **Naming latency** is the time needed to begin saying a word. || For **high-frequency** words, exception and regular words are both named in about 540 ms. For **low-frequency** words, exception words take about 583 ms. Regular words take only about 557 ms. || An **interaction** means the effect of one factor depends on the other factor. >> The regularity cost is not a fixed amount added to every word. >> It is small for frequent words and large for rare words. || So the finding means that experience matters most when pronunciation conflicts with the usual spelling pattern. It has influenced other areas, most immediately **inflectional morphology**.
18|One interpretation says word recognition has **two separate processes**. This is the **dual-route** account. >> The **sublexical process** is decoding, which builds a pronunciation from spelling units. >> The **lexical process** is knowledge of a particular word, which supplies its learned pronunciation. || Each kind of item depends on the processes differently. >> For **high-frequency (HF)** words, the lexical process is so fast and efficient that the sublexical process has little influence. >> For **low-frequency (LF)** words, the lexical process is less efficient. So the sublexical process shows up. >> For **nonwords**, there is no stored word. So the sublexical process shows up there too. || For an LF exception word, decoding gives a regularized pronunciation. That output competes with the correct lexical one, so naming slows. || So two routes explain why regularity matters most for less familiar words. This is one interpretation of the interaction, not the interaction itself.
19|**Acquired phonological dyslexia** is reading difficulty caused by brain injury. It is called **acquired**, as opposed to **developmental** dyslexia, because it follows an injury. >> These patients have difficulty specifically with **pseudoword naming**. || **Cacciopo van Vliet and colleagues (2003)** tested two patients, M.O. and I.B. Both read real words and nonwords aloud. >> For **real words**, both patients were mostly correct, at about 85 to 100 percent. >> This held for concrete and abstract words, for every word class and for high and low frequency. >> For **nonwords**, plausible responses were much lower, often under half. >> Nonwords with low **grapheme-to-phoneme correspondence** were hardest, at about 15 to 20 percent. A grapheme is a written unit that stands for a sound. || So **familiar-word reading can survive while decoding of new forms fails**. The deficit is relative, not total.
20|**Acquired surface dyslexia** is also reading difficulty after brain injury. Here the difficulty is specifically with **irregular words**. >> The mispronunciations tend to be **regularizations**. A regularization reads a word by the common spelling-sound pattern. || DEAF is read as “deef”, SAID as “sayed” and BROAD as “brode”. >> SHOE is read as “show”, ONE as “own” and FLOOD as “flude”. >> GONE is read as “goan”, STEAK as “steek”, SEW as “sue” and SOOT as “suit”. || Two patients, MP and KT, read regular words, exception words and nonwords. >> For **regular words**, both were accurate, at about 89 to 100 percent. >> For **nonwords**, both were accurate, at about 95 to 100 percent. >> For **exception words**, accuracy dropped most for low-frequency items. KT scored 47 percent on high-frequency and 26 percent on low-frequency exceptions. MP scored 93 and 73 percent. || So the errors are **systematic**. The patients apply typical patterns to words that are exceptions.
21|Two patient groups show **opposite patterns** after brain injury. This is a classic **double dissociation**. || In **acquired surface dyslexia**, the difficulty is specifically with irregular words. >> The mispronunciations tend to be regularizations. >> **Pseudoword reading** is relatively spared. || In **acquired phonological dyslexia**, the difficulty is specifically with pseudoword naming. >> **Exception word reading** is relatively spared. || In a double dissociation, one group is impaired on one task but not another. The other group shows the reverse. >> Relatively spared does not mean perfect. || So the two patterns together are hard to explain by saying one task is simply harder for everyone.
22|Double dissociations are powerful because the **impairment reverses** across patients. Suppose task A were just harder than task B. Then damage would hurt A more in every patient. >> That could not explain another patient who shows the reverse pattern. || In reading, **phonological dyslexia** hurts pseudowords more. **Surface dyslexia** hurts exception words more. >> So pseudowords and exception words place different demands on the reading system. || The reversal points to **separable processes** behind the two tasks. >> First, each patient's relative costs are compared. Then the two profiles are compared. >> Two low scores without an opposite pattern are not a double dissociation. || So the evidence is strong because it rules out a single ranking of difficulty. It still needs a theory of what the processes are.
23|Double dissociations provide evidence that the processes behind **pseudoword** and **irregular word** reading are **separable**. **Morton's logogen model**, from **Morton and Patterson (1980)**, locates where damage could occur. >> **Logogens** are stored word units. Visual input logogens recognize written words. Output logogens supply their spoken forms. >> **Grapheme-phoneme conversion** turns letters into sounds without using a stored word. || In this model, each dyslexia comes from different damage. >> **Phonological dyslexia** results from specific damage to the **grapheme-phoneme conversion module**. Nonwords have no stored word, so they are read poorly. >> **Surface dyslexia** results from damage to **input or output logogens**, or the connections between them. Exception words then rely on conversion, so they come out regularized. || These are theoretical components, not a map of brain regions. So the model turns the observed dissociation into claims about damaged components.
24|**Patterson (1981)** gave early hints that the dual-route account is **not so simple**. In that account, a word is read either from its stored whole-word pronunciation or by **non-lexical grapheme-phoneme translation**. >> Surface dyslexia errors were thought to come from the non-lexical route. So they should show no lexical influences. || **Marcel (1980)** looked closely at the error data and found several **lexical influences**. >> **Neologisms**, which are made-up non-words, occur less often than a purely non-lexical process predicts. >> One patient read incense as “increase”. Part of the difficulty was the ambiguous consonant c. >> If that were the only factor, the patient should have produced the neologism “inkense”. || This poses a **classic dilemma** with two options. >> One option changes the view of surface dyslexia. It would reflect a more complicated combination of the two routes. >> The other option changes the view of non-lexical reading. It would be a more complicated route. || Models of normal reading ask the same question about non-words, as in **Glushko (1979)**. So Patterson argued that combining normal and patient evidence holds the best promise.
