2|**Signed languages** and **Braille** are really different cases. >> A **signed language** is a **natural language** with its own linguistic structure. >> **Braille** is a system that lets blind people read a spoken language they share with sighted people. >> So Braille is a way of reading a language, not a separate language. || The two cases are studied together for one reason. >> Both reveal the **plasticity** of sensory systems. Plasticity is the ability of the brain to change its organization with experience. >> Both reveal how the brain gets organized differently when language input comes from a different **modality**. A modality is the sense the input arrives through, such as vision or touch. || So the shared theme is modality. The linguistic status of the two cases still differs, and a comparison should keep that difference.
3|**Emmorey and colleagues (2004)** describe several obvious ways that **sign languages** differ from spoken languages. >> The first difference is **modality**. Sign languages are seen rather than heard. >> The second difference is the **articulators**, the body parts that produce the language. In sign, the hands, face and upper body produce it. >> The third difference is the **use of space**. Where a sign is made can carry linguistic relations. || The fourth difference is **iconicity**. A sign is iconic when its form resembles its meaning. >> Iconicity is **easy to exaggerate**. It seems strongest when you look at single signs with their glosses. >> A **gloss** is the translation word used to name a sign. Once you know the gloss, a resemblance looks obvious. >> A learner still has to learn the forms and structure of the language. || So sign languages differ from speech in surface form. They are still **structured languages**, not collections of transparent gestures.
4|**Fluent signing** coordinates several articulators at the same time. >> The **hands**, the **face** and the **upper body** move together. >> **Eye contact** is also part of the interaction. >> So the linguistic signal is spread across all of these, not carried by one hand at a time. || Fluent signing is also **fluid** and **fast**. >> Signs run into each other in a continuous stream. >> Can you segment the words? For a learner, the **word boundaries** are often not obvious. >> Fluent speech works the same way, because words in a spoken stream also run together. || That is why the thing to study is coordinated language production, not an isolated hand shape.
5|Like speech, **sign** can be described with **features**. This is the **phonology of sign language**. >> **Emmorey and colleagues (2003)** list three features that can differ **contrastively** between signs. >> **Hand configuration** is the shape the hand takes. >> **Location** is where the gesture is made. >> **Movement** is how the hand moves. || A feature is **contrastive** when changing it changes which sign it is. >> For **"please"** and **"sorry"**, the two signs are closely related forms with different meanings. They differ in **hand configuration**. >> For **"onion"** and **"apple"**, the two signs are also closely related forms with different meanings. >> In speech, changing one feature of a sound can change the word in the same way. || So phonology is about how contrastive form is organized. It is not only about sound.
6|The research question is whether signers show **categorical perception** for these sign features. >> **Categorical perception** means perceiving a smooth series of small changes as a few separate categories. >> It shows up as a sharp identification boundary and better discrimination near that boundary. || **Emmorey and colleagues (2003)** built two **continua** of hand configurations. Each continuum steps through intermediate hand shapes. >> The **phonemic** continuum runs from the sign **PLEASE** to the sign **SORRY**. Its two ends are different signs. >> The **allophonic** continuum runs from **open-N** to **closed-N** in the sign **SAY-NO-TO**. Its two ends are the same sign. || **Deaf signers** did two tasks with these images. >> In **identification**, they decided which sign each image was. >> In **discrimination**, they decided whether two images were different. || Both continua change visually, but only the phonemic one crosses a contrast. So comparing them separates a physical change from a category change. The answer is yes, but only for the phonemic continuum.
7|The **deaf signers** of **Emmorey and colleagues (2003)** showed the familiar pattern of categorical perception. They showed it for the phonemic continuum only. >> For the **phonemic** continuum, identification changed sharply between **stimuli 4 and 6**. >> That sharp change is the **categorical boundary**. >> **Discrimination** peaked at that same boundary. So steps that crossed the boundary were easier to tell apart. || For the **allophonic** continuum, the pattern was different. >> The identification function was **much shallower**. So many more stimuli were ambiguous. >> There was **no peak** in discrimination. || Equal physical steps were treated differently depending on the linguistic contrast. So categorical perception follows the **phonemic contrast** in sign, not any visible change in the hand.
8|**Sign language** uses **space** to mark relationships between words in a sentence. >> A **classifier sign** stands for an object. Where it is made gives the position of that object. >> In a very transparent case, the **relative position of two objects** is given by where the classifier sign is made. || Sentences of any complexity work this way too. >> A signer places things at locations in the signing space. >> Later signs relate back to those locations. >> So the person watching must **keep track of where signs were made**. || That is why **spatial processing** is part of understanding sign grammar. It is not just background vision.
9|**Campbell, MacSweeney and Waters (2008)** compared brain activity for sign comprehension and spoken language. >> They looked at **deaf signers** and **hearing signers** watching sign. >> They also looked at **hearing participants** watching **audio-visual speech**. || Some activity was **similar** across sign and speech. >> Both were **left lateralized**, meaning activity was stronger in the left hemisphere. >> Both used the **classical language areas**. These are **Broca's area** (inferior frontal) and **Wernicke's area** (posterior superior temporal). || Some activity was **different** for sign. >> Sign used more **visual areas**. >> Sign also used **superior parietal** areas, which are classic spatial areas. >> These fit the visual and spatial demands of a signed signal. || So sign and speech share a core language network. Sign adds areas tied to its modality.
10|**Bavelier and colleagues (2001)** asked whether **deafness**, **experience with sign**, or both affect **spatial attention**. >> They tested **hearing controls** who did not sign. >> They tested **deaf signers** who were native signers. >> They tested **hearing signers** who were also native signers. || Participants watched a field of dots. >> They monitored for brief changes in **luminance**, which is brightness. >> Or they monitored for brief changes in **velocity**, which is speed of motion. >> Attention was directed either to the **center** of the screen or to the **periphery**. || **Deaf participants** showed an **advantage** when attention was directed to the **periphery**. >> Hearing signers did not share it, so signing experience alone does not explain the advantage. >> Deafness and signing come bundled in deaf signers. The hearing signers pull them apart. || So the answer is that **deafness** is tied to better peripheral attention.
11|Deafness and signing experience have **two different effects**. >> Each effect goes with a different group. || **Learning sign language** changes the **laterality** of motion perception areas. >> Laterality is which hemisphere a function relies on more. >> In hearing nonsigners, motion areas lean to the **right**. >> In **deaf signers** and **hearing signers**, they lean to the **left**. >> Perhaps motion perception needs to "talk to" left-lateralized language networks in people who learn sign. || **Enhanced peripheral visual attention** is found only in **deaf** individuals. >> Hearing signers do not show it, even though they sign. >> Perhaps hearing people rely more on **auditory cues** for spatial attention at the far periphery. || So **signing** changes lateralization, while **deafness** enhances peripheral attention.
12|Researchers wanted to separate two things that sign comprehension mixes together. >> One is the **visual, spatial and motor processing** of hand movements. >> The other is **language processing**. || They compared sign comprehension directly with **tic-tac**. >> **Tic-tac** is a sophisticated and outmoded **gestural system**. >> It was used in the UK to communicate odds at horse races. || Both use organized, sequenced hand movements that carry meaning. >> Only sign is a natural language. >> So the hand-movement demands are shared by both. >> Any extra activity for sign then reflects **language**. || That is why tic-tac is a better control than no movement at all.
13|**Campbell, MacSweeney and Waters (2008)** compared brain activity for sign language and for tic-tac. >> Sign language produced **stronger activity around Wernicke's area**. >> Wernicke's area is in the **posterior superior temporal** region. || Hand movement alone cannot explain this. >> Both sign and tic-tac involve organized hand movements. >> Only sign is a language. >> So the extra activity near Wernicke's area goes with **language**. || The **frontal activation** in this comparison sits **superior to Broca's area**. >> So it should not be called a Broca's area effect. || That is why sign is processed as language, not just as hands moving through space.
14|The evidence about signed language comes down to four points. || Sign language has **phonological structure**. >> **Native signers** show **categorical perception** for some contrastive features. >> This holds for phonemic hand configuration but not for an allophonic change. || Learning a signed language leads to some **brain reorganization**. >> The clearest case is the **lateralization of motion processing**. >> It shifts from right to left in signers. || Attention to the **visual periphery** matters for both hearing and deaf signers. >> Still, only **deaf signers** show **enhanced** peripheral attention. || Signed sentences activate **posterior STG**, which is **Wernicke's area**. >> STG is the superior temporal gyrus. >> The comparison was with **tic-tac**, which also uses organized, sequenced hand movements. || So sign is a real language, and deafness and signing each shape the brain in their own way.
15|**Emmorey (2002)** studied ASL descriptions of where a table sits in a room. >> The table is in the same place in both descriptions. >> Only the **perspective** of the description changes. || The description can come from the **speaker's perspective**. >> The signer signs **I-ENTER**, **TABLE**, **THERE**. >> The layout is set up as the signer would see it. || The description can come from the **addressee's perspective**. >> The signer signs **YOU-ENTER**, **TABLE**, **THERE**. >> The layout is set up as the viewer would see it. || Signers **comprehend better** when the room is described from the **speaker's perspective**. >> This holds even though the viewer must **mentally transform** the layout. || So the easier description is not the one that removes every spatial transformation.
16|**Braille** is **tactile reading**, which means reading through touch. >> Braille reading is in many ways **equivalent to reading print**. >> It is still reading the same spoken language. || Braille differs from print in two ways. >> The **symbols** are **optimized for tactile discrimination**. So they are easy to tell apart by touch. >> Reading Braille by sight is really hard, because the symbols were not built for the eye. >> The **fingers take in letters one at a time**. So letters arrive in sequence, not as a whole word at once. || So the task stays reading while the sensory input changes. That is why Braille is useful for studying how reading networks work with touch.
17|**Reading Braille activates visual cortex** in blind individuals. >> The input is touch, not sight. >> Visual cortex still responds to it. || The comparison is between **early blind** and **late blind** participants. >> For **early blind** participants, visual activity to tactile stimuli is **more extensive**. >> For **late blind** participants, it is less extensive. >> So the **earlier sight is lost**, the more visual cortex responds to touch. || The **visual word form area** follows the same pattern. >> It is a region linked to processing written words. >> It is **strongly activated** in early blind participants. >> It is activated **more** in early blind than in late blind participants. || So a region's usual sense does not fully decide what it can process. The timing of sensory loss shapes how it gets used.
18|An earlier question was whether **competition** could keep hearing people from developing enhanced peripheral visual attention. >> Competition means that a strong input can crowd out a weaker one. >> **Merabet and colleagues (2008)** tested competition in a different way. || They asked **sighted** people to **wear blindfolds for five days**. >> During those days, they **trained** them to read **Braille**. >> A **control group** was also trained but did not wear blindfolds. || The **blindfolded group learned more quickly** than the control group. >> The blindfold removed visual input. >> So touch faced less competition from vision. || That is why removing a competing input can speed up learning, even in adults over a short time.
19|**Merabet and colleagues (2008)** also scanned the blindfolded participants while they read Braille. >> On **Day 1**, primary visual cortex showed little response to Braille. >> Over the course of the experiment, **primary visual cortex** started to respond. >> By **Day 5**, the response was largest. || On **Day 6**, the **blindfold was removed**. >> The effect was **completely reversed**. >> Visual cortex no longer responded to Braille in the same way. || So the recruitment of visual cortex for touch was **rapid** and **reversible**. >> It appeared within days. >> It disappeared once vision came back. >> That is why it looks like a change in competition, not a new pathway built from scratch.
20|**Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)** was used to **disrupt** activity in visual cortex. TMS uses a magnetic pulse to interfere with a brain area. >> For the **blindfolded group**, TMS **disrupted Braille reading** on Day 5. Their **error rate** went up. >> For the **non-blindfolded group**, TMS did not disrupt reading in the same way. >> On **Day 6**, after the blindfold came off, the effect was gone. || This is evidence that blindfolded participants were somehow **using visual cortex for tactile processing**. It is still marked with a "maybe." >> Activation alone only means a region responds. >> Disruption changing behavior means the region **contributes** to the task. || Visual cortex may get **tactile input all the time**, even in sighted people. >> That input is **too weak to compete** with the stronger, more consistent visual input. >> So even short-term removal of vision can **"unmask"** tactile responses in visual cortex.
21|**Language circuitry is resilient** despite differences in modality. >> For **sign language**, the **classical language areas** are activated. This happens even though the input differs dramatically in surface form. >> For **Braille**, reading seems to engage networks that are important for **processing text**. || **Cross-modal plasticity** is the reuse of brain areas by input from a different sense. >> It suggests that brain organization is **shaped over development by competition**. >> When **auditory input is unavailable**, putative auditory areas are used to process sign. >> In the same case, **visual attention is enhanced at the periphery**. || Competition also mattered for second-language learning. >> Competing cognitive and perceptual processes that shape language processing were important in the **Competition Model** of second-language learning. || So competition among inputs and processes shapes language processing across modalities and across languages.
