2|**Orthographic depth** describes how consistently a script's spelling matches its pronunciation. A **shallow** script has regular letter-sound matches. A **deep** script has many irregular ones. >> **English** is a relatively deep script because of its irregularities. >> **Finnish** and **Korean** are very shallow scripts. >> **Pinyin** for Mandarin and **French** are relatively regular. || A **"mispronouncing contest"** made the point with words like COLONEL and PARADIGM. >> People willfully mispronounced these words by following their letters. >> The contest made clear that **phonemes are not letters**. It also made clear that **letters are not phonemes**. >> In COLONEL and PARADIGM, the letters do not reliably give the sounds. || So a reader of a deep script cannot always decode a word from its letters alone. That is why English readers must learn many exceptions through experience.
3|Finnish is a very **transparent** writing system. The **Orthographic Depth Hypothesis** calls such a system **shallow**. >> Each letter corresponds more or less unambiguously to one **phoneme**. >> **Doubled letters** mark length. A doubled consonant is a **geminated** consonant, which is held longer. A doubled vowel is a long vowel. || A beginning Finnish lesson lets a listener follow the sounds along with the written text. >> After about a minute, most listeners get the idea of how letters map onto sounds. || Finnish children learn to **decode** very quickly. Decoding means producing a pronunciation from a written form. >> One learned letter-sound match works across many different words. || So a shallow script makes decoding fast to learn and easy to generalize.
4|English is an **outlier** among languages that use the **Roman alphabet**. Its spelling is far less regular than most of them. || The comparison uses **pseudowords**. A pseudoword is a made-up word that follows the spelling and sound patterns of a language. >> A child has never seen a pseudoword, so it cannot be read from memory. >> The child must **decode** it from learned letter-sound patterns. || In **Grade 1**, children learning a wide variety of other languages read pseudowords well. They produce plausible pronunciations at a **much higher rate** than English learners. >> For **children learning English**, the spelling patterns are less predictable. >> For **children learning more regular scripts**, the patterns are consistent, so decoding develops faster. || So one result of English's depth is that **children learn to decode more slowly in English**.
5|Korean writing is **even more regular than Finnish**. Its symbols for **similar phonemes** look similar to one another. >> So the shape of a letter reflects how its sound is made. >> Letters are grouped into **syllable blocks** with initial, medial and sometimes final positions. || **Velar place of articulation** has its own written feature. Velar sounds are made with the back of the tongue at the soft palate. >> The velar consonants share the basic angular shape **ㄱ**. >> Doubling the shape or adding a stroke tells the related velar consonants **g**, **kk** and **k** apart. || So in Korean, a family of related sounds is written with a family of related shapes.
6|The **frequency by regularity interaction** is a landmark finding in psycholinguistics. It is measured with **naming latency**, the time needed to start saying a written word. >> The **frequency effect** is slower naming of low-frequency words than high-frequency words. >> A **regular word** follows the usual spelling-sound pattern. An **exception word** does not. || The frequency effect is **much larger for exception words** than for regular words. >> For **high-frequency words**, exception and regular words are both named in about 540 ms. >> For **low-frequency words**, exception words rise to about 580 ms. Regular words rise only to about 555 ms. || An **interaction** means the effect of one factor depends on the other factor. >> Here the cost of being an exception depends on how frequent the word is. >> The finding has impacted other areas, most immediately **inflectional morphology**. || So experience matters most when a word's pronunciation conflicts with its spelling pattern.
7|One interpretation of the interaction uses **two separate processes** in word recognition. >> The **sublexical process** is decoding. It computes a pronunciation from spelling units. >> The **lexical process** is knowledge of a particular word. It supplies that word's learned pronunciation. || The two processes matter differently case by case. >> For **high-frequency (HF) words**, the lexical process is fast and efficient. So the sublexical process has little influence. >> For **low-frequency (LF) words**, the lexical process is slower. So the sublexical output shows up. >> For **nonwords**, there is no stored word. So reading depends on the sublexical process. || A low-frequency exception word gets a regularized pronunciation from decoding. That output conflicts with the real pronunciation and slows naming. || That is why regularity matters most for low-frequency words in this account.
8|**Seidenberg and McClelland (1989)** built a **PDP model** of visual word recognition. A PDP model is a network that learns connections between spelling and sound. || It makes different assumptions from dual-route models. >> **Rules** are just generalizations from lots of words. They are not a separate process. >> The **lexicon** is also distributed. Some whole-word information is stored in the **orthography to phonology (O to P) mappings**. Some is stored in other parts of the system. || It also uses a different research strategy. >> Dual-route models reverse-engineer the reading system from adult and **neuropsychological** data. >> The PDP approach explains reading with **domain-general learning mechanisms**. These mechanisms are not specific to reading or language. || So in a PDP model, word-specific knowledge and general patterns live in one learned system.
9|The **Seidenberg and McClelland (1989)** model captured the **frequency by regularity interaction**. It did not assume that frequency and regularity come from different processes. || The model learns from the statistics of many words. >> **Regular words** are consistent with the statistical patterns of many other words. So they get support from those words. >> **Exception words** are inconsistent with these statistics. So they get no such support. >> **Frequency** affects how well each word is learned. A frequent word is trained many times. || The two factors combine case by case. >> A **high-frequency exception** is learned so well that its inconsistency matters little. >> A **low-frequency exception** has weak learning and no support from other words. So it is the slowest. >> A **low-frequency regular word** still gets support from the shared patterns. || So one learning mechanism can produce the whole interaction.
10|A PDP model reads **words and nonwords in the same route**. A **nonword** is a made-up letter string with no stored entry. >> In principle, nonwords are named with the **same set of weights** used for words. **Weights** are the learned connection strengths in the network. >> No separate rule system for nonwords is needed. || **Generalization** to nonwords uses the same **statistical patterns** that produce regularity effects. >> A regular word is easy because it shares patterns with many words. >> A new nonword is pronounced by applying those same shared patterns. || So, in principle, one learned mapping handles familiar words and new items alike.
11|In practice, the **Seidenberg and McClelland** model did not read nonwords well. Its **nonword accuracy** was not very good. || The failure is a classic case of **ambiguity in model evaluation**. A poor result has two possible causes. >> One cause could be that the **theoretical claim** is wrong. The claim is that nonwords and exceptions can be read by the same associative mechanism. >> The other cause could be something specific to **how the model was made**. That is a problem of implementation, not of theory. || The way to decide is to repair the model. >> If a better implementation reads nonwords well, the theory survives. >> The later improvement came from better **phonological representations**. || So a failed simulation does not by itself reveal why it failed.
12|Nonword reading is tied to **phonological representations**, the way a system codes speech sounds. >> Many studies find a relationship between **decoding** and **phonological processing**. Decoding here means nonword reading. || **Seidenberg and McClelland (1989)** used odd phonological representations called **Wickelfeatures**. >> Wickelfeatures captured sound similarity poorly. >> So the model's nonword reading suffered. || **Plaut and colleagues (1996)** improved the phonological representations. Their model produced **better performance on nonwords**. >> The improvement came without adding a separate rule system. || So at least part of the earlier failure came from the implementation, not the theory.
13|**Generalization depends on similarity**. A network handles a new item through its similarity to items it has learned. This principle is inherited from **behaviorism**. || **Poor phonological representations**, like Wickelfeatures, fail to capture the **similarity space** of words in the language. The similarity space is how words relate to each other in sound. >> When similarity is coded badly, learning cannot transfer well to new forms. || The damage differs for words and nonwords. >> For **familiar words**, the network has **word-specific information** from training on that word. That information does not depend entirely on generalization. So it is **more robust** to poor representations. >> For **nonwords**, there is no word-specific information. Reading depends fully on generalization. So nonwords suffer most. || That is why poor phonology hurts nonword reading more than word reading.
14|**Harm and Seidenberg (1999)** studied the link between phonology and nonword generalization more systematically. They manipulated the amount of **noise** in the model's phonological representations. >> More noise makes the sound representations less reliable. >> The rest of the learning model stays the same. || The noisy models replicated key findings on **developmental dyslexia**, a reading impairment that appears during development. >> The degree of **phonological deficit** went with the degree of **phonological dyslexia**. Phonological dyslexia is poor nonword decoding. >> **Word recognition** was relatively spared. || So one weakened phonological system can produce a selective-looking reading deficit.
15|**Pinker and Ullman (2002)** proposed a **dual-route model** for the past tense. The input is a **word stem** such as walk or hold plus a **grammatical feature** such as past tense. >> The **lexicon** route works by lookup and association. It supplies irregulars such as held. >> The **grammar** route combines a stem with a suffix. It produces regulars such as walk plus -ed. >> A **blocking** connection lets a retrieved irregular stop the regular form. || The two routes belong to different memory systems. >> The lexicon is part of **declarative memory**, tied to words and facts. Its main substrate is temporo-parietal cortex. >> The grammar is part of the **procedural system**, tied to rules and skills. Its main substrate is frontal cortex and basal ganglia. || The model is **similar** to the reading dual-route model. Both separate **word-specific knowledge** from a **productive operation**. >> It is also **different**. Reading converts spelling into sound. The past-tense model changes a stem according to a grammatical feature. || So similar architectures can solve different problems.
16|The past-tense dual-route model looks like the reading model, but it has **different origins**. || The reading dual-route model was built from **neuropsychological double dissociations**. These are patients whose impairments pattern in opposite ways. || **Pinker and colleagues** were motivated by other concerns. >> **Generative linguistics** of the time was mostly about categorical, symbolic, rule-based grammars. >> These rules often have **exceptions**. >> So their model keeps the rule system apart from exceptional lexical information. || So similar diagrams do not mean the theories grew from the same evidence.
17|Linguists often left **exceptions** out of the grammar. They did not want to make the grammar inelegant by adding machinery for them. || The **dual-process model** proposes a way to handle the exceptions. The grammar stays elegant, symbolic and categorical. || The second process has all the properties the rule route lacks. >> It is **word-specific**, which means lexical. >> It is **sensitive to frequency** of occurrence. >> It is **sensitive to phonological similarity**. Keep-kept, weep-wept and leap-leapt form a family of similar irregulars. || So exceptions go to an associative lexicon, and the rule stays simple.
18|**Rumelhart and McClelland (1987)** offered an alternative approach. In their approach, the past tense is **all associative learning**. || One trainable network learns the past tense across all verbs. >> It encodes the sound of a verb root as **Wickelfeatures**. >> A **pattern associator** with modifiable connections maps the root pattern onto a past-tense pattern. >> The output pattern is then decoded into a past-tense form. || The network has no separate -ed rule. >> **Regular forms** are a pattern learned from many examples. >> **Irregular forms** are learned in the same system. || So one mechanism is proposed for both regulars and exceptions.
19|Rumelhart and McClelland's model was one of the first learning models of a language process. So its focus was on capturing **developmental phenomena**. || Its main target was **U-shaped learning**. >> First, children learn some frequent irregular verbs quite early, such as run-ran. >> Then, spontaneously, children begin producing **regularizations** such as runned. A regularization applies the regular past-tense pattern to an irregular verb. >> Eventually, children produce the correct forms again. || The learning is called **U-shaped** because children often get worse before they get better. Performance goes down and then goes back up. || So a theory must explain the whole path of learning, including the temporary errors.
20|U-shaped learning matters to the **words and rules** approach because the approach explains it with distinct stages and processes. || Each stage gets its own process. >> At first, early correct forms are just **imitations**. They do not depend on the same mechanisms as adult processing. >> When the **rule** is first learned, it is applied to all cases. The rule is the best hypothesis for all items generally. So the child says runned. >> Over time, **exceptions** are learned associatively, one by one. They become part of lexical knowledge. >> The **blocking process** also develops. A retrieved irregular then blocks the regular form. || So in this account, the dip in performance marks the arrival of the rule.
21|Rumelhart and McClelland's model also produces **U-shaped learning** with only one network. || The **training set** is based on data about the input to children. That input changes over time. >> Early on, **irregular verbs** predominate. The highest-frequency verbs tend to have irregular past tenses, such as GIVE, TAKE, HAVE and GO. >> So the model first learns these irregular mappings. || Later the vocabulary expands to include more **regular forms**. The model then reorganizes to capture those regularities. >> This reorganization **interferes** with some early-learned forms. So the model starts getting them wrong. >> With further training, the irregular responses recover. || So a change in experience can produce the U shape without a separate rule component.
22|The model was trained on a **small number of verbs**. So the authors could run a **"wug" test with real words**. A wug test asks a learner to produce the past tense of a word it has not learned. >> A real English verb is still new to the model if the model was never trained on it. || The test used **unfamiliar low-frequency regular verbs**. >> Many responses were correct regular forms, such as guarded, mapped and typed. >> Some responses were odd, such as shipped for shape and toureder for tour. || The model generated **92 percent** of **Wickelfeatures** correctly for regular verbs. It generated 84 percent for irregular verbs and 91 percent overall. >> A high share of correct features does not mean the same share of whole words came out right. || So generalization is measured relative to what the learner was trained on.
23|The model also produced some **unusual outputs**. Just four responses added or changed consonants. >> It gave maded as a past tense of mate. >> It gave squawked for squat. >> It gave membled for mail. >> It gave toureder for tour. || The model's authors said **humans are unlikely to make these errors**, especially the last two. >> These responses were, for the most part, **near threshold**. >> The authors suggested adding an **auto-associative network** among the output units. It could clean up the output pattern and avoid bizarre responses. >> They had not yet implemented this suggestion. || So a good overall learning curve is not enough if a model makes errors that people would not make.
24|**Joanisse and Seidenberg (1999)** built a newer model of the past tense. It links **speech input**, **semantics** and **speech output** in one system. || The model rests on two theoretical premises. >> **Rule-like generalization** is supported by **phonological representations**. So regular and new forms lean mainly on sound. >> **Irregular forms** require greater support from **semantics**, the meaning of the particular word. || So the difference between regulars and irregulars comes from different reliance on phonology and semantics. There is no separate rule route.
25|The **Joanisse and Seidenberg (1999)** model targets a **dissociation** in neuropsychological data. Some patients do worse on **irregular** past tenses. Others do worse on **nonword** past tenses. || The model explains each side with a different kind of support. >> A **nonword** like wug has no meaning. So its past tense depends on **phonology**, and damage to phonology should hurt nonwords most. >> An **irregular** verb does not follow the regular pattern. So it depends more on **semantics**, and damage to semantics should hurt irregulars most. || So one architecture can produce opposite patterns of impairment.
26|**Broca's aphasia** gives one side of the dissociation. >> It comes from injury to **left anterior** brain regions. >> Patients have **poor speech production**. || These patients have problems with **regular morphology**, especially with **nonwords**. >> They struggle with forms like wug to wugged and blick to blicked. >> Nonwords were the lowest, at about 69 percent correct. >> Regular verbs were about 82 percent correct. Irregular verbs were about 85 percent correct. || A nonword has no stored past tense, so it must be built by generalization. So this pattern points to a **phonological** contribution to the deficit.
27|The other side of the dissociation is **problems with irregulars**. >> It appears in **Wernicke's** and **Alzheimer's** patients. >> It also appears in **anomic** patients with injury to inferior or middle temporal brain regions. || These patients are **poor on irregular** past tenses. >> Irregular verbs were about 60 percent correct. >> Regular verbs were about 89 percent correct. Nonwords were about 84 percent correct. || So these patients show the **reverse** of the Broca's pattern. That fits damage to the **semantic** support that irregulars need.
28|The classical view of aphasia is an **attractive dichotomy** between two syndromes. || **Wernicke's aphasia** produces "word salad" or "Jabberwocky," often with **neologisms**, which are made-up words. >> Syntax stays roughly intact. >> One patient described his poor vision as "My wires don't hire right." >> Another patient described a fork as "a need for a schedule." || **Broca's aphasia** produces **telegraphic** speech. >> Patients have difficulty with **word order** and **grammatical particles**. >> Important **content words** are relatively spared. >> One patient described his hospital visit in fragments such as "Two doctors ... and ah ... teeth." || So Wernicke's speech is fluent but hard to interpret. Broca's speech is effortful and made mostly of content words.
29|The dichotomy treats **Wernicke's aphasia** as problems with **activation** in the lexicon, or perhaps inhibition. It treats **Broca's aphasia** as problems with **grammar**. || This view is fairly distant from how **Broca** described his original cases. >> His patient **Leborgne**, called "Tan," could only produce one jargon syllable. >> He seemed otherwise to have his senses. He also seemed to understand what was being said. || This point is confusing. Broca's actual patients would **not necessarily be classified as Broca's aphasics**. >> They were arguably unable to produce speech at all. >> The cause was apparently **motor control** issues. >> Today they would be classified as having **apraxia of speech**, a problem planning or coordinating speech movements. || So a syndrome's name does not settle which level of processing is damaged.
30|The aphasia dichotomy is attractive partly because it matches a sharp distinction in late 20th century **formal linguistics**. That distinction separates the **grammar** from the **lexicon**. >> The grammar combines forms into sentences. >> The lexicon holds words and their properties. || The distinction starts with **Chomsky's** formulation of the **computational problem of language learning**. >> People recognize a new sentence because the grammar they have internalized generates it. >> The new sentence does not have to match any familiar sentence. || **Chomsky (1955)** also argued for the **independence of syntax and semantics**. >> Neither "colorless green ideas sleep furiously" nor "furiously sleep ideas green colorless" had occurred in an English speaker's experience. >> Yet the first is grammatical and the second is not. || So a grammar and lexicon split seemed to fit the two aphasia types neatly.
31|The speech production model also separates a **functional level** from a **positional level**. >> It begins at the **message level**, which arouses concepts and event structure. >> The **functional level** arouses word meanings and semantic roles. >> The **positional level** gets word forms into a grammatically appropriate order. >> **Phonological encoding** and the **speech gesture level** come after these. || The two levels differ in kind. >> **Lemmas** at the functional level have an **arbitrary** relationship to their referents. A lemma is the word representation chosen during word selection. >> **Syntactic trees** and **affixes** at the positional level are products of **abstract syntactic processes**. || Words like HOUSEBOAT and DOGHOUSE seem to raise an objection, because their meaning is clearly not arbitrary. >> Speech errors answer this objection. Errors such as "easy enoughly" place **derivational morphology** at the positional level. || So the functional and positional levels line up with the lexicon and grammar split.
32|Most languages talk about nearby space **egocentrically**. Egocentric terms use the speaker's own body as the reference point. >> Examples are left and right, in front of and behind. || **Tzeltal** does not do this. It is a Western Mayan language spoken in Chiapas. >> Tzeltal uses **absolute directions** instead. Absolute directions stay fixed when a person turns. >> A small number of other languages do the same. They are mostly unrelated and broadly distributed, for example in Bali and Queensland. || For English speakers, a request to "raise your east hand" feels strange. || So languages differ in which spatial frame they use for nearby things.
33|Tzeltal has a historically **landmark-based** system. >> Things are either **uphill** or **downhill** from one another. >> They are not to the left or right of the speaker or the listener. || "Uphill" and "downhill" are used as **cardinal directions**. So Tzeltal has an **absolute** system. >> The terms come from the slope of the landscape around Tenejapa. >> In use, they act as fixed directions. They do not simply point to the nearest hill. >> **Levinson and colleagues (2002)** described this system. || So a direction keeps the same meaning as the speaker turns around.
34|**Majid and colleagues (2004)** used a **non-linguistic test** of **relative** versus **absolute** coding of space. It is called the **chips task**. || A participant sees a card at Table 1 with a large dot and a small dot. >> After a 30-second delay, the participant is **rotated 180 degrees** and led to Table 2. >> The participant then picks the matching card from a set of four. || The rotation makes the two codings predict different cards. >> A **relative** response keeps the arrangement the same with respect to the person's body. >> An **absolute** response keeps the arrangement the same with respect to the environment, such as north. || **Dutch** speakers gave overwhelmingly **relative** responses. **Tzeltal** speakers gave mostly **absolute** responses. >> So nonverbal spatial memory matched each language's preferred frame.
35|**Majid and colleagues (2004)** also tested **spatial inference**. The task combines relations learned at two tables. || At Table 1, participants see a cube and a cone. >> They are rotated 180 degrees to Table 2 and see the cube with a cylinder. >> They are rotated back to Table 1, where the cone stands alone. >> They must place the cylinder next to the cone, consistent with what they saw. || The two codings give different answers. >> With **relative** coding, the cube is right of the cone and the cylinder is right of the cube. So the cylinder goes right of the cone. >> With **absolute** coding, the cube is south of the cone and the cylinder is further south. So the cylinder goes south of the cone. || **Dutch** participants gave **relative** responses. **Tzeltal** participants gave **absolute** responses. So each group used its preferred frame even when reasoning about space.
36|**Li and Gleitman** questioned the Tzeltal results. They tried **inducing landmark-based similarity in English speakers**. || They looked at the conditions of testing. >> **Tzeltal speakers** were tested outside, with visible and familiar **landmarks**. >> Typical psychology lab studies are done in **windowless rooms**. || Useful landmarks can encourage absolute coding. A windowless room offers none, so relative coding is more convenient. >> So the language difference could partly be a **testing-environment** difference. || That is why they tested English speakers under different viewing conditions.
37|**Li and Gleitman** tested English speakers on the rotation task under three conditions. || With the **blinds down**, English speakers overwhelmingly used **relative position**. They behaved like the Dutch speakers in prior studies. >> With the **blinds up** and a view of the street, their relative preference was **less pronounced**. >> **Outdoors**, their relative preference was also less pronounced. || The language group stayed the same. Only the available landmarks changed. >> A weaker relative preference is not a full switch to absolute coding. || So the testing environment can change which spatial frame people use.
38|**Levinson and colleagues** were not impressed by Li and Gleitman's results. They raised two objections. || First, Li and Gleitman made it too **transparent** that they were testing rotation. >> They **reduced memory load**. >> They shortened the **time and distance** between the stimulus and the test. >> An easy, obvious task invites a deliberate strategy instead of habitual coding. || Second, the participants were **Ivy League students**. >> Such students excel at figuring out what the experimenter wants and then making it happen. || So the landmark effect might reflect guessing the purpose, not normal spatial memory.
39|The research question asks whether **categorical perception** occurs for sign language **hand configuration**. In categorical perception, a gradual change is perceived as a sudden switch between categories. || Two continua of signs were created. >> The **phonemic** continuum changes hand configuration from the sign PLEASE to the sign SORRY. So it crosses a contrast between two signs. >> The **allophonic** continuum changes from open-N to closed-N in the sign SAY-NO-TO. So it changes the hand without changing the sign. || Observers make **identification** and **discrimination** judgments along each continuum. >> Categorical perception predicts a sharp identification boundary. >> It also predicts the best discrimination at that boundary. || So the test separates a physical change from a linguistically meaningful one.
40|**Deaf signers** show **categorical perception** for the **phonemic** hand-configuration contrast. Their curves have the familiar categorical shape. || For the **phonemic** continuum, identification has a fairly sharp **categorical boundary** between stimuli 4 and 6. >> This boundary lines up with the **peak** in the **discrimination function**. >> So pairs that cross the boundary are the easiest to tell apart. || For the **allophonic** continuum, the identification function is **much shallower**. >> There are many more ambiguous stimuli. >> There is **no peak** in the discrimination function. || So categorical perception follows the linguistic contrast, not just any visible change in the hand.
41|Brain activity during sign language comprehension has both **similarities** to and **differences** from spoken language. **Campbell, MacSweeney and Waters (2008)** compared deaf signers, hearing signers and hearing people perceiving audio-visual speech. || Sign and speech are similar in two ways. >> Both are **left lateralized**. >> Both use the **classical language areas**. These are Broca's area in inferior frontal cortex and Wernicke's area in posterior superior temporal cortex. || Sign differs in two ways. >> It recruits more **visual areas** and fewer **auditory areas**. >> It also recruits **superior parietal** cortex, a classic spatial area. || So sign uses the same core language network, plus areas suited to a visual and spatial signal.
42|Sign language has **phonological structure**. Native signers show **categorical perception** for some contrastive features. || Learning a signed language leads to some **brain reorganization**. >> The clearest case is the **lateralization of motion processing**. || Attention to the **visual periphery** is critical for both hearing and deaf signers. >> Only **deaf signers** show enhanced processing of visual attention in the periphery. >> So this enhancement comes with deafness, not with signing alone. || Signed sentences activate **posterior STG**, which is Wernicke's area. >> The comparison was non-language stimuli that also involve organized, sequenced hand movements. >> So this activation reflects language, not just hand movement. || So sign is a full language with its own phonology, and learning it also reorganizes the brain.
43|**Johnson and Newport (1989)** described two versions of the **critical period hypothesis**. Both say that early in life humans have a superior capacity for acquiring languages. || The **exercise hypothesis** ties the outcome to use. >> If the capacity is not exercised early, it disappears or declines with maturation. >> If the capacity is exercised, later language learning abilities remain intact throughout life. || The **maturational state hypothesis** ties the outcome to maturation alone. >> The capacity disappears or declines with maturation, whatever the early experience. || Johnson and Newport see the **maturational state hypothesis** as more consistent with their data. >> Their late second-language learners had already exercised the capacity with a first language. >> Yet later starters still learned the second language less well. || This changes how the **L1 critical period** data can be read. >> Late first-language learners never exercised the capacity early. So both versions predict their poor outcome. >> Only second-language data can tell the two versions apart. Early first-language experience still matters.
44|**Mayberry, Lock and Kazmi (2002)** asked how early language experience affects later language learning. || Many **deaf children** get no sign language input, but they have an otherwise typical upbringing. >> Children aged 9 to 15 have difficulty learning **American Sign Language (ASL)** when it is their first language. >> A comparison group went deaf after first learning a **spoken language**. These children do much better. >> The differences **persist into adulthood**. || Deaf adults were tested on ASL sentence recall. >> Those with no early language scored about 36 percent. Those with an early spoken language scored about 78 percent. || Adults were also tested on English grammar judgments. >> Deaf adults with no early language scored about 52 percent, near the 50 percent chance level. >> Adults with an early signed or early spoken language scored about 80 percent. || So what matters is early access to some language, whether spoken or signed.
45|A **semantic blocking effect** is slower naming of objects when same-category objects are named together. One possible **confound** is **visual object recognition**. >> Objects in the same category may look alike. >> So the effect might come from seeing the objects, not from naming them. || There were two responses to this confound. >> First, the stimuli were **normed**. On average, same-category objects were not more visually similar than objects from different categories. >> Second, a **control experiment** blocked objects by **visual similarity** instead. It found different results. || In the control, the visual blocking effect appeared in the **first block of trials**. >> The semantic effect appears only after the first cycle. || So the semantic blocking effects are not likely due to visual similarity.
46|**Hermens and colleagues** had people see **multiple objects simultaneously**. They found **no blocking effect**. >> This raised a worry that the effect depends on seeing objects one at a time, in serial. || **Belke and colleagues** addressed this worry. >> Participants viewed displays of **four items simultaneously**. >> They produced the four names **in serial**, one after another. || This design separates how objects are shown from how names are produced. >> All objects are visible at once. >> Naming still requires selecting one word after another. || So if blocking appears here, it does not depend on viewing objects in serial.
47|**Belke and colleagues** measured **eye movements** while participants named the four objects. || Each object gives two timing measures. >> **Gaze duration** is how long the eyes stay on an object. >> **Eye-speech lag** is the time between the eyes leaving an object and the start of saying its name. || The eyes run ahead of speech. >> A speaker often looks at the next object before articulating the current name. >> So looking at an object and saying its name are separate events in time. || So each measure can track a different stage of preparing a name.
48|Each timing measure maps onto a stage of speech production. The comparison is **homogeneous** blocks, with same-category objects, against **heterogeneous** blocks, with mixed objects. || **Overall speech duration** decreases over the experiment. It shows **no blocking effect**. >> Speech duration reflects the **gesture level**, which is articulation. || **Gaze duration** shows a **blocking effect**, but only after the **first cycle**. >> Gaze duration reflects **lemma selection**. || **Eye-speech lag** shows **no effect**. >> Numerically the effect seems to go the opposite way, but this is **not significant**. >> Lag reflects **phonological encoding**. || So the blocking effect arises at **lemma selection**, not at articulation or phonological encoding.
49|**McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)** asked how **phoneme identification** feeds **word recognition**. >> **Thresholded** processing, like a lumber mill, passes on only a finished category decision. >> **Cascaded** processing, like a food web, lets graded information flow on before the decision is final. || They tested a **/b/ to /p/ continuum** in a **visual world** display with eye tracking. >> The continuum varies **voice onset time (VOT)**. VOT is the delay between releasing the consonant and the start of voicing. >> Each display held a target such as a pear and a competitor such as a bear. It also held two fillers, a lamp and a ship. || So looks to the competitor can reveal whether fine phonetic detail reaches word recognition.
50|**McMurray and colleagues (2003)** first measured participants' final choices. Participants used the mouse to select which of the pictures matched what they heard. >> For example, they chose between a **bear** and a **pear**. || The mouse choices formed a typical **step-like identification function**. >> Short VOTs gave /b/ responses, so participants chose the bear. >> Long VOTs gave /p/ responses, so participants chose the pear. >> The **category boundary** was at about 17.5 ms of VOT. || A categorical final choice does not prove that earlier processing was categorical. So eye movements are needed to track the processing before the choice.
51|Eye movements reveal that **ambiguity is gradient**. There are **more looks to the competitor** close to the category boundary. || The effect holds within one chosen category. >> When participants chose the **bear**, VOTs closer to the boundary drew more looks to the pear. >> When participants chose the **pear**, VOTs closer to the boundary drew more looks to the bear. >> VOT mainly changes how **long** the competitor stays active. || So fine phonetic detail inside a category still affects **lexical activation**. That favors **cascaded** processing over thresholded processing.
52|The gradient effect is clearest when competitor looks are compared step by step across **VOT**. Looks are split into an **early window** of 300 to 1100 ms and a **late window** of 1100 to 1900 ms. || For **bear responses**, looks to the pear rise as VOT moves from 0 ms up toward the boundary. >> For **pear responses**, looks to the bear rise as VOT moves from 40 ms down toward the boundary. >> The pattern appears in both the early and the late window. || So the closer a sound is to the boundary, the more the competitor word stays active. That is why the results support **cascaded** processing.
