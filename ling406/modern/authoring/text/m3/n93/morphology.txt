2|**Morphology** is **generative**, which means speakers can form words they have not memorized one by one. || **Derivational morphology** turns one kind of word into another kind. >> English has a process for turning verbs into nouns. >> At some point there was no noun "generative," only the verb "to generate." >> The derivational process created the new word. || **Inflectional morphology** marks grammatical information on a word. >> In English, inflection is mostly the **plural** and the **past tense**. >> These two give clear cases of a speaker extending a pattern to a new item. || Generalizing can also produce playful forms. >> One person wrote "She found me crying and she crew too and we both crode." >> The forms crew and crode extend irregular-sounding patterns to cry. >> They are creative forms, not standard English past tenses. || So speakers produce forms they never heard, and the open question is whether that needs symbolic rules or can come from learning.
3|**Jean Berko Gleason (1958)** ran a classic study called the **wug test**. It measures how readily people generalize an inflection to a new word. || A child sees a drawing of a made-up creature. >> The text under it says "This is a WUG." >> Then a second creature appears, and the text says "Now there is another one. There are two of them." >> The text ends with "There are two" and a blank for the child to fill. >> Saying **WUGS** applies plural knowledge to a new noun. || The word is new, so the child cannot have memorized its plural. >> The child must extend a learned pattern instead. >> The same logic tests the **past tense** with made-up verbs. >> Adults generalize readily, and children do this too. || Success means the child has **productive knowledge** of the pattern. || So people readily extend inflection to words they have never heard.
4|The English past tense has **regular** and **irregular** forms. || **Regular** verbs add **-ed**. >> Real verbs follow this pattern, as in show-showed, bake-baked and wade-waded. >> Made-up verbs follow it too, as in blick-blicked, trog-trogged and wumble-wumbled. >> The made-up verbs test whether the pattern extends to new words. || **Irregular** verbs do not follow the -ed pattern. >> Examples are go-went, take-took, eat-ate and sleep-slept. >> Each irregular has to be handled as an exception. >> Some irregulars share smaller patterns with other verbs. || This split echoes the **dual-route model** of reading. >> That model also separated a general pattern from word-specific knowledge. || So a theory of the past tense must explain both productive extension and familiar exceptions.
5|**Pinker and colleagues** proposed the **Words and Rules** account. It treats words and rules as **separate processes** with separate representational formats. || For **regular** verbs, a **symbolic rule** makes the past tense. >> The rule is something like "add -ed." >> The rule needs no stored entry for each past tense. >> So the rule can apply to a brand-new verb. || For **irregular** verbs, the past tense is **lexicalized** within an **associative network**. >> Lexicalized means the form is stored as part of that word's own entry. >> The network holds word-specific information. >> Frequency and similarity can influence it. || The account also has to stop a stored irregular from being replaced by a regularization. || So in this account, productive rule use and remembered exceptions are two separate kinds of knowledge.
6|**Pinker and Ullman (2002)** proposed a **dual-route model** for the past tense. The input is a word stem, such as walk or hold, plus a grammatical feature, such as past tense. || The **lexicon** route looks up a stored form. >> For hold, it retrieves held. >> Its computation is lookup and association. >> It is used for roots, idioms, irregulars and some regulars. >> It belongs to **declarative memory**, which holds words and facts. >> Its principal substrate is temporo-parietal cortex. || The **grammar** route combines pieces. >> For walk, it combines walk with the suffix -ed. >> Its computation is combination and unification. >> It is used for phrases, sentences and any regular form. >> It belongs to the **procedural system**, which holds rules and skills. >> Its principal substrate is frontal cortex and basal ganglia. || A **blocking** connection runs from the lexicon to the grammar. >> A retrieved irregular stops the regular form from competing. || The model is **similar** to the dual-route model of reading. >> Both separate word-specific knowledge from a productive operation. || The model is **different** in its task and its origins. >> Reading converts spelling into sound. >> The past-tense model changes a stem according to a grammatical feature. >> It came from grammar theory, not from patient dissociations. || So two models can look alike while solving different problems.
7|The past-tense model and the reading model look alike, but they have **different origins**. || The **dual-route model of reading** was built on neuropsychological **double dissociations**. >> A double dissociation means each of two abilities can be damaged while the other is spared. || **Pinker and colleagues** were motivated by other concerns. >> **Generative linguistics** of the time was mostly about **categorical, symbolic, rule-based grammars**. >> These rules often have **exceptions**. >> The Words and Rules account keeps the rule system apart from the exceptions. || So the reading model came from patient data, while the past-tense model came from a theory of grammar.
8|Linguists often just left exceptions out of the grammar. Adding machinery to handle them would make the grammar **inelegant**. || The **dual-process model** proposes a way to handle the exceptions. >> It keeps the grammar **elegant, symbolic and categorical**. >> It puts the exceptions into a second process. || The second process has all the properties the rule route does not have. >> It is **word-specific**, or lexical. >> It is **sensitive to frequency** of occurrence. >> It is **sensitive to phonological similarity**, as in keep-kept, weep-wept and leap-leapt. >> Those verbs share a sound pattern, so an exception can belong to a small family. || So separating the exceptions keeps the rule simple, but it adds a second mechanism that must also be explained.
9|**Rumelhart and McClelland (1987)** proposed an alternative. In their model, the past tense is all **associative learning**. || One trainable network learns both regular and irregular verbs. >> There is no separate "add -ed" rule. >> Regularity is a pattern learned from many examples. >> Irregular forms are learned in the same system. || The model has three parts. >> A **fixed encoding network** turns the phonological representation of the root form into a **Wickelfeature** representation. >> A **pattern associator** with **modifiable connections** maps root Wickelfeatures onto past-tense Wickelfeatures. >> A **decoding and binding network** turns those features into the phonological representation of the past tense. >> Wickelfeatures represent sound patterns. The modifiable connections store the learned mapping. || So one learning mechanism has to explain both the regular pattern and the exceptions.
10|**Rumelhart and McClelland** aimed their model at developmental data. It was one of the first learning models of a language process. || The target was **U-shaped learning** of the past tense. >> First, children learn some frequent irregular verbs quite early, such as run-ran. >> Then, spontaneously, children start producing **regularizations** such as runned. >> A regularization applies the regular -ed pattern to an irregular verb. >> Eventually, children produce the correct forms again. || Learning is called U-shaped because children get worse before they get better. >> Performance goes down, then goes back up. || So a theory must explain the whole path of development, not just the final adult form.
11|U-shaped learning matters to the **Words and Rules** account because each part of the U is a **distinct stage** with a distinct process. || In the first stage, early correct forms are just **imitations**. >> They do not depend on the same mechanisms as adult processing. >> A child can say ran without having any rule. || In the second stage, the child learns the rule. >> The rule is applied to all cases. >> The rule is the child's **best hypothesis** for all items generally. >> So the child now says runned. || In the third stage, exceptions are learned **associatively**, one by one. >> They become part of **lexical knowledge**. >> The **blocking process** also develops. Blocking means a retrieved irregular stops the regular form. >> So ran blocks runned, and the child is correct again. || That is why this account reads the dip in the U as rule use that exceptions do not yet hold back.
12|**Rumelhart and McClelland's** model also produces **U-shaped learning**. It does this with one network and no rule. || The **training set** is based on data about input to children. >> That input changes over time. || Early on, **irregular verbs** predominate. >> The highest-frequency verbs tend to have irregular past tenses, such as GIVE, TAKE, HAVE and GO. >> So the model learns these irregulars early. || Later, the vocabulary expands to include more regular verbs. >> The model **reorganizes** to capture those regularities. >> This **interferes** with some early-learned irregulars. || For high-frequency irregular verbs, correct features dip briefly early in training. >> Regular verbs climb quickly and stay high. >> The irregulars recover and climb more slowly. || Response strength tells the same story. >> Early on, the regularized form briefly beats the correct irregular form. >> With more training, the correct form wins and the regularized form fades. || So a change in the input can make one network get worse before it gets better.
13|**Generalization testing** checks whether the model learned structure it can transfer to new verbs. || The model was trained on a small number of verbs. >> So many real English verbs were new to the model. >> That let the researchers run a **wug test** with real words. || The model received unfamiliar low-frequency regular verbs. >> Many responses were correct regular forms, such as shaped, sipped and typed. >> Other responses were odd, such as stepted for step, squawked for squat and membled for mail. || The model generated most **Wickelfeatures** correctly. >> The proportion correct was .92 for regulars and .84 for irregulars. >> It was .91 overall. >> Correct features do not guarantee that every whole word came out right. || So the model generalized well at the level of features, but whole words could still go wrong.
14|Some model outputs changed consonants in ways people would not. || Just four responses added or changed consonants. >> The model gave maded as the past tense of mate. >> It gave squawked for squat. >> It gave membled for mail. >> It gave toureder for tour. || The model's authors said humans are unlikely to make these errors, especially the last two. >> They noted that most of these responses were **near threshold**, meaning only weakly produced. >> They suggested adding an **auto-associative network** among the output units. >> Such a network could clean up the output pattern and avoid bizarre responses. >> They had not yet built it. || So matching the learning curve is not enough when the model makes words people would never produce.
15|**Prince and Pinker (1988)** criticized the model for **blending** outputs. || The model uses **feature-only representations**. >> With features alone, representing two things at once is a problem. >> In vision, features alone cannot tell a green circle near a red square from a red circle near a green square. || In the Rumelhart and McClelland model, subregularities and the regular rule compete. >> They all try to activate Wickelfeatures for the past form. >> When sing is input, features for sing, sang, sung, singed and sanged can all be active at once. >> The model does not put out a single word. >> It puts out an unordered collection of features, many of them contradictory. || So the model cannot keep competing outputs apart. >> It blends them into bizarre hybrids. >> Examples are toureder as the past of tour and membled for mail. || That is why Prince and Pinker located the problem in the representation itself.
16|**Joanisse and Seidenberg (1999)** built a newer model of the past tense. || One system links **speech input**, **semantics** and **speech output**. >> Speech input and semantics both connect through a middle layer to speech output. || Their theoretical premise has two parts. >> **Rule-like generalization** is supported by **phonological representations**, which are the sound forms of words. >> **Irregular** forms require greater support from **semantics**, which is word meaning. || So regulars and irregulars differ in how much they rely on sound and meaning, not in using separate routes.
17|**Joanisse and Seidenberg (1999)** aimed their model at a **dissociation** in patient data. || Some patients are worse at **irregular** past tenses. >> Other patients are worse at **nonword** past tenses. || The model has to produce both patterns from one system. >> A nonword has no stored meaning, so it relies on phonological generalization. >> An irregular is poorly predicted by the main pattern, so it relies more on semantics. >> Damaging phonology or semantics should then give opposite impairment profiles. || So the test is whether damage to one shared system can explain both patient groups.
18|**Broca's aphasia** comes from injury to **left anterior** brain regions. || Patients have **poor speech production**. || They have problems with **regular morphology**, especially **nonwords**. >> Nonword examples are wug and wugged, and blick and blicked. >> A nonword has no stored past tense, so it must be produced by generalizing. || Their accuracy follows a clear order. >> Irregulars are about 85 percent correct. >> Regulars are about 82 percent correct. >> Nonwords are about 69 percent correct. >> The nonword drop is much larger than the gap between irregulars and regulars. || So these patients seem to have trouble with productive inflection, which raises the question of a phonological cause.
19|**Joanisse and Seidenberg** damaged the **phonological** part of their model. || The damage **reduced** the model's ability to compute past-tense forms. || The model's pattern matched the Broca's patients. >> **Nonwords** were worse than regulars and irregulars. Model nonwords were about 72 percent correct, and patient nonwords were about 69 percent. >> **Regulars** were numerically worse than irregulars. Model regulars were about 80 percent, and model irregulars were about 85 percent. || Familiar words keep support that a new nonword lacks. >> So weak sound processing hurts nonwords most. || So a phonological impairment can look like a morphological deficit without removing any rule.
20|Other patients show the reverse problem. They are **poor on irregulars**. || These groups are **Wernicke's** patients and **Alzheimer's** patients. >> Another group is **anomic** patients with injury to **inferior or middle temporal** brain regions. >> Anomic patients have trouble retrieving words. || Irregulars are the lowest condition, at about 60 percent correct. >> Regulars are about 89 percent correct. >> Nonwords are about 84 percent correct. || So these patients show the opposite side of the dissociation from Broca's patients.
21|Damaging **semantics** in the model produced the irregular problem. || **Irregulars** were worse than regulars and nonwords. >> Model irregulars were about 62 percent correct. >> Alzheimer's patients were about 60 percent on irregulars. || **Nonwords** were a bit worse than regulars. >> Model nonwords were about 77 percent, and model regulars were about 83 percent. || Irregulars rely on meaning more than other forms do. >> So losing semantic support hurts them most. >> Regulars and nonwords still get support from typical sound patterns. || So the model reproduces the reverse profile without deleting any list of exceptions.
22|Two studies tested whether the patient dissociation really separates rules from words. || **Bird, Patterson, Lambon-Ralph, McClelland and Seidenberg (2003)** studied **Broca's patients**. >> They compared regular and irregular past tenses. >> They controlled the items adequately for **phonology**. >> The difference between regulars and irregulars was **reduced**. >> So part of the gap came from sound differences between the items. || **Patterson, Lambon-Ralph, Hodges and McClelland (2001)** studied **semantic dementia** patients. >> Semantic dementia is a loss of word meaning. >> They compared each patient's irregular impairment with the severity of the semantic deficit. >> Irregular impairment **correlated strongly** with semantic deficit severity. >> So irregular trouble grows as meaning is lost. || So past-tense problems are tied to phonological and semantic abilities, not only to rules and words.
23|This account parallels the **triangle model** of dyslexia. || **Phonological representations** support **generalization**. >> Generalization comes from **statistical learning** about patterns in the mapping from present to past. >> Impaired phonological representations hurt these generalizations. || **Semantic representations** support **idiosyncratic** forms, which are forms particular to one word. >> Forms that deviate from the overwhelmingly regular "add -ed" pattern need support from semantics. >> Some irregular forms can use **sub-patterns** shared with similar verbs. >> Those irregulars are likely to be **spared**. || So regulars and irregulars draw on the same systems to different degrees.
24|**Specific language impairment (SLI)** is **impaired language development** with "normal" performance or **nonverbal IQ**. || It affects **grammar** in two areas. >> **Morphology**, the structure of words, is affected. >> **Syntax**, the structure of sentences, is affected. || It also affects abilities beyond grammar. >> These are **phonology**, **reading**, **working memory** and **speech perception**. || So SLI is broader than grammar alone. A theory that puts the problem only in grammar has to explain these other difficulties too.
25|**Past-tense deficits** are a **sine qua non** of SLI. That means they are an essential feature of it. || Earlier claims said the SLI deficit was **specific to rules**. >> **Gopnik, van der Lely, Pinker, Ullman and Clahsen** made these claims. >> On this view, SLI is a **deficit in learning rules**. || A rule deficit makes a clear prediction. >> People with SLI should have problems with **regulars** and **nonwords**. >> They should not have problems with **irregulars**, because irregulars are stored words. || If this held, SLI could be evidence that language acquisition requires **domain-specific mechanisms**. >> Those are mechanisms used only for language. || So the account depends on whether irregulars are really spared in SLI.
26|**van der Lely and Ullman (2001)** compared past-tense accuracy in an **SLI group** and **same-age controls**. || They tested regulars, irregulars and nonwords. || The **SLI group** was low on all three. >> Regulars were about 22 percent correct. >> Irregulars were about 18 percent correct. >> Nonwords were about 7 percent correct. || The **controls** were much higher on all three. >> Regulars were about 95 percent correct. >> Irregulars were about 88 percent correct. >> Nonwords were about 74 percent correct. || In SLI, irregulars were **not spared**. >> So the deficit is broader than a rule deficit predicts. >> Nonwords were still the hardest. || So the data fit a broad impairment with an extra cost for nonwords.
27|A model with weakened **phonology** learned the past tense poorly, and **nonwords** suffered most. || With a **severe phonological deficit**, scores were lowest. >> Regulars were about 39 percent correct. >> Irregulars were about 17 percent, and nonwords were about 7 percent. || With a **weaker phonological deficit**, scores were higher but still low. >> Regulars were about 55 percent correct. >> Irregulars were about 38 percent, and nonwords were about 15 percent. || The **intact** model did much better. >> At the end of training, regulars were about 100 percent correct. Irregulars were about 94 percent, and nonwords were about 80 percent. >> At 700K iterations, regulars were about 90 percent. Irregulars and nonwords were both about 75 percent. || The impaired models have the same order as the SLI group. >> Regulars are best, irregulars are next, and nonwords are worst. || So weak phonology lowers all three forms without any missing rule.
28|The model makes clear how **phonology** affects the development of **all** past-tense forms. || Every spoken past tense uses phonology. >> So weak phonology lowers regulars, irregulars and nonwords. || Phonology is **especially important** for **nonwords**. >> A nonword has no training history of its own. >> So it depends heavily on generalizing a sound pattern. >> Familiar words get extra support from item-specific learning. || So nonwords show the largest deficit, but every form still depends on phonology.
29|This account is similar to the **triangle model** of reading. || There are no **special rules** for the past tense. >> There are only mappings between **phonology** and **semantics**. || There is no **separate lexicon** that stores the exceptions. || The ideas of **rule** and **exception** only approximately describe the system. || Two associations support the account. >> **Irregular** forms are associated with **semantic processing**. >> **Wug test** performance is associated with **phonological processing**. || So past-tense behavior comes from learned mappings that lean on sound and meaning to different degrees.
