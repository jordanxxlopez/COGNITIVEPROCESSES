2|**Dual-route models** of visual word recognition propose two separate routes for reading. Six reading findings motivate this kind of model. >> **Pseudoword reading** is fast and automatic. >> Word reading is faster than pseudoword reading. >> Reading is fastest for **high-frequency words**. >> **Regularity** also affects accuracy and response time. >> **Frequency and regularity interact** with each other. >> In **acquired dyslexia**, pseudoword reading and irregular word reading are **dissociable**. Either one can be impaired while the other stays intact. || So the model hypothesizes two routes. >> The **sublexical route** does **grapheme-to-phoneme conversion**, also called **decoding**. A grapheme is a written unit that stands for a sound. >> The **lexical route** recognizes real words by using knowledge of particular words. || The dissociation in dyslexia carries the most weight. Two abilities that can be impaired independently suggest two independent processes. That is why the model has one route for decoding and one route for known words.
3|**Coltheart and colleagues (2001)** built the **Dual Route Cascaded model**, called the **DRC**. It joins an interactive lexical route to a separate rule route. || Three design points matter. >> The lexical part of the DRC is just the **Interactive Activation model**. >> The DRC has **no implementation of semantics**. The **orthographic lexicon** holds written word forms. The **phonological lexicon** holds spoken word forms. Both lexicons are independent of meaning. >> The **Grapheme-Phoneme Rule System** has completely different processing assumptions from the interactive part. || Both routes send activation toward a spoken response. The rule system joins the output instead of becoming another interactive layer. || So the DRC uses two different kinds of computation. That is why it counts as two routes and not one network.
4|The **Grapheme-Phoneme Rule System** in the DRC has four important features. >> Its rules are activated **serially**, letter by letter. >> It is **not affected by frequency** of occurrence in any way. >> It has **no feedback**. >> It **does not interact** with the lexical route, except at one place. || That place is the **phoneme response buffer**. Both routes connect to the same output units there. >> Because they share output units, the two routes can **compete** to activate different patterns. >> For an exception word, the lexical route favors the correct pronunciation. The rule route favors a regularized pronunciation. The two pronunciations compete in the buffer. || So the routes stay separate inside but meet at the output. That is why competing pronunciations can affect naming even without feedback into the rules.
5|**GPC rules** map written units onto phonemes. Coltheart and colleagues list them as the DRC's nonlexical rules. >> **Multiletter rules** convert a group of letters into one phoneme. Examples include eigh, ough, tsch and ph. >> **Context-sensitive rules** convert a grapheme only when certain letters come before or after it. For instance, c becomes s before e. >> Each rule has a **position**. It may apply at all positions, at the beginning, in the middle or at the end. || A list of rules is not enough by itself. The system also needs **meta-rules** for when to apply rules at different levels of analysis. >> One meta-rule says that **larger-unit rules supersede smaller-unit rules**. So a multiletter rule wins over the rules for its single letters. || So the GPC procedure is more than one sound per letter. It needs both the rules and an order for using them.
6|The GPC rules are **not totally independent of statistics**. Each rule is often based on the **most frequent pronunciation** for a letter or group of letters. || But the rules are **fixed and categorical**. A rule either applies or it does not, with no strength attached. >> For **EIGH**, /eI/ has a proportion of 0.667 and /aI/ has 0.333. When the final letter is not T, /eI/ rises to 1. >> For **OUGH**, support is spread over five pronunciations. /ɔ/ is most common at 0.53. /әf/ and /oʊ/ follow at 0.16 each. >> When the final letter is not T, /ɔ/ and /әf/ tie at 0.27 for OUGH. >> So the OUGH rule has **weaker statistical support** than the EIGH rule. || Even so, both are rules, and they **behave the same way during processing**. Statistics help choose each rule. Graded statistics play no part while reading. || That is why the DRC treats a weak rule and a strong rule identically.
7|The DRC's GPC rules are kind of silly, because they **seem to miss a lot of cases**. Its designers saw this as a **feature, not a bug**. || Better rules would be possible. >> Rules could take statistics into account more flexibly. >> The DRC's rules did go through several revisions to improve its **nonword reading**. || The DRC argues that people **do not use finely tuned rules** for the GPC route. >> If people did, there would be no pattern of **regularization errors** in **surface dyslexia**. A regularization error gives an exception word the pronunciation its spelling rules predict. >> There would also be no **slow-down for low-frequency exception words**. || So the simple rules are meant to explain human errors as well as correct reading. That is why a rule set that misses cases can be the better model of people.
8|**Informational encapsulation** means a process does not use every source of relevant knowledge. It works only with its own kind of information. || Models of syntax used the same idea. >> The parsing strategies **late closure** and **minimal attachment** were motivated by syntactic complexity only. >> The aim was to explain syntactic processing as encapsulated from world knowledge and lexical co-occurrence statistics. || The dual-route models are designed to be **modular** in the same way. >> The GPC route chugs along without lexical feedback. It produces correct pronunciations for many words. >> It produces incorrect responses to some **exceptions** to its rules. || The GPC route's operation can be observed only occasionally. >> It appears when people produce **regularization errors** on exception words. >> It appears when people are made to **name pseudowords**, which have no stored entry. || So encapsulation predicts errors even when another part of the system knows the right answer.
9|The DRC can **simulate many phenomena** in visual word recognition. **Coltheart and colleagues (2001)** listed them by task. >> In **reading aloud**, it simulates frequency, lexicality, regularity and consistency effects. It also simulates the interaction of regularity with frequency. >> In **lexical decision**, it simulates the word frequency effect and the pseudohomophone effect, among others. >> In **acquired dyslexias**, it simulates patterns in surface dyslexia and phonological dyslexia. || The authors relied on past reading research instead of backpropagation for ideas about the architecture. They took the same view as **Grainger and Jacobs (1998)**. >> That view holds that **human behavioral data** are currently the major source of constraint on models of cognition. || So the DRC is judged on how many human findings one architecture reproduces. Which assumptions stay fixed across tasks also matters.
10|Modeling **lexical decision** in the DRC sometimes means **going to extremes** to make contact with data. Lexical decision is deciding whether a written item is a word. || First, the model **reduces letter-word inhibition**. This change makes the task harder. Without it, the results make no sense. || The model answers **yes** by either of two criteria. >> If any single word has activity above **0.69**, it answers yes. >> If total activity over the **orthographic input lexicon** exceeds a threshold called **S**, it answers yes. >> S is 10 from 0 to 20 cycles. It drops to 1.98 if lexicon activity exceeds 0.2 at 20 cycles. || The model answers **no** if neither criterion is met by a deadline called **D**. >> D is 42 cycles, unless lexicon activity exceeds 0.112 after 20 cycles. In that case D is set to 48. >> This deadline is nearly twice as fast as the model normally reads aloud. || So the decision rules add many assumptions beyond the two routes. That is why this fit counts as going to extremes.
11|The key feature of the DRC is **modularity**. The GPC route and the lexical route are modular in three ways. >> Each route has its **own processing assumptions**. >> The routes are **informationally encapsulated** from one another. So neither route feeds information into the other. >> The routes are **domain-specific**. The GPC rules can only take letters as input. They can only pass phoneme activations to the phoneme system. || These are three separate claims. One concerns how each route computes. One concerns how the routes communicate. One concerns what input each route accepts. || So modularity is more than having two pathways. That is why two arrows in a model do not make it modular by themselves.
12|**Seidenberg and McClelland (1989)** took an **emergentist approach** to visual word recognition with a **PDP model**. In this approach, reading skill **emerges from learning**. || They saw reading skill as mappings among three kinds of information. >> **Orthography** is the spelling of words. >> **Phonology** is pronunciation, or sound structure. >> **Semantics** is the meaning of words. || They modeled how these mappings are learned. >> They trained a **simple associative network**. >> They studied the mapping from **orthography to phonology** in a **single route**. Every kind of word used that one mapping. || So reading is not a hand-built list of rules and word entries in this model. It is what the network learns from experience with many words.
13|The PDP model of **Seidenberg and McClelland (1989)** makes **different assumptions** from dual-route models. || Two of its assumptions differ. >> **Rules** are just **generalizations** from lots of words. They are not a separate process. >> The **lexicon** is **distributed**. Some whole-word information is stored in the orthography to phonology mapping. The rest is stored in other parts of the system. || The **research strategy** differs too. >> PDP models explain reading with **domain-general learning mechanisms**. These are learning mechanisms not special to reading. >> Dual-route models instead **reverse-engineer** the system from adult and neuropsychological data. || So one learned system holds both shared patterns and facts about single words. Distributed does not mean the model has no memory of individual words.
14|The PDP model captured the **frequency by regularity interaction**. It did this **without** assuming that the two effects come from **different processes**. || The model answers how in three steps. >> **Regular words** are consistent with statistical patterns from many other words. So they get support from those words. >> **Exception words** are inconsistent with these statistics. So they lack that support. >> **Frequency** affects how well each word is learned. So highly frequent words are **less affected** by inconsistency with other words. || The hardest case is a **low-frequency exception word**. It has weak learning of its own. It also lacks support from other words. || So both effects come from one learning mechanism. That is why they interact without a separate rule system.
15|PDP models **redefine regularity** as **consistency**. Consistency depends on a word's **friends** and **enemies**. Friends are similar spellings with the same pronunciation. Enemies are similar spellings with a different pronunciation. || The four categories run from most support to least. >> **Regular consistent** words have many friends and no enemies, like LATE and DUST. >> **Regular inconsistent** words have many friends but at least one enemy. GAVE has the enemy HAVE. MINT has the enemy PINT. >> **Ambiguous** words have about equal numbers of friends and enemies. Examples are DOWN and KNOWN, and POUR and SOUR. >> **Exception** words have few if any friends and many enemies. HAVE has the enemy GAVE. PINT has the enemy MINT. || So spelling-to-sound support comes in degrees. A plain regular or irregular label hides those degrees.
16|A PDP model reads **words and nonwords** in the **same route**. In principle, this works in two ways. >> **Nonwords** are named with the **same set of weights** that the network learned for words. >> **Generalization** uses the same statistical patterns that give rise to **regularity effects**. || A new spelling overlaps with familiar spellings. So the network can pronounce it without a stored entry for that nonword. || So nonword reading does not require a separate GPC rule module. That claim still has to be checked against how the model actually performs.
17|In practice, the PDP model's **nonword accuracy was not very good**. This is a **classic case of ambiguity in model evaluation**. || The failure has two possible causes. >> The **theoretical claim** might be wrong. That claim is that nonwords and exceptions are read by the same **associative mechanism**. >> Or the problem might be **specific to how the model was made**. || The evidence points to the second cause. >> The model's **phonological representations**, called **Wickelfeatures**, were poor. >> A later model with better phonological representations read nonwords better. || So a failed simulation does not reveal its own cause. That is why improving the implementation while keeping the theory helps tell the two causes apart.
18|**Nonword reading** is related to **phonological representations**. Many studies find a link between **decoding**, meaning nonword reading, and **phonological processing**. || Seidenberg and McClelland used odd phonological representations called **Wickelfeatures**. These represented the sound structure of words poorly. || **Plaut and colleagues (1996)** improved the phonological representations. Their model produced **better performance on nonwords**. || So part of the early failure came from the representation, not the theory. That is why decoding improved without adding a rule system.
19|**Generalization depends on similarity**. This principle is inherited from **behaviorism**. || Poor phonological representations, like **Wickelfeatures**, fail to capture the **similarity space** of words in the language. So the network cannot generalize well to new forms. || Words and nonwords suffer differently. >> **Word-specific information** is more robust to poor representations. It does not depend entirely on generalization. >> A **nonword** has no word-specific information. So it depends much more on generalization. || So poor phonology hurts nonwords more than words. That is why a nonword deficit can arise without a separate nonword mechanism.
20|**Harm and Seidenberg (1999)** studied this link more systematically. They manipulated the amount of **noise** in the model's phonological representations. More noise makes the sound representations less reliable. || The noisy models replicated key findings on **developmental dyslexia**. >> The degree of **phonological deficit** was related to the degree of **phonological dyslexia**. More noise went with worse nonword decoding. >> **Word recognition** was **relatively spared**. || Noise was the property they changed. Word and nonword reading were the outcomes. || So a reading deficit that looks selective can come from a weak general phonological system. That is why nonwords, which rely on generalization, suffer most.
21|The PDP account also has to explain **double dissociations**. Nonword reading can already be specifically impaired without a specific nonword reading mechanism. || **Plaut and colleagues (1996)** explored the other end of the dissociation, **surface dyslexia**. Surface dyslexia is a specific impairment on exception words. >> They implemented a **simplified semantic system**. >> They **lesioned** semantics, meaning they damaged that part of the model. >> The lesion caused a **specific impairment on exception words**. || Exception words get little support from typical spelling-to-sound patterns. So they rely more on support from meaning. || So damage to semantics produces surface dyslexia in the model. That is why no separate exception-word mechanism is needed.
22|The triangle model is **not just the dual-route model renamed**. Three differences explain why. >> **Semantic representations** are directly involved. The DRC instead commits to a lexicon independent of semantics. >> A later model by **Harm and Seidenberg (2004)** implemented featural semantics. It also simulated the mapping of spelling to meaning. >> The **OP** (orthography to phonology) and **SP** (semantics to phonology) mappings share **processing assumptions** and a learning algorithm. Both mappings are **probabilistic**. >> The pathways **interact over learning**. The OP pathway develops differently next to an OSP pathway than it would alone. || This interactivity and gradedness explain why double dissociations are **never entirely clean**. >> **Phonological dyslexics** are not perfect on exception words. >> **Surface dyslexics** are not perfect on nonwords. || So multiple pathways do not make a model modular. That is why the triangle model and the DRC stay different accounts.
23|**Woollams and colleagues (2007)** tested a prediction of the triangle model. The model predicts a **strong association** between **semantic abilities** and **exception word reading**. || They compared model runs with patients. >> In the models, weaker semantic activation went with lower accuracy on **low-frequency exception words**. The fit was strong, with an R squared of 0.84. >> In patients with **semantic dementia**, lower semantic scores went with lower exception word accuracy. The fit was weaker, with an R squared of 0.50. || So poorer semantics goes with poorer exception reading in both models and patients. Individual patients still vary around the trend.
24|The triangle model **misses a few data points**. A few patients are **statistical outliers**. >> Some patients have unusually high semantic scores. >> Some patients have unusually high exception word accuracy, even with lower semantic scores. || Woollams and colleagues say what to make of this. They would treat these patients **the way outliers are normally treated** when evaluating a model's fit to data. || A graded association does not predict zero variability. So a few outliers do not refute the model by themselves. || The real question is whether the outliers show a stable separate pattern. Repeated testing of the same patients addresses that question.
25|Woollams and colleagues studied a **progressive condition**, **semantic dementia**. So they could follow **exception word reading** as **semantic abilities declined**. || In most cases, a patient's **reading ability declined** when her **semantic abilities declined** over time. || Repeated observations also showed **regression to the mean**. Unusual first scores moved closer to the general trend on later tests. >> If the dissociation between semantics and exception reading came from **idiosyncratic outliers**, it would show just this pattern. || So repeated testing strengthens the association. That is why a one-time unusual score is weak evidence against the triangle model.
26|**Coltheart and colleagues (2009)** simulated the same surface dyslexia data with the **DRC**. || They generated **40,000 model patients**. These varied on two parameters. >> One parameter was the **integrity of the lexical route**. >> The other parameter was the **integrity of the GPC rules**. || For each patient in the Woollams data, they **selected the model run** that best fit that patient. || This produced an **excellent fit**. Patient and model accuracy on low-frequency exception words stayed close as semantic impairment grew more severe. || So the selection step is part of how the fit was achieved. That is why this fit differs from predicting each patient in advance.
27|A better fit can be a **bad thing** when it comes from **flexibility**. The DRC fit was chosen from 40,000 model patients after seeing each patient's data. || The two models reached their fits differently. >> The **DRC** picked the best match for each patient from many parameter settings. So it accommodated each case after the outcome. >> The **triangle model** predicted the association between semantics and exception reading. || Close agreement is not bad in itself. A flexible model can match many possible patterns. So its match does not strongly constrain the explanation. || That is why goodness of fit is only one part of evaluating a model.
28|**Dual-route** and **triangle models** both cover a range of reading data. They share four basic facts. >> **Decoding** is fast and automatic, as in pseudoword reading. >> Words that diverge from typical spelling-to-sound mappings are **harder to read**. >> This **regularity** or **consistency effect** interacts with **word frequency**. >> Pseudoword reading and exception or inconsistent word reading are **dissociable** in neuropsychological patients. || The models agree on these facts but not on mechanisms. Dual-route models use specialized routes. Triangle models use learning across interacting systems. || So the contest is over explanation, not over whether the facts exist.
29|The two models give **different explanations** for the same four phenomena. || For **pseudoword reading and regularity effects**, the **triangle** model uses statistical regularities learned over many words. The **dual-route** model uses a dedicated **GPC rule system**. || For **frequency effects**, the triangle model says more exposure to a word gives better learning for that word. The dual-route model uses distinct input and output lexicons, separate from the GPC rules. || For **phonological dyslexia**, the triangle model points to a deficit in **phonological processing** generally. The dual-route model points to damage in the **GPC route**. || For **surface dyslexia**, the triangle model points to a deficit in **semantic processes** generally. The dual-route model points to damage in the **lexical route**. || So each finding stays the same while its proposed cause differs.
30|The **key contrast** is between **dissociations** and **associations**. || **Dual-route models** are generally motivated by **double dissociations**. Pseudowords and exception words can be impaired independently. So they should be modeled as independent processes. || Models informed by **statistical learning** instead reveal **associations**. >> **Phonology** is important for learning and using orthography to phonology generalizations. So phonological damage hurts pseudoword reading. >> **Semantics** is important for words where that generalization is ambiguous. So semantic damage hurts exception words. || So the triangle account ties each dyslexia to a broader language ability. That is why phonological and semantic measures help explain reading deficits.
