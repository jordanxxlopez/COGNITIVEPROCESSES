2|**Linguistic relativity** asks whether differences in language change how people organize or use concepts. >> The quotations from **Sapir (1929)** and **Whorf (1956)** suggest that **linguistic categories** can shape how experience is interpreted. >> The strongest reading says language determines what a person can think. >> A weaker reading says habitual language use biases attention, memory, or preferred ways of representing a situation. || These two readings need different evidence. >> The quotations are broad theoretical ideas, not experiments. || **Influencing thought** is different from **completely determining thought**. >> Keep that difference in mind when interpreting a difference between language groups. >> So the real question is how strong the claim is, and what evidence can test it.
3|**Language is embedded in cultural practices and experience.** >> A difference between language communities may go along with factors other than linguistic structure. >> Sapir and Whorf should not automatically be given the strongest reading of their quotations. >> In the first half of the twentieth century, linguistics was part of **anthropology**. >> So treating language as part of culture seemed more natural then than it does to linguists today. || **Testing linguistic relativity needs a narrower claim.** >> **Domains** (specific areas of experience) such as space and time let researchers test preferred ways of representing things. >> A precise study must identify which **cognitive process** (a mental operation such as remembering) is affected. >> It must also check which alternative explanations the comparison controls. || Historical framing explains why language and culture were discussed together. >> A study has to separate their contributions.
4|**A location description needs a frame of reference.** >> A **frame of reference** is the set of directions used to say where something is. >> Saying where the ball is requires choosing what counts as left, front, or a stable direction. >> The ball can be located relative to the viewer, relative to the chair's own orientation, or by a direction independent of both. || **Changing the reference changes the relation being described.** >> The object does not have to move for its verbal description to change. >> Different descriptions can identify the same physical position. >> The question is which reference determines the ball's location. >> The chair and ball are the meaningful stimuli, not their appearance.
5|**An intrinsic frame uses an object's own orientation.** >> "At the chair's front" is an intrinsic description. >> **A relative frame** uses the observer's viewpoint, so "to the left of the chair from my viewpoint" depends on the observer. >> **An absolute frame** uses a stable direction, so "north of the chair" does not reverse when the observer turns around. || These are different ways to specify the same location. >> The object's orientation, the observer's body, and a stable outside direction are not interchangeable. >> The ball and chair example keeps the scene the same and changes only the reference. >> Rotating the observer changes relative left and right, but it does not change north. || This classification comes from **Levinson and colleagues (2002)**.
6|**Most languages describe nearby space egocentrically.** >> **Egocentric** descriptions use the speaker's own position and orientation. >> Left, right, in front, and behind are familiar examples for nearby objects. || **Tzeltal commonly uses absolute directions instead.** >> Tzeltal is a Western Mayan language spoken in Chiapas. >> It uses absolute directions in places where English speakers might use relative terms. >> A small number of other languages also use absolute directions. >> Those languages are mostly unrelated to one another and broadly distributed, such as in Bali and Queensland. || **The contrast raises a question about habits.** >> The question is whether habitual language use relates to nonverbal spatial coding. >> The comparison concerns preferred spatial descriptions.
7|**Tzeltal's uphill and downhill terms work as an absolute directional system.** >> Things are uphill or downhill from one another, not to the left or right of the speaker. >> "Uphill" and "downhill" are used as cardinal directions. >> The system was historically based on landmarks. || **A landmark origin and an absolute use are different properties.** >> Each use does not simply point to the nearest visible hill. >> The direction is kept as a stable reference instead of being recalculated from the speaker's current left and right. || **A stable direction keeps positions consistent when the speaker changes orientation.** >> The relevant feature is how the reference behaves across changes in location and orientation.
8|**New Yorkers use uptown and downtown as if they were cardinal directions.** >> These terms do not work outside Manhattan. >> So they are literally a landmark-based system. >> The subway map is also confusing about north and south. >> Manhattan is angled with respect to magnetic north. || **A local landmark system is not an absolute frame that works everywhere.** >> Uptown and downtown organize movement within Manhattan. >> A city's conventional map orientation can differ from magnetic north. >> The Manhattan example is about reference systems, not about causation.
9|**Majid and colleagues (2004)** used a non-linguistic test for relative and absolute coding of space. >> In the **chips task**, the participant is rotated 180 degrees. >> The participant sees a configuration, waits, and then chooses its match at a second table. || A **relative response** keeps the arrangement with respect to the person's viewpoint. >> An **absolute response** keeps its orientation in the environment. >> After rotation, the two responses diverge and predict different choices. || **Dutch speakers predominantly gave relative responses.** >> **Tzeltal speakers gave more absolute responses.** >> The north arrow stays fixed while the person changes orientation. >> Red bars mark relative choices and blue bars mark absolute choices. >> The association fits each group's preferred linguistic frame. >> It does not isolate language from all other experience.
10|**Spatial inference can combine relations learned in different displays.** >> Participants learn the relation between two objects. >> They rotate to another display and learn a second relation involving a shared object. >> They then place one object relative to another using the combined information. || **Relative and absolute coding lead to different placements.** >> The person's orientation has changed between the displays. >> The cube appears in both learning displays, linking the cone and cylinder relations. >> The final placement separates a viewer-relative inference from one that keeps environmental direction. || **Dutch and Tzeltal response patterns differ again.** >> Each pattern follows the direction associated with that group's preferred spatial frame. >> The task asks whether a frame is used when combining relations. >> That goes beyond reproducing one remembered arrangement.
11|**Testing conditions may contribute to the apparent language difference.** >> **Li and Gleitman** noted that Tzeltal participants were tested outside, with visible, familiar landmarks. >> Typical psychology lab studies are done in windowless rooms. >> An environment with useful landmarks can encourage landmark-based coding. >> A windowless room can make viewer-relative descriptions more convenient. || **A language comparison can be confounded with a setting comparison.** >> The environment must be considered before the whole difference is assigned to linguistic structure. >> The critical contrast is access to spatial cues, not simply the label "laboratory." >> Windows, outdoor views, and familiar surroundings can alter which references are useful. || **Manipulating landmarks within English speakers tests this directly.** >> The test checks whether the behavior changes without changing native language.
12|**English speakers overwhelmingly used relative position when the blinds were down.** >> This resembles the Dutch speakers in earlier studies. >> The preference was less pronounced with the blinds up and a view of the street. >> It was also less pronounced outdoors. || **Changing the landmarks changes responses within one language group.** >> The language group stays the same while the spatial information changes. >> So task context can influence the response. >> This challenges an explanation in which language alone fixes the chosen frame. || **The change is a weakening, not a complete switch to absolute coding.** >> The observed change should be described without exaggerating it into an all-or-none reversal.

14|**If language groups are tested under different conditions, their response difference may partly reflect those conditions.** >> Visible landmarks and familiarity can covary with native language in a cross-community study. >> A controlled comparison must separate the effect of the environment from any contribution of habitual linguistic coding. || One possibility is that the bias toward relative reasoning is an **artifact** (a result produced by how the study is run). >> So an uncontrolled comparison cannot separate language from testing conditions. || **A confound is a factor that changes with the variable of interest.** >> It offers another explanation for the result. >> It is not just any feature present in the experiment.
15|**Levinson and colleagues were not impressed with the design.** >> They claimed that Li and Gleitman made the rotation too transparent. >> Low memory load and short time or distance between displays make it easy to see what is being tested. >> Li and Gleitman's participants were Ivy League students. >> Ivy League students are good at figuring out what the experimenter wants. || **Participants who infer the purpose can change their responses.** >> This is a **demand-characteristic explanation** (the study's apparent purpose shapes participants' answers). >> Interpreting the experiment can shift the response apart from normal coding preference. >> So the response focuses on memory load and task transparency. >> It does not deny that landmarks can ever matter.
16|**Levinson and colleagues (2002) re-ran the animals in a row experiment with Dutch college students.** >> They added a fourth animal and lengthened the delay to increase memory load. >> They enlarged the distance between the stimulus and response tables to reduce the salience of the rotation. || **Under these conditions, the bias to use relative framing remained.** >> The extra item and longer delay make memory more demanding. >> The larger distance makes the rotation less conspicuous. >> So the environmental effect depends on how the task is arranged. || **Each procedural change has a purpose.** >> A relative preference can persist when the memory task is more demanding. >> This narrows the conditions under which the landmark explanation accounts for behavior.
17|**Memory can make the relative preference hard to interpret.** >> The bias toward relative frames is robust to the environment when memory is a significant component of the task. >> It is also robust when the experimenter's interest in rotation is not obvious. >> Under memory demands, the relative preference could come from a verbal memory as well as a spatial one. || **Remembering the task can recruit verbal strategies as well as spatial ones.** >> So researchers need to ask how the sequence is stored during the delay. >> They should not look only at which direction is chosen at the end. || **A task can be nonverbal in its response while still allowing verbal recoding internally.** >> The two conditions say when the effect is robust.
18|**Participants might remember the animals as a verbal list.** >> One example is the list "lion, tiger, bear." >> Participants could reconstruct that sequence from left to right. >> That reconstruction can produce a response that looks relative. || **This strategy connects language to the task through working memory and ordering habits.** >> The same choice can arise from different internal codes. || **A list preserves order but not compass direction.** >> Translating that order into a left-to-right display can mimic a spatial frame preference. >> A later page links reading direction to how sequences are arranged in space. >> That link may help explain why left to right feels like a natural translation of a temporal sequence.
19|**Metaphorical language expresses one kind of experience using terms from another.** >> Calling an argument ineffective describes how well it works. >> It is not a literal action with a blade. >> Physical and spatial terms can help express abstract relations. >> Time is especially useful for studying whether such habits go with nonlinguistic representations. || **An expression can be meaningful without being literal.** >> Whether spatial phrases about time go with spatial thinking about time is an empirical question. || **A lyric from Brown and Byrd (1970) is one example of metaphorical expression.** >> It raises the question of how much of language can safely be described as non-metaphorical.
20|**Boroditsky and colleagues (2010)** compared spatial metaphors for time in different languages. >> English and Mandarin both use horizontal spatial language for time. >> Mandarin also frequently uses vertical terms. >> An earlier event can be described with an "up" term, and a later event with a "down" term. || **The existence of these expressions is the starting observation.** >> A behavioral task is needed to investigate whether the vertical contrast extends beyond words. || **Habitual ways of talking about time suggest testable predictions about processing.** >> In these examples, "up" for an earlier month is a temporal use. >> It is not a claim that the month occupies a physical location.
21|**Boroditsky (2001)** ran a priming experiment on spatial and temporal processing. >> Participants first process a horizontal or vertical spatial relation. >> They then respond to questions about whether one event is earlier or later. >> English speakers respond faster after horizontal primes than after vertical primes. >> Mandarin speakers have the opposite pattern in the comparison tested. || **Short-term training changes the pattern.** >> Training English speakers on vertical metaphors for time moves their results toward a vertical prime advantage. >> This suggests sensitivity to experience, not an unchangeable division between language groups. || **The prime changes which representation is active before the temporal judgment.** >> Response time differences reveal compatibility between the prime and the later task. >> Lower reaction times mean faster processing. >> So horizontal and vertical results should be compared within each group.
22|**Casasanto and Bottini (2005)** point to reading direction as an alternative explanation. >> Reading and writing habits can also organize sequences spatially. >> Differences attributed to temporal metaphors may covary with whether text is read left to right, right to left, or vertically. >> Casasanto and Bottini use differences in text orientation and arrangement preferences to support this idea. || **Language-group differences can bundle linguistic and literacy experiences together.** >> So reading direction needs to be measured or controlled. >> The diagrams distinguish starting side from line direction. >> The table lists several arrangement patterns within the sampled groups, so individual experience matters.
23|**Boroditsky and colleagues (2010)** excluded Chinese readers who reported reading top to bottom. >> This tests whether the vertical temporal effect can be explained entirely by that reported reading experience. >> English speakers have an advantage for a left-is-earlier horizontal mapping. >> Mandarin speakers also have an advantage for a top-is-earlier vertical mapping in the comparison tested. || **Retaining a vertical effect under this restriction weakens that reading explanation.** >> A targeted control tests one specific alternative. || **Lower reaction times indicate a mapping advantage.** >> White bars are canonical mappings, and black bars are noncanonical mappings. >> For vertical trials, canonical means top is earlier.
24|**Casasanto and Bottini (2010)** had participants classify events as past or future with colored buttons. >> The color to category mapping was reversed across blocks. >> So the past response was sometimes on the left and sometimes on the right. || **With standard text reading, responses are faster when past maps left and future maps right.** >> Because the response concerns time rather than an explicit spatial judgment, the effect reveals an implicit spatial association. >> Color alone cannot explain the pattern, since the mappings are **counterbalanced** (the two mappings are used in turn). || **The effect concerns response time across mappings.** >> It does not change which events count as past or future. >> The left to right temporal mapping has the lower reaction times.
25|**Casasanto and Bottini (2010)** had participants read reversed text and then perform the past and future button task. >> By the second mirror-reading block, the compatibility effect reverses. >> Responses are then faster when earlier maps right and later maps left. || **This result supports a contribution of reading direction experience.** >> The association between space and time can change over a short period. >> A flexible temporal mapping is hard to explain as a fixed consequence of native language grammar alone. >> Recent perceptual and reading experience contributes to performance. || **The first block keeps the usual advantage.** >> **The second block** favors the opposite mapping. >> Compare the two colors within each block before comparing overall reaction times between blocks.
26|**Pullum (1989)** criticized an account of different word roots that became an exaggerated story about language and thought. >> His critique targets exaggerated claims about language and thought. >> Claims about a language's vocabulary are not automatic evidence for cognitive limits. >> Languages can express distinctions through separate roots, derived words, or phrases. >> Counting one of these forms without considering the others can misrepresent expressive capacity. || **A reliable claim requires accurate linguistic description and an appropriate behavioral test.** >> Repeating a striking anecdote does not meet that standard. || **Languages can package the same distinctions in different ways.** >> The excerpt distinguishes roots from **derivationally related forms** (words built from another word) and phrases. >> That distinction is why simple vocabulary counts can be misleading.
