2|**Scripts differ in how regularly their spelling matches their sounds, and English is a relatively deep script.** This difference is called **orthographic depth** (how consistently spelling corresponds to pronunciation). >> A **deep** script has many irregular spellings. >> A **shallow** script has regular letter-sound matches. || The script types line up as follows. >> **English** is relatively deep, due to its irregularities. >> **Finnish** and **Korean** are very shallow. >> **Pinyin** for Mandarin and **French** are relatively regular. || A **"mispronouncing contest"** made the point. >> People willfully mispronounced COLONEL and PARADIGM by following their letters. >> **Phonemes are not letters.** >> **Letters are not phonemes** either.
3|**Finnish spelling is so transparent that children learn to decode it very quickly.** The **Orthographic Depth Hypothesis** calls a transparent script **shallow**. >> Each letter corresponds more or less unambiguously to one **phoneme** (a sound category of the language). >> **Doubled consonants** stand for **geminated** (held longer) consonants. >> **Doubled vowels** stand for long vowels. || Following a beginning Finnish lesson with its text makes the system clear. >> After about a minute, a listener gets the idea. || **Decoding** (producing a pronunciation from a written form) is easy to learn here. >> One letter-sound match works across many words.
4|**Children learn to decode more slowly in English than in most other alphabetic languages.** English is an **outlier** among languages that use the Roman alphabet. || The test uses **pseudowords** (made-up words that follow a language's spelling and sound patterns). >> A pseudoword cannot be read from memory. >> It must be decoded from letter-sound patterns. || The comparison is children in **Grade 1**. >> Children learning many other languages give plausible pseudoword pronunciations at a **much higher rate**. >> Children learning English give them at a lower rate. >> English spelling is less predictable, so the patterns are harder to use.
5|**Korean writing is even more regular than Finnish, because similar sounds get similar symbols.** The letters sit in **syllable blocks** with initial, medial and sometimes final positions. >> The symbols for **similar phonemes** look similar to one another. >> So a letter's shape reflects how its sound is made. || **Velar place of articulation** (made with the back of the tongue at the soft palate) has a shared written feature. >> The velar consonants share the angular shape **ㄱ**. >> Doubling the shape or adding a stroke separates **g**, **kk** and **k**.
6|**The frequency effect in naming is much larger for exception words than for regular words.** This landmark finding is the **frequency by regularity interaction**. >> **Naming latency** (time to start saying a written word) is the measure. >> A **regular word** follows the usual spelling-sound pattern. An **exception word** does not. || The two frequency levels give different results. >> **High-frequency** exception and regular words are both named in about 540 ms. >> **Low-frequency** exception words take about 580 ms. >> **Low-frequency** regular words take only about 555 ms. || An **interaction** means the effect of one factor depends on the other. >> Being an exception costs time mainly when a word is rare. >> The finding has impacted other areas, most immediately **inflectional morphology**.
7|**One interpretation says reading uses two separate processes, and only rare words and nonwords reveal the slower one.** >> The **sublexical process** is decoding from spelling units. >> The **lexical process** is knowledge of a particular word. || Each word type relies on the processes differently. >> **High-frequency (HF) words** use a lexical process so fast that decoding has little influence. >> **Low-frequency (LF) words** use a slower lexical process, so decoding shows up. >> **Nonwords** have no stored entry, so they depend on decoding. || For a low-frequency exception, decoding gives a regularized pronunciation. >> That wrong output competes with the real one. >> So low-frequency exceptions are slow.
8|**Seidenberg and McClelland's (1989) PDP model explains reading with general learning instead of separate rules and a separate lexicon.** A **PDP model** is a network that learns connections between spelling and sound. || Its assumptions differ from dual-route models. >> **Rules** are just generalizations from lots of words, not a separate process. >> The **lexicon** is distributed. >> Some whole-word information sits in the **O to P mappings** (orthography to phonology). >> Some sits in other parts of the system. || Its research strategy also differs. >> Dual-route models reverse-engineer reading from adult and **neuropsychological** data. >> The PDP approach uses **domain-general learning mechanisms** (learning not specific to reading).
9|**The PDP model produced the frequency by regularity interaction from one learning process.** It did not assume that frequency and regularity come from different processes. || Each factor works through learning. >> **Regular words** are consistent with statistical patterns from many other words. >> **Exception words** are inconsistent with these statistics. >> **Frequency** affects how well a word is learned. || The factors combine. >> **High-frequency** words are learned well, so inconsistency affects them less. >> **Low-frequency exceptions** get weak learning and no support from other words. >> So low-frequency exceptions are the hardest.
10|**In a PDP model, nonwords are read through the same route as words.** A **nonword** is a made-up letter string with no stored entry. >> Nonwords are named with the **same set of weights** (learned connection strengths) used for words. >> No separate rule system is needed. || **Generalization** uses the same **statistical patterns** that create regularity effects. >> Regular words benefit from patterns shared with many words. >> New nonwords are pronounced by applying those same patterns.
11|**The Seidenberg and McClelland model read nonwords poorly, and the cause was unclear.** This is a classic case of **ambiguity in model evaluation**. || Two causes are possible. >> The **theoretical claim** might be wrong. The claim is that nonwords and exceptions use the same associative mechanism. >> Or the problem might be specific to **how the model was made**. || Repairing the model decides between them. >> A better implementation that reads nonwords well keeps the theory alive. >> Later work improved the **phonological representations** (how the model codes speech sounds).
12|**Better phonological representations made a later PDP model much better at reading nonwords.** Many studies relate **decoding** (nonword reading) to **phonological processing**. || The first model used odd representations. >> **Seidenberg and McClelland (1989)** coded sounds as **Wickelfeatures**. >> Wickelfeatures captured sound similarity poorly. >> So nonword reading suffered. || The later model fixed the representations. >> **Plaut and colleagues (1996)** improved the phonological representations. >> Their model produced **better nonword performance**. >> No separate rule system was added.
13|**Poor sound coding hurts nonwords more than words, because nonwords depend entirely on generalization.** **Generalization depends on similarity**, a principle inherited from **behaviorism**. || Wickelfeatures were a weak code. >> They failed to capture the **similarity space** (how words in the language relate in sound). >> So learning transferred poorly to new forms. || Words and nonwords fare differently. >> **Familiar words** have **word-specific information** from their own training. >> That information is **more robust** to poor representations. >> **Nonwords** have no word-specific information. >> So nonwords suffer most.
14|**Harm and Seidenberg (1999) added noise to a model's phonology and reproduced the pattern of developmental dyslexia.** **Developmental dyslexia** is a reading impairment that appears during development. || They manipulated the amount of **noise** in the phonological representations. >> More noise means less reliable sound codes. >> The rest of the model stayed the same. || The noisy models replicated two key findings. >> A bigger **phonological deficit** went with more **phonological dyslexia** (poor nonword decoding). >> **Word recognition** was relatively spared.
15|**Pinker and Ullman (2002) proposed a dual-route past-tense model with a lexicon for irregulars and a grammar for regulars.** The input is a **word stem** (walk, hold) and a **grammatical feature** (past tense). >> The **lexicon** uses lookup and association, giving held. >> The **grammar** combines stem and suffix, giving walk plus -ed. >> A **blocking** link lets a retrieved irregular stop the regular form. || The two routes match two memory systems. >> The lexicon is **declarative memory** (words and facts) in temporo-parietal cortex. >> The grammar is the **procedural system** (rules and skills) in frontal cortex and basal ganglia. || It resembles the reading dual-route model. >> Both split **word-specific knowledge** from a **productive operation**. || It also differs. >> Reading turns spelling into sound. >> This model changes a stem by a grammatical feature.
16|**The past-tense dual-route model looks like the reading model, but it came from different motives.** || The reading model came from patient data. >> It was built on **neuropsychological double dissociations** (patients whose impairments pattern in opposite ways). || The past-tense model came from linguistics. >> **Pinker and colleagues** were motivated by other concerns. >> **Generative linguistics** of the time was mostly about categorical, symbolic, rule-based grammars. >> These rules often have **exceptions**. >> So the model keeps exceptions apart from the rules.
17|**The dual-process model gives exceptions their own process, so the grammar can stay elegant.** Linguists often left exceptions out of the grammar rather than add machinery for them. || The second process has every property the rule route lacks. >> It is **word-specific** (lexical). >> It is **sensitive to frequency** of occurrence. >> It is **sensitive to phonological similarity**. || Similar irregulars form families. >> Keep-kept, weep-wept and leap-leapt share a pattern. >> The grammar stays symbolic and categorical.
18|**Rumelhart and McClelland (1987) proposed that the past tense is all associative learning.** One trainable network learns past tenses across all verbs. || The network has three parts. >> It encodes a verb root's sound as **Wickelfeatures**. >> A **pattern associator** with modifiable connections maps it onto a past-tense pattern. >> The output pattern is decoded into a past-tense form. || There is no separate -ed rule. >> **Regular forms** are a pattern learned from many examples. >> **Irregular forms** are learned in the same system.
19|**Children learn past tenses in a U shape, getting worse before they get better.** Rumelhart and McClelland's model targeted this **U-shaped learning**, because early learning models focused on development. || The three stages come in order. >> Children learn some frequent irregular verbs quite early, such as run-ran. >> Then, spontaneously, children produce **regularizations** (regular endings on irregular verbs) such as runned. >> Eventually, children produce correct forms again. || Performance goes down and then back up. >> That dip and recovery gives the U shape.
20|**The words and rules approach explains U-shaped learning as a series of distinct stages and processes.** This is why U-shaped learning matters to it. || Each stage has its own process. >> Early correct forms are just **imitations**. They do not use adult mechanisms. >> When the **rule** is first learned, it is applied to all cases. >> The rule is the best hypothesis for all items generally, so runned appears. >> Over time, **exceptions** are learned associatively, one by one. >> The **blocking process** develops, so a stored irregular blocks the rule.
21|**Rumelhart and McClelland's single network produced U-shaped learning because its training input changed over time.** The **training set** is based on data about input to children. || Early training is mostly irregular. >> The highest-frequency verbs tend to be irregular, such as GIVE, TAKE, HAVE and GO. >> The model learns these irregular forms first. || Later training adds regulars. >> As vocabulary expands, more **regular forms** enter. >> The model reorganizes to capture those regularities. >> This **interferes** with some early-learned forms. >> With further training, the irregular forms recover.
22|**Because the model learned only a few verbs, real English verbs could serve as a "wug" test of generalization.** A **wug test** asks for the past tense of a word the learner has not learned. || The test used **unfamiliar low-frequency regular verbs**. >> Many responses were correct, such as guarded, mapped and typed. >> Some were odd, such as shipped for shape. || Feature accuracy was high. >> The model generated **92 percent** of **Wickelfeatures** correctly for regular verbs. >> It generated 84 percent for irregular verbs and 91 percent overall. >> Correct features do not guarantee a fully correct word.
23|**The model sometimes gave bizarre past tenses that people would be unlikely to produce.** Just four responses added or changed consonants. >> Maded for mate. >> Squawked for squat. >> Membled for mail. >> Toureder for tour. || The authors responded in three ways. >> They said humans are unlikely to make these errors, especially the last two. >> They noted that most of these responses were **near threshold**. >> They suggested an **auto-associative network** among output units to clean up the output. >> They had not yet implemented it.
24|**Joanisse and Seidenberg (1999) proposed that phonology supports regular generalization and semantics supports irregular forms.** Their model links **speech input**, **semantics** (word meaning) and **speech output**. || The model has two premises. >> **Rule-like generalization** is supported by **phonological representations**. >> **Irregular forms** require greater support from **semantics**. || So the regular and irregular difference comes from shared systems. >> No separate rule route is needed.
25|**The Joanisse and Seidenberg (1999) model aims to explain why some patients fail on irregulars and others fail on nonwords.** This target is a **dissociation** in neuropsychological data. || Each item type leans on a different support. >> **Nonwords** have no meaning, so they depend on **phonology**. >> **Irregulars** break the regular pattern, so they depend more on **semantics**. || Damage predicts opposite patterns. >> Phonological damage should hurt nonwords most. >> Semantic damage should hurt irregulars most.
26|**Patients with Broca's aphasia are worst at putting nonwords into the past tense.** || The syndrome has three features. >> Injury is to **left anterior** brain regions. >> **Speech production** is poor. >> **Regular morphology** is impaired, especially for **nonwords**. || Typical items are wug to wugged and blick to blicked. >> Nonwords were about 69 percent correct. >> Regular verbs were about 82 percent correct. >> Irregular verbs were about 85 percent correct. || A nonword has no stored past tense. >> So this pattern points to a **phonological** problem.
27|**Wernicke's, Alzheimer's and anomic patients are worst at irregular past tenses.** || The anomic patients have injury to inferior or middle temporal brain regions. || Their scores reverse the Broca's pattern. >> Irregular verbs were about 60 percent correct. >> Regular verbs were about 89 percent correct. >> Nonwords were about 84 percent correct. || Irregulars need **semantic** support. >> So this pattern fits damage to semantics.
28|**The classical aphasia dichotomy treats Wernicke's aphasia as fluent nonsense and Broca's aphasia as halting grammar-poor speech.** || **Wernicke's aphasia** gives "word salad" or "Jabberwocky." >> It often includes **neologisms** (made-up words). >> Syntax is roughly intact. >> One patient said "My wires don't hire right" about his poor vision. >> Another called a fork "a need for a schedule." || **Broca's aphasia** gives **telegraphic** speech. >> Patients have trouble with **word order** and **grammatical particles**. >> Important **content words** are relatively spared. >> One patient's answer was fragments like "Two doctors ... and ah ... teeth."
29|**Broca's own patients would not necessarily count as Broca's aphasics today.** The dichotomy treats **Wernicke's aphasia** as a lexicon activation problem, perhaps inhibition. It treats **Broca's aphasia** as a **grammar** problem. || Broca described his cases differently. >> His patient **Leborgne** ("Tan") could only produce one jargon syllable. >> He seemed otherwise in possession of his senses. >> He seemed to understand what was said. || These patients were arguably unable to produce speech at all. >> The cause was apparently **motor control**. >> Today they would be classified as having **apraxia of speech** (trouble planning speech movements).
30|**The aphasia dichotomy is appealing because it matches the split between grammar and lexicon in formal linguistics.** Late 20th century formal linguistics drew this sharp distinction. || It starts with **Chomsky's** formulation of the **computational problem of language learning**. >> A new sentence is recognized because the internalized grammar generates it. >> It does not have to match a familiar sentence. || **Chomsky (1955)** argued for the **independence of syntax and semantics**. >> "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" is grammatical. >> "Furiously sleep ideas green colorless" is not. >> Neither had occurred in an English speaker's experience.
31|**The speech production model splits an arbitrary word-meaning level from a structural grammar level.** These are the **functional** and **positional** levels. || The levels run in order. >> The **message level** arouses concepts and event structure. >> The **functional level** arouses word meanings and semantic roles. >> The **positional level** puts word forms in grammatical order. >> **Phonological encoding** and **articulation** follow. || The levels differ in kind. >> **Lemmas** (word representations chosen for meaning) relate arbitrarily to their referents. >> **Syntactic trees** and **affixes** come from abstract syntactic processes. || HOUSEBOAT and DOGHOUSE seem non-arbitrary. >> But errors like "easy enoughly" place **derivational morphology** at the positional level.
32|**Most languages describe nearby space egocentrically, but Tzeltal uses absolute directions.** **Egocentric** terms use the speaker's body, such as left, right, in front and behind. || **Tzeltal** is a Western Mayan language spoken in Chiapas. >> It uses **absolute directions** (directions that stay fixed when a person turns). >> A few other languages do too, mostly unrelated and broadly distributed. >> Examples are found in Bali and Queensland. || To an English speaker, "raise your east hand" feels unnatural.
33|**Tzeltal's uphill and downhill are used as fixed cardinal directions, which makes the system absolute.** The system is historically **landmark-based**. || Objects are placed by slope terms. >> Things are **uphill** or **downhill** from one another. >> They are not left or right of the speaker or listener. || The terms act as **cardinal directions**. >> They come from the landscape around Tenejapa. >> They do not simply point to the nearest hill. >> **Levinson and colleagues (2002)** described the system.
34|**In a nonverbal memory task, Dutch speakers used relative space and Tzeltal speakers mostly used absolute space.** **Majid and colleagues (2004)** used the **chips task**. || The task has three steps. >> A participant sees a card with a large and a small dot at Table 1. >> After a 30-second delay, the participant is **rotated 180 degrees** to Table 2. >> The participant picks the matching card from four. || The rotation splits the two answers. >> A **relative** choice keeps the layout fixed to the body. >> An **absolute** choice keeps the layout fixed to the world, such as north. || The results matched each language. >> **Dutch** responses were overwhelmingly relative. >> **Tzeltal** responses were mostly absolute.
35|**Dutch and Tzeltal speakers also used their own spatial frames when combining relations to make an inference.** **Majid and colleagues (2004)** used a transitive inference task. || The steps run across two tables. >> Table 1 shows a cube and a cone. >> After a 180-degree turn, Table 2 shows the cube and a cylinder. >> Back at Table 1, the participant places the cylinder next to the cone. || The two frames predict different placements. >> **Relative** coding puts the cylinder to the right of the cone. >> **Absolute** coding puts the cylinder to the south of the cone. || **Dutch** participants gave relative responses. >> **Tzeltal** participants gave absolute responses.
36|**Li and Gleitman argued that the testing setting, not only language, could explain the Tzeltal results.** They tried **inducing landmark-based similarity in English speakers**. || The settings differed. >> **Tzeltal speakers** were tested outside, with visible, familiar **landmarks**. >> Typical psychology lab studies happen in **windowless rooms**. || Landmarks can encourage absolute coding. >> A windowless room makes relative coding more convenient.
37|**English speakers used relative coding less when they could see landmarks.** **Li and Gleitman** varied the view during testing. || Three conditions gave different preferences. >> With the **blinds down**, English speakers overwhelmingly chose **relative** position, like the Dutch. >> With the **blinds up** and a street view, the preference was **less pronounced**. >> **Outdoors**, the preference was also less pronounced. || Only the setting changed. >> The language group stayed the same. >> A weaker preference is not a full switch to absolute coding.
38|**Levinson and colleagues argued that Li and Gleitman's task made its purpose too easy to guess.** || They raised a task objection. >> Li and Gleitman made the rotation too **transparent**. >> They **reduced memory load**. >> They shortened the **time and distance** between stimulus and test. || They raised a participant objection. >> The participants were **Ivy League students**. >> Such students excel at figuring out what the experimenter wants and making it happen.
39|**Researchers asked whether signers show categorical perception for hand configuration.** **Categorical perception** means a gradual change is perceived as a sudden switch between categories. || Two continua were made. >> The **phonemic** continuum runs from the sign PLEASE to the sign SORRY. >> The **allophonic** continuum runs from open-N to closed-N within the sign SAY-NO-TO. || The predictions are specific. >> Categorical perception predicts a sharp **identification** boundary. >> It predicts the best **discrimination** at that boundary. >> It should appear only for the phonemic continuum.
40|**Deaf signers perceive the phonemic hand contrast categorically, but not the allophonic one.** The curves have the familiar categorical shape. || The **phonemic** condition is sharp. >> Identification has a fairly sharp **boundary** between stimuli 4 and 6. >> The boundary matches the **peak** in the **discrimination function**. || The **allophonic** condition is shallow. >> The identification function is much shallower, with many more ambiguous stimuli. >> The discrimination function has **no peak**.
41|**Sign language comprehension uses the same core language areas as speech, plus visual and spatial areas.** **Campbell, MacSweeney and Waters (2008)** compared deaf signers, hearing signers and hearing people perceiving audio-visual speech. || Sign is similar to speech. >> It is **left lateralized**. >> It uses **Broca's area** (inferior frontal) and **Wernicke's area** (posterior superior temporal). || Sign is different from speech. >> It uses more **visual areas** and fewer **auditory areas**. >> It uses **superior parietal** cortex, a classic spatial area.
42|**Sign language is a true language, and learning it also reshapes the brain.** || Four findings support this. >> Sign has **phonological structure**, and native signers show **categorical perception** for some contrastive features. >> Learning sign causes **brain reorganization**, especially **lateralization of motion processing**. >> Only **deaf signers** show enhanced visual attention in the **periphery**, though the periphery matters to all signers. >> Signed sentences activate **posterior STG** (Wernicke's area) more than organized, sequenced non-language hand movements do.
43|**Johnson and Newport (1989) see the maturational state hypothesis as the better fit to their data.** Both versions of the **critical period hypothesis** start from a superior early capacity for languages. || The versions differ on what causes decline. >> The **exercise hypothesis** says unused capacity declines with maturation. >> It says exercised capacity stays intact for life. >> The **maturational state hypothesis** says the capacity declines with maturation regardless. || Their data favor maturation. >> Their late learners had exercised the capacity on a first language. >> Late starters still did worse on the second language. || This changes the reading of **L1 critical period** data. >> Late L1 learners never exercised the capacity. >> So both versions predict their poor outcome. >> Early L1 experience still matters.
44|**Mayberry, Lock and Kazmi (2002) found that a first language learned late is learned poorly, while an early language helps later learning.** || The groups differed in early experience. >> Many **deaf children** get no sign input but have an otherwise typical upbringing. >> Children aged 9 to 15 struggle with **ASL** (American Sign Language) as a first language. >> Children who went deaf after learning a **spoken language** do much better. >> The differences **persist into adulthood**. || Adult scores matched this. >> On ASL recall, no early language gave about 36 percent. Early spoken language gave about 78 percent. >> On English grammar, no early language gave about 52 percent, near chance. Early signed or spoken language gave about 80 percent.
45|**The semantic blocking effect is not likely due to visual similarity between same-category objects.** The **semantic blocking effect** is slower naming when same-category objects are named together. || The worry is a **confound** (an alternative cause). >> Same-category objects might look alike. >> So the effect might arise in **visual object recognition**. || Two responses answer it. >> The stimuli were **normed**, so same-category objects were not more visually similar on average. >> A **control experiment** blocked by visual similarity and found different results. >> The visual effect appeared in the **first block of trials**. >> The semantic effect appears only after the first cycle.
46|**Belke and colleagues showed four objects at once but had people name them one by one.** || The design answers an earlier null result. >> **Hermens and colleagues** showed multiple objects simultaneously and found no blocking effect. >> Maybe blocking depends on seeing objects in serial. || The new design separates viewing from naming. >> Participants viewed **four items simultaneously**. >> They produced the names **in serial**. >> Naming still requires one selection after another.
47|**Belke and colleagues recorded eye movements to time each stage of naming.** || Two measures come from each object. >> **Gaze duration** is how long the eyes stay on an object. >> **Eye-speech lag** is the time from leaving an object to starting its name. || The eyes run ahead of speech. >> A speaker may look at the next object before saying the current name. >> So looking and speaking are separate events.
48|**The blocking effect appears only in gaze duration, so it arises at lemma selection.** **Homogeneous** blocks (same category) are compared with **heterogeneous** blocks (mixed). || Each measure maps to a stage. >> **Speech duration** decreases over the experiment with no blocking effect. It reflects the **gesture level**. >> **Gaze duration** shows a blocking effect after the **first cycle**. It reflects **lemma selection**. >> **Lag** shows no effect. It reflects **phonological encoding**. || The lag result is not a reversal. >> Numerically it goes the opposite way. >> This difference is **not significant**.
49|**McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003) asked whether phoneme identification is thresholded or cascaded during word recognition.** || The two options differ. >> **Thresholded** processing (the lumber mill) passes on only a finished category. >> **Cascaded** processing (the food web) passes graded information along. || They used eye tracking with a **visual world** display. >> Listeners heard a **/b/ to /p/ continuum**. >> It varied **voice onset time** (VOT, the delay between consonant release and voicing). >> Displays held a target (pear), a competitor (bear) and two fillers.
50|**Mouse choices between bear and pear formed a typical step-like identification function.** **McMurray and colleagues (2003)** had participants click the item most consistent with the word. || The choices were categorical. >> Short VOTs gave /b/ responses (bear). >> Long VOTs gave /p/ responses (pear). >> The **category boundary** was about 17.5 ms. || A step-like final choice can hide graded processing. >> Eye movements measure the processing before the choice.
51|**Eye movements show that ambiguity is gradient, with more competitor looks near the boundary.** || The gradient holds within each category. >> After a **bear** choice, VOTs nearer the boundary drew more looks to the pear. >> After a **pear** choice, VOTs nearer the boundary drew more looks to the bear. >> VOT mainly changes how **long** the competitor stays active. || So fine phonetic detail reaches **lexical activation**. >> That supports **cascaded** processing.
52|**Competitor looks rise steadily as VOT approaches the category boundary from either side.** || The effect is clearer step by step. >> For **bear responses**, looks to the pear rise from 0 ms toward the boundary. >> For **pear responses**, looks to the bear rise from 40 ms toward the boundary. || It holds across time. >> The **early window** is 300 to 1100 ms. >> The **late window** is 1100 to 1900 ms. >> Both windows show the gradient. || So the result favors **cascaded** processing over thresholded processing.
