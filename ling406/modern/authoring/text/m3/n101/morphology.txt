2|**Morphology is generative, so speakers can form words they have never memorized.** Inflection in English mostly means the plural and the past tense. >> **Derivational morphology** (a process that turns one kind of word into another) once turned the verb "to generate" into the noun "generative." >> **Inflectional morphology** (marking grammatical information such as plural or past tense on a word) is the focus here. >> Generalizing can produce playful forms, as in "She found me crying and she crew too and we both crode." >> Crew and crode extend irregular-sounding patterns to cry. They are creative, not standard English. || The open question is how speakers do this. >> One option is symbolic rules. >> The other option is learning from patterns.
3|**The wug test by Jean Berko Gleason (1958) found that people readily generalize inflection to new words.** Children do this too. >> A child sees one made-up creature with the text "This is a WUG." >> A second creature appears with "Now there is another one. There are two of them." >> The text ends with "There are two" and a blank. >> The child supplies **WUGS**, a plural never heard before. || A new word rules out memory as the source of the answer. >> The child cannot have stored the plural of wug. >> So the child must extend a learned pattern. >> The same method tests the **past tense** with made-up verbs. >> Success shows **productive knowledge** (the ability to apply a pattern to new items).
4|**The English past tense has regular forms that add -ed and irregular forms that do not.** >> **Regulars** with real verbs include show-showed, bake-baked and wade-waded. >> **Regulars** with made-up verbs include blick-blicked, trog-trogged and wumble-wumbled. >> **Irregulars** include go-went, take-took, eat-ate and sleep-slept. || The two kinds test different abilities. >> Made-up verbs test whether a pattern extends to new words. >> Irregulars test whether exceptions are handled correctly. >> Some irregulars share smaller patterns with other verbs. || The split echoes the **dual-route model** of reading. >> That model also separated a general pattern from word-specific knowledge.
5|**Pinker and colleagues proposed that words and rules are separate processes with separate formats.** This is the **Words and Rules** account. >> **Regulars** use a **symbolic rule** (an abstract operation such as "add -ed"). >> **Irregulars** are **lexicalized** (stored in the word's own entry) within an **associative network**. || The rule needs no stored past tense. >> So the rule applies to new verbs. || The associative network holds word-specific information. >> Frequency and similarity influence it. >> The account must also keep a stored irregular from being replaced by a regularization.
6|**Pinker and Ullman (2002) proposed a dual-route model with a lexicon route for stored forms and a grammar route for combining pieces.** The input is a stem such as walk or hold plus a past-tense feature. >> The **lexicon** retrieves held for hold by lookup and association. >> It handles roots, idioms, irregulars and some regulars. >> It belongs to **declarative memory** (words and facts) in temporo-parietal cortex. >> The **grammar** combines walk with -ed by combination and unification. >> It handles phrases, sentences and any regular form. >> It belongs to the **procedural system** (rules and skills) in frontal cortex and basal ganglia. >> A **blocking** link lets a retrieved irregular stop the regular form. || The model is **similar** to dual-route reading. >> Both separate word-specific knowledge from a productive operation. || The model is **different** in task and origin. >> Reading turns spelling into sound. >> This model changes a stem according to a grammatical feature. >> It came from grammar theory, not from patient data.
7|**The past-tense model looks like the reading model but has different origins.** >> The reading model was built on neuropsychological **double dissociations** (two abilities that can each be damaged while the other is spared). >> **Pinker and colleagues** were motivated by other concerns. || **Generative linguistics** of the time drove the past-tense model. >> It was mostly about **categorical, symbolic, rule-based grammars**. >> These rules often have **exceptions**. >> The Words and Rules account keeps rules and exceptions apart. || The reading model came from patient data. >> The past-tense model came from a theory of grammar.
8|**The dual-process model handles exceptions in a second process so the grammar can stay elegant.** >> Linguists often just left exceptions out of the grammar. >> Adding machinery for exceptions would make the grammar **inelegant**. >> The second process keeps the grammar **symbolic and categorical**. || The second process has all the properties the rule route lacks. >> It is **word-specific** (lexical). >> It is **sensitive to frequency** of occurrence. >> It is **sensitive to phonological similarity** (sharing sound patterns), as in keep-kept, weep-wept and leap-leapt. || An exception can belong to a small family of similar verbs. >> The rule stays simple, but a second mechanism now needs explaining.
9|**Rumelhart and McClelland (1987) proposed that the past tense is all associative learning in one network.** >> No separate "add -ed" rule exists in the model. >> Regularity is a pattern learned from many examples. >> Irregulars are learned in the same system. || The model has three stages. >> A **fixed encoding network** turns the sound of the root form into **Wickelfeatures** (units that represent sound patterns). >> A **pattern associator** with **modifiable connections** maps root Wickelfeatures onto past-tense Wickelfeatures. >> A **decoding and binding network** turns those features into the sound of the past tense. >> The modifiable connections store the learned mapping.
10|**Rumelhart and McClelland aimed their model at U-shaped learning in children's past tense.** It was one of the first learning models of a language process, so it focused on development. >> First, children learn some frequent irregulars early, such as run-ran. >> Then, spontaneously, they produce **regularizations** (applying -ed to an irregular verb), such as runned. >> Eventually, they produce correct forms again. || The name comes from the shape of performance over time. >> Performance goes down. >> Then performance goes back up. >> Children get worse before they get better. || A theory must explain the whole path. >> That path includes the temporary errors.
11|**In the Words and Rules account, U-shaped learning matters because each part of the U is a distinct stage with a distinct process.** >> Early correct forms are just **imitations** and do not use adult mechanisms. >> When the rule is first learned, it is applied to all cases. >> The rule is the **best hypothesis** for all items generally, so runned appears. >> Over time, exceptions are learned **associatively**, one by one, into **lexical knowledge**. >> The **blocking process** (a stored irregular stopping the regular form) also develops. || Blocking explains the recovery. >> Ran blocks runned. >> The child is correct again. || The dip is rule use that exceptions do not yet hold back.
12|**Rumelhart and McClelland's single network produces U-shaped learning when its input changes over time.** The **training set** is based on data about input to children. >> Early on, **irregular verbs** predominate. >> The highest-frequency verbs tend to be irregular, such as GIVE, TAKE, HAVE and GO. >> As vocabulary expands to more regulars, the model **reorganizes** to capture them. >> This reorganizing **interferes** with some early-learned irregulars. || The model's curves follow the U. >> Correct features for high-frequency irregulars dip briefly, then climb slowly. >> Regulars climb fast and stay high. >> The regularized response briefly beats the correct irregular. >> With training, the correct form wins and the regularized form fades.
13|**Because the model was trained on few verbs, it could take a wug test with real words.** >> Untrained English verbs were new to the model. >> They were unfamiliar low-frequency regular verbs. || Many responses were correct regular forms. >> Examples are shaped, sipped and typed. || Some responses were odd. >> Examples are stepted for step, squawked for squat and membled for mail. || The proportion of **Wickelfeatures** generated correctly was high. >> It was .92 for regulars. >> It was .84 for irregulars. >> It was .91 overall. >> Correct features do not guarantee correct whole words.
14|**Some model outputs added or changed consonants in ways people would not.** >> The model gave maded for mate. >> It gave squawked for squat. >> It gave membled for mail. >> It gave toureder for tour. || The model's authors admitted this problem. >> Humans are unlikely to make these errors, especially the last two. >> Most of these responses were **near threshold** (only weakly produced). >> An **auto-associative network** among output units could clean up the output and avoid bizarre responses. >> They had not yet built that network. || A good learning curve is not enough if the words are implausible.
15|**Prince and Pinker (1988) argued that the model blends competing past tenses into bizarre hybrids.** >> The model uses **feature-only representations** (representations made only of features). >> With features alone, representing two things at once is a problem. >> Features alone cannot tell a green circle near a red square from a red circle near a green square. || The same problem arises in the past tense. >> Subregularities and the regular rule all compete to activate Wickelfeatures for the past form. >> For sing, features of sing, sang, sung, singed and sanged are superimposed. >> The output is an unordered collection of features, many contradictory, not a single word. || The result is blended hybrids. >> Toureder is a hybrid past of tour. >> Membled is a hybrid past of mail.
16|**Joanisse and Seidenberg (1999) proposed that sound supports regular patterns while meaning gives extra support to irregulars.** >> **Rule-like generalization** is supported by **phonological representations** (sound forms of words). >> **Irregular** forms require greater support from **semantics** (word meaning). || One system holds both kinds of information. >> **Speech input** and **semantics** connect through a middle layer to **speech output**. >> No separate rule route exists. || Regulars and irregulars differ in how much they rely on sound and meaning.
17|**Joanisse and Seidenberg (1999) aimed their model at a dissociation between irregular and nonword past tense in patients.** >> Some patients are worse on **irregulars**. >> Other patients are worse on **nonwords**. || The model must produce both patterns from one system. >> Nonwords have no stored meaning, so they rely on phonological generalization. >> Irregulars fit the main pattern poorly, so they rely more on semantics. >> Damaging phonology or semantics should give opposite profiles. || The test is whether damage to shared systems explains both patient groups.
18|**Broca's aphasia patients have problems with regular morphology, especially nonwords.** >> The cause is injury to **left anterior** brain regions. >> Speech production is poor. >> Nonword examples are wug becoming wugged and blick becoming blicked. || Accuracy follows a clear order. >> Irregulars are about 85 percent correct. >> Regulars are about 82 percent correct. >> Nonwords are about 69 percent correct. || The nonword drop is the largest. >> A nonword has no stored past tense. >> It must be produced by generalizing. >> This raises the question of a phonological cause.
19|**Damaging phonology in the model reproduced the Broca's pattern, with nonwords worst.** >> The damage **reduced** the model's ability to compute past-tense forms. || **Nonwords** were worse than regulars and irregulars. >> Model nonwords were about 72 percent correct. >> Patient nonwords were about 69 percent. || **Regulars** were numerically worse than irregulars. >> Model regulars were about 80 percent. >> Model irregulars were about 85 percent. || Familiar words keep support a new nonword lacks. >> So weak sound processing hurts nonwords most. >> No rule had to be removed.
20|**Wernicke's, Alzheimer's and anomic patients show the reverse problem, with irregulars worst.** >> The anomic patients (people who have trouble retrieving words) have injury to **inferior or middle temporal** brain regions. || Accuracy follows the reverse order. >> Irregulars are about 60 percent correct. >> Regulars are about 89 percent correct. >> Nonwords are about 84 percent correct. || This is the other side of the dissociation. >> Broca's patients are worst on nonwords. >> These patients are worst on irregulars.
21|**Damaging semantics in the model made irregulars worst, as in Alzheimer's patients.** >> **Irregulars** were worse than regulars and nonwords. >> **Nonwords** were a bit worse than regulars. || Model and patients matched on irregulars. >> Model irregulars were about 62 percent correct. >> Patient irregulars were about 60 percent. || Model regulars were about 83 percent, and model nonwords about 77 percent. || Irregulars rely most on meaning. >> So losing semantic support hurts them most. >> Typical sound patterns still support regulars and nonwords.
22|**Two studies tied the patient dissociation to phonology and semantics rather than to rules and words.** || **Bird, Patterson, Lambon-Ralph, McClelland and Seidenberg (2003)** tested **Broca's patients**. >> They controlled regular and irregular items adequately for **phonology**. >> The regular and irregular difference was **reduced**. >> So part of the gap came from sound differences between items. || **Patterson, Lambon-Ralph, Hodges and McClelland (2001)** tested **semantic dementia** patients (people losing word meanings). >> They compared irregular impairment with severity of the semantic deficit. >> Irregular impairment **correlated strongly** with semantic deficit severity. >> So irregular trouble grows as meaning is lost.
23|**As in the triangle model of dyslexia, phonology supports generalization and semantics supports idiosyncratic forms.** || **Phonological representations** support **generalization**. >> Generalization comes from **statistical learning** about patterns from present to past. >> Impaired phonological representations hurt these generalizations. || **Semantic representations** support **idiosyncratic** forms (forms particular to one word). >> Forms that deviate from the overwhelmingly regular "add -ed" pattern need semantic support. >> Some irregulars use **sub-patterns** shared with similar verbs. >> Those irregulars are likely to be **spared**.
24|**Specific language impairment (SLI) is impaired language development with normal nonverbal IQ.** Performance outside language is "normal." >> It affects grammar, in both **morphology** (word structure) and **syntax** (sentence structure). >> It also affects **phonology** (sound structure). >> It also affects **reading**. >> It also affects **working memory**. >> It also affects **speech perception**. || So the impairment reaches beyond grammar. >> A grammar-only theory must explain these too.
25|**Earlier claims said SLI is a deficit in learning rules.** **Past-tense deficits** are a **sine qua non** (essential feature) of SLI. >> Gopnik, van der Lely, Pinker, Ullman and Clahsen made the claims. >> A rule deficit predicts problems with **regulars**. >> It predicts problems with **nonwords**. >> It predicts no problem with **irregulars**, which are stored words. || If true, SLI would be evidence for **domain-specific** mechanisms (used only for language) in language acquisition. >> The test is whether irregulars are spared.
26|**van der Lely and Ullman (2001) found that an SLI group was poor on all past-tense types, not only regulars and nonwords.** >> They compared the SLI group with **same-age controls**. || The SLI group was low on every type. >> Regulars were about 22 percent correct. >> Irregulars were about 18 percent. >> Nonwords were about 7 percent. || Controls were much higher on every type. >> Regulars were about 95 percent correct. >> Irregulars were about 88 percent. >> Nonwords were about 74 percent. || Irregulars were **not spared** in SLI. >> The deficit is broader than a rule deficit predicts. >> Nonwords were still hardest.
27|**A model with weakened phonology learned all past-tense forms poorly, and nonwords suffered most.** >> **Severe phonological deficit** gave about 39 percent for regulars, 17 for irregulars and 7 for nonwords. >> **Weaker phonological deficit** gave about 55 percent for regulars, 38 for irregulars and 15 for nonwords. >> **Intact, final** gave about 100 percent for regulars, 94 for irregulars and 80 for nonwords. >> **Intact at 700K iterations** gave about 90 percent for regulars and 75 for both irregulars and nonwords. || The impaired models follow the SLI order. >> Regulars are best. >> Nonwords are worst. >> No rule is missing.
28|**The model makes clear that phonology affects development of all past-tense forms and matters most for nonwords.** >> Every spoken past tense uses phonology. >> Weak phonology lowers regulars, irregulars and nonwords. || Nonwords depend on phonology most. >> A nonword has no training history of its own. >> It relies heavily on generalizing a sound pattern. >> Familiar words get extra support from item-specific learning. || Nonwords show the largest deficit, but every form still depends on phonology.
29|**In this account there are no special past-tense rules, only mappings between phonology and semantics.** It is similar to the **triangle model** of reading. >> There is no **separate lexicon** that stores exceptions. >> **Rule** and **exception** only approximately describe the system. || Two associations support the account. >> **Irregular** forms are associated with **semantic processing**. >> **Wug test** performance is associated with **phonological processing**. || Past-tense behavior comes from learned mappings that lean on sound and meaning to different degrees.
