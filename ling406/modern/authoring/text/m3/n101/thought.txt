2|**Linguistic relativity asks whether differences in language change how people organize concepts.** The quotations from **Sapir (1929)** and **Whorf (1956)** suggest that **linguistic categories** can shape how experience is interpreted. >> The strongest reading says language determines what a person can think. >> A weaker reading says habitual language use biases attention, memory, or preferred ways of representing a situation. || **Influencing thought** is different from **completely determining thought**. >> The two readings need different evidence. >> The quotations are broad ideas, not experiments.
3|**Language is embedded in cultural practices and experience.** A difference between language communities may go along with factors other than linguistic structure. >> Sapir and Whorf should not automatically get the strongest reading of their quotations. >> In the first half of the twentieth century, linguistics was part of **anthropology**. >> A precise study must separate the contributions of language and culture. || **Testing linguistic relativity needs a narrower claim.** >> **Domains** (specific areas of experience) such as space and time let researchers test preferred ways of representing things. >> A study must identify which **cognitive process** (a mental operation such as remembering) is affected. >> It must also check which alternative explanations the comparison controls.
4|**A location description needs a frame of reference.** A **frame of reference** is the set of directions used to say where something is. >> The ball can be located relative to the viewer. >> The ball can be located relative to the chair's own orientation. >> The ball can be located by a direction independent of both. || **Changing the reference changes the relation being described.** >> The object does not have to move for its description to change. >> Different descriptions can identify the same physical position. >> The question is which reference determines the ball's location.
5|**Each frame of reference fixes direction in a different way.** Three types are intrinsic, relative, and absolute. >> An **intrinsic frame** uses the object's own orientation. >> A **relative frame** uses the observer's viewpoint. >> An **absolute frame** uses a stable direction. || **The same location can be described in all three ways.** >> "At the chair's front" uses the chair's intrinsic front. >> "To the left of the chair from my viewpoint" depends on the observer. >> "North of the chair" does not reverse when the observer turns around. || **The ball and chair example keeps the scene fixed.** >> Rotating the observer changes left and right. >> Rotating the observer does not change north. >> This classification comes from **Levinson and colleagues (2002)**.
6|**Most languages describe nearby space egocentrically.** **Egocentric** descriptions use the speaker's own position and orientation. >> Left, right, in front, and behind are familiar examples. >> These words describe nearby objects. || **Tzeltal commonly uses absolute directions instead.** >> Tzeltal is a Western Mayan language spoken in Chiapas. >> A small number of other languages also use absolute directions. >> Those languages are mostly unrelated and broadly distributed, such as in Bali and Queensland. >> An absolute direction stays fixed when the person turns. || **The contrast raises a question about habits.** >> The question is whether habitual language use relates to nonverbal spatial coding. >> The comparison concerns preferred descriptions.
7|**Tzeltal's uphill and downhill terms work as an absolute directional system.** The system was historically based on landmarks. >> Things are uphill or downhill from one another, not to the left or right of the speaker. >> "Uphill" and "downhill" are used as cardinal directions. || **A landmark origin and an absolute use are different properties.** >> A term can come from a slope. >> Each use does not simply point to the nearest visible hill. || **A stable reference keeps positions consistent.** >> The direction is not recalculated from the speaker's current left and right. >> So positions can be described consistently when the speaker changes orientation.
8|**New Yorkers use uptown and downtown as if they were cardinal directions.** These terms work only inside Manhattan. >> So they are literally a landmark-based system. >> Uptown and downtown organize movement within Manhattan. >> They do not give a compass system that works everywhere. || **A local convention is not automatically an absolute frame.** >> Manhattan is angled with respect to magnetic north. >> The subway map is off on north and south. >> The Manhattan example is about reference systems, not causation.
9|**Majid and colleagues (2004) used a non-linguistic test to separate relative and absolute coding of space.** The **chips task** rotates the participant 180 degrees. >> A participant sees a configuration, waits, and then chooses its match at a second table. || A **relative response** keeps the arrangement with respect to the person's viewpoint. >> An **absolute response** keeps the arrangement's orientation in the environment. >> After rotation, the two responses predict different choices. || **Dutch speakers mostly gave relative responses.** >> **Tzeltal speakers gave more absolute responses.** >> The north arrow stays fixed while the person changes orientation. >> These patterns fit each group's preferred frame.
10|**Spatial inference can combine relations learned in different displays.** Participants learn a relation between two objects. >> They rotate to another display and learn a second relation. >> That second relation involves a shared object. >> They then place one object relative to another. || **Relative and absolute coding lead to different placements.** >> The person's orientation has changed between the displays. >> The cube appears in both displays, linking the cone and cylinder relations. >> The final placement separates a viewer-relative inference from one that keeps environmental direction. || **Dutch and Tzeltal patterns again differ.** >> Each pattern follows the direction linked to that group's preferred frame.
11|**Testing conditions may contribute to the apparent language difference.** **Li and Gleitman** noted that Tzeltal participants were tested outside, with visible, familiar landmarks. >> Typical psychology lab studies are done in windowless rooms. >> Useful landmarks can encourage landmark-based coding. || **A language comparison can be confounded with a setting comparison.** >> The environment must be considered before assigning the whole difference to linguistic structure. >> The critical contrast is access to spatial cues. >> Windows, outdoor views, and familiar surroundings change which references are useful. || **Manipulating landmarks within English speakers tests this directly.** >> The test checks whether the behavior changes without changing native language.
12|**English speakers strongly preferred relative responses with the blinds down.** This matches the Dutch speakers in earlier studies. >> With the blinds up and a view of the street, the preference is less pronounced. >> Outdoors, the preference is also less pronounced. || **Changing landmarks changes responses within one language group.** >> The language group stays the same while the spatial information changes. >> So task context can influence the response. >> This challenges an explanation in which language alone fixes the frame. || **The change is a weakening, not a full switch.** >> A reduced relative preference is not a complete move to absolute coding. >> Describe the change without making it an all-or-none reversal.

14|**If language groups are tested under different conditions, their difference may partly reflect those conditions.** Visible landmarks and familiarity can go along with native language in a cross-community study. >> A controlled comparison must separate the environment from habitual linguistic coding. || **A confound is a factor that changes with the variable of interest.** >> It offers another explanation for the result. >> It is not any feature that happens to be present. || **The bias might be an artifact of the way psychology tests are usually run in universities.** >> An **artifact** is a result produced by how tests are run. >> So an uncontrolled comparison cannot separate language from testing conditions.
15|**Levinson and colleagues argued that the simplified task made the experimenter's purpose too obvious.** Low memory demands and short time or distance between displays make the rotation easy to notice. >> Li and Gleitman's participants were Ivy League students. >> Ivy League students are good at working out what the experimenter wants. || **Participants who infer the purpose can change their answers.** >> That change reflects interpretation of the experiment. >> It does not reflect habitual spatial coding. >> This is a **demand-characteristic explanation** (an account where the apparent purpose of a study shapes responses). || **The response focuses on memory load and task transparency.** >> It does not deny that landmarks can ever matter.
16|**Levinson and colleagues (2002) re-ran the animals in a row experiment with Dutch college students.** They added a fourth animal and a longer delay to raise memory load. >> They also increased the distance between the stimulus and response tables. >> That larger distance reduces how obvious the rotation is. || **The bias toward relative framing remained under these conditions.** >> The preference for relative coding stayed in place. >> The environmental effect depends on how the task is arranged. || **Demanding memory keeps the relative preference in place.** >> This narrows the conditions under which the landmark explanation accounts for behavior.
17|**Memory can make the interpretation of a relative preference harder.** Under memory demands, the preference could come from a verbal memory as well as a spatial one. >> Remembering the task can recruit verbal strategies as well as spatial ones. >> The bias is robust when memory is significant and the experimenter's interest is not obvious. || **How the sequence is stored during the delay matters.** >> Researchers should ask how the sequence is stored, not only which direction is chosen. >> A nonverbal response can follow verbal recoding inside the head. || **These two conditions say when the effect is robust.** >> Memory must be a significant part of the task, and the experimenter's interest in rotation must not be obvious.
18|**Participants might remember the animals as a verbal list.** An example list is "lion, tiger, bear." >> Participants could reconstruct that sequence from left to right. >> That reconstruction can produce a response that looks relative. || **The strategy connects language to the task through working memory.** >> The same choice can arise from different internal codes. || **A list keeps order but not compass direction.** >> Translating that order into a left-to-right display can mimic a spatial frame preference. >> A later page links reading direction to how sequences are arranged in space.
19|**Metaphorical language expresses one kind of experience using terms from another.** Calling an argument ineffective describes how well it works. >> It is not a literal action with a blade. >> Physical and spatial terms can help express abstract relations. || **An expression can be meaningful without being literal.** >> Time is useful for studying whether such habits go with nonlinguistic representations. || **A lyric from Brown and Byrd (1970) is one example of metaphor.** >> It raises the question of how much of language can safely be described as non-metaphorical.
20|**Boroditsky and colleagues (2010) compared spatial metaphors for time in English and Mandarin.** English and Mandarin both use horizontal spatial language for time. >> Mandarin also often uses vertical terms. >> An earlier event can take an "up" term, and a later event can take a "down" term. || **These expressions are the starting observation.** >> A behavioral task is needed to test whether the vertical contrast goes beyond words. || **Habitual talk about time suggests testable predictions about processing.** >> The "up" in an earlier month is a temporal use. >> It does not claim that the month occupies a physical location.
21|**Boroditsky (2001) ran a priming experiment on temporal judgments.** Participants first process a horizontal or vertical spatial relation. >> They then answer whether one event is earlier or later. >> English speakers respond faster after horizontal primes than after vertical primes. >> Mandarin speakers have the opposite pattern in the comparison tested. || **Short training on vertical metaphors for time changes the pattern.** >> This suggests sensitivity to experience. >> It is not an unchangeable split between language groups. || **Reaction time differences reveal how compatible the prime is with the judgment.** >> Lower reaction times mean faster processing. >> So horizontal and vertical results should be compared within each group.
22|**Casasanto and Bottini (2005) offer reading direction as an alternative explanation.** Reading and writing habits can also organize sequences spatially. >> Differences attributed to temporal metaphors may go along with whether text is read left to right, right to left, or vertically. >> Casasanto and Bottini use differences in text orientation and arrangement preferences to support this. || **Language groups can bundle linguistic and literacy experience together.** >> Reading direction must be measured or controlled. >> The diagrams separate the starting side from the line direction. >> The table lists several arrangement patterns within the groups. || **Individual experience matters.** >> A writing system label does not perfectly predict each person's habits.
23|**Boroditsky and colleagues (2010) excluded Chinese readers who reported reading top to bottom.** This tests whether the vertical effect is explained entirely by that reading experience. >> English speakers have an advantage for a left-is-earlier horizontal mapping. >> Mandarin speakers have an advantage for a top-is-earlier vertical mapping in the comparison tested. || **A vertical effect that remains weakens that particular explanation.** >> A targeted control tests one specific alternative. || **Lower reaction times mean a mapping advantage.** >> Canonical mappings are the white bars. >> Noncanonical mappings are the black bars. >> For vertical trials, canonical means top is earlier.
24|**Participants sort events as past or future by pressing colored buttons.** The mapping from color to category is reversed across blocks. >> So the past response is on the left half the time and on the right the other half. || **Responses are faster when past maps left and future maps right.** >> **Casasanto and Bottini (2010)** used standard text reading for this comparison. >> Color alone cannot explain the pattern, since the mappings are **counterbalanced** (the two mappings are used in turn). || **The effect concerns response time across mappings.** >> It does not change which events count as past or future. >> The left to right temporal mapping has the lower reaction times.
25|**Short-term reading experience can change the link between space and time.** **Casasanto and Bottini (2010)** had participants read reversed text, then perform the past and future button task. >> By the second mirror-reading block, the compatibility effect reverses. >> Responses are then faster when earlier maps right and later maps left. || **A flexible temporal mapping is hard to explain as a fixed result of grammar alone.** >> Recent perceptual and reading experience contributes to performance. >> The first block keeps the usual advantage. >> The second block favors the opposite mapping. || **Compare the two colors within each block.** >> Comparing overall reaction times between blocks can mislead.
26|**Claims about a language's vocabulary are not automatic evidence for cognitive limits.** **Pullum (1989)** criticized how an account of different word roots became an exaggerated story about language and thought. >> Languages can express distinctions through separate roots, derived words, or phrases. >> Counting one form without the others can misrepresent expressive capacity. || **A reliable claim needs accurate linguistic description.** >> It also needs an appropriate behavioral test. >> Repeating a striking anecdote is not enough. || **Languages can package the same distinctions in different ways.** >> **Derivationally related forms** are words built from another word. >> Roots, derived forms, and phrases differ, which is why simple counts mislead.
