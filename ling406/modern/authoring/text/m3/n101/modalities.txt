2|**Sign language and Braille are really different cases, but both reveal how the brain organizes language input from different senses.** >> A **signed language** is a **natural language** (a language with its own linguistic structure). >> **Braille** is a system that lets blind people read a spoken language they share with sighted people. >> So Braille is a way to read a language, not a separate language. || They are still grouped together, because both reveal the **plasticity** (the ability of sensory systems to change with experience). >> Both show how the brain gets organized differently when language input comes through a different **modality** (the sense that carries the input). >> The linguistic status of the two cases still differs.
3|**Sign languages differ from spoken languages in modality, articulators, use of space and iconicity.** >> **Emmorey and colleagues (2004)** list these differences. >> **Modality** is the sense the language uses, which is vision for sign. >> **Articulators** are the body parts that produce the language, which are the hands, face and upper body. >> **Use of space** matters, because where a sign is made can carry relations between things. >> **Iconicity** means a sign's form resembles its meaning, and some signs are iconic. || Iconicity is **easy to exaggerate**. >> It looks strongest when a single sign is studied with its **gloss** (the translation word for that sign). >> A resemblance looks obvious only after the translation is known. >> A learner still has to learn the forms and structure of the language.
4|**Fluent signing coordinates the hands, face, upper body and eye contact in one continuous stream.** >> **Eye contact** is part of the interaction. >> The **hands**, **face** and **upper body** work together as one linguistic signal. >> The signal is spread across all of these articulators, not produced by one hand movement at a time. || Fluent signing is also **fast** and **fluid**. >> Signs run into each other, so it is hard to find where one word ends. >> A learner may struggle to **segment** the stream (find the boundaries between words). >> Fluent speech has the same problem, because it is also continuous. || The thing to study is coordinated language production, not one isolated hand pose. >> A video of fluent signing is available to watch for this point.
5|**Signs can differ contrastively in hand configuration, location and movement, as speech sounds can differ contrastively.** >> **Emmorey and colleagues (2003)** name these three features. >> **Hand configuration** is the shape of the hand. >> **Location** is where the gesture is made. >> **Movement** is how the hand moves. || The signs for "please" and "sorry" differ, and so do the signs for "onion" and "apple." >> Changing one feature can turn one sign into a different sign. >> Visual similarity alone does not decide whether two forms belong to the same category. || A feature is **contrastive** when changing it changes the sign's identity. >> The pairs of signs share some properties and contrast on others. >> Handshape, the location of the gesture and its movement are separate dimensions to check. >> Sign has its own **phonology** (the system of contrastive form that separates signs).
6|**Researchers asked whether categorical perception applies to these sign features.** >> **Categorical perception** (a pattern where a gradual change is sorted into separate categories) can be tested by changing a sign feature in steps. >> Observers identify each form and also tell pairs of forms apart. >> One continuum crosses a **phonemic** contrast (a difference that changes which sign is meant), between "please" and "sorry." >> The other continuum changes hand configuration without changing the sign's identity, which is an **allophonic** change. || Comparing equal steps is more informative than comparing only the endpoints. >> A step that crosses a linguistic boundary can be compared with an equal step inside one category. >> Both continua vary visually, but only one crosses the relevant contrast. || The results show categorical perception for the phonemic change but not for the allophonic change. >> A sharp identification boundary with better discrimination near it would indicate category-sensitive perception.
7|**Deaf signers show a sharper categorical boundary for the phonemic change than for the allophonic change.** >> For the phonemic change, **identification** (labeling each form as one sign or the other) shifts steeply between stimuli 4 and 6. >> That steep change marks the boundary between the two categories. || **Discrimination** (telling two forms apart) peaks at that same boundary. >> Pairs of forms that cross the boundary are easier to tell apart. >> This matches the peak in the discrimination function. || The allophonic change looks very different. >> Identification is much shallower, so many more forms are ambiguous. >> There is no peak in discrimination. >> The perceptual pattern follows the linguistic contrast, not the amount of visible change. || **A sharp identification shift and a discrimination peak together support categorical perception.** >> Identification alone would be a less complete test.
8|**Sign language uses space to indicate relationships between words in a sentence.** >> A **classifier sign** (a sign whose form stands for a kind of object) is made at a location. >> The relative position of two objects is given by where that classifier sign is made. >> This example is very transparent. || **Understanding sentences of any complexity in sign requires keeping track of where signs were made.** >> Each entity introduced in space has to be remembered at its location. >> Later signs relate back to those locations. >> So spatial processing contributes to comprehension, and it is not only background vision.
9|**Brain activity during sign comprehension is similar to spoken language in some ways and different in others.** >> Signing is **left lateralized** (its activity is concentrated in the left hemisphere), as spoken language is. >> Classical language areas are active too, including **Broca's area** (the inferior frontal region) and **Wernicke's area** (the posterior superior temporal region). || Signing also differs. >> It engages **visual areas** and the **superior parietal** region, which are classic spatial areas. >> This is because signing makes perceptual and spatial demands. || **Campbell, MacSweeney and Waters (2008)** compared deaf signers, hearing signers and hearing people who watched audio-visual speech. >> Hearing signers are useful, because signing experience is not limited to deaf people. >> Overlap in these regions does not require identical activation everywhere.
10|**Bavelier and colleagues (2001) asked whether deafness or experience with sign changes spatial attention.** >> The groups were hearing controls, deaf signers and hearing signers. >> Participants monitored for brief changes in **luminance** (brightness) or **velocity** (speed of motion) of dots. >> Attention was directed either to the center of the screen or to the periphery. || **Deaf participants had an advantage when attention was directed to the periphery.** >> Hearing signers did not show that advantage. >> So signing experience alone does not explain the enhancement. || Including hearing signers helps separate sign experience from the lack of auditory input. >> A deaf versus hearing comparison alone would bundle deafness and signing together. >> If all signers had the advantage, signing alone would be the stronger explanation. >> The task measures attention to brief visual changes, not sign comprehension.
11|**Learning sign language and being deaf have different effects on visual processing.** >> Learning sign language moves the **laterality** (which side of the brain does the work) of motion perception from right to left. >> Both deaf and hearing signers show this leftward shift compared with hearing nonsigners. || **Enhanced peripheral attention is found only in deaf individuals.** >> Hearing signers do not show this enhancement. >> One proposed reason is that hearing people depend more on auditory cues when things are at the far periphery. || One proposed reason for the leftward shift is that motion perception needs to talk to left-lateralized language networks in people who learn sign. >> These two effects should not be merged into one advantage. >> They track different aspects of experience, one in motion areas and one in center-versus-periphery attention.
12|**Comparing sign with tic-tac separates language processing from the processing of hand movements.** >> **Tic-tac** is an outdated gesture system used to communicate about horse-racing odds in the UK. >> Both tasks involve meaningful, sequenced hand movements. >> So the control shares movement demands with sign but differs in language. || The control is not a natural language, and that is why the remaining difference is informative. >> A difference between sign and tic-tac says more about language than a comparison with no movement. >> The comparison does not assume that every meaningful gesture is a natural language.
13|**Sign comprehension produces stronger activity around Wernicke's area than tic-tac does.** >> **Campbell, MacSweeney and Waters (2008)** reported this comparison. >> Wernicke's area lies in the posterior superior temporal region. >> This supports a difference between processing organized hand movements and processing a natural language. || Shared visual movement does not explain the whole neural response to signing. >> The frontal activation is above **Broca's area**, so it should not be called a precise Broca's effect. >> The posterior temporal difference is the central language-related result. >> The comparison weakens a simple explanation based only on hands moving through space.
14|**Signed languages have phonological structure, and native speakers show categorical perception for some contrastive features.** >> Learning a signed language causes some brain reorganization, especially in the lateralization of motion processing. >> Attention to the visual periphery matters for both hearing and deaf signers. >> Only deaf signers show enhanced peripheral attention. || Compared with other organized hand movements that are not language, signed sentences activate the **posterior superior temporal gyrus** (Wernicke's area). >> This region is a language-related region, so the response is not only about hand movement. || **A full account has to separate shared language organization from sensory adaptation.** >> Neither "sign is just gesture" nor "modality makes no difference" fits the evidence. >> Each result answers a different question, so one result cannot establish the others.
15|**Comprehending a signed spatial description can require a perspective transformation.** >> A room can be described from the **speaker's perspective** (the signer's own viewpoint) or the **addressee's perspective** (the viewpoint of the person being addressed). >> Comprehension was better for descriptions from the speaker's perspective. >> That is true even though it requires a mental transformation. || The most natural representation is not always the one with no spatial transformation. >> Spatial comprehension depends on conventions as well as geometric convenience. >> The two signing sequences describe the same table location. >> The difference is whose perspective organizes the description, not where the table is.
16|**Braille reading is in many ways like reading print, but its symbols are built for touch.** >> **Braille symbols** are optimized for **tactile discrimination** (telling symbols apart by touch). >> Reading Braille by sight is really hard. >> Fingers take in letters one at a time. || Braille represents written language through touch. >> It is a way to read a language, rather than a separate natural language with its own grammar. >> This makes it useful for asking how reading networks work when the input comes through touch. || Sequential tactile sampling is a processing constraint. >> It does not mean Braille readers must consciously name every dot before understanding a word.
17|**Reading Braille activates visual cortex in blind people, and more visual cortex is recruited after earlier sight loss.** >> The comparison includes **early-blind** and **late-blind** participants. >> Early-blind participants show stronger involvement of the **visual word form area** (a region involved in visual word processing). >> The earlier sight is lost, the more extensive the visual activity to tactile stimuli. || A region's usual sensory label does not completely determine the information it can help process. >> Visual cortex can take part in reading even when the input is touch. >> Early- and late-blind groups give a comparison of developmental sensory history. || Compare how much activation there is, rather than assuming that every colored spot has the same function.
18|**Sighted adults who wore blindfolds for five days learned Braille more quickly than a control group.** >> **Merabet and colleagues (2008)** asked sighted adults to wear blindfolds for five days while they trained to read Braille. >> The control group trained without the same continuous removal of visual input. >> This suggests that removing competing visual input can affect tactile learning over a short time. || The study tests a question about **competition** (inputs competing for the same brain areas). >> The question was whether competition could block hearing people from building strong peripheral attention. >> Because the participants were sighted adults, the study tests a different question from lifelong or early blindness. || **Cross-modal changes do not have to be limited to early development.** >> A short-term change can reveal **latent** (hidden) contributions to tactile processing. >> The procedure included baseline testing, training, repeated imaging and blindfold removal. >> Comparing the groups separates the effect of training from the extra effect of continuous blindfolding.
19|**Visual cortex responses to Braille developed during blindfolding and reversed after the blindfold was removed on Day 6.** >> Over the course of the experiment, sighted blindfolded participants began to show activity in **primary visual cortex** (the first cortical area for vision). They showed this activity while reading Braille. >> The change was rapid and reversible. || Removing visual input lets tactile-related activity become more evident. >> This pattern fits **unmasking** (existing inputs becoming visible) rather than building a new pathway in a few days. >> Restoring vision shifts that balance again. || **Day 6 is the post-blindfold condition, not another day of blindfolding.** >> The comparison covers Days 1, 3, 5 and 6.
20|**Disrupting visual cortex with TMS (transcranial magnetic stimulation, a method that briefly interferes with brain activity) impaired Braille reading only in the blindfolded group.** >> TMS was used to disrupt activity in visual cortex. >> On Day 5, the blindfolded group's error rate rose under 1 Hz TMS compared with its baseline and with sham (fake) stimulation. >> The nonblindfolded group did not show the same selective disruption. >> Solid symbols mark the blindfolded participants. || The vertical axis is error rate, so an upward change means worse performance. >> **Activation and necessity are different kinds of evidence.** >> Interfering with a region and changing behavior is stronger evidence that the region contributes to the task. >> The effect is no longer present in the same way after blindfold removal, which supports a temporary contribution of visual cortex to tactile performance. || **One proposed explanation is that visual cortex always receives weak tactile input.** >> That input is too weak to compete with the much stronger and more consistent visual input. >> So a short removal of visual input can unmask tactile responses in visual cortex.
21|**Language circuitry stays resilient across sensory modalities, but experience still shapes how it is organized.** >> Classical language areas are active for sign, even though the input is dramatically different in its surface form. >> Braille reading seems to engage networks that are important for processing text. || **Brain organization is shaped over development by competition between inputs.** >> When auditory input is unavailable, putative (assumed) auditory areas are used to process sign. >> Visual attention is enhanced at the periphery. >> When an input is absent or reduced, other information can make a larger contribution. || The competing processes that shape language are also important in the Competition Model for second language learning. >> Distinguish the language-network overlap from the visual-attention and tactile-recruitment findings, since they come from different experimental comparisons.
