2|The first thing to remember about **phonemes** is that phonemes are **not letters**. This is because a phoneme is a category of speech sound, and a letter is a written symbol. A writing system stands for a language. But its symbols do not have to match the sound categories one to one. || One letter can be part of different pronunciations. One phoneme can be written with different spellings. So picking out a letter is a different job from picking out a phoneme. || The question is about how sounds relate to writing. It is not about how many letters a word has. Since sounds and letters do not match one to one, they are kept apart.
3|Letters are not phonemes either, and scripts differ in how well spelling tells you the sound. This is because in a **mispronouncing contest**, people mispronounced words like COLONEL and PARADIGM on purpose. Reading those words by their usual letter patterns gives the wrong sound. || This difference between scripts is called **orthographic depth** (how consistently spelling matches pronunciation). **English** is a relatively **deep** script. This is because English has many irregular spellings. So its letters often do not tell you the sound. || **Finnish** and **Korean** are very **shallow** scripts. Their spelling matches sound very predictably. **Pinyin** for Mandarin and **French** are also fairly regular. || A deep script is harder to predict, but it is not random. The difference is in the writing system, not in how able its readers are.
4|Finnish children learn to **decode** (figure out a pronunciation from a written word) very quickly. This is because Finnish spelling is very **transparent**, or **shallow** in the terms of the **Orthographic Depth Hypothesis**. Each letter matches one phoneme more or less without ambiguity. So learning one letter-sound match helps with many words. || Doubled letters mark length. A doubled consonant stands for a **geminated** consonant (a consonant held longer). A doubled vowel stands for a **long vowel**. || Following a beginning Finnish lesson along with its text makes this clear. After about a minute, a listener gets the idea. Since the matches are so consistent, children can read words they have never seen.
5|Children learn to **decode** more slowly in English than in most other languages that use the Roman alphabet. This is because English is an **outlier** among those languages. Its spelling-sound matches are less predictable. || **Aro and Wimmer** had children read **pseudowords** (made-up words that follow the spelling and sound patterns of their language) aloud. A pseudoword is not a word the child already knows. So the child has to decode it. || In **Grade 1**, children learning French, German, Dutch, Spanish, Swedish or Finnish read about 85 to 93 percent correctly. English learners in Grade 1 read only about **half** correctly. English learners got to about 88 percent by Grade 4. || Since English spelling is harder to predict, English children take longer to learn decoding. They can still decode in the end.
6|In Korean, the angular shape ㄱ goes with **velar** consonants (sounds made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate). This is because Korean writing, **Hangul**, gives similar phonemes similar symbols. That makes Korean even more regular than Finnish. || The letter ㄱ is g. Doubling it gives ㄲ, which is kk. Adding a stroke gives ㅋ, which is k. All three share the same basic shape. || Letters are put together into **syllable blocks**. Each block has an **initial** spot, a **medial** spot and sometimes a **final** spot. Since related sounds look related, the letter shapes themselves show how the sounds are organized.
7|You can read GLORP, SPLING, JONK, FEESH and QUISP aloud even though they are not English words. This is **decoding** in action. This is because each **pseudoword** follows enough English spelling patterns to build a believable pronunciation. || You have never stored a pronunciation for these items. So you cannot be remembering them. Instead, you use knowledge that **generalizes** (carries over) from the words you know. || These items test decoding, not vocabulary or meaning. Since you can read brand-new letter strings, you know more than a list of memorized words.
8|People read nonwords almost as fast and as accurately as real words. This is because decoding happens fairly **naturally**, **automatically** and **implicitly** (without conscious effort). || **Lupker, Brown and Colombo (1997)** had people read items aloud. They measured **naming time** (how quickly a pronunciation becomes available). They compared pseudowords, here called **nonwords**, with real words. || Nonwords took only **5 to 12 percent longer** than low-frequency words. The comparison is with low-frequency words, not with all real words. Since the gap is small, decoding is fast and not a slow, deliberate effort. || A later question is how to describe decoding. Computational models of word reading ask whether it works by **categorical rules** or **probabilistic constraints**. Rules and probabilistic constraints could both produce fast decoding. So both kinds of model must explain it.
9|Some pseudowords can be read in more than one reasonable way. VONTH, PHANGE, STREAD and SOUGH are examples. This is because each one has a spelling pattern that English says in more than one way. || When you read one, you use spelling-sound patterns from words you already know. Different familiar words can push you toward different answers. So these items have no single agreed pronunciation. || These items test how readers deal with **ambiguity** (more than one possible reading) in going from spelling to sound. Since more than one answer feels fine, generalization can be **graded** instead of all-or-none.
10|It is hard to imagine a rule set that explains why both /strɛd/ and /strid/ feel acceptable for STREAD. This is because a **categorical rule** gives one output. Readers accept two outputs here. || **Generalization** is a good way to test what patterns people have learned about a system. This is because a new item cannot have been memorized. So the answer reveals the pattern the reader learned. The **Wug Test** is another example of this kind of test. || The two readings of STREAD differ only in the vowel. The rest of the word stays the same. A **statistical account** can give each reading its own amount of support. Since both readings feel fine, the learned knowledge is not a single fixed rule.
11|Irregular words are harder to recognize than regular words. This is called the **regularity effect**. This is because some form of decoding is part of normal reading. Decoding an **irregular** (or **inconsistent**) word by the usual patterns gives the wrong sound. || This is clear for PINT, ACHE and CHOIR. Their usual spelling patterns point to the wrong pronunciation. || It is less clear for **neighborhoods** (groups of words that share a spelling pattern). ROLL, POLL and TOLL sound alike, but DOLL does not. COOK, TOOK and BOOK sound alike, but SPOOK does not. Since a word's neighbors can disagree, its difficulty depends partly on how they are pronounced.
12|Readers clearly use **sublexical information** (information about units smaller than a whole word), especially to turn spelling into sound. This is because people read nonwords quickly and accurately. Also, words that follow typical spelling-to-sound patterns are easier to read than words that do not. || That leaves the question of **lexical processes**. **Lexical information** is knowledge of particular words. Nonwords test general patterns. Differences among familiar words can reveal knowledge of particular words. Since decoding explains only part of reading, word knowledge needs its own account.
13|Knowing a word helps you figure out an unclear letter inside it. This is because of **feedback** in the **Interactive Activation Model** of **McClelland and Rumelhart (1981)**. Visual features activate letters. Letters activate possible words such as WEAR, WORK, WORD and WEAK. Then active words send activation back to their letters. || In a word perception example, blots cover parts of the letters in WORK. The visible features of the last letter fit K and R, but not D. Only K makes a real word, WORK. So WORK sends activation back to K. || Over time, **K** goes up close to the maximum. **R** goes up a little, then drops back down. The letter **D** stays low. Since K wins gradually, recognition builds up over time.
14|In the **Interactive Activation Model**, known words boost the letters they contain. This is because **feedback** from activation for known words increases the activation for letters in those words. || Activation goes in both directions. Letters send activation up to words as evidence. Words send activation back down to support their letters. So recognition is **interactive**. It does not only flow upward from features to a finished word. || Since words boost their own letters, an unclear letter is easier to recognize inside a familiar word.
15|Real words are named faster than nonwords, and frequent words are named faster than rare words. These are two **lexical effects**, the **lexicality effect** and the **word frequency effect**. This is because experience with a particular word makes it faster to say. || In one naming study, high-frequency words took about 442 ms. Low-frequency words took about 482 ms. Nonwords took about 541 to 552 ms. || As with **picture naming**, the more times you have seen a word, the faster you can name it. **Balota and colleagues (2004)** used data from the **English Lexicon Project**. Naming time went down steadily as frequency went up. The effect held up over a very large number of observations.
16|Frequency effects do not seem to be just a side effect of word length. This is because frequency still predicted reaction time after length and other variables were included. || The worry was a **confound** (another variable that changes along with frequency). Frequent words could also be shorter, so length might cause the speedup. || A **multiple regression analysis** (a method that measures each predictor's effect while including the others) checked this. It used about 3000 words read by 30 young and older adults. The tasks were **lexical decision** (judging whether a letter string is a real word) and **naming**. || **Objective** and **subjective frequency** had **negative** coefficients in every column. A negative coefficient means more of the predictor goes with less time. So higher frequency went with **faster** responses.
17|The **frequency effect** is much bigger for **exception words** than for **regular words**. This is the **frequency by regularity interaction**, a landmark finding in psycholinguistics. It means experience matters most when a word breaks the usual spelling pattern. || This is because exception and regular words differ much more at low frequency than at high frequency. **Naming latency** is the time it takes to start saying a word. For **high-frequency** words, both kinds take about 540 ms. For **low-frequency** words, exception words take about 583 ms. Regular words take only about 557 ms. || An **interaction** means the effect of one factor depends on the other. So the cost of being irregular is not the same for every word. This finding has affected other areas, most immediately **inflectional morphology**.
18|One explanation is that word recognition has **two separate processes**. One is the **sublexical process**, which is decoding. The other is the **lexical process**, which is knowledge of a particular word. || This explains the interaction because the lexical process works differently by frequency. For **high-frequency (HF)** words, the lexical process is so fast and efficient that decoding barely matters. || For **low-frequency (LF)** words, the lexical process is less efficient. So the sublexical process shows up. For **nonwords**, there is no stored word. So the sublexical process shows up there too. || For an LF exception word, decoding gives a regularized answer. That wrong answer competes with the right one, so naming slows down. Since HF words skip that competition, regularity matters little for them.
19|People with **acquired phonological dyslexia** have trouble specifically with reading pseudowords aloud. It is called **acquired** (as opposed to **developmental**) because it comes from brain injury. || **Cacciopo van Vliet and colleagues (2003)** tested two patients, M.O. and I.B. Both read real words and nonwords aloud. || For **real words**, both got about 85 to 100 percent right. This held for concrete and abstract words, every word class and both frequencies. For **nonwords**, they gave believable answers much less often, often under half. || The hardest nonwords had low **grapheme-to-phoneme correspondence** (a weak match between written units and sounds). Those were only about 15 to 20 percent. Since known words were fine and new forms were not, decoding was what broke down.
20|People with **acquired surface dyslexia** have trouble specifically with **irregular words** after brain injury. This is because they read words by the common spelling pattern. So their mistakes tend to be **regularizations**. || DEAF is read as “deef”, SAID as “sayed”, BROAD as “brode” and SHOE as “show”. ONE is read as “own”, FLOOD as “flude” and GONE as “goan”. STEAK is read as “steek”, SEW as “sue” and SOOT as “suit”. || Two patients, MP and KT, read regular words, exception words and nonwords. Regular words were about 89 to 100 percent correct. Nonwords were about 95 to 100 percent correct. || Exception words dropped, most of all at low frequency. KT got 47 percent on high-frequency and 26 percent on low-frequency exceptions. MP got 93 and 73 percent. Since decoding still works, these patients fall back on it.
21|Surface dyslexia and phonological dyslexia form a classic **double dissociation**. This is because the two groups show opposite patterns after brain injury. || In **acquired surface dyslexia**, irregular words are hard and come out regularized. But **pseudoword reading** is relatively spared. || In **acquired phonological dyslexia**, pseudowords are hard. But **exception word reading** is relatively spared. || A double dissociation means one group fails task A but not task B, and another group shows the reverse. Relatively spared does not mean perfect. Since the patterns are opposite, one task cannot simply be harder for everyone.
22|Double dissociations are so powerful because the damage pattern flips from one patient to another. This is because if task A were just harder than task B, damage would hurt A more in everyone. No patient would show the reverse. || In reading, phonological dyslexia hurts pseudowords more. Surface dyslexia hurts exception words more. So the two tasks must lean on different parts of the reading system. || You first compare the costs within each patient. Then you compare the two patients' patterns. Two low scores without an opposite pattern are not a double dissociation. Since the pattern reverses, the tasks rely on **separable processes**. A theory is still needed for what those processes are.
23|Double dissociations give evidence that pseudoword reading and irregular word reading use **separable** processes. This is because each dyslexia can come from damage to a different part of **Morton's logogen model**, from **Morton and Patterson (1980)**. || **Logogens** are stored word units. Visual input logogens recognize written words. Output logogens hold their spoken forms. **Grapheme-phoneme conversion** turns letters into sounds without a stored word. || **Phonological dyslexia** results from specific damage to the grapheme-phoneme conversion module. Nonwords have no stored word, so they cannot get through. || **Surface dyslexia** results from damage to input or output logogens, or the connections between them. Then exception words must go through conversion, so they come out regularized. These are parts of a model, not a brain map.
24|**Patterson (1981)** gave early signs that the dual-route idea is not so simple. This is because errors that should come from one route show signs of the other. || In the dual-route view, a word is read from its stored pronunciation or by **non-lexical grapheme-phoneme translation**. Surface dyslexia errors were thought to come from the non-lexical route. So they should show no word influences. || **Marcel (1980)** found several word influences in the errors. **Neologisms** (made-up non-words) happened less often than a purely non-lexical process predicts. One patient read incense as “increase”, partly because the c is ambiguous. A purely letter-based error would be “inkense”. || So there is a **classic dilemma**. Either surface dyslexia mixes the two routes in a more complicated way. Or non-lexical reading is a more complicated route. Models of normal reading ask the same question, as in **Glushko (1979)**. Patterson thought combining normal and patient evidence holds the best promise.
