2|Sign language and Braille are really different cases. This is because a **signed language** (a natural language with its own linguistic structure) is a language in its own right. **Braille** (a system that lets blind people read a spoken language) is a way to read a language that sighted people also read. || The two cases are still grouped together. This is because both reveal the **plasticity** (the ability of sensory systems to change with experience). Both also show how the brain gets organized differently when language input comes through a different **modality** (the sense that carries the input). The linguistic status of the two cases still differs.
3|Sign languages differ from spoken languages in four ways. This is because sign uses a different **modality** (the sense that carries the language), which is vision instead of hearing. Sign also uses different **articulators** (the body parts that produce the language), which are the hands, face and upper body. Sign also uses **space** in a linguistic way, and some signs are **iconic** (their form looks like their meaning). || **Emmorey and colleagues (2004)** list these differences. Iconicity is easy to exaggerate. This is because it looks strongest when a single sign is studied with its **gloss** (the translation word for that sign). Once the translation is known, a resemblance looks obvious. A learner still has to learn the forms and structure of the language.
4|Fluent signing uses the hands, face, upper body and eye contact together. This is because the linguistic signal is spread across all of these articulators, not one hand movement at a time. **Eye contact** is also part of the interaction. || Fluent signing is also fast and fluid. This is because signs run into each other as one continuous stream. Since the stream is continuous, a learner may find it hard to **segment** (find where each word begins and ends). Fluent speech is also continuous, so it has the same problem. || The thing to study is coordinated language production. This is because one isolated hand pose misses the coordination that makes the signal work.
5|Signs can differ in ways that change their meaning, just as speech sounds can. The main features are **hand configuration** (the shape of the hand), **location** (where the sign is made) and **movement** (how the hand moves). **Emmorey and colleagues (2003)** name these features. || The signs for "please" and "sorry" differ, and so do the signs for "onion" and "apple." This is because changing one feature can turn one sign into a different sign. Visual similarity alone does not decide whether two forms are the same sign. || A feature is **contrastive** (changing it changes the sign's identity). This is because a changed feature changes which sign is meant. Handshape, location and movement are separate dimensions to check. Since they are separate, each one has to be checked on its own.
6|Researchers asked whether **categorical perception** (a pattern where a gradual change is sorted into separate categories) applies to these sign features. The answer depends on the kind of change. This is because the test changes hand configuration in steps, from one form toward another. || Observers identify each form and also tell pairs of forms apart. This is because the two responses together show whether the change is sorted into categories. One continuum crosses a **phonemic** change (a difference that changes which sign is meant), between "please" and "sorry." The other continuum makes an **allophonic** change (a change that does not change the sign's identity). || Comparing equal steps is more informative than comparing only the endpoints. This is because a step that crosses a linguistic boundary can be compared with an equal step inside one category. So the test can separate a physical change from a change in category.
7|Deaf signers show a sharper category boundary for the phonemic change than for the allophonic change. For the phonemic change, **identification** (labeling each form as one sign or the other) shifts steeply between stimuli 4 and 6. Discrimination (telling two forms apart) peaks at that same boundary. || This is because pairs of forms that cross the boundary are easier to tell apart. For the allophonic change, identification is much shallower. This is because many more forms are ambiguous. There is no peak in discrimination. || So the perceptual pattern follows the linguistic contrast, not the amount of visible change. A sharp identification shift with a discrimination peak supports categorical perception. Identification alone would be a less complete test.
8|Sign language uses space to show how words in a sentence relate. This is because a **classifier sign** (a sign whose form stands for a kind of object) is made at a location. The relative position of two objects is given by where that classifier sign is made. || Understanding sentences in sign requires keeping track of where signs were made. This is because each object introduced in space has to be remembered at its location. Later signs relate back to those locations. So spatial processing helps comprehension, and it is not just background vision.
9|Brain activity in sign comprehension is similar to spoken language in some ways and different in others. This is because signing is **left lateralized** (its activity is concentrated in the left hemisphere), as spoken language is. Classical language areas are also active, including **Broca's area** (the inferior frontal region) and **Wernicke's area** (the posterior superior temporal region). || Signing also activates **visual areas** and the **superior parietal** region. This is because signing makes visual and spatial demands. These are classic spatial areas. || **Campbell, MacSweeney and Waters (2008)** compared deaf signers, hearing signers and hearing people who watched audio-visual speech. Hearing signers are useful. This is because signing experience is not limited to deaf people. Overlap in regions does not require identical activation everywhere.
10|**Bavelier and colleagues (2001)** asked whether deafness or experience with sign changes spatial attention. They compared hearing controls, deaf signers and hearing signers. Participants monitored for brief changes in **luminance** (brightness) or **velocity** (speed of motion) of dots. Attention was aimed at the center of the screen or at the periphery. || Deaf participants had an advantage when attention was aimed at the periphery. Hearing signers did not show that advantage. This is why signing experience alone does not explain the enhancement. || Hearing signers help separate sign experience from the lack of auditory input. This is because a deaf versus hearing comparison alone would bundle the two together. If all signers had the advantage, signing alone would be the stronger explanation.
11|Learning sign language and being deaf have different effects on visual processing. Learning sign language moves the **laterality** (which side of the brain does the work) of motion perception from right to left. Deaf and hearing signers both show this leftward shift compared with hearing nonsigners. || Enhanced peripheral attention is found only in deaf individuals. This is because hearing signers do not show it. One proposed reason is that hearing people depend more on auditory cues at the far periphery. || One proposed reason for the leftward shift is that motion perception needs to talk to left-lateralized language networks. So the two effects should not be merged into one advantage, because they track different aspects of experience.
12|Comparing sign with **tic-tac** helps separate language processing from hand movement processing. This is because tic-tac is an outdated gesture system used for horse-racing odds in the UK. Both tasks involve meaningful, sequenced hand movements. So the control shares movement demands with sign. || The control is not a natural language. This is why the remaining difference is informative. A difference between sign and tic-tac says more about language than a comparison with no movement. The comparison does not assume every meaningful gesture is a language.
13|Sign produces stronger activity around Wernicke's area than tic-tac does. **Campbell, MacSweeney and Waters (2008)** reported this comparison. Wernicke's area is in the posterior superior temporal region. This is why the comparison points to language processing rather than hand movement alone. || Shared hand movement does not explain the whole neural response to signing. This is because the frontal activation lies above Broca's area. So it should not be called a precise Broca's effect. The posterior temporal difference is the central result. The comparison weakens an explanation based only on hands moving through space.
14|Signed languages have phonological structure. Native speakers show categorical perception for some contrastive features. Learning a signed language causes some brain reorganization, especially in motion processing. || Attention to the visual periphery matters for both hearing and deaf signers. Only deaf signers show enhanced peripheral attention. || Compared with organized hand movements that are not language, signed sentences activate the **posterior superior temporal gyrus** (Wernicke's area). This is because that region is tied to language. || A full account has to separate shared language organization from sensory adaptation. This is because neither "sign is just gesture" nor "modality makes no difference" fits the evidence.
15|Understanding a signed spatial description can require a perspective change. A room can be described from the **speaker's perspective** (the signer's own viewpoint) or the **addressee's perspective** (the viewpoint of the person being addressed). Comprehension was better for descriptions from the speaker's perspective. This is true even though the listener has to make a mental transformation. || The most natural representation is not always the one with no spatial transformation. The two signing sequences describe the same table location. The difference is whose perspective organizes the description, not where the table is.
16|Braille reading is much like print reading in many ways, but its symbols are built for touch. This is because **Braille symbols** are optimized for **tactile discrimination** (telling symbols apart by touch). Reading Braille by sight is really hard. Fingers take in letters one at a time. || Braille is a way to read written language. This is because it is not a separate natural language with its own grammar. This makes Braille useful for asking how reading networks work when input comes through touch. || Sequential tactile sampling is a processing constraint. This is because fingers take in letters one at a time. It does not mean a Braille reader must name every dot before understanding a word.
17|Reading Braille activates visual cortex in blind people. The earlier the sight is lost, the more extensive the visual activity to touch. Early-blind participants show stronger involvement of the **visual word form area** (a region involved in visual word processing) than late-blind participants. || A region's usual sensory label does not completely decide what it can process. This is because visual cortex can take part in reading even though the input is touch. Early- and late-blind groups give a comparison of developmental sensory history. || Compare how much activation there is. Do not assume that every colored spot has the same function.
18|Sighted adults who wore blindfolds for five days learned Braille more quickly than a control group. **Merabet and colleagues (2008)** asked sighted adults to wear blindfolds for five days while they trained to read Braille. The control group trained without the same continuous removal of visual input. This suggests that removing competing visual input can affect tactile learning over a short time. || The study tests a question about **competition** (inputs competing for the same brain areas). The question was whether competition could block hearing people from building strong peripheral attention. Because these participants were sighted adults, the study tests a different question from early blindness. || Cross-modal changes do not have to be limited to early development. This is because a short-term change can reveal **latent** (hidden) contributions to tactile processing. Comparing the groups separates training effects from the extra effect of continuous blindfolding.
19|Visual cortex responses to Braille developed during blindfolding and reversed after the blindfold was removed on Day 6. This is because removing visual input lets tactile activity become more evident. Over the course of the experiment, sighted blindfolded participants began to show activity in **primary visual cortex** (the first cortical area for vision). They showed this activity while reading Braille. || The change was rapid and reversible. This is because the pattern fits **unmasking** (existing inputs becoming visible) rather than building a new pathway in a few days. Restoring vision shifts the balance again. || Day 6 is the post-blindfold condition. It is not just another day of blindfolding. The comparison covers Days 1, 3, 5 and 6.
20|Disrupting visual cortex with **TMS** (transcranial magnetic stimulation, a method that briefly interferes with brain activity) impaired reading in the blindfolded group only. On Day 5, error rate rose under 1 Hz TMS compared with baseline and with sham (fake) stimulation. The nonblindfolded group did not show the same selective disruption. Solid symbols mark the blindfolded participants. || The vertical axis is error rate, so an upward change means worse performance. Interfering with a region and changing behavior is stronger evidence than activity alone. This is because it shows the region contributes to the task. The effect is no longer present in the same way after blindfold removal. || One proposed explanation is that visual cortex always gets weak tactile input. This is because that input is too weak to compete with the stronger and more consistent visual input. So a short removal of visual input can unmask tactile responses in visual cortex.
21|Language circuitry stays resilient across sensory modalities. This is because classical language areas are active for sign, even though its input is very different. Braille reading seems to engage networks that are important for processing text. || Brain organization is shaped over development by competition between inputs. This is because when auditory input is unavailable, putative (assumed) auditory areas are used to process sign. Visual attention is also enhanced at the periphery. When an input is absent or reduced, other information can make a larger contribution. || The same kind of competition appears in the **Competition Model** for second language learning. Distinguish the language-network overlap from the visual-attention and tactile-recruitment findings, since they come from different experimental comparisons.
