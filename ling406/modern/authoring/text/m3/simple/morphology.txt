2|Morphology is **generative**, which means people can make new words they never memorized. This is because the language has processes for building words. For example, at some point there was no noun "generative." There was only the verb "to generate." English has a process for turning verbs into nouns, and that is **derivational morphology** (changing one kind of word into another kind). || The focus here is **inflectional morphology** (adding grammatical information to a word). In English, this pretty much means the **plural** and the **past tense**. || People sometimes stretch these patterns in playful ways. One person wrote "She found me crying and she crew too and we both crode." Crew and crode take irregular-sounding patterns and put them on cry. They are not standard English, but they come from using a pattern on a new word. Since people make forms like these, the open question is whether they use symbolic rules or patterns they learned.
3|People easily put inflections on words they have never heard. **Jean Berko Gleason (1958)** found this with the **wug test**. Children do this too. || This is because the test uses a made-up word. A child sees a drawing of a made-up creature. The words under it say "This is a WUG". Then a second creature appears. The words now say "Now there is another one. There are two of them". The child finishes "There are two" by filling in the blank. Saying **WUGS** puts the plural on a new noun. || The word is new, so the child cannot have memorized its plural. That means the child must be using a pattern they learned. The same method works for the **past tense** with made-up verbs. || Success means people have **productive knowledge** (the ability to apply a pattern to new items). Since nobody heard WUGS before, saying it means extending a pattern.
4|The English past tense has two kinds of forms, **regular** and **irregular**. || **Regular** verbs add **-ed**. This is because the regular pattern works on almost any verb. It works on real verbs like show-showed, bake-baked and wade-waded. It also works on made-up verbs like blick-blicked, trog-trogged and wumble-wumbled. The made-up verbs check whether people extend the pattern to new words. || **Irregular** verbs do not add -ed. Examples are go-went, take-took, eat-ate and sleep-slept. Each one has to be learned as an exception. Some irregulars share smaller patterns with other verbs. || This split should seem familiar from the **dual-route model** of reading. That model also kept a general pattern apart from word-specific knowledge. Since the past tense has both a pattern and exceptions, any theory has to explain both.
5|**Pinker and colleagues** said words and rules are two separate processes. This is the **Words and Rules** account. It says the two use separate representational formats. || **Regular** verbs get their past tense from a **symbolic rule** (an abstract operation like "add -ed"). This is because the rule does not need a stored past tense for each verb. So the rule can work on a brand-new verb. || **Irregular** verbs are **lexicalized** (stored as part of that word's own entry) in an **associative network**. This is because each irregular past tense is specific to its word. The network keeps word-specific information. Frequency and similarity affect it. || The account also has to stop a stored irregular from being replaced by a regularization. Since rules and stored words are different kinds of knowledge here, each kind of verb uses its own process.
6|**Pinker and Ullman (2002)** proposed a **dual-route model** for the past tense. It has two routes, a lexicon and a grammar. The input is a word stem like walk or hold, plus a grammatical feature like past tense. || The **lexicon** looks up stored forms. For hold, it finds held. It does lookup and association. It is used for roots, idioms, irregulars and some regulars. It is part of **declarative memory** (memory for words and facts), in temporo-parietal cortex. || The **grammar** puts pieces together. For walk, it adds the suffix -ed. It does combination and unification. It is used for phrases, sentences and any regular form. It is part of the **procedural system** (rules and skills), in frontal cortex and basal ganglia. || A **blocking** link lets a found irregular stop the regular form. || It is similar to the dual-route reading model because both separate word-specific knowledge from a productive operation. It is different because reading turns spelling into sound. This model changes a stem to match a grammatical feature. It also came from grammar theory, not patient data.
7|The past-tense model and the reading model look alike, but they started from different places. || The reading model came from patients. This is because it was built on neuropsychological **double dissociations** (two abilities that can each be damaged while the other is spared). || The past-tense model came from grammar theory. This is because **Pinker and colleagues** had other concerns. **Generative linguistics** at the time was mostly about **categorical, symbolic, rule-based grammars**. These rules often have **exceptions**. So the Words and Rules account keeps the rules apart from the exceptions. || Since the two models came from different starting points, they look alike but rest on different evidence.
8|The **dual-process model** puts exceptions in a second process so the grammar can stay neat. This is because linguists often just left exceptions out of the grammar. Adding machinery for them would make the grammar **inelegant** (messy and less simple). So the model handles exceptions somewhere else and keeps the grammar **elegant, symbolic and categorical**. || The second process has every property the rule route does not have. It is **word-specific** (lexical). It is **sensitive to frequency** of occurrence. It is **sensitive to phonological similarity** (how much words sound alike). || Keep-kept, weep-wept and leap-leapt are an example. These verbs sound alike and change in the same way. So an exception can belong to a small family of similar verbs. || Since the exceptions now live in their own process, the rule stays simple. The cost is a second mechanism that also needs explaining.
9|**Rumelhart and McClelland (1987)** said the past tense is all **associative learning**. This is because their model has one network that learns every verb, with no "add -ed" rule. Regularity is just a pattern the network picks up from many examples. Irregulars are learned in the same network. || The model works in three steps. First, a **fixed encoding network** turns the sound of the root form into **Wickelfeatures** (units that stand for sound patterns). Second, a **pattern associator** with **modifiable connections** maps those features onto past-tense Wickelfeatures. Third, a **decoding and binding network** turns the features into the sound of the past tense. || The learning happens in the modifiable connections. They store the mapping from present to past. Since one network handles every verb, it has to explain both the regular pattern and the exceptions.
10|Rumelhart and McClelland wanted their model to copy **U-shaped learning** in children. This is because it was one of the first learning models of a language process, so it aimed at how children develop. || U-shaped learning happens in three steps. First, children learn some frequent irregular verbs early, like run-ran. Then, on their own, they start saying **regularizations** like runned. A regularization is putting -ed on an irregular verb. Finally, they go back to saying ran. || It is called U-shaped because children get worse before they get better. Performance goes down and then comes back up. || Since the child goes right, then wrong, then right again, a theory has to explain the whole path.
11|U-shaped learning matters to the **Words and Rules** account because each part of the U is a different stage. Each stage uses a different process. || This is because early correct forms are just **imitations**. The child copies ran without using adult mechanisms. || Next, the child learns the rule and uses it on every verb. The rule is the child's **best hypothesis** for all items. So the child starts saying runned. || Over time, the child learns the exceptions **associatively**, one by one. They become part of **lexical knowledge** (the child's stored word knowledge). The **blocking process** also develops. Blocking means a stored irregular stops the regular form from being used. So ran blocks runned. || Since the rule comes in before the exceptions are stored, the child goes through a dip and then recovers.
12|Rumelhart and McClelland's model also gets worse before it gets better, with no rule at all. This is because its input changes over time. The **training set** is based on data about what children hear. || Early on, **irregular verbs** make up most of the input. The highest-frequency verbs tend to have irregular past tenses, like GIVE, TAKE, HAVE and GO. So the model learns these irregulars first. || Later, the vocabulary grows to include more regular verbs. The model **reorganizes** to pick up the regular pattern. That reorganizing **interferes** with some early irregulars. || For high-frequency irregulars, correct features dip for a short time and then climb slowly. Regular verbs climb fast and stay high. Early on, the regularized response briefly beats the correct irregular. With more training, the correct form wins and the regularized one fades. || Since the input shifts from irregulars to regulars, one network shows a U without a rule.
13|The model could take a **wug test** using real English words. This is because it was trained on only a small number of verbs. So many real verbs were brand new to the model. || The researchers gave it unfamiliar low-frequency regular verbs. Many answers were correct regular forms, like shaped, sipped and typed. Some answers were strange, like stepted for step, squawked for squat and membled for mail. || The model got most **Wickelfeatures** right. It got .92 of them right for regulars and .84 for irregulars. Overall it got .91 right. || Getting features right is not the same as getting whole words right. A word with mostly correct features can still come out wrong. Since the scores count features, the model generalized well on features but could still produce odd words.
14|Some of the model's answers changed consonants in ways people would not. This is because just four responses added or changed consonants, and they were strange. The model said maded for mate. It said squawked for squat. It said membled for mail. It said toureder for tour. || The model's authors admitted humans are unlikely to make these errors, especially the last two. They said most of these answers were **near threshold** (only weakly produced). || They suggested adding an **auto-associative network** among the output units. That network could clean up the output and make bizarre answers less likely. They had not built it yet. || Since people would never say membled or toureder, getting the learning curve right is not enough. The model also has to make words people would actually say.
15|**Prince and Pinker (1988)** said the model mixes competing past tenses into bizarre hybrids. This is because it uses **feature-only representations** (representations made only of features). With features alone, it is hard to represent two things at once. || They gave a vision example. Features alone cannot tell a green circle near a red square from a red circle near a green square. || The past tense has the same problem. In the Rumelhart and McClelland model, subregularities and the regular rule all compete to turn on Wickelfeatures for the past form. For sing, features for sing, sang, sung, singed and sanged can all be on at once. || So the model does not put out one word. It puts out an unordered pile of features, and many contradict each other. That is how hybrids like toureder for tour and membled for mail appear. Since the features blend, the model cannot keep the competing answers separate.
16|**Joanisse and Seidenberg (1999)** said sound supports regular patterns, while meaning gives extra help to irregulars. This is because their model links **speech input**, **semantics** (word meaning) and **speech output** in one system. Speech input and semantics both connect through a middle layer to speech output. || **Rule-like generalization** comes from **phonological representations** (the sound forms of words). The model learns the common sound pattern from present to past. That lets it extend the pattern to new words. || **Irregular** forms need more support from **semantics**. This is because an irregular does not follow the common sound pattern. So the model leans on what the word means to get it right. || Since both kinds of verb use the same system, regulars and irregulars differ only in how much they use sound and meaning.
17|Joanisse and Seidenberg built their model to explain a patient **dissociation**. This is because some patients are worse at **irregular** past tenses, while other patients are worse at **nonword** past tenses. || The model has to produce both patterns from one system. A nonword has no stored meaning. So it depends on phonological generalization. An irregular does not fit the main pattern well. So it depends more on semantics. || If that is right, damaging phonology should hurt nonwords most. Damaging semantics should hurt irregulars most. || Each item type leans on a different part of the system. So damage to each part should give opposite profiles.
18|**Broca's aphasia** patients have trouble with **regular morphology**, especially **nonwords**. This is because of injury to **left anterior** brain regions. These patients also have **poor speech production**. || Nonwords are the hardest. Examples are wug becoming wugged and blick becoming blicked. A nonword has no stored past tense, so the patient has to build it from the pattern. || Their scores follow a clear order. Irregulars are about 85 percent correct. Regulars are about 82 percent correct. Nonwords are about 69 percent correct. || The drop for nonwords is much bigger than the gap between irregulars and regulars. Since nonwords depend most on building a form from sound, this raises the question of whether the real problem is phonology.
19|When the model's **phonology** is damaged, it makes the same pattern as Broca's patients. This is because the damage **reduced** the model's ability to compute past-tense forms. || **Nonwords** came out worse than regulars and irregulars. Model nonwords were about 72 percent correct. Patient nonwords were about 69 percent. || **Regulars** came out a little worse than irregulars, but only in the numbers. Model regulars were about 80 percent. Model irregulars were about 85 percent. || Familiar words still get support from their training. A new nonword has none of that support. So weak sound processing hurts nonwords the most. || Since the model had no rule to lose, a phonological problem can look like a morphology problem.
20|Some patients have the opposite problem, and they are **poor on irregulars**. This is true of **Wernicke's** and **Alzheimer's** patients. It is also true of **anomic** patients (patients who have trouble finding words) with injury to **inferior or middle temporal** brain regions. || Their scores follow the reverse order. Irregulars are about 60 percent correct. Regulars are about 89 percent correct. Nonwords are about 84 percent correct. || This is the other half of the dissociation. Broca's patients do worst on nonwords. These patients do worst on irregulars. Since the two groups fail on opposite item types, a theory has to explain both.
21|When the model's **semantics** is damaged, irregulars come out worst. This is because irregulars lean on meaning more than other verbs do. So losing meaning hurts them the most. || **Irregulars** were worse than regulars and nonwords. Model irregulars were about 62 percent correct. Alzheimer's patients were about 60 percent on irregulars. || **Nonwords** were a bit worse than regulars. Model nonwords were about 77 percent. Model regulars were about 83 percent. Nonwords are a bit worse because they have no training of their own. || Regulars and nonwords still get help from typical sound patterns. Since only meaning was damaged, the model matched these patients without deleting any list of exceptions.
22|Two studies found that the patient pattern depends on sound and meaning, not on rules versus words. || **Bird, Patterson, Lambon-Ralph, McClelland and Seidenberg (2003)** tested **Broca's patients**. They controlled the regular and irregular items adequately for **phonology**. The gap between regulars and irregulars got **smaller**. This is because part of the gap came from sound differences between the items. || **Patterson, Lambon-Ralph, Hodges and McClelland (2001)** tested **semantic dementia** patients (people losing word meanings). They compared how bad each patient was on irregulars with how bad the semantic deficit was. The two **correlated strongly**. This is because irregulars depend on meaning, so they get worse as meaning is lost. || Since both effects line up with sound and meaning, the dissociation does not need separate rules and words.
23|This account is similar to the **triangle model** of dyslexia. Sound supports generalization, and meaning supports odd forms. || **Phonological representations** support **generalization**. This is because generalization comes from **statistical learning** (picking up patterns from many examples) about the mapping from present to past. So if phonological representations are damaged, these generalizations get worse. || **Semantic representations** support **idiosyncratic** forms (forms particular to one word). This is because forms that break the overwhelmingly regular "add -ed" pattern need help from meaning. || Some irregulars can use **sub-patterns** shared with similar verbs. Those irregulars are likely to be **spared**. Since every form uses both systems, regulars and irregulars differ only in how much they use each one.
24|**Specific language impairment (SLI)** is language development that is impaired, while "normal" performance and **nonverbal IQ** stay intact. || It affects **grammar** in two areas. This is because it hurts both **morphology** (how words are built) and **syntax** (how sentences are built). || It also affects things outside grammar. These are **phonology** (speech sounds), **reading**, **working memory** and **speech perception**. || Since SLI reaches past grammar, a theory that puts the whole problem in grammar has to explain these other problems too.
25|Earlier claims said SLI is a problem with **learning rules**. This is because **past-tense deficits** are a **sine qua non** (essential feature) of SLI. Gopnik, van der Lely, Pinker, Ullman and Clahsen said the deficit was specific to rules. || A rule deficit predicts a clear pattern. People with SLI should have trouble with **regulars**. They should have trouble with **nonwords**. They should not have trouble with **irregulars**, because irregulars are stored words. || If that pattern held, SLI could mean that language acquisition needs **domain-specific** mechanisms (mechanisms used only for language). Since the claim depends on irregulars being fine, the real test is how people with SLI do on irregulars.
26|People with SLI did badly on every kind of past tense, not just regulars and nonwords. This was the finding of **van der Lely and Ullman (2001)**, who compared an **SLI group** with **same-age controls**. || The SLI group was low on all three types. Regulars were about 22 percent correct. Irregulars were about 18 percent. Nonwords were about 7 percent. || The controls were much higher on all three types. Regulars were about 95 percent correct. Irregulars were about 88 percent. Nonwords were about 74 percent. || This is a problem for the rule account because irregulars were **not spared**. Irregulars were almost as low as regulars. Nonwords were still the hardest. Since every type was hurt, the deficit is broader than a rule deficit predicts.
27|When the model's **phonology** was weakened, it learned every past-tense form poorly, and **nonwords** were worst. This is because weaker sound processing makes every form harder to learn. || With a **severe phonological deficit**, regulars were about 39 percent correct. Irregulars were about 17 percent, and nonwords were about 7 percent. || With a **weaker phonological deficit**, regulars were about 55 percent. Irregulars were about 38 percent, and nonwords were about 15 percent. || The **intact** model did much better. At the end of training, regulars were about 100 percent, irregulars about 94 percent and nonwords about 80 percent. At 700K iterations, regulars were about 90 percent, and irregulars and nonwords were both about 75. || The weakened models have the SLI order, with regulars best and nonwords worst. Since only phonology was weakened, no missing rule is needed.
28|Phonology affects the learning of **all** past-tense forms. This is because every spoken past tense uses sound. So when phonology is weak, regulars, irregulars and nonwords all go down. || Phonology is **especially important** for **nonwords**. This is because a nonword has no training of its own. So the only way to make its past tense is to use a sound pattern. || Familiar words get extra help from what was learned about that specific word. So they lose less when phonology is weak. || Since nonwords have nothing else to lean on, they show the biggest deficit. Every other form still depends on phonology too.
29|In this account there are no **special rules** for the past tense. There are only mappings between **phonology** and **semantics**. This is because the system learns how sound and meaning connect, instead of storing a rule. It is similar to the **triangle model** of reading. || There is also no **separate lexicon** that stores the exceptions. The words **rule** and **exception** only roughly describe what the system does. || Two findings back this up. **Irregular** forms are tied to **semantic processing**. **Wug test** performance is tied to **phonological processing**. || Since irregulars go with meaning and new words go with sound, one learning system can explain both without a rule or a list of exceptions.
