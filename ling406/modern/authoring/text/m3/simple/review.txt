2|English spelling is hard to sound out, so English is called a **deep** script. This is because English has many irregular spellings. **Orthographic depth** (how consistently spelling matches pronunciation) is the name for this difference between scripts. A deep script has many irregular spellings. A **shallow** script has regular ones. || Other scripts are much more regular. **Finnish** and **Korean** are very shallow scripts. **Pinyin** for Mandarin and **French** are relatively regular too. || A **"mispronouncing contest"** made the point that **phonemes are not letters** and **letters are not phonemes**. People purposely mispronounced words like COLONEL and PARADIGM. They did this by saying the words the way the letters suggest. The real pronunciations do not follow the letters. Since the letters do not give the sounds, English readers have to learn many words through experience.
3|Finnish is very easy to sound out, so Finnish children learn to **decode** (figure out a pronunciation from the written form) very quickly. This is because Finnish is a **transparent** script. The **Orthographic Depth Hypothesis** calls this **shallow**. Each letter matches more or less one **phoneme** (one sound category). || Double letters also have a fixed meaning. A doubled consonant is a **geminated** consonant (a consonant held longer). A doubled vowel is a long vowel. || Following a beginning Finnish lesson along with its text makes this clear. After about a minute, a listener gets the idea. Since each letter reliably gives one sound, what a child learns from one word works for many other words.
4|Kids learn to decode more slowly in English than in most other alphabetic languages. This is because English is an **outlier** among languages that use the Roman alphabet. Its spelling is much less regular. || The evidence comes from reading **pseudowords** (made-up words that follow a language's spelling and sound patterns). A child has never seen a pseudoword before. So the child cannot read it from memory. The child has to sound it out from letter-sound patterns. || In **Grade 1**, kids learning many other languages come up with plausible pseudoword pronunciations at a **much higher rate** than English learners. Since English letter-sound patterns are less predictable, English learners have a harder time sounding out new words.
5|Korean is even more regular than Finnish, because similar sounds get similar-looking symbols. This is because the shape of a Korean letter reflects how its sound is made. So sounds that are made in a similar way look alike on the page. The letters are also grouped into **syllable blocks** with a first, middle and sometimes last position. || The feature for **velar place of articulation** (sounds made with the back of the tongue at the soft palate) is the angular shape **ㄱ**. This is because all the velar consonants share that shape. The sounds **g**, **kk** and **k** all use it. Doubling the shape or adding a stroke tells them apart. Since related sounds share a shape, a reader can see the sound family in the letters.
6|Being rare slows down naming much more for **exception words** than for **regular words**. This is called the **frequency by regularity interaction**, and it is a landmark finding in psycholinguistics. || This is because the two factors work together. **Naming latency** (how long it takes to start saying a written word) is the measure. A regular word follows the usual spelling-sound pattern. An exception word does not. For **common words**, exception and regular words both take about 540 ms. For **rare words**, exception words go up to about 580 ms. Regular rare words go up only to about 555 ms. || An **interaction** means the effect of one factor depends on the other factor. Since the exception cost shows up mainly for rare words, experience matters most when spelling and sound disagree. The finding has impacted other areas, most immediately **inflectional morphology**.
7|One way to explain the interaction is that reading uses **two separate processes**, and the slower one only shows up for rare words and nonwords. || This is because the two processes have different jobs. The **sublexical process** is decoding, which sounds a word out from its spelling. The **lexical process** is knowing a particular word. || For **high-frequency (HF) words**, the lexical process is very fast. So decoding has little chance to affect the answer. For **low-frequency (LF) words**, the lexical process is slower. So the decoded pronunciation shows up. For **nonwords**, there is no stored word at all. So reading depends on decoding. || For a rare exception word, decoding gives the regular, wrong pronunciation. That wrong answer gets in the way of the right one. Since this conflict only happens when the lexical process is slow, regularity matters most for rare words.
8|**Seidenberg and McClelland (1989)** built a **PDP model** that needs no separate rules and no separate lexicon. A PDP model is a network that learns connections between spelling and sound. || This is because of two different assumptions from dual-route models. First, **"rules"** are just generalizations from lots of words. They are not a separate process. Second, the **"lexicon"** is spread out across the system. Some whole-word information is stored in the **O to P mappings** (spelling to sound connections). Some is stored in other parts of the system. || The research strategy is also different. Dual-route models work backward from adult and **neuropsychological** data to figure out the parts of the system. The PDP approach explains reading with **domain-general learning mechanisms** (learning that is not special to reading). Since one learning system does everything, word knowledge and patterns live in the same place.
9|The PDP model produced the **frequency by regularity interaction** without separate processes for frequency and regularity. || This is because both factors come from learning. **Regular words** match the patterns of many other words. So those other words help the model with them. **Exception words** do not match those patterns. So they get no help. **Frequency** affects how well a word is learned. A common word is practiced many times. || A common exception word is practiced so often that not matching the pattern hardly matters. A rare exception word gets little practice and no help from other words. Since a rare exception word has both problems, it is the hardest of all.
10|A PDP model reads **nonwords** (made-up letter strings) with the same machinery it uses for real words. This is because nonwords are named with the **same set of weights** (learned connection strengths) used for words. So no separate rule system is needed. || The model sounds out a new nonword by **generalization**. This is because a nonword shares spelling patterns with many real words. Those same **statistical patterns** also make regular words easy. Since the patterns that help regular words also apply to new strings, one route can handle both words and nonwords.
11|In practice, the **Seidenberg and McClelland** model was not very good at reading nonwords. || This is a classic case of **ambiguity in model evaluation** (not knowing what a bad result means). This is because a bad result has two possible causes. The **theoretical claim** might be wrong. That claim is that nonwords and exceptions can be read by the same associative mechanism. Or the problem might be something about **how the model was made**. || The way to tell is to fix the model and keep the theory. If the fixed model reads nonwords well, the theory survives. Since the later fix came from better **phonological representations** (how the model codes speech sounds), the problem was at least partly in the build.
12|Better sound coding made a later model much better at nonwords. This is because nonword reading is tied to **phonological representations** (how speech sounds are coded). Many studies find that **decoding** (nonword reading) goes along with **phonological processing**. || The first model used odd sound codes. **Seidenberg and McClelland (1989)** used **Wickelfeatures**. Wickelfeatures did a poor job of coding which words sound alike. So the model's nonword reading suffered. || **Plaut and colleagues (1996)** improved the phonological representations. Their model did **better on nonwords**. They did not add a separate rule system. Since only the sound coding changed, the first model's failure came at least partly from how it was built.
13|Poor sound coding hurts nonwords more than real words. This is because **generalization depends on similarity**, a principle that comes from **behaviorism**. A network handles a new item by how similar it is to items it learned. || Poor representations like Wickelfeatures fail to capture the **similarity space** (which words in the language sound alike). So learning does not carry over well to new forms. || Real words are protected. This is because the network has **word-specific information** (training on that exact word). That information does not depend entirely on generalization. So it holds up even with poor representations. || Nonwords are not protected. They have no word-specific information. So they depend completely on generalization. Since generalization is what breaks, nonwords suffer most.
14|**Harm and Seidenberg (1999)** made a model's sound coding noisy. The model then ended up with the reading pattern of **developmental dyslexia** (a reading problem that appears during development). || This is because they changed only the amount of **noise** in the phonological representations. More noise makes the sound codes less reliable. The rest of the learning model stayed the same. || The noisy models matched two key findings. First, the bigger the **phonological deficit**, the worse the **phonological dyslexia** (trouble sounding out nonwords). Second, **word recognition** was relatively spared. Since only sound coding changed, a weak sound system alone can produce this pattern.
15|**Pinker and Ullman (2002)** made a dual-route model for the past tense. It is similar to the reading dual-route model in one way and different in another. || The model takes a **word stem** (like walk or hold) and a **grammatical feature** (like past tense). The **lexicon** route looks the form up, so hold gives held. The **grammar** route adds a suffix, so walk gives walk plus -ed. A **blocking** link lets a found irregular stop the regular form. || The lexicon is part of **declarative memory** (memory for words and facts) in temporo-parietal cortex. The grammar is part of the **procedural system** (rules and skills) in frontal cortex and basal ganglia. || It is similar because both models split **word-specific knowledge** from a **general operation**. It is different because reading turns spelling into sound. This model instead changes a stem based on a grammatical feature.
16|The past-tense dual-route model looks like the reading model, but it came from different reasons. || The reading model came from **neuropsychological double dissociations** (patients whose problems show up in opposite ways). This is because those patients suggested two separate routes. || **Pinker and colleagues** were motivated by other concerns. This is because **generative linguistics** at the time was mostly about categorical, symbolic, rule-based grammars. These rules often have **exceptions**. So a second route gives the exceptions somewhere to go. Since the two models came from different evidence, a similar diagram does not mean similar reasons.
17|The dual-process model gives exceptions their own process so that the grammar can stay neat. This is because linguists usually just left exceptions out of the grammar. They did not want to make the grammar messy with extra machinery. The dual-process model handles the exceptions and keeps the grammar elegant, symbolic and categorical. || The second process has all the properties the rule route does not have. It is **word-specific** (lexical). It is **sensitive to frequency** of occurrence. It is **sensitive to phonological similarity** (how alike words sound). Keep-kept, weep-wept and leap-leapt are a family of similar-sounding irregulars. Since the lexicon takes all the messy cases, the rule can stay simple.
18|**Rumelhart and McClelland (1987)** said the past tense is **all associative learning**, with no separate rule. || This is because one trainable network learns the past tense for every verb. It turns the sound of a verb root into **Wickelfeatures** (the model's sound code). A **pattern associator** with changeable connections turns that pattern into a past-tense pattern. Then the output pattern is turned back into a past-tense form. || Regular forms are just a pattern the network picks up from many examples. Irregular forms are learned in the same network. Since one network learns both, there is no need for a separate -ed rule.
19|Children get past tenses right, then get worse, then get better again, which is called **U-shaped learning**. Rumelhart and McClelland focused on this because their model was one of the first learning models of a language process. || This is because learning happens in three steps. First, children learn some common irregular verbs quite early, like run-ran. Then, on their own, children start producing **regularizations** (regular endings on irregular verbs), like runned. Eventually, they produce the correct forms again. || The name comes from the shape of performance over time. Performance goes down before it goes back up. Since the errors come after early success, a theory has to explain the whole path, not just the end.
20|U-shaped learning matters to the **words and rules** approach because the approach explains it with separate stages and processes. || This is because each stage uses a different process. At first, early correct forms are just **imitations**. They do not use the same mechanisms as adult processing. || When the **rule** is first learned, children apply it to every verb. The rule is their best guess for all items. So they say runned. || Over time, children learn **exceptions** one by one, by association. These become part of their word knowledge. The **blocking process** also develops. So a stored irregular now stops the rule. Since the rule arrives before the blocking, there is a dip in the middle.
21|Rumelhart and McClelland's single network also got worse before it got better, because its training input changed over time. || This is because the **training set** is based on data about the input to children. Early on, **irregular verbs** make up most of the input. The most common verbs tend to have irregular past tenses, like GIVE, TAKE, HAVE and GO. So the model learns these irregular forms first. || Later the vocabulary grows to include more **regular forms**. The model reorganizes to pick up those regular patterns. This **interferes** with some of the early-learned forms. So the model starts getting them wrong. With more training, the irregular forms come back. Since the dip comes from a change in input, no separate rule is needed.
22|The model was trained on only a **small number of verbs**, so real English verbs could work as a **"wug" test**. A wug test asks for the past tense of a word the learner has not learned. This works because a real verb is still new to the model if the model never trained on it. || The test used **unfamiliar low-frequency regular verbs**. Many answers were correct, like guarded, mapped and typed. Some were odd, like shipped for shape. || The model got **92 percent** of **Wickelfeatures** right for regular verbs. It got 84 percent right for irregular verbs and 91 percent overall. Getting most features right is not the same as getting whole words right. Since the test words were new to the model, these scores measure how well it generalized.
23|The model sometimes made strange past tenses that people would probably never make. || There were just four responses that added or changed consonants. The model gave maded for mate. It gave squawked for squat. It gave membled for mail. It gave toureder for tour. || The authors agreed that humans would be unlikely to make these errors, especially the last two. This is because these responses were mostly **near threshold** (weak, borderline outputs). The authors suggested adding an **auto-associative network** among the output units. That network could clean up the output and avoid bizarre answers. They had not yet built it. Since people do not make these errors, a good learning curve alone is not enough.
24|**Joanisse and Seidenberg (1999)** made a newer model where sound handles regular patterns and meaning helps with irregular forms. Their model connects **speech input**, **semantics** (word meaning) and **speech output**. || This is because of two starting ideas. First, **rule-like generalization** is supported by **phonological representations** (sound codes). Second, **irregular forms** need more support from **semantics**. An irregular cannot be figured out from sound alone, so the meaning of that particular word helps. || Since both kinds of verbs use the same shared systems, there is no separate rule route.
25|The **Joanisse and Seidenberg (1999)** model tries to explain why some patients fail on **irregular** past tenses while others fail on **nonword** past tenses. This pattern is a **dissociation** in neuropsychological data. || This is because each item type depends on something different. A **nonword** like wug has no meaning. So its past tense depends on **phonology** (sound). An **irregular** verb does not follow the usual pattern. So it depends more on **semantics** (meaning). || So damage to sound should hurt nonwords most. Damage to meaning should hurt irregulars most. Since one model has both parts, it can produce both patient patterns.
26|Patients with **Broca's aphasia** have the most trouble putting **nonwords** into the past tense. || This group has injury to **left anterior** brain regions. They have **poor speech production**. They have problems with **regular morphology** (regular word endings), especially for nonwords like wug to wugged and blick to blicked. || The scores make this clear. Nonwords were the lowest, at about 69 percent correct. Regular verbs were about 82 percent correct. Irregular verbs were about 85 percent correct. || This is because a nonword has no stored past tense. So it has to be built from sound patterns. Since that depends on sound, this pattern points to a **phonological** problem.
27|**Wernicke's** and **Alzheimer's** patients have the most trouble with **irregular** past tenses. So do **anomic** patients with injury to inferior or middle temporal brain regions. || The scores make this clear. Irregular verbs were about 60 percent correct. Regular verbs were about 89 percent correct. Nonwords were about 84 percent correct. || This is the reverse of the Broca's pattern. This is because irregulars need help from **semantics** (word meaning). These patients' problems fit damage to that support. Since nonwords and regulars do not need that help, they stay fairly good.
28|The classical view splits aphasia into two neat types, which is an **attractive dichotomy**. || **Wernicke's aphasia** gives "word salad" or "Jabberwocky," often with **neologisms** (made-up words). Syntax stays roughly intact. One patient said "My wires don't hire right" about his poor vision. Another called a fork "a need for a schedule." So the speech flows but makes little sense. || **Broca's aphasia** gives **telegraphic** speech. Patients have trouble with **word order** and **grammatical particles** (small grammar words). Important **content words** are relatively spared. One patient described his hospital visit in bits like "Two doctors ... and ah ... teeth." So the speech is halting but keeps the main words.
29|Broca's own patients would not necessarily be called Broca's aphasics today. This is because they were probably unable to produce speech at all. The cause seems to have been **motor control** problems. Today they would be classified as having **apraxia of speech** (trouble planning speech movements). || The modern dichotomy treats **Wernicke's aphasia** as problems with **activation** in the lexicon, or maybe inhibition. It treats **Broca's aphasia** as problems with **grammar**. That is fairly far from how Broca described his cases. || His patient **Leborgne**, called "Tan," could only produce one jargon syllable. He otherwise seemed to have all his senses. He even seemed to understand what was said to him. Since he could not speak at all, his problem looks like movement, not grammar.
30|The aphasia dichotomy is appealing because it lines up with the sharp split between **grammar** and **lexicon** in late 20th century formal linguistics. || This is because that split starts with **Chomsky's** framing of the **computational problem of language learning**. People recognize a new sentence because their internal grammar generates it. The sentence does not have to match any familiar sentence. || Chomsky also argued that **syntax and semantics are independent**. **Chomsky (1955)** gave two word strings. Neither "colorless green ideas sleep furiously" nor "furiously sleep ideas green colorless" had come up in an English speaker's experience. Yet the first is grammatical and the second is not. Since grammar works apart from meaning, a grammar problem and a word problem seemed like separate aphasias.
31|The speech production model also separates an arbitrary word level from a grammar level. These are the **functional level** and the **positional level**. || The levels go in order. The **message level** comes first and sets up concepts and events. The **functional level** brings up word meanings and semantic roles. The **positional level** puts word forms in a grammatical order. **Phonological encoding** and **articulation** come last. || The two middle levels work differently. This is because **lemmas** (word representations chosen at the functional level) have an **arbitrary** link to what they mean. The **syntactic trees** and **affixes** at the positional level come from abstract syntactic processes. || Words like HOUSEBOAT and DOGHOUSE seem to be a problem, because their meaning is not arbitrary. But speech errors like "easy enoughly" show **derivational morphology** happens at the positional level.
32|Most languages describe nearby things **egocentrically** (from the speaker's own body), but Tzeltal uses **absolute directions** (directions that stay fixed when a person turns). || Egocentric words are left and right, in front and behind. They change whenever the speaker turns around. || **Tzeltal** is a Western Mayan language spoken in Chiapas. It does not describe nearby space this way. This is because Tzeltal uses absolute directions even for nearby things. A small number of other languages do too. They are mostly unrelated to each other and spread widely, for example in Bali and Queensland. || To an English speaker, being asked to "raise your east hand" sounds strange. Since English uses left and right, absolute terms feel unnatural to its speakers.
33|In Tzeltal, things are **uphill** or **downhill** from each other, not to the left or right. This is because Tzeltal has a historically **landmark-based** system. The terms come from the slope of the land around Tenejapa. || "Uphill" and "downhill" are used as **cardinal directions** (fixed directions like compass directions). So Tzeltal is an **absolute** system. The terms do not just point to the nearest hill. They stay the same no matter which way the speaker faces. || **Levinson and colleagues (2002)** described this system. Since the directions do not depend on the speaker, they stay the same when the speaker turns.
34|In a memory task with no words, **Dutch** speakers remembered space relative to their body, and **Tzeltal** speakers mostly remembered it in absolute terms. **Majid and colleagues (2004)** ran this **chips task**. || A person sees a card at Table 1 with a large dot and a small dot. After a 30-second wait, the person is **turned 180 degrees** and led to Table 2. Then the person picks the matching card out of four. || This is because turning around makes the two ways of remembering give different answers. A **relative** answer keeps the dots the same compared to the person's body. An **absolute** answer keeps the dots the same compared to the world, such as north. || Dutch answers were overwhelmingly relative. Tzeltal answers were mostly absolute. Since no language was used, the choices reflect how each group remembers space.
35|Dutch and Tzeltal speakers also used their own spatial frames when combining two facts to place an object. **Majid and colleagues (2004)** ran this harder task. || At Table 1, the person sees a cube and a cone. The person turns 180 degrees to Table 2 and sees the cube with a cylinder. Then the person turns back to Table 1, where the cone stands alone. The task is to place the cylinder next to the cone. || This is because the two frames give different answers. With **relative** coding, the cube is right of the cone and the cylinder is right of the cube. So the cylinder goes right of the cone. With **absolute** coding, the cube is south of the cone and the cylinder is further south. So the cylinder goes south of the cone. || **Dutch** speakers gave relative answers. **Tzeltal** speakers gave absolute answers.
36|**Li and Gleitman** argued that where people were tested, not just their language, might explain the Tzeltal results. They tried **inducing landmark-based similarity in English speakers**. || This is because the testing places were different. The **Tzeltal speakers** were tested outside, where familiar **landmarks** were visible. Typical psychology lab studies happen in **windowless rooms**. || Visible landmarks can push people toward absolute coding. A windowless room has no landmarks. So relative coding is easier there. Since the setting differed along with the language, the setting could explain part of the difference.
37|English speakers used relative position less when they could see landmarks outside. **Li and Gleitman** found this by changing the view during testing. || With the **blinds down**, English speakers overwhelmingly used **relative** position. They acted like the Dutch speakers in earlier studies. With the **blinds up** and a view of the street, the relative preference was **weaker**. **Outdoors**, it was also weaker. || This is because only the setting changed. The language stayed the same. So the change must come from the available landmarks. A weaker preference is not a full switch to absolute coding. Since the same speakers changed with the setting, language alone does not set the spatial frame.
38|**Levinson and colleagues** were not convinced by Li and Gleitman, because they thought the task made its purpose too easy to guess. || This is because Li and Gleitman made it too **transparent** that the task was about rotation. They **reduced the memory load**. They also cut down the **time and distance** between seeing the display and being tested. So people could figure out the trick instead of using their normal memory. || The participants were also **Ivy League students**. Such students are very good at figuring out what the experimenter wants and then doing it. Since these students could guess the goal, their answers might not show how they normally remember space.
39|The question is whether signers show **categorical perception** (seeing a gradual change as a sudden jump between categories) for **hand configuration** in sign language. || This is tested with two step-by-step series of signs. The **phonemic** series changes the hand from the sign PLEASE to the sign SORRY. So it crosses a contrast between two different signs. The **allophonic** series changes the hand from open-N to closed-N in the sign SAY-NO-TO. So the hand changes, but the sign stays the same. || People **identify** each step and **discriminate** pairs of steps. Categorical perception predicts a sharp switch in identification. It also predicts the best discrimination right at that switch. Since only the phonemic series crosses a real contrast, only it should show this.
40|Yes, deaf signers show **categorical perception** for the phonemic hand contrast, but not for the allophonic one. The curves have the familiar categorical shape. || This is because the **phonemic** series has a fairly sharp **boundary** between stimuli 4 and 6. That boundary lines up with the **peak** in the **discrimination function** (how well people tell two steps apart). So pairs that cross the boundary are the easiest to tell apart. || The **allophonic** series is different. Its identification function is **much shallower**. That means many more steps are ambiguous. Its discrimination function has **no peak**. Since only the meaningful contrast shows the pattern, the perception follows language, not just the look of the hand.
41|Brain activity for sign language is a lot like brain activity for speech, with some differences. **Campbell, MacSweeney and Waters (2008)** compared deaf signers, hearing signers and hearing people perceiving audio-visual speech. || Sign is similar to speech. It is **left lateralized** (mostly on the left side of the brain). It uses the **classical language areas**. These are Broca's area in the inferior frontal region and Wernicke's area in the posterior superior temporal region. || Sign is also different. It uses more **visual areas** and fewer **auditory areas**. This is because sign is seen, not heard. It also uses **superior parietal** areas, which are classic spatial areas. Since sign uses space, those spatial areas get involved.
42|Sign language is a real language with its own sound-like structure, and learning it changes the brain. || Sign language has **phonological structure** (organized form units). Native signers show **categorical perception** for some contrastive features. || Learning a signed language leads to some **brain reorganization**. The clearest example is the **lateralization of motion processing** (motion processing shifting to one side). || Attention to the edges of vision, the **periphery**, matters for both hearing and deaf signers. But only **deaf signers** show better processing of attention in the periphery. So that change comes with deafness. || Signed sentences activate **posterior STG** (Wernicke's area). This is compared with non-language hand movements that are also organized and sequenced. Since plain hand movements do not do this, the activation comes from language.
43|Johnson and Newport see the **maturational state hypothesis** as fitting their data better. Both versions of the **critical period hypothesis** say people start life with a superior ability to learn languages. || The **exercise hypothesis** says the ability fades only if it is not used early. If it is used, later language learning stays intact for life. The **maturational state hypothesis** says the ability fades with age either way. || This is because **Johnson and Newport (1989)** studied people who already had a first language. So their ability had been exercised. They still learned a second language less well when they started later. || This does change how the **L1 critical period** data are read. People who learn a first language late never exercised the ability. So both versions predict they will do poorly. Since both predict it, L1 data cannot tell the versions apart. Early first-language experience still matters.
44|**Mayberry, Lock and Kazmi (2002)** found that people who had no language early do much worse at learning a language later. || Many **deaf children** get no sign language input but have an otherwise normal upbringing. Kids aged 9 to 15 have a hard time learning **American Sign Language (ASL)** if it is their first language. Kids who went deaf after learning a **spoken language** do much better. These differences last into adulthood. || As adults, people with no early language recalled about 36 percent of ASL sentences. People with an early spoken language recalled about 78 percent. On English grammar judgments, people with no early language scored about 52 percent, near the 50 percent chance level. People with early signed or spoken language scored about 80 percent. || This is because an early language helps with learning any later language. Since early signed and spoken language helped equally, what matters is having some language early.
45|The **semantic blocking effect** (slower naming when same-category objects are named together) probably does not come from objects looking alike. || The worry is that objects in the same category might look similar. Then the effect could come from **visual object recognition**, not from naming. That would be a **confound** (another possible cause). || This is ruled out in two ways. First, the stimuli were **normed**. On average, same-category objects were not more similar-looking than objects from different categories. Second, a **control experiment** grouped objects by **visual similarity** instead. It found different results. The visual blocking effect showed up in the **first block of trials**. The semantic effect only shows up after the first cycle. Since the timing differs, the semantic effect is not a visual effect.
46|**Belke and colleagues** showed four objects at the same time but had people name them one after another. || This is because of a worry from another study. **Hermens and colleagues** showed people several objects at once. They found no blocking effect. So maybe blocking only happens when objects are seen one at a time. || In the Belke design, people viewed displays of **four items at once**. They said the four names **in order**. All the objects were visible together. But the names still had to be picked one after another. Since viewing and naming are now separated, the design can tell whether serial viewing matters.
47|**Belke and colleagues** tracked eye movements to time each step of naming. || This is because the eyes and the voice do not move together. **Gaze duration** is how long the eyes stay on one object. **Eye-speech lag** is the time between the eyes leaving an object and starting to say its name. || A speaker often looks at the next object before saying the current name. So looking at an object and saying its name happen at different times. Since the two measures cover different times, each one can track a different stage of getting a word ready.
48|The blocking effect shows up only in gaze duration, so it happens at **lemma selection** (choosing the word). The comparison is **homogeneous** blocks (same category) against **heterogeneous** blocks (mixed). || This is because each measure matches a different stage. **Overall speech duration** gets shorter over the experiment. It shows **no blocking effect**. It reflects the **gesture level** (moving the mouth). || **Gaze duration** shows a **blocking effect**, but only after the **first cycle**.
