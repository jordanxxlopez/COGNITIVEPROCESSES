2|The model proposes two reading routes, because reading has several findings that one route would have trouble explaining. This is because **pseudoword reading** (reading pronounceable made-up words) is fast and automatic. Word reading is faster than that. Reading is fastest for **high-frequency** words, which are words that appear often in language. || **Regularity** (how consistently a spelling maps onto one pronunciation) also affects accuracy and response time. **Frequency and regularity interact**, so the effect of one depends on the other. || In **acquired dyslexia** (reading loss after brain damage), pseudoword reading and irregular word reading can be **dissociated**. This means one can be impaired without the other. This is why two separate routes seem needed. || The model has a **sublexical route** and a **lexical route**. The sublexical route does **grapheme-to-phoneme conversion** (turning written units into sounds), which is also called **decoding**. The lexical route recognizes real words.
3|The Dual Route Cascaded model joins an interactive word route to a separate rule route. **Coltheart and colleagues (2001)** published this model in Psych Review, and it is called the DRC. || The lexical part of the DRC is just the **Interactive Activation model**. The model has **no implementation of semantics** (meaning). This is because both its **orthographic lexicon** (store of written word forms) and its **phonological lexicon** (store of spoken word forms) are independent of semantics. || The **Grapheme-Phoneme Rule System** has processing assumptions that are completely different from the interactive part. This is because it works by applying rules to letters, which is a different kind of processing. So the DRC uses two different kinds of computation.
4|The **Grapheme-Phoneme Rule System** works letter by letter, and word frequency does not change how it works. This is because its rules are activated **serially** (one after another), moving through the letters in order. || The rule system gets **no feedback** from the lexical route. This is because it does not interact with the lexical route, except at one place. || That place is the **phoneme response buffer** (the shared output stage). Both routes connect to the same output units there. Since both routes use the same output units, they can **compete** to activate different patterns.
5|**GPC rules** map written units onto **phonemes** (sound categories). This is because each rule says which sound a written unit gets. Some rules cover single letters, and others cover several letters or a particular letter context. || **Context-sensitive rules** only apply when specified surrounding letters or positions are present. This is because the rule depends on what is around the letter. || The model also needs **meta-rules** (rules about which rules to use) for each level of analysis. This is because more than one rule can apply to the same letters. For example, a **larger-unit rule** takes priority over smaller-unit rules.
6|The GPC rules are often based on the most frequent pronunciation, but they work as fixed rules. This is because the rules follow statistics about spelling and sound, yet each rule is either applied or not, with no in-between. || The rule for **-OUGH** has weaker statistical support than the rule for **-EIGH**. This is because the pronunciations of OUGH are more divided across alternatives. || Even so, both are rules, and both behave the same way during processing. This is because the strength of support does not change how a rule works. || A **probabilistic account** is different. It lets each alternative pronunciation have its own strength of support. A spread of support across many alternatives means more uncertainty.
7|The DRC's simple rules miss many cases, and this was seen as a feature, not a bug. This is because the rules are meant to explain errors people make, not only correct reading. || The DRC's rules went through several revisions to improve nonword reading. Finer rules could pronounce more exceptions correctly. This is because they would then remove behavior the model is meant to explain. || The argument is that people do not use such finely tuned rules for the GPC route. This is because if they did, two patterns would not appear. One pattern is **regularization errors** (pronouncing an exception word as if it followed the usual rule) in **surface dyslexia**. The other is a slowdown for low-frequency exception words. || A **cognitively useful model** should reproduce the relevant failures, not only maximize accuracy. The crucial test is whether the same processing assumptions produce the observed pattern of success, interference and regularization.
8|**Informational encapsulation** means a process does not freely use every source of relevant knowledge. This is because the process is kept apart from some information, such as world knowledge. || **Parsing strategies** like **late closure** and **minimal attachment** were motivated by syntactic complexity alone. This is because they were meant to work from structure only, without world knowledge. || The dual-route models are designed to be similarly modular. The GPC route keeps running and gives correct pronunciations for many words. It gives incorrect responses to some exceptions. We can see this when people make **regularization errors** or when they are forced to name **pseudowords** (pronounceable made-up words). || In dual-route accounts, GPC conversion works without whole-word meaning or lexical feedback. This is because the GPC route is kept apart from them. **Pseudoword naming** exposes this best, because no familiar word entry is available.
9|The DRC can simulate many phenomena in visual word recognition. **Coltheart and colleagues (2001)** tested it on effects such as **frequency**, **lexicality** (whether an item is a word or a nonword), **regularity** and their **interactions**. || The tested effects also include **lexical decision** (deciding whether a written item is a word) and patterns found in acquired dyslexia. || Accounting for several effects with one architecture is stronger than fitting a single result. This is because one design explains many results at once. || Evaluation also means checking which assumptions stay fixed and which change between tasks. Reading aloud, lexical decision and patient performance are related but different tasks. So any adjustment made for one task has to be named as such.
10|The DRC models **lexical decision** by adding decision criteria to its recognition activity. This is because activity alone does not say when to answer yes or no. || The model first reduces **letter-word inhibition**, which makes the task harder. This is because without that change, the results make no sense. || A "yes" answer happens in two ways. First, any single word with an activity above a threshold of 0.69 gives yes. Second, the total activity over the orthographic input lexicon can exceed a threshold S. S is 10 for cycles 0 to 20, and it drops to 1.98 if lexicon activity is above 0.2 at cycle 20. || A "no" answer happens if neither criterion is met by the deadline D. The deadline is 42 cycles, unless lexicon activity is above 0.112 after 20 cycles. In that case the deadline is set to 48. This is described as nearly twice as fast as the model normally reads aloud. || The implementation also changes inhibition and uses time-dependent thresholds. This is because these choices help the model fit the data. They add assumptions beyond the basic two-route diagram.
11|The DRC's routes are **modular**, which means each route has its own processing assumptions. This is because the two routes are **informationally encapsulated** from each other. || Informational encapsulation means no lexical feedback reaches the GPC rules. || The routes are also **domain-specific**. This means the GPC rules take only letters as input. They pass phoneme activations to the phoneme system. || Modularity has three related senses. They are restrictions on inputs, rules of computation, and restrictions on communication. || Having two pathways alone does not establish these stronger restrictions. This is because two arrows in a model say nothing about how the parts operate or communicate.
12|**Seidenberg and McClelland (1989)** treated reading skill as emerging from learned mappings among spelling, pronunciation and meaning. This is because they trained a network to learn these mappings. || Their model used **PDP models** (parallel distributed processing networks). It trained a simple associative network. Orthography is spelling, phonology is sound structure, and semantics is meaning. || The model studied mappings from orthography to phonology in a single route. Reading skill emerges from learning. It is not a hand-built list of rules and lexical entries. || A connection between domains represents a learned mapping. It is not an extra stored dictionary entry for each item.
13|In the PDP account, **rules** are generalizations learned from many words. They are not a separate process. This is because the same learning mechanism produces them. || Whole-word knowledge is **distributed**. Some is stored in orthography-to-phonology mappings, and some is stored in other parts of the system. || The same learning mechanism can capture patterns shared by many words and information about one familiar word. Distributed means knowledge is spread across the network. The model still has memory for individual words. || The research strategy differs from dual-route models. It asks whether **domain-general learning** (learning that is not specific to reading) can explain reading. This contrasts with reverse-engineering the system from adult and neuropsychological data.
14|The network produces the **frequency by regularity interaction** without assuming separate processes for frequency and regularity. This is because regular words fit statistical patterns from many other words. || Exception words do not fit those patterns. This is because they conflict with the many words that follow the pattern. || Frequency also affects how well words are learned. Highly frequent words are less affected by inconsistency with other words. || A low-frequency exception has weak item learning and poor support from the main pattern. Since both factors work against it, the disadvantage is larger than either factor alone. || Producing this interaction does not by itself settle whether the model contains symbolic rules. Both architectures must explain it.
15|**Regularity** is redefined as **consistency**. Consistency depends on how many similar spellings support a pronunciation. Similar spellings that support the same pronunciation are **friends**, and similar spellings that support a different one are **enemies**. || A **regular consistent** word has many friends and no enemies, such as LATE and DUST. This is because all the similar spellings agree. || A **regular inconsistent** word has many friends and at least one enemy, such as GAVE and MINT. || An **ambiguous** word has about equal numbers of friends and enemies, such as DOWN and KNOWN, or POUR and SOUR. || An **exception** has few if any friends and many enemies, such as HAVE and PINT. PINT has relatively little support compared with its competing neighborhood. || Friends and enemies describe pronunciation relations among spellings, not meanings. A simple regular or irregular label can hide differences in degree.
16|In principle, the network pronounces nonwords using the same **learned weights** it uses for words. This is because a new spelling activates patterns that overlap with familiar spellings. || The network generalizes to the new spelling. No stored entry for that nonword is needed. || The same statistical patterns that give rise to regularity effects also support nonword reading. This is proposed as a way to decode without a separate GPC rule module. || Novel pronunciations come from applying learned statistical structure. Whether one learned mapping handles both words and new items still has to be checked against the model's performance.
17|Poor nonword accuracy can challenge the theory or the implementation. This is because a failed simulation does not say what caused its failure. || In the early model, nonword accuracy was not very good. This is a classic case of ambiguity in model evaluation. || One possibility is that the theory is wrong, so one associative mechanism cannot read nonwords and exceptions. The other possibility is that the model was built badly. Its representations might miss the similarities needed for generalization. || Improving the implementation while keeping the theory helps tell these apart. A **representational improvement** is a different move from adding a separate nonword route.
18|Nonword reading depends on how sounds are represented in the model. Many studies show that **decoding** (nonword reading) is related to **phonological processing**. || The early Seidenberg and McClelland model used **Wickelfeatures** (the early model's way of coding speech sounds). These were an unusual phonological representation. || **Plaut and colleagues (1996)** improved the phonological representations. Their model then performed better on nonwords. || Changing the representation can improve decoding without adding a symbolic rule system. Better performance after that change is evidence about implementation. It does not prove that every associative model will succeed.
19|**Generalization** depends on **similarity**. This is because a network applies what it learned to new items that look similar to old ones. The principle is inherited from behaviorism. || Poor phonological representations, like Wickelfeatures, do not capture which words are similar in sound. This is because they fail to capture the **similarity space** of words in the language. || **Word-specific information** is more robust to poor representations. This is because it does not depend entirely on generalization. || A nonword has no item-specific experience. So it depends more heavily on generalization, and poor representations hurt it more. || The same weakness can affect words and nonwords unequally, without any dedicated nonword mechanism. A weaker shared representation is different from damage to a separate route, and the two locate the explanation differently.
20|**Harm and Seidenberg (1999)** added noise to the phonological representations. Increasing noise makes the sound representations less reliable. The general learning framework stays the same. || This produced poorer nonword decoding and **relative sparing of word recognition**, which means word recognition was spared compared with nonword decoding. || The model replicated several key findings from the developmental dyslexia literature. These included a link between the degree of phonological deficit and **phonological dyslexia** (a reading pattern tied to weak sound processing). || The noise is the manipulated property. Word and nonword performance are outcomes. So the simulation tests a direction, from representation to reading, rather than only seeing a correlation. || A selective-looking reading deficit can come from degraded shared representations. This is because nonwords are especially dependent on reliable generalization.
21|Damaging semantics produced a **specific impairment on exception words**. A **lesion** here is a controlled change to the computational model, not an injury seen in a patient. || **Plaut and colleagues (1996)** explored the other end of the dissociation, which is **surface dyslexia** (a reading pattern with trouble on exception words). They built a simplified semantic system into the **triangle model**, which links orthography, phonology and semantics. || This is because an exception has weak support from typical spelling-sound regularities. Meaning-related support can help produce its learned pronunciation. || Reducing that support creates a surface-dyslexia-like pattern. This happens without defining a separate exception-word list as the sole explanation. || Nonword reading can be specifically impaired even though the model has no dedicated nonword mechanism. This is the other side of the same dissociation.
22|The triangle model is not simply the dual-route model renamed. This is because **semantic representations** are directly involved in it, while the dual-route lexicon is defined independently of meaning. || A later model, **Harm and Seidenberg (2004)**, implemented **featural semantics** (meaning represented as features). It simulated the mapping of spelling to meaning. || The **OP mapping** (orthography-to-phonology) and the **SP mapping** (semantics-to-phonology) have the same processing assumptions and learning algorithm. Both mappings are **probabilistic** too. || These pathways interact during learning. This is because the OP pathway develops differently when the SP pathway is present. || This interactivity and gradedness lets the models capture that **double dissociations** (two impairments that each occur without the other) are never entirely clean. Phonological dyslexics are not perfect on exception words. Surface dyslexics are not perfect on nonwords. || Multiple pathways alone do not make a model modular. Learning rules, information types and interactions set the accounts apart. The OP and SP mappings use the same general probabilistic assumptions, unlike the DRC's distinct GPC and lexical procedures.
23|**Woollams and colleagues (2007)** tested the prediction of a strong association between semantic ability and exception-word reading. The patients had **semantic dementia** (a progressive condition). || Patients with lower semantic scores had poorer low-frequency exception reading. The model and patient results match overall, although individual patients vary. || This links a reading weakness to a broader semantic measure. It is not only a reading-task dissociation. || On the semantic score scale, values fall from left to right. So the downward trend means that poorer semantics goes with poorer exception reading. It does not mean that better semantics harms reading.
24|Some patients fall outside the general trend. **Woollams and colleagues** would treat them as **statistical outliers** (scores that sit far from the general pattern) when evaluating model fit. || Patients who are left of the vertical boundary and above the horizontal boundary count as outliers. This means the model misses a few data points. || These points are treated the way outliers are usually treated. This is because an outlier does not automatically refute the model. An account of graded association does not predict zero variability. || The real question is whether these cases form a stable separate pattern or arise within a noisy overall association. Judge them by their location relative to the broader group and by repeated measurements.
25|Following patients over time tests whether semantic and reading ability decline together. This is because semantic dementia is progressive, so repeated testing can reveal a trajectory rather than one unusual score. || In most cases, when a patient's semantic ability declined, the patient's reading ability also declined. || **Regression to the mean** (unusual scores moving back toward typical values on retesting) is the pattern that idiosyncratic outliers would show. || Observations from the same person are connected. So movement toward lower scores on both measures is **coupled decline**. || A one-time exceptional score is less convincing if the patient's later scores follow the general relation.
26|**Coltheart and colleagues (2009)** simulated surface dyslexia with the DRC. They generated 40,000 model patients. || The model patients varied on two parameters. These were the integrity of the lexical route and the integrity of the GPC rules. || For each patient in the Woollams and colleagues data, the researchers selected the model run that best fit that patient's data. This produced an excellent fit. || The selection step is part of how the fit was achieved. So the fit is different from predicting each patient's pattern in advance. || The close agreement of the lines describes **goodness of fit**. The large search over simulated patients describes **flexibility**.
27|A **better fit** can be less informative when it comes from much greater flexibility. This is because choosing the best match from many combinations can reproduce data without strongly constraining the explanation. || The issue is not that close agreement is bad in itself. The issue is whether the account predicts the relation from principled assumptions, or fits each case after seeing the outcome. || Goodness of fit is only one part of model evaluation. A model should explain why the pattern occurs, not only contain a setting that resembles it. || Comparing theories requires attention to both fit and the freedom that produced it. This does not justify rejecting a model just because its fit is strong.
28|Both models must explain rapid **decoding** of pseudowords (pronounceable made-up words), which is fast and automatic. || Words that diverge from typical spelling-to-sound mappings are harder to read. This is the **regularity** effect, and it interacts with word frequency. || Pseudoword reading and **exception word** reading can be dissociated in neuropsychological patients. This is why both models need to explain the dissociation. || The models compete over explanation, not over whether these reading patterns exist. Agreement on phenomena does not mean agreement on mechanisms. || One account emphasizes specialized routes. The other emphasizes learning across interacting representational systems. A useful comparison holds the phenomenon constant and asks what each model changes or computes to produce it.
29|The two models explain the same patterns with different mechanisms. For pseudoword reading and regularity effects, the triangle model relies on statistical regularities learned over many words. || The DRC relies on a dedicated **grapheme-phoneme conversion** rule system for those effects. This is because it assumes separate rules for conversion. || For frequency effects, the triangle model says more exposure to a word leads to better learning for that word. || The DRC uses distinct lexicons for input and output, separate from the GPC rules. Word-specific exposure therefore affects lexical processing in the DRC. || For phonological dyslexia, the DRC points to damage in the GPC route. The triangle model points to deficit or damage in phonological processing generally. || For surface dyslexia, the DRC points to damage in the lexical route. The triangle model points to deficit or damage in semantic processes generally. || Each row gives competing explanations for the same finding. The two columns are not two names for the same part.
30|The two approaches differ on whether explanations begin with separate reading routes or with broader language associations. **Dual-route models** mostly use **double dissociations** (two impairments that each occur without the other) to argue for separate processes. || Pseudowords and exception words can each be impaired on their own. So dual-route models treat them as independent processes. || Models informed by statistical learning reveal associations instead. Phonology matters for learning and using **OP generalizations** (orthography-to-phonology). Semantics matters for words where OP generalization is ambiguous. || Both dissociations and associations matter. A theory should explain selective weaknesses and the related abilities that vary with them. || The dyslexia rows of the comparison contrast GPC damage with phonological damage, and lexical-route damage with semantic damage. These are the key theoretical alternatives, not just different labels.
