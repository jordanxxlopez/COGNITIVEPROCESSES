2|**“The horse raced past the barn fell”** is a grammatical English sentence, even though it usually feels broken on first reading. The difficulty comes from how the beginning of the sentence leads the reader to build the wrong structure. >> Most readers first take **raced** as the main verb, so “the horse raced past the barn” seems to describe a complete event in which the horse did the racing. >> When **fell** arrives, there is no place left for it in that structure, and the sentence seems to fall apart. || The sentence describes **two events**. Someone raced the horse past the barn, and the horse then fell. >> The intended meaning is **“The horse that was raced past the barn fell.”** >> In that reading, “raced past the barn” describes which horse is being talked about, and **fell** is the main verb that says what happened to it. >> The horse is the one that fell. It is not a second participant introduced by the last word. || The sentence can be **disambiguated** by putting back the missing words, as in “the horse that was raced past the barn fell.” >> Adding “that was” makes the modifying relationship explicit, so raced can no longer be taken as the main verb. >> Sentence comprehension depends on assigning relationships among words, not only on recognizing their meanings. Here, one action belongs inside the description of the horse and the other is the main event. || This sentence could fairly be called **the sentence that launched a thousand experiments**. It has been used again and again to study how readers build sentence structure as words arrive.
3|**“The horse raced past the barn fell”** is almost as famous as **“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously.”** Both are well-known example sentences in the study of language. >> “Colorless green ideas sleep furiously” is grammatical but meaningless. >> “The horse raced past the barn fell” is grammatical and meaningful, but very hard to process. || It is the classic example of the **“garden path” effect**. A **garden path sentence** tricks the reader into one interpretation early on and then reveals, as more words arrive, that this interpretation was wrong. >> The phrase comes from the expression “to lead someone down the garden path,” meaning to mislead them. >> In this sentence, raced looks like the main verb, and fell then conflicts with the structure already built. >> Understanding requires going back and reinterpreting “raced past the barn” as meaning “that was raced past the barn.” || A dinosaur comic strip pokes fun at this sentence. One dinosaur explains garden path sentences and offers the horse example. A second dinosaur insists nobody would ever say such a thing, and the first replies that farmers could say it if horses were constantly falling over, adding that farmers call them nature’s dominoes. >> The joke rests on the sentence being grammatical even though it sounds wrong. || **Grammaticality and processing difficulty are different things**. >> A sentence can have a perfectly acceptable structure and still be very hard to understand as it unfolds. >> The garden path concerns which analysis is chosen first and how hard it is to replace, not whether a correct analysis exists.
4|**Syntactic explanations** of the garden path effect start with the idea of a **parser**. A parser is a mental process whose job is to build a **syntactic tree** for the sentences we hear or read. A syntactic tree shows how words group into phrases and how those phrases relate to one another. >> The tree for the correct reading of the horse sentence has **S**, for sentence, at the top, splitting into a large **NP**, or noun phrase, and a **VP**, or verb phrase. >> The large NP contains **D** for determiner, “the,” and **N** for noun, “horse,” plus a reduced clause, marked **S′**, holding “that was raced past the barn.” The words “that” and “was” are silent in the actual sentence, and an empty position marked **e** stands where the missing subject would be. >> Inside that clause, raced is a verb with a **prepositional phrase**, or **PP**, “past the barn,” and the main **VP** at the far right contains only **fell**. || The **failed parse** is drawn below the words. >> In that tree, “the horse” forms an ordinary NP, and “raced past the barn” forms the VP, with raced as the main verb. >> **Fell** is left dangling with nowhere to attach, and the failed tree ends with the exclamation **“¡ACK!”** || The trees and the dinosaur comic both come from a Language Log piece on this sentence. || Any parser has to cope with the fact that it runs inside a cognitive system with **limited memory** and **time constraints**. >> Words arrive quickly, one after another, and the parser cannot wait until the end of the sentence to start building structure. >> It also cannot keep track of every possible structure at once. A processing account has to explain how the parser handles these limits when a sentence is ambiguous.
5|The **grammatical version** of the horse sentence is **much more complex syntactically** than the failed parse. Specifically, it has **many more nodes**. A **node** is a point in a syntactic tree that represents a grammatical grouping, such as an NP, a VP or an S. >> The correct tree needs extra nodes for the embedded clause, S′ and S, plus nodes for the silent “that” and “was.” >> The failed parse simply attaches “raced past the barn” as the main VP, which takes far fewer nodes. || One strategy that saves space is to **initially prefer smaller trees**. This strategy is called **“minimal attachment.”** >> Under minimal attachment, the parser attaches each new word into the existing structure using the fewest possible new nodes. >> Building fewer nodes means less structure to construct and hold in memory at that moment. || In the horse sentence, minimal attachment favors treating **raced as the main verb**, because that analysis needs the fewest nodes. >> The alternative, where raced past the barn modifies horse, needs an embedded relative clause. >> Choosing the smaller structure reduces the immediate processing load, but it creates a garden path. When fell arrives, it requires the larger structure that was not chosen, and the reader has to rebuild the tree.
6|Another parsing strategy in this framework is **“late closure.”** Late closure is a bias to **keep the current clause “open” as long as possible**, so that new words get attached to the phrase currently being built. >> Keeping a clause open means the parser keeps adding incoming material to it rather than closing it off and starting a new structure. || In **“The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite,”** the phrase **“with the dynamite”** wants to associate with the big **verb phrase** starting with **“blew open.”** Frazier (1987) gives two trees for this sentence. >> In the **preferred** tree, the PP “with the dynamite” attaches to the **VP**. This is **verb-phrase attachment**, and it means the dynamite is the **instrument** the burglar used to blow open the safe. >> In the **unpreferred** tree, the PP attaches to the **NP** “the safe.” This is **noun-phrase attachment**, and it means the phrase describes which safe was blown open, namely the safe that had the dynamite. || Both attachments are grammatical, but they express different relationships. >> The preferred VP attachment is also the minimal attachment here, because it needs fewer nodes than building an extra NP around “the safe with the dynamite.” >> The parser’s preference makes the instrument reading easier to reach first, and for this sentence the preferred reading is also the sensible one.
7|Late closure can be seen at work in a sentence that **requires the “unpreferred” parse to be sensible**, such as **“The burglar blew open the safe with the jewels.”** >> Jewels are not something a person would use to blow open a safe. >> The sensible meaning is that the burglar blew open **the safe that contained the jewels**. That meaning requires attaching “with the jewels” to the noun phrase “the safe,” which is the unpreferred NP attachment. || In **careful tests of reading time and eye movements**, people are **slower to read sentences where the sensible reading requires the unpreferred parse**. >> Reading time measures how long readers spend on each part of a sentence. >> Eye movement recordings show where readers look and when they jump back to reread earlier words. >> The extra time appears at the point where the parse has to change. || Readers **momentarily entertain the idea that the jewels were used to blow open the safe**, and then have to **retrace their steps**. >> The parser first attaches the phrase to the verb phrase, following its usual preference. >> The meaning of jewels conflicts with the instrument reading, so the parser revises the attachment to the noun phrase. >> The final meaning is easy to understand, but the route to it carries a processing cost.
8|This syntactic explanation rests on the idea of a **parser that deals exclusively with the abstract syntactic structure** of the sentence. This parallels the **“lumber mill”** model of thresholded processing in speech perception. >> In the lumber mill picture, each stage works on its own kind of input and hands a finished product to the next stage. >> Here, the parser builds a syntactic structure using only grammatical categories, without consulting meaning or context. Meaning is worked out afterward from the finished structure. || The parser has **its own operating characteristics**. >> It wants to make **the simplest tree possible**, which is **minimal attachment**. >> It accomplishes this partly by **leaving clauses open as long as possible**, which is **late closure**. >> These preferences limit how much structure must be built and held in memory at once. || This is one way of dealing with ambiguity **very efficiently**. The strategy is to **ignore ambiguity until it becomes a problem**. >> The parser commits to one likely structure instead of building every possible structure. >> Most of the time the first choice works, and no extra effort is needed. >> When a later word does not fit, the structure has to be revised, and that revision is the source of garden path difficulty.
9|There are **problems with a purely syntactic explanation**. One is that **syntactically “difficult” sentences are not always difficult to parse**. >> A table of examples gives sentences containing **reduced relative clauses**, with the verb that introduces each reduced relative written in capital letters. >> A **reduced relative clause** is a relative clause with the words “that was” or “that were” left out, as in “the horse raced past the barn fell.” || The examples come from published research articles. >> “Thus, a noun phrase **FOLLOWED** by a **MORPHOLOGICALLY AMBIGUOUS VERB** … will be temporarily ambiguous,” from Trueswell, Tanenhaus and Garnsey (1994). >> “Referential information **PROVIDED** by the discourse is of no help,” from Britt, Perfetti, Garrod and Rayner (1992). >> “Recent research **REPORTED** by **FERREIRA AND CLIFTON** (1986) has demonstrated that syntactic processing is quite independent and that the initial syntactic analysis **ASSIGNED** to a sentence is little influenced by the semantic information **ALREADY ANALYZED**,” from Frazier and Rayner (1987). >> “The pattern of results **OBTAINED** with global reading times is reflected by differences **LOCATED** on the critical disambiguating phrase,” from Altmann and Steedman (1988). || In every case, the capitalized verb begins a reduced relative with exactly the structure of the horse sentence. **But these are not impossibly difficult sentences**. Readers understand them without any feeling of being led down a garden path. >> In “recent research reported by Ferreira and Clifton,” reported describes research that someone reported. Research is not the one doing the reporting. || The alternative argument, from **MacDonald, Perlmutter and Seidenberg (1994)**, is that **semantic factors and statistical regularities interact with parsing from very early on**. >> Semantic factors concern meaning. >> Statistical regularities are patterns of how often words appear in particular structures. >> Under this view, the structure alone does not decide difficulty. Particular words and their familiar uses can support a reduced relative reading from the start.
10|The table of reduced relatives is a **wonderfully passive-aggressive** table. **Many of its examples are drawn from papers that use the difficulty of parsing reduced relatives as evidence for an autonomous parser**. An **autonomous parser** is one that builds syntactic structure without help from meaning or context. >> The authors of those papers argued that reduced relatives are hard to process. >> Yet they wrote reduced relatives in their own articles, and readers understood them easily. || More examples from the table make the same point. >> In “a noun phrase **FOLLOWED** by a morphologically ambiguous verb,” followed modifies noun phrase. >> In “the initial syntactic analysis **ASSIGNED** to a sentence,” assigned modifies analysis. >> In “differences **LOCATED** on the critical disambiguating phrase,” located modifies differences. >> “A person **PRESENTED** with the evidence **EXAMINED** by the lawyer may interpret the evidence as a theme, not an agent, because examined requires an animate agent,” from Ferreira and Clifton (1986). Here presented modifies person, and examined modifies evidence. || A note on the table states that the examples cited were **not the only reduced relatives** in those articles. >> The rate of reduced relative use was about **one per journal page** in this sample of articles. >> Reduced relatives are therefore common in ordinary scientific writing. || Understanding each example requires attaching each modifier to the correct noun, and readers do this easily in context. >> A formally complex structure is not necessarily difficult to understand in every context.
11|The argument is that, when we read **“Recent research reported by…,”** several factors besides syntax come into play. >> **Semantics**. Research is an **inanimate** object, meaning it is not a living thing. It is unlikely to be the **subject** of a verb like **report**, because research cannot report anything. Taking research as something that was reported fits the meanings of the words. >> **Lexical statistics**. **“Reported” occurs frequently in reduced relatives**. Lexical statistics are learned patterns of how often particular words appear in particular structures. >> **Lexical statistics and “world knowledge.”** “Reported” occurs very frequently in reduced relatives **in scientific papers, especially when following the word “research.”** World knowledge here means knowledge of how people actually write in this setting. || These and **many other factors interact with the syntax of the sentence very early on**. >> They do not wait until a syntactic structure has been built. >> They help decide which structure is chosen in the first place. || A pair of sentences makes the role of meaning clear. >> **“The burglar arrested in the parking lot was charged today.”** Burglars are likely to be arrested, so it is easy to take arrested as part of a reduced relative describing the burglar. >> **“The policeman arrested in the parking lot was charged today.”** Policemen are likely to be the ones doing the arresting, so arrested is first taken as the main verb, and “was charged” causes a garden path. >> The grammar of the two sentences is identical. The difference in difficulty comes from what we know about burglars and policemen.
12|**“World knowledge” can be very local**. It can come from the immediate visual situation, not just from general knowledge. **Spivey and colleagues (1995)** tested this in a visual world task. >> Participants saw real objects, namely **a pencil, a box, an empty towel, and an apple sitting on a towel**. >> They heard the instruction **“Put the apple on the towel in the box”** and carried it out while their eye movements were recorded. || With **minimal attachment**, “the towel” would temporarily seem like the end of the clause **“put the apple on the towel.”** >> On that reading, “on the towel” names the **destination**, the place the apple should go. >> In the intended reading, “on the towel” describes **which apple** to move, the one already on a towel, and **“in the box”** is the destination. || People **do seem to look at the empty towel while planning their movements**, as though they are considering it as a target, before planning a movement to the box. >> The diagram of a typical sequence of eye movements, in the **one-referent context** with one apple, marks each look with a letter. **A** is a look to the apple, **B** is a look to the empty towel, the incorrect destination, and **C** is a look to the box. >> For the **ambiguous** instruction, the timeline puts A near 1,000 milliseconds, B shortly after, and C near 2,000 milliseconds. >> For the **unambiguous** instruction, **“Put the apple that’s on the towel in the box,”** the looks go from the apple, marked **A′**, straight to the box, marked **B′**, with no look to the empty towel. || The listener recognizes every word correctly. The error comes from treating the location phrase as the destination, which briefly changes the planned action.
13|A **two-referent context** changes the display so that it contains **two apples**. One apple sits on a towel, and the other sits elsewhere in the display. The empty towel and the box are still present. >> Now “the apple” alone does not say which apple to move. >> The phrase **“on the towel”** becomes useful, because it picks out one apple from the two. || The diagram gives the typical sequence of eye movements in the two-referent context. >> Participants look first at the apple on the towel, marked **A**, and then at the box, marked **C**. >> Looks to the empty towel, marked **B**, are much less common. >> The note under the figure states that **the sequence and the timing of eye movements**, measured relative to the nouns in the speech stream, **did not differ** for the ambiguous and the unambiguous instructions. || In this setting, the ambiguous instruction is processed just like the explicit instruction with “that’s on the towel.” >> The visual context makes the modifier reading the natural one from the start. >> The usefulness of the phrase depends on the objects available, so context can shape the grammatical interpretation even though the sentence itself has not changed.
14|With **two apples in the display**, it suddenly becomes **felicitous** to provide more information about **which apple** the speaker means. **Felicitous** means appropriate and natural in the situation. >> With only one apple, there is no need to say which apple, so “on the towel” seems like extra information and is easily taken as a destination. >> With two apples, “on the towel” has a clear job, which is to identify the right apple. || So **people do not look at the empty towel as much**. The bar graph plots the **proportion of trials with eye movements to the incorrect destination**, the empty towel, for ambiguous and unambiguous instructions in each context. >> In the **one-referent context**, ambiguous instructions led to looks at the incorrect destination on about **55%** of trials. Unambiguous instructions led to almost none. >> In the **two-referent context**, ambiguous instructions led to such looks on only about **17%** of trials, close to the roughly **12%** for unambiguous instructions. || The large ambiguity cost appears only when the display contains one apple. >> This is an effect of **referential context**, meaning the set of objects that a phrase could refer to. >> Referential context influences which grammatical relationship listeners assign while the sentence is still being heard.
15|The Spivey results are **a big problem for purely syntactic accounts**. **Different parses of the same sentence are favored under different semantic and pragmatic conditions, and this effect happens very early**. >> **Semantic** conditions concern meaning. >> **Pragmatic** conditions concern how language is used in a particular situation, such as whether a speaker needs to specify which object they mean. || In the bar graph of looks to the incorrect destination, the same ambiguous sentence produces looks to the empty towel on about 55% of trials with one apple, but only about 17% with two apples. >> The words are identical in both conditions. >> Only the visual situation changes, and it changes the parse the listener first adopts. || The difference shows up in **eye movements during the sentence**, before the action is carried out. >> Context therefore affects the initial parse, not just a later correction of a finished structure. >> A purely syntactic parser that always picks the same first structure regardless of context cannot explain why the garden path nearly disappears with two apples.
16|**Mouse movements** have more recently been used to study these questions. **Farmer and colleagues (2007)** tracked the path of the computer mouse while participants carried out spoken instructions in a version of the apple and towel display. >> It is not just eye movements but **the actual execution of arm movements** that can be influenced by **syntactic ambiguity** and its **interaction with the visually defined context**. >> Mouse tracking records the full path of the hand as the instruction is processed, so a competing interpretation can pull the movement toward another location before the hand reaches the target. || In the **one-referent context**, the plotted trajectories move from the apple at the top left toward the box at the bottom right. >> The **ambiguous trajectory** curves slightly toward the empty towel at the top right, compared with the **unambiguous trajectory**. >> Points along each path mark the 30th, 40th, 50th and 60th time steps, and the two paths separate most in the middle of the movement. || In the **two-referent context**, the ambiguous and unambiguous trajectories lie almost on top of each other. >> The second apple makes the location phrase useful for identification, so it is no longer taken as a destination. || A separate graph plots the **Euclidean distance between the ambiguous and unambiguous trajectories** over normalized time. Euclidean distance is the straight-line distance between the two paths at each moment. >> In the one-referent context, the distance rises to a peak of about 200 pixels around 40% of the way through the movement. >> In the two-referent context, it peaks lower, at about 140 pixels, and earlier, around 30%, and then drops off. >> Comprehension and action planning interact while both are underway. A correct endpoint does not mean the competing interpretation had no effect on the movement.

18|**The** is a **determiner**, a word that introduces a **noun phrase**. When the parser encounters “the,” it expects a noun to follow, possibly with an adjective or other modifier in between. >> “The” does not name anything by itself. >> It limits which kinds of words can come next, which helps the parser anticipate the shape of the phrase. || This expectation is about a **grammatical category**, not about a particular word. >> Many different nouns could follow, such as pizza, dog or book. >> An adjective could also come first, as in “the hot pizza.” The parser predicts the form of the phrase while its specific meaning is still open.
19|**Pizza** is a **noun**, and it completes the noun phrase begun by “the.” It can serve as the **head of the noun phrase**, the central word around which the rest of the phrase is organized. >> “The” combines with “pizza” to form “the pizza,” which can now act as the subject of the sentence. >> At this point the parser has a complete noun phrase and expects a verb phrase to follow. || Recognizing the word makes two kinds of information available. >> Its **category**, noun, tells the parser how it can combine with other words. >> Its **meaning** identifies a kind of food, which is what the phrase refers to. Word recognition therefore supplies more than a sound or a spelling to sentence processing.
20|**Was** is a past-tense form of the verb **be**. It can act as an **auxiliary**, which is a helping verb that combines with another verb to express grammatical information such as tense or voice. >> In “the pizza was eaten,” was helps form a **passive** construction with the participle eaten. >> In “the pizza was in the oven,” was links the subject to a location phrase. || When “was” arrives, the sentence could continue in several ways. >> A location phrase, an adjective or a participle could all follow. >> The parser has to keep the developing structure available while more words arrive. The grammatical role of was depends partly on what comes next.
21|**In** is a **preposition**, a word that expresses a relationship such as location or containment. It begins a **prepositional phrase**. >> “In” does not name the location by itself. >> It sets up the expectation of a noun phrase that will supply the location, as in “in the oven” or “in the box.” || After “the pizza was,” the arrival of “in” narrows the possible structures. >> The sentence is now heading toward a statement about where the pizza was. >> The parser expects a noun phrase to complete the prepositional phrase, and understanding the phrase requires combining the preposition with that noun phrase.
22|**The** begins a new **noun phrase** inside the prepositional phrase “in the …” It is the start of the phrase that will name the location. >> “The” itself does not supply the location. >> A noun is still needed, possibly with modifiers before it, as in “in the hot oven.” || The parser now strongly expects a **noun**. This expectation concerns a **word category**, not just a sensible topic. >> Whatever comes next must work grammatically as part of a noun phrase. >> Knowing that the sentence is about pizza makes words like oven or box likely, but the grammatical requirement holds regardless of meaning.
23|**Eaten** is the **past participle** of the verb **eat**. It is a verb form, not a noun, so it cannot complete the noun phrase that “in the” has set up. >> The phrase **“in the eaten”** places a verb form exactly where a noun is required. >> This is a **word-category violation**, meaning a word of the wrong grammatical category appears at a position that demands a different category. || This kind of error differs from a strange but grammatical meaning. >> “The pizza was in the ocean” is odd in meaning but grammatically fine, because ocean is a noun. >> “The pizza was in the eaten” cannot be fixed by imagining an unusual situation. The problem lies in the grammatical type of the arriving word, not in the event it describes.
24|The **early left anterior negativity**, or **ELAN**, is an **ERP** response elicited by **word class violations**. An **ERP**, or **event-related potential**, is the pattern of electrical brain activity recorded from the scalp and averaged across many trials, time-locked to an event such as the onset of a word. >> A **word class** is a grammatical category, such as noun, verb or preposition. || Some classic ELAN stimuli are word-category violations. >> **“The pizza was in the eaten,”** where a participle appears where a noun is expected. >> **“The scientist criticized Max’s of proof the theorem,”** where the preposition **of** appears where a noun is expected after “Max’s.” || The ELAN has three main features. >> It is **early**, appearing about **100–200 milliseconds** after the critical word. >> It is **left and anterior**, meaning it is strongest over the left front of the scalp. This location has raised the question of whether it is language-specific. >> It occurs **specifically for word category violations**, such as a noun when a verb is expected, or a preposition in place of a noun. || The grand average ERPs from the syntactic-category violation condition overlay waveforms for **correct** and **incorrect** sentences at many electrode sites. >> At the left frontal sites, such as F7, F3, AL and SL, circled in red, the incorrect sentences produce an early negative deflection, shaded black, soon after the critical word. >> Gray shading marks a left frontal negativity, and dotted shading marks a positivity over the back of the head. >> The ELAN appears so quickly that it was taken as evidence for a very fast syntactic analysis that checks word categories before meaning is processed.
25|**The ELAN came from visual cortex** when words were presented visually. **Visual cortex** is the region at the back of the brain that processes what we see. This finding comes from **Dikker, Rabagliati, Farmer and Pylkkänen (2010)**, in a paper titled “Early occipital sensitivity to syntactic category is based on form typicality.” The **occipital** lobe is where visual cortex is located. >> The graph plots **dipole strength** in nanoampere-meters, a measure of brain activity in a localized source, from −100 to 400 milliseconds after the word. >> For **bimorphemic nouns**, nouns that end in an identifiable noun suffix, the response to **“The beautifully princess …”** peaks higher than the response to **“The beautiful princess …”** at about **120 to 130 milliseconds**. >> Beautiful is an adjective, which correctly predicts a noun. Beautifully is an adverb, which predicts a verb or an adjective, so princess is unexpected. || This suggests that, when we are expecting a verb, for example, **that prediction can feed back down to the earliest parts of the visual system** and predict visual patterns that are consistent with **the way verbs are written**. >> A predicted grammatical category implies likely visual forms, such as typical word endings. >> The prediction does not need to identify one exact word. It favors a set of likely forms. || The very early response to the error might reflect **a mismatch between that predictive process and what finally appears on the screen**. >> When the arriving word has visual properties that conflict with the expected category, the visual system responds within about 130 milliseconds. >> An early category-sensitive response therefore does not have to come from a separate mechanism working only on abstract syntax. || This raises a question. **Does this only work for items that are morphologically marked as nouns or verbs?** Or does it work for **open-class versus closed-class** words? >> A word is **morphologically marked** when it contains a part, such as a suffix, that signals its category. >> **Open-class** words are content words such as nouns and verbs, which constantly gain new members. **Closed-class** words are function words such as prepositions and determiners, which form a small fixed set.
26|Many English words can function as either a **noun** or a **verb**, and the rest of the sentence decides which. >> **(3a)** “Chris and Ben are glad that the bird **perches seem** easy to install.” Here perches is a **noun**, meaning the bars that birds sit on, and seem is the verb. >> **(3b)** “Chris and Ben are glad that the bird **perches comfortably** in the cage.” Here perches is a **verb**, describing what the bird does. >> **(4a)** “The teacher told the principal that the student **needs were** not being met.” Here needs is a **noun**, meaning the student’s requirements. >> **(4b)** “The teacher told the principal that the student **needs to be** more focused.” Here needs is a **verb**. || Recognizing the word form alone does not settle its grammatical role. The **continuation** after the ambiguous word resolves it. || Words also differ in **phonological form typicality**, which is how much a word’s sound pattern resembles the sound patterns of typical nouns or typical verbs. The scatterplot covers **3,158 words** from a corpus analysis. >> Each word is placed according to its **mean distance** in phonological feature space from **all nouns**, on the horizontal axis, and from **all verbs**, on the vertical axis. A small distance means the word sounds similar to that category. >> **Nouns**, shown as gray squares, tend to cluster in the upper left, closer to other nouns. **Verbs**, shown as black diamonds, tend to cluster in the lower right, closer to other verbs. >> Labeled points mark the centers of **noun-like nouns**, **verb-like nouns**, **verb-like verbs** and **noun-like verbs**. A word can sound statistically like one category while actually belonging to the other.
27|In terms of form typicality, **“perches” is noun-like**, and **“needs” is verb-like**. >> These are learned statistical tendencies in how words sound. They are not rules about how the words can be used. >> Both words can still be used as nouns or verbs. || For **perches**, the noun-like form matches one sentence and clashes with the other. >> In **“the bird perches seem easy to install,”** perches is used as a noun, which matches its noun-like form. >> In **“the bird perches comfortably in the cage,”** perches must be a verb, which goes against its noun-like form. || For **needs**, the pattern is reversed. >> In **“the student needs to be more focused,”** needs is used as a verb, which matches its verb-like form. >> In **“the student needs were not being met,”** needs must be a noun, which goes against its verb-like form. || Comparing these four sentences separates what a word’s form suggests from what the grammar of the sentence requires. >> On the scatterplot of 3,158 words, perches would fall among the noun-like forms and needs among the verb-like forms. >> The question is whether readers have more trouble when the required category conflicts with the form’s typical category.
28|Reading is **slower when a word’s required category conflicts with its form typicality**. The bar graph plots **mean length-adjusted difference scores** in milliseconds, which is reading time at **disambiguation** minus reading time at the **point of ambiguity**. >> The **point of ambiguity** is the ambiguous word itself, such as perches or needs. >> **Disambiguation** is the point where the continuation makes the intended category clear, such as “seem” or “comfortably.” >> **Length-adjusted** means the times are corrected for how long the words are. >> Rising bars indicate that reading times increased from the point of ambiguity to disambiguation, which signals difficulty. || For **noun-like homonyms** such as perches, the **noun-resolved** sentences show an increase of about **50 milliseconds**. The **verb-resolved** sentences show a much larger increase, about **120 milliseconds**. >> A noun-like form is harder to resolve as a verb. || For **verb-like homonyms** such as needs, the **noun-resolved** sentences show an increase of about **110 milliseconds**. The **verb-resolved** sentences show almost none, under 10 milliseconds. >> A verb-like form is harder to resolve as a noun. || The ambiguous word stays the same in each pair, and only the continuation changes. >> The extra cost appears specifically when the continuation demands the category that does not match the form. >> Parsing therefore uses learned statistics about how word forms sound, along with the structure the sentence finally requires.
29|The **ELAN effect for nouns where a verb is expected** is limited to cases where there is either **syntactic information**, meaning morphemes that indicate noun-ness, or **statistical form-based information**, meaning “nouniness,” that is **physically available** in the word. **No effect was observed for neutral nouns like “infant” that lack strong cues**. >> A **morpheme** is the smallest meaningful part of a word, such as the suffix that marks a noun. >> “Nouniness” is how much a word’s form resembles typical nouns, even without a noun suffix. || The figure compares three kinds of nouns, with responses plotted as dipole strength from −100 to 400 milliseconds and the **M100** response marked. The M100 is a brain response peaking about 100 milliseconds after a word appears. >> **(a) Bimorphemic nouns**, in “The beautiful princess …” versus “The beautifully princess …” Princess has a noun-marking suffix, and the unexpected version produces a significantly larger peak. >> **(b) Typical nouns**, in “The tasteless soda …” versus “The tastelessly soda …” Soda has a statistically noun-like form, and the unexpected version again produces a significantly larger peak. >> **(c) Neutral nouns**, in “The cute infant …” versus “The cutely infant …” Infant has neither a suffix nor a typical noun form, and the two waveforms do not differ. >> Asterisks mark significant differences between expected and unexpected nouns, and they appear for the bimorphemic and typical nouns but not for the neutral nouns. || An abstract category mismatch alone is therefore not enough to produce this earliest visual response. >> An expected category generates predictions about what the next word will look like. >> The early response appears only when the arriving word carries physical evidence that conflicts with that prediction.
30|**Sentence processing can be summarized in four sentences**, each tied to one key idea. >> **“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously.”** **Syntax is independent of semantics to some extent**. In the limit, a sentence can be grammatically correct but meaningless. Words can fit a grammatical structure without describing a sensible event. || **“The horse raced past the barn fell.”** The syntactic structure of a sentence is often highly **ambiguous**, or more precisely **vague**, but we do not notice except where things fail. >> **Reading times** for preferred versus unpreferred structures suggest that syntactic processes **take shortcuts to save memory**, rather than activating every possible parse of a sentence. >> When the shortcut picks the wrong structure, as in the horse sentence, the reader is led down the garden path. || **“Put the apple on the towel in the box.”** **Context, pragmatics and other factors interact with syntactic processing from very early on**. >> With two apples in view, listeners take “on the towel” as a description of the apple rather than a destination. >> The visual situation shapes the parse during the sentence, not only afterward. || **“The bird perches were comfortable.”** **The very earliest syntactic effects observable in the brain are driven in part by form typicality**, not necessarily by morphosyntactic structures. >> **Morphosyntactic** structure is grammatical structure signaled by word parts and word order. >> These early effects seem to be related to **predictions about the physical forms of words**. Perches has a noun-like form, so its use as a noun here matches the expectation set up by its form.
