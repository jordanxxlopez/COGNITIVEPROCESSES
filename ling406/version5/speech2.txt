2|In **Klatt’s speech-perception model (1979)**, sound reaches the **cochlea**, part of the inner ear. The peripheral auditory system analyzes the waveform into its frequency components. **Acoustic property detectors** respond to properties of that sound, and their activity changes as the input changes. || **Phonetic feature detectors** use those outputs to estimate features such as voicing. **Segmental analysis** organizes the features into a sequence of speech segments. A **segmental feature matrix** records that sequence, and **lexical search** uses it to activate possible words, or lexical hypotheses. >> A strict staged system finishes identifying the phonemes before identifying the word. Some physical differences are lost as the variable sound becomes a set of categories, allowing different recordings to activate the same unit. || **Cascaded processing** passes partial information onward before a stage has finished. **Feedback** passes information in the reverse direction, allowing word knowledge to influence the interpretation of a phoneme. These are different ways in which stages can exchange information.
3|The **beach–peach continuum** changes **voice onset time**, or VOT. VOT is the delay between the release of the initial stop consonant and the start of vocal-fold vibration. A shorter delay supports the voiced /b/ in beach, while a longer delay supports the voiceless /p/ in peach. The rest of the word stays largely similar. || In **identification**, listeners give one word label through steps 1–5, mixed labels around step 6, and the other label for steps 7–9. The abrupt change marks the **category boundary**, where the sound is most ambiguous between the alternatives. || **Discrimination** is strongest near that change, particularly for pairs 4–6 and 5–7. Those pairs are easier to tell apart than equally separated pairs inside a category. The physical difference is held constant, while the category relationship affects how noticeable it is.
4|In a **threshold model**, activity passes to the next level only after reaching a required amount, the **threshold**. Evidence can change at the lower level without yet producing activity above it. The higher level waits for enough support to pass that requirement. || Feature activity fluctuates between evidence for **+voiced** and **−voiced**. Early changes stay below both thresholds, so the phoneme level remains inactive. A final rise crosses the +voiced threshold, and activation for **/b/** appears abruptly. || In this account, categorical perception occurs when the phoneme is identified. Word recognition receives the selected /b/ output, rather than every earlier fluctuation in the evidence. Two sounds that both cross the same threshold can therefore supply the same completed phoneme to the next stage.
5|In **cascaded processing**, activity passes forward continuously while processing is still underway. The phoneme level can respond to changing feature evidence instead of waiting until one feature has crossed a fixed threshold. A partially resolved input can already influence the next level. || More evidence for voicing gradually increases support for **/b/**. Evidence against voicing favors **/p/**. Different amounts of uncertainty remain available, so two sounds that eventually receive the same label can still produce different patterns of activation on the way to that choice. || A task can require one **categorical final answer** even when processing is graded. Clicking beach rather than peach does not establish that uncertainty disappeared at the earliest stage. The listener has to select one alternative, but several candidates can have been active before the decision.
6|A **lumber mill** transforms trees into lumber. A **furniture factory** accepts that lumber and produces unassembled furniture. An **IKEA customer** accepts the parts and assembles usable furniture. Each operation needs a particular product from the operation before it. || The factory cannot use an unprocessed tree as though it were finished lumber. The customer also cannot assemble the intended furniture directly from a tree. The required transformation has to occur before the next operation receives an input it can use. || **Thresholded processing** has that waiting relationship. A higher stage begins with the completed representation required from the lower stage. Partial work is not continuously passed onward in whatever unfinished form happens to be available.
7|In **thresholded activity**, each level accepts a specific kind of input that the previous level has already transformed completely. The next operation waits until the input has the required form. A changing but unfinished representation does not yet become its working material. || Applied to speech, word recognition waits for a completed phoneme output. It does not continuously receive every unresolved acoustic fluctuation while the phoneme is still being identified. The required product is the identified segment, just as furniture production requires lumber rather than an unprocessed tree. || This arrangement limits what later processing can know about earlier uncertainty. If several different sounds become the same completed phoneme, the word level receives that shared output. Distinctions removed during the transformation are no longer available through that strict forward pathway.
8|In a **food web**, connected populations affect each other while they are still changing. A drought reduces desert plants, which affects kangaroo rats and the snakes that depend on them. A downstream population responds to the changing food supply without waiting for the earlier population to reach a final state. || **Cascaded processing** has the same continuous dependence. Changing feature evidence can affect phonemes, and changing phoneme activity can affect words. The information does not have to be complete before influencing the next level. || Food-web relations also include reciprocal effects among hawks, snakes, lizards, rats, insects, scorpions, and desert foxes. **Feedback** in a processing network means that activity at a higher level can influence a lower level. Cascading concerns passing incomplete information forward, while feedback concerns influence in the reverse direction.
9|A **thresholded account** of word recognition waits for a completed phoneme decision. A **cascaded account** allows developing phoneme evidence to affect possible words while that decision is still unresolved. The difference concerns what information reaches word recognition before the final answer. || Two sounds can both receive a **/b/** label while differing in how ambiguous they are between /b/ and /p/. In a strict threshold system, the shared /b/ output makes them alike for the later word stage. In a cascade, the more ambiguous sound can continue to provide some support for the competing /p/ word. || Both systems can therefore predict the same final word choice while differing in the competition that comes before it. Measuring that earlier competition makes the accounts distinguishable even when final identification looks categorical.
10|**McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin, and Spivey (2003)** used a **visual-world eye-tracking task** to measure competition during spoken-word recognition. Participants heard sounds along a /b/–/p/ continuum while looking at objects. Changing VOT changed the evidence about the initial consonant. || The display contained **bear**, its sound-related competitor **pear**, and unrelated pictures of a **lamp and ship**. Participants selected the object matching what they heard. The target and competitor differed in their initial phoneme, while the fillers did not provide close word alternatives. || Eye movements recorded which pictures attracted attention before the final selection. A listener could eventually choose bear while having looked at pear during recognition. Those looks measured temporary support for a losing word, rather than requiring the losing word to become the final answer.
11|Participants used a **mouse click** to select the picture that best matched the spoken input. As VOT changed from a /b/-like onset toward a /p/-like onset, pear choices increased sharply. The identification boundary was near **17.5 milliseconds**. || The final responses formed a **step-like identification pattern**. Several different /b/-side tokens produced bear choices, while several /p/-side tokens produced pear choices. The task ended with one selected word even when nearby sounds differed physically. || A click records the **winning interpretation**. It does not record every candidate considered before that winner was selected. Categorical final choices therefore leave open whether the recognition process treated all sounds on one side of the boundary identically.
12|**Ambiguity is gradient**, meaning it varies by degree. Near the bear–pear boundary, more looking is directed toward the competing picture. This happens even when the compared trials end with the same word selected. || Among trials ending in **bear**, VOTs closer to the boundary produce more looks to **pear**. Among trials ending in pear, sounds closer to the boundary produce more looks to bear. The final label stays fixed within each comparison, but competitor activity changes. || Competitor looks rise after word onset and then decline as the target wins. More ambiguous sounds keep the competing word involved longer. The listener’s final answer is categorical, while the amount and duration of competition depend on the remaining acoustic uncertainty.
13|Each listener’s **category boundary** provides a reference for comparing competitor looks. Among bear responses, approaching that boundary increases looks to pear. Among pear responses, approaching it from the other side increases looks to bear. The effect is present within a final response category rather than only between different final choices. || The **early interval** is **300–1100 milliseconds** after onset. The **late interval** is **1100–1900 milliseconds**. Greater competitor looking near the boundary remains present in the later interval as well as during the earlier processing period. || Uncertainty therefore affects how long lexical competition lasts. A sound can support one eventual answer while still allowing a rival word to attract attention. Aligning the analysis to the listener’s own boundary connects that competition to the degree of ambiguity the listener experiences.
14|In a **cascade**, features pass developing evidence to phonemes, and phonemes pass it to words. A sound can consistently receive a /b/ label while varying in how strongly it supports the **voiced** feature. That variation can influence word candidates before one of them wins. || **/b/ and /p/** are both **bilabial stops**. Bilabial means produced with both lips, and a stop involves a closure followed by a release. Those shared properties support both sounds, leaving voicing as the uncertain property separating them. || Partial support for the losing phoneme also supports its word. Greater /p/ support during a bear response can keep pear active and increase looks toward its picture. The uncertainty is carried into lexical competition rather than erased immediately. || **TRACE** represents features, phonemes, and words at different possible times in the input. Repeated units allow a candidate to become active at a particular position as the spoken signal unfolds.
15|**Andruski and colleagues (1994)** found **semantic priming from nonwords** that partly matched real words. Semantic priming means that an earlier item makes a meaning-related target easier to process. It tests whether partial sound information can affect a word’s meaning. || Hearing **beace** facilitated a response to **war** because beace partly activates **peace**. Hearing **ging** facilitated **queen** because ging partly activates **king**. Neither altered input has to be accepted as a correctly formed word for a related lexical representation to receive some activation. || Ordinary related word primes, such as **bill before drug**, were compared with unrelated primes. Related inputs produced faster responses, while changing the prime’s initial sound could weaken that benefit. The amount of phonetic match therefore affected how much lexical and semantic support became available, rather than simply switching all access on or off.
16|As a sound approaches the **category boundary**, looks toward the other member of the **minimal pair** increase gradually. A minimal pair contains two words distinguished by one speech sound. The graded looking effect occurs even among sounds given the same final phoneme label. || A **cascaded account** retains the partial evidence that differs across those sounds. More support for the competing phoneme can pass into stronger activity for its word. The later word process receives information about degree of uncertainty, not only the eventual category name. || A strict **threshold-only account** passes the same completed phoneme for all those tokens. If their earlier differences have been discarded, the word level lacks the changing information needed to explain different amounts of competitor looking. The difficulty concerns the loss of distinctions before lexical processing begins.
17|**Lexical feedback** means that an activated word sends support back to the phonemes that make up that word. A partly matching sound can activate a possible word, and that word can then favor the phoneme interpretation compatible with its full form. || Influence can therefore operate in both directions. Sound evidence changes which words are active, while active words change the balance between interpretations of the sounds. The word level does not merely wait for a final lower-level output. || **Cascading and feedback are separate properties**. Cascading passes partial information forward, whereas feedback sends influence back toward an earlier level. A model can use both while the listener is still recognizing the word.
18|**Ganong (1980)** tested how **word knowledge affects phoneme identification**. Participants heard continua with a real word at one end and a nonword at the other. In **corpse–gorpse**, choosing one initial consonant produces a familiar word, while choosing the other does not. || The initial consonant changed across its category boundary in **5-millisecond steps**. This varied acoustic evidence while keeping a lexical difference between the endpoint interpretations. The listener had information about both the sound and whether a possible interpretation belonged to the vocabulary. || An ambiguous consonant could therefore receive extra support for the interpretation forming a real word. The task concerns whether lexical knowledge affects the way that consonant is identified, rather than only whether listeners can recognize the clear word endpoint.
19|A **late-correction account** lets phoneme perception proceed normally and adds a preference for reporting a real word afterward. With **corpse–gorpse**, a listener is biased toward saying corpse after the initial sound has already been categorized. The word preference affects the report rather than the earlier phoneme decision. || This predicts a **general reporting bias** across the continuum. The tendency to favor the word should not be restricted to only the most acoustically ambiguous region. Word knowledge supplies a later adjustment to the response. || The **word–nonword, neutral, and nonword–word conditions** differ in which consonant interpretation produces a real word. A late correction can shift reporting in those conditions without changing the location of the perceptual category boundary itself. That is a different prediction from having word knowledge resolve uncertainty during phoneme identification.
20|Under a **boundary-shift account**, word knowledge changes where the listener switches between phoneme categories. An ambiguous sound is more likely to receive the interpretation that forms a real word. The effect concerns the category decision, rather than a correction added only after that decision. || The largest lexical effect is expected **near the category boundary**. Clear endpoint sounds strongly support one consonant, so there is little uncertainty for word knowledge to resolve. Near the boundary, both interpretations are plausible and lexical information can exert more influence. || Differences between the lexical conditions should therefore grow in the ambiguous region and shrink at the endpoints. This pattern differs from a uniform report bias across all positions. The location of the effect is part of what separates the two explanations.
21|**Ganong’s results** have the largest word-related differences near the **phoneme category boundary**, with smaller differences toward the clearer endpoints. The identification curves shift depending on which consonant interpretation forms a real word. The effect is not an equal adjustment at every point in the continuum. || This pattern is consistent with a **shift in phoneme labeling** rather than only a uniform preference for saying a word afterward. Acoustic clarity still matters because the lexical effect is concentrated where the consonant is uncertain. || Ganong considered guessing strategies, while **interactive models** produce the effect through **lexical-to-phoneme feedback**. An active word supports its compatible sounds. This mechanism allows knowledge of a whole word to change the balance between interpretations of an ambiguous part.
22|A **TRACE simulation of the Ganong effect** begins with an onset ambiguous between **/b/ and /p/**. The following input supplies **/l/**, the vowel in plug, and a final **/g/** over time. Both onset candidates initially receive support because the first sound is uncertain. || Partial /b/ evidence can support **bl-** words, while partial /p/ evidence supports **pl-** words. Candidates receive more or less activation as additional sounds match or conflict with them. Recognition proceeds while the input is still arriving. || **Plug, blood, and blush** can receive support before the ending settles the competition. The final /g/ matches plug but conflicts with the endings of blood and blush. Activation for plug increases, competing words lose support, and feedback favors the phonemes compatible with plug.
23|As the input unfolds, **plug** gains support, especially when the **final /g/** arrives. **Blood and blush** can fit some earlier information, but their endings conflict with /g/. A candidate that was plausible at the beginning can lose support when the rest of the word arrives. || Stronger activation of **plug** sends feedback to **/p/**. The word’s beginning becomes favored because it matches the interpretation supported by the complete input. Later information therefore changes the balance between interpretations of an earlier ambiguous sound. || This is **top-down processing** within TRACE. A word-level representation influences a phoneme-level representation through the model’s connections. Lexical context contributes during recognition instead of being applied only as a final correction to an already completed report.
24|**TRACE** adapts the **Interactive Activation model** of written-word recognition to spoken language. Representations at different levels support compatible interpretations, while competing alternatives limit one another’s activity. A partially supported candidate can affect other levels before a final response. || In written-word recognition, visual features support letters and letters support words such as **WORK, WORD, WEAR, and WORM**. Word activity can also support compatible letters. Incomplete lower-level information can therefore be interpreted partly through knowledge of whole words. || Speech arrives over time rather than as one completed letter string. TRACE includes feature, phoneme, and word units at possible temporal positions. A word can receive early support and later lose it when new sound information no longer fits. The changing time course is part of the representation, rather than an afterthought added to a finished word decision.
25|**Categorical perception** means that equally separated sounds are harder to distinguish within a category than across categories. The category relationship affects sensitivity even when the physical difference is held constant. The final response can therefore divide a continuous acoustic range into distinct labels. || At the same time, acoustic ambiguity affects **word recognition**, and word knowledge affects **phoneme categorization**. Tokens with one final label can produce different amounts of lexical competition. Activated words can also support one interpretation of an unclear sound. || A categorical final answer therefore does not require completely isolated processing stages. Spoken-word recognition can begin while phonetic uncertainty is still being resolved. **Partial forward activation and lexical feedback** allow the levels to interact during that process.

27|In **Allopenna and colleagues’ (1994) task**, listeners hear a word while viewing possible objects. For **beaker**, **beetle** is a **cohort competitor** because it shares the beginning. **Speaker** is a **rhyme competitor** because it shares the ending, and **carriage** is unrelated. || Looks toward the target and cohort competitor rise early because the initial sounds fit both beaker and beetle. When later sounds distinguish them, beaker looks keep increasing and beetle looks decline. The changing input removes support from a candidate that initially fit. || Rhyme competition follows a different time course because the overlapping sounds occur later. Eye movements record when candidates gain and lose support as the word unfolds. The final correct choice can therefore be preceded by temporary attention to more than one plausible object.
28|**Eye movements** measure changes in support for competing words over time. Target and cohort competition rise early, while rhyme competition follows a later and smaller pattern. The difference depends on when the overlapping sound information becomes available. || The **TRACE simulation** predicts a changing activation pattern for the same types of competitors. A candidate can fit the beginning, gain support, and then lose support as later sounds rule it out. A rhyme competitor receives useful matching information at a different point in the signal. || A final correct response does not preserve that history of competition. **Fixation probability across milliseconds** records when an alternative attracted looking and when it stopped doing so. That time course can be compared with the model’s changing activity, rather than judging the model only by which word ultimately wins.
