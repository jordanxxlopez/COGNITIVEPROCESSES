2|**Lenneberg (1967)** linked a favorable language-learning period with the development of **cerebral dominance**. Cerebral dominance means increasing specialization of the hemispheres. **Language laterality** concerns how strongly language functions are associated with one side rather than the other. || The proposal combined language milestones and neural-development observations with evidence from injury. Children often had better language outcomes than adults after extensive brain damage or surgery. These age differences suggested that the organization supporting language changes over development. || The hypothesis therefore connects learning capacity with the brain’s maturational state. Greater early flexibility would allow different outcomes from those expected in the more specialized adult system. The explanation concerns a developmental change in organization, not simply older learners having heard more words.
3|Much evidence about **first-language sensitive periods** comes from rare injuries or unusual deprivation histories. These cases are **idiosyncratic**, meaning that their individual circumstances differ substantially. Delayed language input can occur alongside other important developmental influences. || **Second-language learning** is much more common and begins at many ages. A larger population provides variation in both starting age and eventual success. These outcomes can be compared without relying entirely on a few unusual first-language histories. || The question is whether **age at the start of learning** predicts later attainment. That comparison offers evidence about developmental constraints, while also requiring attention to experience and the learner’s existing first language. A later L2 is learned by someone who already has linguistic knowledge, unlike a delayed first language.
4|**Johnson and Newport** tested **46 native Korean or Chinese speakers** who had arrived in the United States between **ages 3 and 39**. They had lived there for **3–26 years** at testing. The comparison concerned long-term English proficiency across different arrival ages. || Participants judged whether recorded English sentences were grammatical. The **grammaticality judgment task** covered multiple constructions. It measured eventual performance rather than only the speed of the first weeks of learning. || Earlier arrivals generally performed better. The age relationship was strong before puberty, while adult-arrival scores were lower overall but varied widely. Adult scores did not follow the same strong ordering by arrival age. || Measured differences in English experience, motivation, self-consciousness, and American identification did not explain the reported age effect. Johnson and Newport interpreted the pattern as evidence that **maturational state** affects language learning. That is an interpretation of the age–performance relationship, distinct from the scores themselves.
5|A **grammaticality judgment test, or GJT**, asks whether a sentence follows the language’s grammar. In Johnson and Newport’s **untimed** task, participants listened to recordings and judged the items without a strict response deadline. The question concerned grammatical form rather than whether the event was plausible. || **“The farmer bought two pigs at the market”** contrasts with **“two pig”** to test plural marking. **“The little boy is speaking to a policeman”** contrasts with **“is speak”** to test the required verb form. || **“Tom is reading a book in the bathtub”** contrasts with the missing article in **“reading book.”** **“The boys are going to the zoo”** contrasts with **“A boys,”** while **“Larry went home”** contrasts with **“went the home.”** The constructions require different choices about grammatical forms. || **“When will Sam fix his car?”** contrasts with **“When Sam will fix his car?”** **“What do they sell at the corner store?”** contrasts with **“What they sell at the corner store?”** These pairs test auxiliary placement and the requirements of English questions.
6|For arrivals at about **3–15 years**, grammaticality accuracy decreases as **age of arrival, or AOA**, increases. The reported correlation is about **−0.87**. A negative correlation means that later arrival tends to accompany a lower score, and its large magnitude indicates a strong relationship in this range. || For arrivals at about **17–39 years**, average scores are lower but much more dispersed. The correlation is about **−0.16**, a weak negative relationship. An older adult arrival does not reliably score below every younger adult arrival. || The result combines a strong early age gradient with considerable later individual variation. It is not one equally strong straight decline across all ages. Describing the full pattern requires distinguishing the two ranges rather than only reporting that earlier learners perform better overall.
7|Learners arriving **up to about age eight** are often near-perfect and **native-like** on the grammaticality test. Later childhood arrival is associated with progressively lower accuracy. The early group therefore contains both a high-performing youngest range and a declining pattern farther into childhood. || Adult-arrival scores vary substantially without the same strong ordering by age. The description **“nearly random distribution”** concerns the weak relationship between adult arrival age and individual accuracy. It does not mean that late learners answer every sentence at chance. || Individual adults can differ greatly despite similar starting ages. The population pattern favors early learning, while the later range leaves more variation to explain. Age and score therefore relate differently in the early and late parts of the sample.
8|**Johnson and Newport** interpreted the decline in childhood-arrival performance as a loss of some **language-learning capacity over development**. Earlier exposure occurred during a more favorable maturational state. Later learning took place after those conditions had changed. || The timing did not match every detail of **Lenneberg’s** original formulation. His developmental account relied on sparse neural information between early childhood and adolescence. The later study supplied a behavioral age pattern rather than directly measuring the same proposed neural transition. || They attributed the wide spread among late learners to greater dependence on **individual learning abilities** after the favorable period. Under this interpretation, ordinary early acquisition processes contribute less uniformly to later success. The explanation connects different learning conditions with the difference between early regularity and later variability.
9|The **exercise hypothesis** proposes an early superior capacity for acquiring languages. If the capacity is not used during the favorable period, it declines or disappears with maturation. If used early, it remains available for learning additional languages throughout life. || The **maturational-state hypothesis** also proposes an early advantage. Its decline follows development even if the person successfully acquired a language in childhood. Early use does not permanently preserve the same learning state. || The accounts therefore predict different outcomes for someone with a normal early **L1**. Exercise predicts that early use protects the capacity for later languages. Maturational state allows successful L1 acquisition to coexist with reduced later L2-learning capacity. The difference concerns whether using the early ability prevents its later decline.
10|**Implicit knowledge** supports language use without deliberate rule explanation. In **Ellis’s (2005)** distinction, it is procedural knowledge accessed automatically during fluent performance and is difficult to put directly into words. Knowing how to use a construction is different from consciously reciting its rule. || **Explicit knowledge** is consciously available information about grammatical rules and fragments. It is used through controlled processing and can be stated verbally. In the theoretical comparison, it can be learned at any age. || An **untimed GJT** can draw on **metalinguistic knowledge**, conscious knowledge about language itself. A participant can inspect a sentence, remember a taught rule, and choose an answer. A correct answer can therefore come from a different process than the automatic one used in fluent conversation. || The proposed critical-period restriction in this comparison concerns **implicit acquisition**. Explicit learning remains available across ages. A test that permits both sources of knowledge cannot automatically be treated as a pure measure of one of them.
11|An **untimed GJT** removes time pressure and focuses attention on form. It allows the participant to consult explicit grammatical knowledge. Imitation and oral narrative emphasize meaning under more pressured conditions, so the tasks do not make identical processing demands. || In **Ellis (2005)**, years of formal instruction correlate with untimed judgments of ungrammatical items at about **0.27**. Starting age has a significant negative relationship with the **timed GJT**, about **−0.23**, but little relationship with the untimed judgments in the reported comparisons. || **Task design changes what knowledge is measured**. An unlimited-time form judgment can reflect formal education differently from fluent language use. The score has to be interpreted in relation to whether the task encourages deliberate rules, automatic processing, or a mixture of both.
12|**Typological similarity** means similarity in how languages organize linguistic forms. A first language with structures resembling those of L2 can supply useful transfer. A learner with different starting structures may have to acquire distinctions that prior language use did not require. || Johnson and Newport tested **Chinese- and Korean-speaking learners of English**. Their transfer opportunities are not identical to those of every other L1–English pairing. A language-pair comparison must therefore consider what the existing language contributes. || **Cognates and borrowed forms** provide one type of overlap through familiar vocabulary. Grammatical similarities can provide others. An observed age relationship can be intertwined with differences in transfer support, so age cannot be interpreted independently of the language systems involved.
13|**English constructions differ in difficulty** for later learners. **Progressive -ing and word order** are relatively easy in the reported comparison. **Determiners and plural marking** generate substantially more errors for some later-arriving groups. Age does not lower performance by the same amount on every rule. || The categories also include **verb subcategorization, past tense, pronouns, particles, auxiliaries, third-person singular marking, wh-questions, and yes/no questions**. Subcategorization concerns which complements a verb permits. Particles and auxiliaries add grammatical requirements that can differ between languages. || Some difficulties resemble child development, while others fit **transfer**. Chinese lacks the same determiner system as English. Questions that appear late in young children can nevertheless be relatively easy for older L2 learners. The construction’s demands and the learner’s existing knowledge help determine the outcome.
14|**Birdsong and Molis (2001)** tested **Spanish speakers learning English**. The age-of-arrival pattern differed from that reported for Chinese and Korean speakers. Changing the language pair changed the relationship between age and grammaticality attainment. || In the Spanish-speaking sample, early-arrival scores remained high without the same strong childhood decline. The reported early correlation was about **−0.23**. Among late arrivals, the relationship was more strongly negative, about **−0.69**. || Johnson and Newport instead found a strong childhood relationship and a weak adult relationship. A single fixed maturational cutoff does not explain both shapes on its own. The comparison requires an account of how language pairing, transfer, and learning history can affect the age–performance pattern.
15|**Older L2 learners have lower attainment overall**, but the measured result depends on which constructions are tested, how the task works, and which languages are involved. A grammaticality score represents performance on particular items under particular demands. It is not a complete measure of every language ability. || **Age of acquisition** is connected with the quantity and quality of experience. **Transfer** changes which L2 properties are supported by L1. These influences can differ across learners who share an arrival age. || **Competence and performance** also differ. A task can permit deliberate rule use or require rapid, fluent processing. Explaining the overall age advantage requires distinguishing these contributions instead of assuming that every task and language pair measures one uniform developmental effect.
