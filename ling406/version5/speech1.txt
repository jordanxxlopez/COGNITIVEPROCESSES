2|**Speech perception** is the process of recognizing a spoken message from sound. Somehow we can take a continuously changing signal and retrieve “I can see you,” even though the recording does not contain a separate little sound for each written letter. The mouth is moving throughout the utterance, so information about neighboring sounds overlaps. || Different speakers can say the same words with different voices and different patterns of acoustic energy. Even the frequencies associated with a vowel can vary or overlap with those of another vowel. Recognizing the message therefore involves identifying what stays linguistically the same across physical changes in the sound, rather than matching every recording to one exact acoustic pattern.
3|Somehow we have to turn a **highly variable, analog signal into something more abstract**, so the language system can operate on it. **Analog** means that the physical sound can change continuously. A slight change in frequency, timing, or loudness does not automatically mean that a speaker has produced a different speech sound or word. || A **symbolic representation** treats the input as a recognizable language unit, such as a phoneme. A **phoneme** is a sound category that contributes to distinctions between words. Different physical versions can belong to that same category, allowing the language system to treat them alike. || **Categorical perception** is a proposed way of making that translation. Differences within one category become less noticeable, while a similar physical difference across a category boundary becomes easier to hear. The listener can retain a stable identity despite variation in the incoming sound.
4|The **vocal tract** is the passage through which the voice travels as the mouth produces speech. Its shape affects which sound frequencies are amplified most strongly. Moving the tongue or changing the mouth opening changes that shape, so a different set of frequencies becomes prominent. || These amplified resonant frequencies are called **formants**. The vowels in heed, hid, head, had, hod, hawed, hood, and who’d involve different combinations of formants. Recognizing a vowel depends on the pattern of resonances, rather than finding one frequency that identifies every production of it. || A **formant transition** is a change in a formant’s frequency over time. As the speaker moves into or out of a sound, the vocal tract changes shape and its resonances change with it. A steady formant characterizes part of a vowel, while a transition carries information about movement into that vowel.
5|A **spectrogram** represents how the energy in a sound changes across time and frequency. Time is measured in milliseconds and frequency in hertz. Darker areas contain stronger acoustic energy, and the prominent horizontal bands include the vowel’s resonances, or **formants**. || The vowels in **bead, bid, bade, bed, bad, bod, baud, bode, “bood,” and booed** have different formant patterns even though the surrounding consonants are similar. Bead has a relatively low first formant and a high second formant. Toward bad, the first formant is higher, while rounded back vowels near booed have a much lower second formant. || **F1** means the first, or lowest, formant. **F2** means the second-lowest formant. These labels identify two resonances within the same vowel, and their combination supplies information that helps distinguish one vowel from another.
6|The vowel **/u/** has a first formant near **300 Hz** and a second formant near **900 Hz** in this recording. The two values describe resonances within one vowel. They are not two separate sounds that the speaker says one after the other. || A **waveform** records changes in sound pressure over time. A **spectrogram** separates the same sound into its frequency components, making the low F1 and F2 available for analysis. These are two descriptions of one recording rather than two different productions. || **Praat** is software for analyzing speech. It can estimate formant frequencies so that the resonance pattern can be studied and changed. In **source–filter synthesis**, the energy supplied by the voice and the pattern imposed by the vocal tract are handled separately, allowing their contributions to the vowel to be examined.
7|**Praat can automatically find formants** in a recording. For this /u/, it identifies five resonances and tracks their frequencies over time. The analysis saves having to locate each band manually, while still treating the bands as parts of one spoken sound. || **F1, F2, and F3** are usually the formants most relevant to phonemic processing, meaning the identification of speech-sound categories. The number gives the formant’s order from low to high frequency. F1 is the lowest resonance, rather than the first sound spoken. || The manipulation concentrates on **F1 and F2**. Their frequencies are about 300 Hz and 900 Hz for this /u/, with the other resonances higher up. Changing those first two resonances changes part of the vowel’s acoustic pattern without requiring every frequency component to be changed together.
8|A recorded voice combines a **source** with a **filter**. The source supplies the sound energy, and the vocal tract changes which frequencies are emphasized. The formant frequencies describe the resonant pattern associated with that filtering. || An estimated model of the filter can be used to extract the source from the recorded sound. The resulting signal is approximately an **unfiltered voice**, which sounds like a buzz because the vowel’s particular pattern of amplification has been removed. The regression comparison concerns estimating the filter’s contribution within the combined signal. || The remaining source still supplies acoustic energy. Applying a filter to it can restore a vowel-like pattern of resonance. Separating the two contributions makes it possible to change the filtering while keeping the underlying source the same.
9|The original **filter** contains the first four formants estimated from the recording. Applying that pattern to the extracted source approximately restores the original resonances. It provides a comparison for changes to individual formants. || In the second filter, **F1 is raised slightly** while the other formants stay approximately the same. This changes the lowest resonance. In the third filter, **F2 is raised substantially**, changing the second resonance instead of the first. || The **source stays constant** across these versions. The different filters therefore act on the same underlying sound energy. A difference in the resulting vowel can be connected to the changed resonance pattern, rather than to a different speaker, a new recording, or a simultaneous change in every aspect of the voice.
10|**Multiplying the source by the filters gives new vowels** because a filter changes which frequencies in that source are emphasized. The original filter preserves the low-formant pattern. Raising F1 changes the lowest strong resonance, while raising F2 changes the second resonance instead. || **Source–filter synthesis** combines the same sound source with these different resonance patterns. The resulting vowel quality changes even though the underlying source has stayed the same. The sound that reaches the listener depends both on the energy supplied by the source and on how the filter shapes that energy. || Changing the filter is therefore a controlled way of changing vowel quality. A listener receives different acoustic patterns without the researcher having to obtain a new natural utterance for each vowel.
11|**Vowel space** describes vowels using combinations of formant frequencies. F1 and F2 provide useful coordinates because vowel qualities differ in the relationship between these resonances. A vowel occupies a range of possible values, rather than one point that every speaker must reproduce exactly. || **Anatomy, speaking rate, and context** affect those values. Speakers have different vocal tracts, and the sounds before and after a vowel influence how it is produced. These changes can make the measured formants of different vowels overlap. || Formant values can still provide a pretty good description of vowel space, especially for **one talker speaking carefully**. When other properties are held constant, changing the **steady-state formants**, the resonances in the relatively stable part of a sound, can produce recognizable approximations of different vowels. Natural speech also contains changes over time, so a steady resonance pattern is useful without being the entire signal.
12|The **pattern playback synthesizer** produces sound from a painted spectrographic pattern. The pattern specifies which frequencies contain energy at different times. Changing a painted band changes the acoustic pattern that the machine produces, allowing speech-like sounds to be constructed under controlled conditions. || Light passes through a **cylindrical lens** and a rotating **tone wheel**. A lens and a **45-degree mirror** direct the changing light toward the spectrogram pattern. Different parts of that pattern allow different amounts of light through. >> A **light collector and photocell** turn the resulting light fluctuations into an electrical signal. An amplifier strengthens that signal, and a loudspeaker turns it into audible sound. || The researcher can change the frequency pattern without asking a speaker to repeat the utterance. The output depends on the specified acoustic pattern, which makes particular sound properties available for controlled manipulation.
13|The stop consonants **/b/, /d/, and /g/** are distinguished partly by **formant transitions**, the changes in resonance as the vocal tract moves into a vowel. Their audible release occurs while the jaw is opening, so information about the consonant is carried by this movement rather than only by a stable sound. || **Liberman and colleagues (1957)** used synthetic syllables in which the lower formant stayed similar while the upper formant started at different frequencies. The upper transition could rise into the vowel, stay relatively flat, or fall into the same stable vowel region. || The **pattern playback synthesizer** made these controlled speech-like sounds possible. Changing the onset of the upper formant changed the consonant information while leaving much of the following vowel alike. Listeners could therefore respond to differences in the transition without hearing entirely different utterances on every trial.
14|A **speech continuum** is a set of sounds that changes gradually along a controlled acoustic property. In the sequence from stimulus 1 to 13, **F1 stays constant** and **F2 onset frequency changes linearly**. Linearly means that each adjacent pair differs by the same programmed amount. || At one end, F2 starts below the vowel’s stable value and rises into it. As the starting frequency increases, the transition becomes flatter and eventually falls from above that stable value. The physical change is gradual even if the listener assigns different sound labels along the way. || A pair inside one phoneme category can be compared with an equally separated pair crossing a category boundary. Both pairs have the same physical difference. Better discrimination for one pair would therefore concern how the listener organizes the sounds, rather than a larger change built into the stimuli.
15|An **ideal observer model** states what responses would be expected under a particular account of perception. If perception is **noncategorical**, uncertainty should change gradually as a sound moves away from a clear example of a category. A **prototype** is that clear representative, rather than a boundary between categories. || The continuum has **/b/** at one end, **/g/** at the other, and **/d/** in the middle. Moving away from the /b/ prototype makes a sound less characteristic of /b/ and more characteristic of /d/. Under the proposed gradual pattern, stimulus 2 receives about **75% /b/** and **25% /d/** responses. || Categories become “blurry” between their prototypes because neighboring labels become plausible by degrees. The predicted /b/ responses fall as /d/ responses rise, and the same gradual exchange occurs between /d/ and /g/. The model predicts a smooth change in uncertainty rather than an abrupt switch.
16|A **labeling task** asks the listener to identify a sound, such as /b/, /d/, or /g/. A **discrimination task** asks whether two sounds are the same or different. One response names a category, while the other concerns whether a physical difference can be heard. || If perception of F2-onset differences is **linear**, equal physical differences should be equally easy to hear. For example, sounds 1 and 3 should be about as discriminable as sounds 5 and 7. Their positions on the continuum differ, but their programmed separation is equal. || This account predicts that **discrimination is independent of labeling**. Labeling responses can rise and fall around the prototypes because a category must be chosen. Discrimination should remain approximately constant across equally separated pairs, rather than improving specifically where the label changes.
17|**Liberman and colleagues (1957)** found **categorical perception** even though the acoustic continuum changed in equal steps. Early sounds mainly received /b/ labels, middle sounds /d/ labels, and later sounds /g/ labels. Responses changed abruptly near the boundaries instead of becoming gradually uncertain across the whole sequence. || **Discrimination was best near the /b/–/d/ and /d/–/g/ boundaries**. Sounds assigned to different phonemes were easier to distinguish than similarly spaced sounds assigned to the same phoneme. This relationship occurred in the one-step, two-step, and three-step comparisons, with chance near 50% correct. || Greater physical separation generally made discrimination easier, but separation alone did not explain the boundary peaks. Predictions based on labeling captured much of that pattern. Listeners also retained some sensitivity beyond the simplest categorical prediction, so identifying a category and detecting a difference were closely related without becoming the same measurement.

19|In **beach–peach identification**, a listener uses one label through steps 1–5, gives mixed responses near step 6, and uses the other label by step 7. The abrupt change identifies the region where that listener stops treating the input as one word and begins treating it as the other. || For that listener, **discrimination is strongest for pairs 4–6 and 5–7**. Those pairs reach across the identification boundary. Pairs well inside a category are harder to distinguish, while 6–8 is intermediate because step 6 itself receives mixed labels. || Another listener switches labels later, around steps 6–7, and has stronger discrimination nearer that region. The exact boundary can differ between people. The shared relationship is that hearing a difference becomes easier near the point where a listener assigns different identities to the sounds.
20|**Not everyone has exactly the same category boundary**. One listener can change from beach to peach earlier in the continuum than another listener. The pairs that cross that boundary can therefore differ between people, even when everyone hears the same recordings. || **Discrimination is generally best between categories**. Two sounds can differ physically but remain hard to distinguish if both are heard as versions of the same phoneme. A similarly sized difference becomes easier to detect when the sounds receive different identities. || The shortened classroom task contained only **different trials**. Every pair was physically different, so “different” was always the correct physical answer. Responding “same” meant that the listener had failed to detect that difference. The procedure separates a difference present in the recordings from a difference the listener actually hears.
21|The **bear–pear identification** responses contain a fairly clear change in label, but the discrimination responses fluctuate across pairs. The **beach–peach** results also contain irregular changes rather than a perfectly smooth progression. A measured response curve can vary even when the stimuli were constructed systematically. || Identification and discrimination measure different behaviors. Naming a sound and detecting a difference can be affected differently across trials and listeners. A fluctuation at one tested position does not, by itself, mean that the listener has another phoneme category there. || The relevant comparison is between the region where labels change and the pairs that are easier to distinguish. Class data can contain that relationship alongside variability. The actual responses are evidence to evaluate, rather than a requirement that every tested pair reproduce an ideal curve exactly.
22|**Being worse at hearing some differences between sounds** can support recognizing a stable word. Different productions of the same phoneme vary physically. If every small difference created a new language identity, versions of the same sound or word would be treated as unrelated inputs. || **Categorical perception** reduces sensitivity to differences within a category while preserving distinctions between categories. The listener can treat several different acoustic versions as the same phoneme. Those versions then supply a stable unit for identifying a word. || The reduced sensitivity concerns variation that does not require a different linguistic identity. The system can overlook some differences between versions of one category while still responding to a difference that separates categories. Accurate recognition therefore does not require equally strong sensitivity to every physical detail.

24|The **conditioned head turn procedure** measures whether an infant hears a sound change without asking for a spoken answer. The infant learns to turn toward the sound source when a change is associated with a visual reward. A learned turn provides a behavioral response that can be recorded. || **High-amplitude sucking** uses sucking behavior instead. As a sound repeats, sucking can decrease because the infant becomes accustomed to it. If changing the sound produces renewed sucking, the infant has responded differently to the new input. || Both procedures make auditory discrimination measurable before a child can name the sounds. The behaviors differ, but the question concerns whether the infant detects a controlled difference. The response is evidence about sensitivity to the change rather than an adult-style verbal judgment of the category.
25|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** measured infant speech discrimination using **high-amplitude sucking**. Infants heard repeated synthetic syllables until responding declined. The sound then changed across an adult phoneme boundary, changed within a category, or stayed the same. || **Voice onset time**, or **VOT**, is the timing of voicing relative to the consonant release. In the **20D condition**, a change from **+20 to +40 milliseconds** crossed the adult category boundary. Sucking increased again after that change. >> In the **20S condition**, the change was also 20 milliseconds, but **−20 to 0** or **+60 to +80 milliseconds** stayed within a category. Sucking did not recover in the same way. >> In the **0 condition**, the sound did not change and sucking continued to decline. || The larger response to a boundary-crossing change cannot be explained by its physical size alone, because the within-category change was equally large. Infants responded to category-related differences before they could produce word labels.
26|**Perceptual narrowing** means that sensitivity to some sound differences decreases as perception becomes tuned to the surrounding language. English-learning infants distinguish the tested **Hindi and Salish contrasts** more successfully early in infancy than near the end of the first year. || Many reach the discrimination criterion at **6–8 months**. Performance declines at **8–10 months** and is much lower by **10–12 months**. Infants who have experience with Hindi or Salish retain much stronger discrimination of the relevant contrast. || **Werker and Tees** connect this developmental change with language experience. Becoming better adapted to one sound system can accompany reduced sensitivity to distinctions outside that system. A developing speech-perception system therefore changes which differences remain easy to hear, rather than maintaining equal sensitivity to every contrast.
27|One account proposes that **categorical perception is a special property of the language system**. Under this account, the category-organizing abilities used in speech are already present very early. Their early appearance is treated as part of the explanation of how language develops. || The **“citizens of the world”** description proposes that children initially hear contrasts across languages. Exposure then tunes this broad system rapidly to the language the child hears. The surrounding language determines which distinctions remain useful. || In this account, development mainly narrows an initially broad set of abilities. The child preserves relevant early distinctions and loses sensitivity to others. The proposal concerns both the starting state of perception and the role of experience in shaping it, rather than simply the fact that adults hear their native language well.
28|**Categorical perception is not limited to language or to humans**. Similar category-related responses occur for nonspeech sounds and in animals without human language. A response pattern can therefore be categorical without requiring a mechanism devoted only to human phonemes. || Categorical behavior is also **task-dependent**. Naming a sound, deciding whether a pair differs, and giving a learned response place different demands on the listener. How the experiment asks for a response affects how strongly categorical the resulting performance appears. || **Infant speech perception is not completely universal**. Some early contrasts are easier to detect than others. Development involves differences in initial sensitivity as well as changes associated with language experience. These observations separate early category-related behavior from the stronger claim that every contrast starts equally available in a speech-specific system.
29|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** tested **categorical perception of nonspeech sounds** by changing **rise time**. Rise time is how quickly a sound grows to a stronger amplitude after it begins. A rapid onset sounds more **plucked**, while a slower onset sounds more **bowed**. || The materials included **sawtooth waves and sine waves**, with speech syllables available for comparison. Changing the onset changed an auditory property without requiring the sounds to be different phonemes. The listener could still assign different perceptual labels. || For sawtooth waves, pluck responses decreased and bow responses increased over rise times from about **0 to 80 milliseconds**. Discrimination was strongest near the labeling change, around the middle of the range, rather than constant for equal rise-time differences. The relationship between category labels and discrimination can therefore occur in hearing sounds that are not speech.
30|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** tested whether infants respond categorically to **nonspeech sounds**. They measured sucking after repeated exposure and compared a sound that stayed the same, a change within a category, and a change between categories. || Sucking declined as the repeated sound became familiar in all three conditions. With **no change** or a **within-category change**, responding stayed low. With a **between-category change**, sucking increased again before gradually declining. || The renewed response means that the category-crossing change was more effective at attracting a response than the within-category change. Infants can therefore display category-related discrimination for nonspeech input. Early appearance of this pattern does not make it exclusive to speech perception.
31|Chinchillas were trained through **operant conditioning**, in which a response is learned through its consequences. A **0-millisecond-lag** or **80-millisecond-lag** speech stimulus signaled that responding would lead to food. This rewarded signal is called an **SD**, or discriminative stimulus. || Intermediate **VOT** values tested how the trained response extended across a **/ga/–/ka/** continuum. Responses changed sharply across a limited range of timings in chinchillas and in human comparison listeners. The animals responded to the acoustic distinction without needing human language. || **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)**, with **Kuhl and Miller (1975)** also identified, connect this comparison to speech categorization. Increasing the initial burst and aspiration-noise intensity could shift the boundary toward a shorter VOT or change the curve’s steepness. Categorization therefore depended on acoustic properties in the animal responses as well as in human responses.
32|Some speech contrasts are **less salient**, meaning less noticeable, to infants than others. At **4–5 months**, English-learning infants respond more clearly to the native **[ma]–[na]** change than to the nonnative **[na]–[ŋa]** change. Longer looking after a change provides evidence that it was detected. || These sounds differ in the place where the nasal consonant is produced. **[ma]** begins with a bilabial nasal made with the lips, **[na]** with an alveolar nasal near the ridge behind the teeth, and **[ŋa]** with a velar nasal farther back. The comparison concerns sensitivity to these different places of articulation. || Filipino-learning infants have little same-versus-change difference for their native **[na]–[ŋa]** contrast at **6–8 months**, but a clearer difference at **10–12 months**. Experience can strengthen a distinction that starts weak. Development therefore includes learning to hear some differences more clearly, as well as narrowing sensitivity to others.
