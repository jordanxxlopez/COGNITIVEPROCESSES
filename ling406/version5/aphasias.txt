2|**Aphasia** is a loss or impairment of language function after brain damage. In the **1870s, Lichtheim and Wernicke** proposed that different language problems could result from damage to different centers or the connections between them. A patient could lose one route through the system while retaining another. || In **Lichtheim’s House**, **A** stores sound images, **M** stores speech-movement images, and **B** represents concepts. The acoustic input route **a** carries sound to A, while the motor route **m** carries output from M toward articulation. These labels distinguish incoming sound, stored representations, meaning, and outgoing movement. >> Understanding requires the sound representation in A to reach B. Repeating a heard word requires A to communicate with M so that the sound can be reproduced. || The numbered sites distinguish kinds of damage. **1** damages M and **2** damages A. **3** interrupts the connection between A and M, **4** the connection between M and B, and **6** the connection between A and B. **5** interrupts motor output and **7** acoustic input. Different tasks depend on different combinations of centers and pathways.
3|The **Geschwind–Wernicke model** connects language functions with brain regions and the pathways between them. **Norman Geschwind revived the approach in the 1970s**. **Broca’s area** is toward the front in the frontal region, and **Wernicke’s area** is farther back in the temporal region. The **arcuate fasciculus** is a nerve-fiber pathway connecting them. || The **central sulcus**, also called the **fissure of Rolando**, and the **lateral sulcus**, or **fissure of Sylvius**, are major grooves in the brain. The lateral sulcus lies above the temporal region and helps locate that region relative to frontal areas. || Linguistic theories distinguished **syntax**, the grammatical organization of words, from word knowledge and meaning. A **dissociation** occurs when one ability is impaired while another is relatively preserved. A **double dissociation** adds a patient with the reverse pattern. Such comparisons make it possible to test more specific functional distinctions than simply language present versus absent.
4|In **Fromkin’s (1997)** description, **Broca’s aphasia** includes pauses during word finding, missing grammatical material, and disturbed word order. **Grammatical morphemes** are small words or word parts that express grammatical relationships. Important content words can remain while the connections that normally organize a sentence are reduced. || Everyday spoken comprehension can appear relatively good, but controlled tests can reveal substantial difficulty. **Agrammatism** means difficulty producing or interpreting grammatical structure. Its association with Broca’s aphasia is imperfect. Some patients with Broca-area lesions are not agrammatic, and some agrammatic patients do not fit the neurological classification. || **Wernicke’s aphasia** often involves fluent speech with good pronunciation and intonation but poor word selection and comprehension. Substituted words can be related or unrelated to the intended meaning. **Neologisms** are invented word forms. Fluent delivery therefore does not ensure that the selected words express a coherent message.
5|**Wernicke’s aphasia** can produce fluent **“word salad” or “Jabberwocky”**, including **neologisms**. A patient describing impaired vision said **“My wires don’t hire right.”** The sentence has a recognizable subject and verb pattern, but the selected words fail to express the intended visual problem. || Another health response began **“I felt worse because I can no longer keep in mind from the mind of the minds…”** Grammatical-sounding phrases continue without a coherent message. An aphasic physician also used invented forms such as **“demaploze” and “tubaboys.”** >> A fork was described as **“a need for a schedule,”** and a spoon as **“How many schemes on your throat.”** Words are produced fluently, but their selection does not provide an adequate object description. || **Broca’s aphasia** can instead produce **telegraphic speech**, with important content words and few grammatical connections. A hospital account retained **“Monday,” “Dad,” “hospital,” “two doctors,” “thirty minutes,” “Wednesday,” “nine o’clock,” “Thursday,” “ten o’clock,” and “teeth.”** Pauses and missing grammatical particles make the account difficult to produce and follow, while surviving content words still convey parts of the event.
6|A traditional division assigns **lexical activation or inhibition problems** to Wernicke’s aphasia and **grammar problems** to Broca’s aphasia. The **lexicon** is the system of stored word knowledge. Activation makes a word available, while inhibition reduces competing words that should not be selected. || **Broca’s original patient Leborgne**, nicknamed **Tan**, could produce essentially one syllable. He appeared to understand what was said and retain other abilities. His very restricted output was different from the partly informative but agrammatic speech associated with the later syndrome. || Those original cases may fit **apraxia of speech**, a problem organizing the movements needed to speak. A difficulty planning speech movements and a loss of grammatical knowledge are different proposed causes of limited output. The amount a person can say does not, by itself, identify which operation is impaired.
7|A sharp distinction between **grammar and the lexicon** separates rules for combining words from stored word representations. Under the formal linguistic account, a new sentence is understood by constructing its grammatical structure. It is not necessary for the complete sentence to have been memorized before. || **“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously”** has a grammatical organization despite its unusual meaning. **“Furiously sleep ideas green colorless”** contains the same words without that organization. Knowing the word meanings and a familiar event is insufficient to explain why the two orders differ. || The proposed aphasia division links Wernicke’s aphasia with disrupted access to lexical items and Broca’s aphasia with disrupted grammar. This is a claim about separate processes, beyond the observation that one group tends to speak fluently and another does not. It predicts differences in the operations affected by damage.
8|The **message level** organizes what the speaker intends to communicate, including the concepts, referents, event, and chosen perspective. A **referent** is the person, thing, or event being talked about. These intentions have to be converted into linguistic forms before speech can be produced. || At the **functional level**, word meanings and semantic roles are selected. **Lemmas** link intended concepts with lexical items, whose relation to their referents is generally arbitrary. At the **positional level**, word forms are ordered and grammatical operations organize sentence structure and affixes. >> **Phonological encoding** supplies the sound forms. Speech gestures then organize their articulation. Problems can therefore arise in choosing concepts, selecting words, arranging grammar, encoding sounds, or producing movements. || **HOUSEBOAT and DOGHOUSE** have meanings related to their parts, but their formation still involves combining forms. The error **“easy enoughly”** supports an operation that adds morphology at the positional level. It is not simply the selection of one already stored, indivisible word.
9|An **autonomous parsing account** proposes that sentence structure is computed independently of meaning. A person can use familiar words and context to follow everyday conversation while still struggling with the grammatical relationships required by a controlled test. Conversation supplies several sources of help at once. || In **“The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite,”** the final phrase can describe the instrument of the action or modify the safe. The individual words remain the same, but their grammatical grouping changes. Understanding each word does not automatically determine that grouping. || A test of a **specific parsing deficit** must separate structural demands from easier routes based on familiar events or likely word relationships. Success in ordinary conversation does not establish intact comprehension of every grammatical arrangement. The task has to require the particular operation being investigated.
10|Broca distinguished **articulate speech from comprehension**. A person who understands others but cannot produce intelligible speech provides evidence that these abilities can be affected differently. Poor production alone does not establish a loss of the ability to interpret language. || A proposed **syntactic impairment** is broader than difficulty moving the speech organs. It concerns grammatical organization and can affect both production and comprehension. Testing it therefore requires asking how the person understands sentences with different structural relationships. || A patient might produce little speech because articulation is difficult while retaining more grammatical knowledge than the output expresses. A comprehension task can test the interpretation without requiring a full spoken sentence. The distinction concerns what language knowledge is available versus what the patient can physically produce.
11|**Broca’s aphasia** is almost always accompanied by some motor dysfunction. If speaking is effortful, a person may preserve the words carrying the most information and omit grammatical material. Such output can reflect an attempt to communicate despite impairment, rather than directly revealing that grammar itself has been lost. || **Competence** means linguistic knowledge, and **performance** means using that knowledge in a task. Chomsky’s distinction allows limited output to coexist with greater retained knowledge. Hughlings Jackson’s observation concerns what a patient manages despite impairment, not simply a transparent expression of everything the patient knows. || **Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** investigated comprehension as a way to test the grammatical claim beyond articulation. A sentence-interpretation task can require grammatical relationships without demanding a full spoken answer. Persistent structural difficulty there would extend beyond the physical effort of producing speech.
12|**Conversation contains redundant cues** to a speaker’s intended meaning. In **“The dog bit the man,”** the meanings of dog, bite, and man support a familiar event. Ordinary English **subject–verb–object order** also favors taking the first noun as the agent, the participant performing the action. || A **heuristic** is a simplifying strategy that uses a regular pattern instead of a complete analysis. A listener might choose the likely biter or assume the first noun performs the action. Discourse context can supply further clues, allowing comprehension even if the grammar is not fully analyzed. || **“The dog that the man is biting is angry”** conflicts with those shortcuts. The **man** is doing the biting, and the **dog** receives it despite appearing first. The main-clause subject and the agent inside the relative clause are different roles. Correct understanding requires tracking the sentence’s relationships rather than relying only on plausibility or noun order.
13|**Caramazza and Zurif** compared control sentences with several kinds of **center-embedded sentence**, in which one clause occurs inside another. Controls included **“The girl is kicking a green ball,” “The cat is chasing a blue bird,” “The girl is pulling a red wagon,”** and **“The man is reading a yellow book.”** The other controls involve a boy riding a new bicycle, a girl holding a broken doll, a boy throwing a round ball, and a boy carrying a sharp pencil. Normal order and event knowledge support the same actor. || **Semantically constrained** items contain an embedded clause but make the likely actor clear. Examples are **“The apple that the boy is eating is red,” “The wagon that the horse is pulling is green,” “The book that the girl is reading is yellow,”** and **“The bat that the boy is carrying is brown.”** Other items involve a boy holding a bicycle, a girl wearing a coat, a woman washing a dress, and a clown holding a balloon. Plausibility supplies a strong cue alongside grammar. || **Reversible** items allow either participant to act plausibly. They include **“The cow that the monkey is scaring is yellow,” “The horse that the bear is kicking is brown,” “The cat that the dog is biting is black,”** and **“The fish that the frog is biting is green.”** Other pairs concern a tiger chasing a lion, a boy hitting a girl, a woman hugging a man, and a boy kicking a girl. Grammar must distinguish who does what because either direction is possible. || **Improbable** items make ordinary expectations favor the wrong actor. They include **“The horse that the bird is kicking is brown,” “The lion that the baby is scaring is yellow,” “The bird that the worm is eating is blue,”** and **“The dog that the man is biting is black.”** Other items make a horse ride a man, a boy spank a woman, a robber chase a policeman, and a cat pat a girl. The noun inside the embedded clause performs the action even when that event is unusual.
14|In **sentence–picture matching**, a patient hears a sentence and selects the picture that matches its meaning. The controlled conditions vary whether ordinary event knowledge helps identify the actor or whether grammar must carry that information. Accuracy is the measured outcome. || **Healthy controls** perform about equally well across controls, semantically constrained, reversible, and improbable sentences. **Wernicke’s patients** perform poorly overall. **Broca’s patients** do better when plausible event roles support the grammatical meaning and worse when they must track the roles without that help. || **Caramazza and Zurif** interpreted the Broca pattern as evidence of a **syntactic comprehension impairment**. The difficulty is not limited to producing speech. It appears when understanding who acted on whom requires the structural relationships, particularly when ordinary expectations cannot substitute for them.
15|Distinguishing **grammar from the lexicon** makes the proposed impairment more specific than a label such as fluent or nonfluent. Grammar concerns relationships among words. Lexical processing concerns the stored words and how the relevant entries become available. Different impaired operations can produce different patterns across tasks. || A person can know a content word’s meaning but fail to assign it the correct role in a complex sentence. Another person can produce a fluent frame but choose words that do not express the intended message. The same broad measure of speaking ability would conceal that difference. || A **structure–function hypothesis** links a particular operation with the system supporting it. It must explain the controlled behavioral pattern, not only the patient’s general appearance. Testing specific operations allows more precise claims about what a lesion has affected.
16|In **Dick and colleagues’ task**, listeners select **which animal performs the action**. The event remains the dog biting the cat, while the grammar changes. Recognizing the animal names alone is insufficient because the task asks which one is the actor. || **“The dog is biting the cat”** is an **active** sentence. **“It’s the dog that is biting the cat”** is a **subject cleft**. Both introduce the dog before the cat and put the actor first. || **“It’s the cat that the dog is biting”** is an **object cleft**. **“The cat is bitten by the dog”** is a **passive**. Both begin with the participant receiving the action. A first-noun-is-actor strategy gives the wrong answer, so grammar must distinguish the dog’s role from the cat’s role.
17|**Healthy aging controls** identify the actor accurately across active, subject-cleft, object-cleft, and passive sentences. Aphasia groups have different levels and patterns of difficulty. **Anomic aphasia**, associated with word-finding difficulty, has better overall performance than several other groups but still declines on harder forms. || **Broca’s aphasia and conduction aphasia** produce a marked decline for object clefts and poorer performance for passives than for the easier forms. **Wernicke’s patients** have low accuracy across all four structures. The groups are not interchangeable in either overall accuracy or sensitivity to sentence form. || Broca’s patients are particularly vulnerable to **noncanonical structures**, forms that depart from the usual actor-first arrangement. Their active-sentence accuracy is also below the healthy level. The pattern therefore combines a general performance reduction with an additional cost for harder grammatical relationships.
18|**Healthy undergraduates** also lose comprehension accuracy when processing is stressed. Conditions include faster or compressed speech, **low-pass filtering**, added noise, and remembering auditory or visual digit lists during sentence interpretation. Low-pass filtering removes higher-frequency information, while a digit-memory task adds a competing demand. || **Object clefts and passives** are especially vulnerable across the manipulations. Active sentences and subject clefts remain relatively easier. Combining stresses, including filtering with compression, produces larger losses than ordinary listening. || These listeners have no aphasic brain injury, yet taxing processing produces a disproportionate cost for difficult structures. A similar aphasia pattern can therefore reflect a **performance limitation**, not uniquely the loss of a grammatical rule. The comparison concerns how much processing demand an intact or injured system can handle.
19|**Saygin and colleagues (2004)** asked participants to **match an action with an object**. In a nonverbal condition, an action such as licking is conveyed without spoken language and must be matched to something such as an ice cream. A linguistic condition supplies language-based information for the matching decision. || Aphasic participants are less accurate in both conditions. Their accuracy is about **93.4% for nonlinguistic items and 87.5% for linguistic items**, compared with **98.5% and 99.4%** for controls. Difficulties therefore extend beyond interpreting spoken sentences. || **Semantically related distractors** are more disruptive than unrelated alternatives. A wrong object related in meaning competes more with the correct action–object match. The task tests selecting the relevant meaning even when no grammatical sentence is required.
20|Poorer **nonverbal action matching** is significantly associated with **damage to Broca’s area**. Participants must interpret a pantomimed action and choose the fitting object. The task does not require producing a spoken sentence or extracting the grammar of a heard sentence. || The lesion–performance relationship connects frontal damage with a comprehension demand outside language. A person can struggle to recover the relevant action meaning even when linguistic forms have been removed from the input. || The proposed deficit therefore has to account for action interpretation as well as grammatical findings. An association with Broca’s area does not, by itself, restrict every affected operation to syntax. The behavioral task identifies what information the patient has to use, while the lesion analysis identifies where damage is related to poorer performance.
21|**Saygin and colleagues** compared matching **environmental sounds to pictures** with matching spoken descriptions. Hearing a cow moo requires selecting its picture in the nonverbal condition. Hearing **“cow mooing”** supplies language instead. Alternatives can be meaning-related, such as another animal, or unrelated, such as a violin. || Verbal and nonverbal accuracy is about **99.2% and 98.1% for controls** and **99.1% and 98.2% after right-hemisphere damage**. The corresponding values are **98.6% and 96.7% for anomic aphasia** and **96.3% and 95.2% for Broca’s aphasia**. These comparisons retain high performance in both versions. || **Wernicke’s aphasia** has the lowest accuracy, about **90.0% verbal and 91.3% nonverbal**. The verbal and nonverbal versions do not differ significantly. The difficulty therefore extends to retrieving meaning from environmental sounds, not only recognizing spoken words. Selecting a picture requires connecting either kind of auditory input with what it represents.
22|Deficits in **verbal and nonverbal sound–picture matching** are associated with damage involving **Wernicke’s area**. Patients impaired on the environmental-sound task and patients impaired on the verbal task have overlap in posterior language-related regions. The input differs, but both tasks require connecting a sound with meaning. || A spoken description supplies words, while an environmental sound supplies information from an event such as an animal call. Poor performance across both types indicates that the impairment cannot be described solely as failure to recognize linguistic word forms. || The anatomical comparison connects the distribution of damaged tissue with a measured behavioral difficulty. Posterior damage is associated with problems in auditory comprehension that extend beyond language. The relevant common demand is relating meaningful auditory information to the correct object or event.
23|The **language specificity of aphasia deficits** remains contested. Some patients are especially impaired on particular grammatical constructions. Some also struggle to interpret actions or environmental sounds without language. A complete account must accommodate both the specific linguistic vulnerabilities and the broader task difficulties. || **Controlled behavioral tests** quantify performance under conditions with different demands. They can distinguish word selection, grammatical-role assignment, action matching, and auditory meaning, rather than treating comprehension as one undifferentiated ability. || **Lesion analyses** relate those performance measures to the location of damage. A proposed structure–function relationship has to explain which operation the task requires and why damage in a particular region is associated with reduced success. Fluency or a broad diagnostic label alone does not provide that level of explanation.

25|**Voxel-based lesion–symptom mapping** relates damaged brain locations to behavioral impairment across patients. A **voxel** is a small three-dimensional unit in an MRI image. Comparing damaged and spared voxels with symptom measures tests which locations are associated with particular difficulties. || **Fluency deficits** are more strongly associated with anterior, or front, regions. **Comprehension deficits** are more strongly associated with posterior, or back, regions. These are broad measures of speaking and understanding, rather than direct tests of one grammatical rule. || The pattern supports a rough **double dissociation**. Different damage distributions have different associations with production- and comprehension-related performance. MRI-based mapping makes the relationship measurable across patients instead of relying only on a single case and a broad description of its symptoms.
