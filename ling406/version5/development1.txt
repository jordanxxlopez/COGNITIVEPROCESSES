2|The **nature-and-nurture question** asks how experience and built-in capacities contribute to language acquisition. **Nativist positions** developed from **Chomsky’s critique of Skinner**. Skinner’s behaviorist approach relied on general learning mechanisms such as reward and punishment, while Chomsky argued that these mechanisms could not explain the grammar children acquire. || For Chomsky, the important feature of language is **generativity**. Speakers can understand and produce new sentences they have never heard before. **Acquiring a language means identifying the abstract grammar that produces its sentences**, rather than memorizing a list of earlier utterances. || Grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are not **similar in any straightforward way**. A small change can make a sentence ungrammatical, while two grammatical sentences can look very different. Grouping sentences by surface resemblance does not directly separate everything the grammar allows from everything it excludes. || **Operant conditioning** changes behavior through rewards and punishments. Children are not consistently rewarded for grammatical sentences and punished for ungrammatical ones, so the required **reward/punishment contingencies** do not reliably occur during acquisition. || Nativist approaches propose **Universal Grammar**, innate knowledge about what languages can be like, and **language-specific acquisition processes**. Built-in constraints restrict possible grammars, while learning identifies the surrounding language’s grammar. Chomsky linked children’s rapid acquisition of comparably complex grammars to a specially equipped **“data-handling” or “hypothesis-formulating” ability**.
3|Early theories treated **categorical perception** as **language-specific and species-specific** because it was then known mainly for human speech. Language-specific means restricted to language, and species-specific means restricted to a particular species. The proposal was that speech categories reflected a specialized human-language system. || Synthetic syllables change in equal steps along a **formant-transition continuum**, but listeners change labels abruptly among **/b/, /d/, and /g/**. The physical steps remain equal while their perceived identities divide into groups. Similarity is therefore partly organized by the language categories rather than acoustic distance alone. || **Discrimination is better near category boundaries** than inside a category. This relationship appears when sounds are compared one, two, or three acoustic steps apart. Increasing the distance can improve performance, but a fixed distance is also easier to hear when it crosses a phoneme boundary.
4|The **conditioned head turn procedure** measures an infant’s detection of a sound change through an orienting response. An auditory event predicts a rewarding visual event, and the infant learns to turn toward its source. Detecting the auditory difference can therefore be recorded without a spoken judgment. || **High-amplitude sucking** uses a different behavior. Sucking can decline as a sound is repeated and becomes familiar. If changing that sound renews sucking, the infant has responded to a difference in the auditory input. || The procedures measure speech discrimination before infants can name or pronounce the tested sounds. A head turn and a sucking response are not phoneme labels, but they provide measurable evidence of sensitivity. The experiment controls what changes in the sound and examines how the infant’s behavior changes with it.
5|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** varied **voice onset time**, the delay from stop-consonant release to vocal-fold vibration. Infants first heard repeated synthetic syllables, and sucking declined during repetition. A controlled sound change then tested whether responding recovered. || In **20D**, VOT changed by **20 milliseconds**, from **+20 to +40 milliseconds**, crossing an adult phoneme boundary. Sucking increased again. The infant responded to the difference even though no verbal labeling response was required. >> In **20S**, the same 20-millisecond difference stayed within one adult category. The pairs were **−20 to 0** or **+60 to +80 milliseconds**, and sucking recovered much less. >> In the **0 control**, the sound remained unchanged and responding continued to decline. || The two kinds of change had equal physical size. Their different effects therefore connect infant sensitivity with the category relationship, rather than only the amount of VOT change. Category-related discrimination is present before mature speech production.
6|**Categorical perception** was proposed as a way of turning speech into a **symbolic representation** that language processes could use. A stable sound identity could be recovered from variable acoustic input. Finding this organization very early was taken as possible evidence of an innate speech-processing ability. || At about **four months**, infants are still developing basic motor skills, including using two hands to manipulate objects and holding up their head and chest while lying prone. Color vision and general visual acuity are also still developing. These are broad developmental comparisons, not the speech-discrimination measure itself. || Vocal production is only beginning to include more vowel-like sounds rather than creaky whines. Category-related perception therefore precedes mature production. The early timing contributed to the innateness interpretation, because the infants could respond systematically to speech differences long before speaking in an adult-like way.
7|**Perceptual narrowing** is a developmental reduction in sensitivity to some sound distinctions. Around six months, English-learning infants can distinguish certain contrasts outside their own language. Some of those differences become harder to hear as experience tunes the system to the language used around them. || **Werker and Tees** compared discrimination of **Hindi and Salish contrasts**. Performance is relatively strong at **6–8 months**, lower at **8–10 months**, and much lower at **10–12 months** for English-learning infants. Infants with relevant Hindi or Salish experience retain stronger discrimination. || The developmental change is therefore related to which sound system is experienced. A distinction that can be detected early does not necessarily remain equally available. Adapting to native-language categories changes the differences to which perception stays sensitive.
8|The **“citizens of the world” account** proposes that infants begin with broad, innate knowledge about speech. **Language-general** means that the starting knowledge is not limited to one particular native language. Categorical perception is treated as an early capacity of the language system itself. || Learning then **selects among innately specified contrasts**. The child’s experience identifies which of the available distinctions matter for the surrounding language. The result is a system tuned to that language rather than equally sensitive to every possible contrast. || Under this account, the main developmental change is narrowing a broad initial inventory. Experience preserves or emphasizes useful distinctions instead of having to build every category from an initially undifferentiated signal. This is a proposal about both the starting abilities and the work performed by learning.
9|**Categorical perception occurs beyond language and beyond humans**. Nonspeech sounds can be organized categorically, and animals without human language can produce similar patterns. The behavior therefore does not establish a speech-only mechanism by itself. || The pattern is also **task-dependent**. Naming a stimulus and detecting a difference require different responses, so the form of the task can affect how categorical performance looks. A final label is not a direct record of every uncertainty during processing. || **Infant perception is not uniformly universal**. Some distinctions begin weak and become easier with relevant experience. Development must therefore include gains in sensitivity as well as narrowing. An account that only removes distinctions from a complete starting set leaves those learning-related improvements unexplained.
10|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** tested **categorical perception of nonspeech sounds** by varying **rise time**. Rise time is how long a sound takes to build toward a stronger amplitude. A fast onset sounds more **plucked**, and a slower onset sounds more **bowed**. || The materials include **sawtooth and sine waves**. In the sawtooth continuum, pluck labels decline and bow labels increase across the **0–80 millisecond** range. The sounds differ in an auditory property rather than a phoneme contrast. || Discrimination is strongest near the labeling transition around the middle of the range. Equal changes in rise time are not equally easy to distinguish at every position. The relationship between category labels and sensitivity can therefore arise in ordinary auditory organization without the input containing speech.
11|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** measured infant responses to **nonspeech sound changes**. Sucking declined during repeated exposure, and the stimulus then either stayed the same, changed within a perceptual category, or changed between categories. The conditions distinguish repetition from two kinds of difference. || After **no change** and a **within-category change**, sucking stayed relatively low. A **between-category change** renewed responding before it declined again. The infant’s sensitivity depended on which side of a perceptual boundary the sounds occupied. || This category-related response occurs early and for nonspeech material. Its early developmental appearance is therefore compatible with a broader auditory ability. The result separates evidence for early categorical organization from evidence that the organization operates only on language.
12|Chinchillas learned to respond through **operant conditioning**, with food rewarding the trained behavior. A **0-millisecond-lag** or **80-millisecond-lag** speech sound served as an **SD**, a discriminative stimulus signaling that the response would be rewarded. Training linked an endpoint sound with an outcome. || Intermediate timings tested how the response generalized along a **/ga/–/ka/ VOT continuum**. The response proportion changed sharply over part of the range, broadly resembling the human category pattern. The animal could respond systematically without possessing a human phoneme system. || **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and **Kuhl and Miller (1975)** connect this comparison with speech categorization in nonhuman subjects. Changes in burst and aspiration-noise intensity could shift the boundary or its steepness. Acoustic information and learned responses affected the apparent category organization.
13|**Narayan and colleagues (2010)** compared infant sensitivity to two **nasal place contrasts**. **/ma/–/na/** changes from a bilabial nasal made with the lips to an alveolar nasal near the ridge behind the teeth. **/na/–/ŋa/** changes from that alveolar location to a velar location farther back. || At **4–5 months**, English-hearing infants look longer after a change in /ma/–/na/. Their response differs much less between same and change trials for /na/–/ŋa/. The first distinction is more **salient**, meaning easier to notice, at that age. || Broad early speech sensitivity therefore does not mean equal sensitivity to every contrast. The tested /na/–/ŋa/ onset difference is initially difficult even when language experience is considered. An early starting system can contain uneven sensitivities rather than a complete set of equally strong phonetic distinctions.
14|Exposure in a **Filipino language environment** increases the salience of the native **/na/–/ŋa/** contrast between roughly **6 and 10 months**. Filipino-learning infants at **6–8 months** have similar looking times for same and change trials. At **10–12 months**, looking is longer after the change. || The distinction was weak early, rather than already strong and merely preserved. English-hearing infants also have weak early sensitivity to this contrast despite clearer sensitivity to /ma/–/na/. The relevant language environment is associated with a later gain in discrimination. || **Experience can strengthen a speech distinction**. Learning is therefore not restricted to narrowing an initially complete inventory. For some contrasts, exposure helps establish an effective distinction that was not readily perceived at the beginning.
15|**Emergentist approaches** propose that language patterns can develop through learning from the structure of experience. **Associative learning** is sensitive to events that repeatedly occur together or predict one another. Statistical regularities in the environment change the connections that guide a response. || In conditioning, food produces a response such as salivation. When a bell is repeatedly paired with food, the connection can strengthen until the bell also produces the response. The learning depends on the relationship between experienced events. || Applying this idea to **pattern recognition** means learning which stimulus properties tend to go together. Repeated acoustic relationships can affect how speech sounds become grouped. The categories are shaped by the distribution of the input rather than each having to be fully specified in advance.
16|A **vowel space** locates vowels using **F1 and F2**, the first and second resonant frequencies of the vocal tract. Repeated productions do not occupy one exact point. Each vowel forms a distribution, and the distributions of different vowels can overlap. || In the account associated with **Kuhl and others**, listeners learn regularities in these experienced distributions. Frequently encountered combinations provide more support than rare ones. Learning concerns the pattern of many sound tokens rather than memorizing one perfect recording. || A **prototypical /a/** can reflect a central or especially frequent part of the /a/ distribution. A **mean** is an average location, and a **mode** is the most frequent value or region. The prototype can therefore be learned from what the listener hears, rather than assigned one universal acoustic value before experience.
17|**Maye and colleagues** gave infants passive exposure to different **VOT distributions**. A **bimodal distribution** has two concentrations of examples and supports two categories. A **unimodal distribution** concentrates examples near the middle and supports one category. The difference concerns how often sounds occur at different points in the range. || At test, **stimuli 3 and 6** were either alternated or repeated without alternation. The physical test sounds were the same for both exposure groups. Any group difference therefore concerns the earlier distribution rather than a larger test contrast. >> Six-month-old **unimodal** infants looked for **4.85 seconds alternating and 4.53 seconds non-alternating**. The eight-month-old values were **4.98 and 5.20 seconds**. Neither age group reliably differentiated these conditions. >> Six-month-old **bimodal** infants had values of **5.66 and 6.41 seconds**. Eight-month-olds had **5.45 and 6.15 seconds**. Only the bimodal-exposure groups were sensitive to the difference between conditions. || Exposure to two clusters supported treating the tokens differently, while exposure to one central cluster supported treating them as members of one category. The distribution of experience affected how the same later sounds were organized perceptually.
18|**Social interaction changes the effects of speech exposure**. Nine-month-old American infants received **twelve 25-minute sessions** and were tested on a Mandarin contrast with a head-turn procedure. Live interaction with a Mandarin speaker produced better performance than English exposure. Television and audio-only Mandarin did not produce the same benefit. || The conditions all supplied exposure, but the interactive setting changed what was learned. Hearing another language through a recording did not have the same consequence as taking part in a live exchange with its speaker. || In a separate manipulation, maternal responses were **contingent**, timed to the infant’s vocalization, or **noncontingent**, delivered at unrelated times. Contingent feedback increased syllable-like vocalizations more strongly. || Social responses can therefore reinforce vocal behavior. Proposed accounts include an innate attraction to social interaction and an early ability to use other people’s cues. The social timing and context of input can affect what the learner does with the acoustic information.
19|**Statistical learning with constraints** means that not every acoustic difference becomes a new language category. A **contrastive property** helps distinguish language units. Differences in speaker identity or speaking rate change a sound without necessarily changing its phoneme identity. || **Earlier learning** also shapes later learning. An established perceptual pattern affects which new regularities are easy to notice and acquire. The learner does not approach every new input with exactly the same starting organization. || **Social and interactive context** affects how the input is used. The same sounds can occur during a responsive exchange or through a recording with no interaction. Learning therefore depends on which properties are treated as relevant, what has already been learned, and the conditions under which the sounds are experienced.
