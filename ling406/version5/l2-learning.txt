2|**Adults often learn a second language faster at first than children do**. They already have a first language and more developed general cognitive skills. Early learning can use those established resources instead of beginning from the developmental state of an infant. || An **eighteen-month-old using about twenty words** is still developing pointing, pretend play, simple questions, spatial understanding, and vocal patterns. Adult learners already have many of those abilities. The comparison therefore involves different starting resources, not only different ages exposed to the same instruction. || **Ultimate attainment** is the level eventually reached. Adults are less likely to become fully native-like despite their faster initial progress. Their existing language can support new learning while also affecting it through transfer. Initial learning rate and final proficiency therefore answer different questions about age and acquisition.
3|Some language milestones involve **general conceptual knowledge** as well as language itself. Understanding spatial relations between objects or representing an event is different from knowing the words a particular language uses for them. The concepts of in and out can be available before their new labels are learned. || A later **second-language learner** already has many of these concepts. Learning can concentrate on how another language expresses familiar relationships. The learner does not have to discover every event or spatial distinction from the beginning. || The **first language** also supplies established categories and patterns. These can help with some new forms and interfere with others. Adult learning therefore combines a conceptual advantage with the influence of a language system that is already organized.
4|**Transfer** is using first-language, or **L1**, processes and representations while learning or using a second language, **L2**. An established way of perceiving speech, organizing a sentence, or retrieving a word can be applied to the new language. Prior knowledge continues to influence the task. || **Positive transfer** occurs when that organization fits what L2 requires. The existing pattern supplies a helpful starting point. **Negative transfer** occurs when applying it favors a form or interpretation that does not fit the new language. || Transfer is therefore not inherently helpful or harmful. Its effect depends on the relationship between the two languages and the operation required. A learned distinction can support an L2 contrast, while a learned grouping can make two L2 categories seem too similar.
5|Hindi distinguishes **dental and retroflex consonants**, while native English listeners often place both in an English **alveolar** category. Dental articulation involves the teeth. Alveolar articulation is near the ridge behind them, while retroflex articulation uses a farther-back tongue configuration. || Hindi **minimal pairs**, words differing in one speech sound, can distinguish meanings such as **pressure versus a kind of fruit** or **a saw versus crooked**. The consonant difference changes word identity for a native listener. Treating the two sounds alike loses that distinction. || English learning creates **acquired similarity** among sounds assigned to one category. Reusing that category for both Hindi sounds makes them less distinct perceptually. Negative transfer can therefore make a new contrast difficult because the learner’s existing system has already learned to group its members together.
6|A **classifier** is a grammatical form selected in relation to a noun. Chinese requires classifier choices in expressions where English uses forms such as **a chair, that dog, the car, or one book** without an equivalent choice. The learner must attend to properties that English grammar did not require for this operation. || **Gè** is a general classifier. **Bǎ** is used with handled objects, and **běn** with books and related objects. **Kuài** applies to pieces, **liàng** to wheeled vehicles, and **tái** to devices or gadgets. >> **Xiē** expresses an indeterminate plural quantity. **Zhāng** is used with flat objects, and **zhī** with long thin objects. Semantic and shape properties help determine the form accompanying the noun. || English speakers have learned to use their language without selecting these forms. Acquiring the classifier system therefore requires making previously ignorable properties relevant to a grammatical decision. Knowing the object’s meaning alone does not automatically supply its classifier.
7|**Grammatical gender** classifies nouns and controls related grammatical forms. It is different from social or cultural gender. A book is not socially male because French **livre** is masculine, and liberty is not socially female because **liberté** is feminine. || **Agreement** means coordinating forms such as pronouns and adjectives with the noun’s class. A learner has to make those choices even when the referent provides no obvious gender cue. Similar meanings can also have different classes, as with **auto/voiture, rivière/fleuve, and vélo/bicyclette**. || **Word endings** supply statistical information. The examples include masculine-associated **-age, -r, -t, and -sme**, and feminine-associated **-ie, -ion, -ité/-tié, -ance, -me, -mme, and -lle**. These are form-based tendencies in the examples rather than descriptions of the objects’ identities. || Learning gender therefore involves the noun’s lexical and form properties as well as coordinating agreement. Transferring an expectation that meaning alone determines the choice can leave those grammatical requirements unexplained.
8|**Cognates** resemble one another because they share a historical origin. **Loan words** enter a language through borrowing. Both can make a new word partly familiar to someone who already knows a related form in another language. || French **auto, rivière, and bicyclette** resemble familiar forms connected with a car, a river, and a bicycle. Japanese **コロッケ, korokke**, names a croquette, and **モノレール, monoreru**, names a monorail through adapted borrowed forms. The pronunciation and spelling can be changed while some recognizable overlap remains. || A familiar form–meaning connection can support learning the new label. This is **positive transfer** in vocabulary. The learner has some existing information to connect with the L2 form instead of building an entirely unfamiliar association from scratch.
9|A **false friend** is a similar form that encourages the wrong meaning in another language. Similarity alone does not establish that two words share a meaning or are cognates. An apparently helpful familiar form can therefore lead vocabulary learning in the wrong direction. || Spanish **embarazado** concerns pregnancy rather than the English meaning of embarrassed. **“Arnold esta embarazado”** does not express ordinary embarrassment. Choosing the familiar-looking form without checking its meaning creates the error. || An L2 input can strongly activate a similar L1 word. When the meanings match, that overlap helps. When they do not, the same reliance on resemblance supports a false interpretation. The effect depends on both form overlap and the actual relationship between meanings.
10|**De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** used **paired-associate learning** to test how existing vocabulary affects new-word learning. Participants learned Dutch words paired with made-up forms. They were tested again after **one week**, allowing immediate learning and later retention to be compared. || **Gitaar–gettar**, meaning guitar, is **concrete and cognate-like**. **Tijger–duunze**, meaning tiger, is concrete without the form resemblance. **Domein–domaan**, meaning domain, is **abstract and cognate-like**, while **herstel–groop**, meaning repair, is abstract and non-cognate. || **Concreteness** distinguishes readily imaginable referents from abstract meanings. **Cognate-like status** distinguishes similar forms from dissimilar ones. Crossing the factors tests form resemblance within both kinds of meaning. || Learning requires linking an unfamiliar form to its correct familiar partner. Similarity supplies partial form support, but a successful association still has to be encoded and later retrieved. The design separates that support from whether the referent is concrete or abstract.
11|**Cognate-like associates were learned faster and retained better**. For concrete items, Session 1 recall was **69.7% for cognates and 43.7% for non-cognates**. By Session 3, the values reached **93.4% and 92.0%**. Practice allowed the initially weaker condition to approach the other in immediate accuracy. || After **one week without training**, Session 4 concrete recall was **78.4% versus 69.0%**. Abstract recall was **68.4% versus 36.0%**, compared with **86.0% versus 72.7%** immediately before the delay. The similarity advantage remained visible after the interruption. || **Response times** also favored cognates. Session 4 concrete responses averaged **1,833 versus 2,597 milliseconds**, and abstract responses **2,333 versus 3,412 milliseconds**. Faster retrieval accompanied the accuracy benefit. || Form similarity therefore supported both early learning and later retrieval. Reaching a similar immediate accuracy with practice did not make every condition equally well retained or equally fast. Accuracy and retrieval time capture different aspects of the learned association.
12|A **receptive vocabulary test** gives the unfamiliar associate and asks for its known Dutch partner. A similar-looking associate can help the participant recognize or guess the familiar word. Correct performance in that direction alone would therefore leave uncertainty about how well the new form had been learned. || The concrete cognate pairs include **broer–breur, brood–brodde, gitaar–gettar, jongen–jange, lepel–lippel, lichaam–lechem, naald–nadel, and paraplu–parredu**. The remaining pairs are **rivier–riffer, schaar–skare, stier–strire, trein–trien, varken–vark, viool–vialo, and vrouw–vrauk**. Their form overlap supplies information about the intended known word. || A **productive or expressive test** reverses the direction. The participant must retrieve and produce the unfamiliar learned form from the Dutch word. Success then requires the new associate itself, rather than only finding a familiar meaning from a resemblance. Testing both directions makes the learning claim more specific.
13|In **productive recall**, concrete performance fell from **92.7% immediately to 73.7% after a week**. Abstract performance fell from **79.3% to 52.2%**. Across concreteness conditions, cognates declined from **89.7% to 73.3%**, while non-cognates declined from **82.3% to 52.5%**. || **Receptive recall** was higher overall. Concrete scores declined from **97.7% to 88.9%**, and abstract scores from **91.5% to 64.9%**. Cognates declined from **98.5% to 90.7%**, while non-cognates declined from **90.7% to 63.0%**. || Producing an unfamiliar form and retrieving its familiar partner make different demands on memory. The delay reduces performance in both directions. Better cognate retention also occurs in both, including the direction requiring the new form to be generated. The effect therefore cannot be reduced entirely to guessing a familiar word from a similar input.
14|**Cross-language competition** concerns whether a word in one language becomes active while another language is being used. The activated word does not have to be an allowed or intended response. Its partial activation can still influence processing before a final choice. || **Marian and Spivey (2003)** tested whether similar **L1 words** are active during **L2 recognition**. A visual-world display supplied objects whose names could overlap with the spoken target across languages. Eye movements measured which alternatives attracted looking. || A look toward an object with a similar L1 name provides evidence about an unintended competitor. The task does not require participants deliberately to translate the target. The question concerns which word representations receive activation during recognition in the designated language.
15|**Eye movements** can reveal temporary lexical competition before a final response. In **McMurray and colleagues’ task**, participants saw **bear, pear, lamp, and ship** while hearing a /b/–/p/ continuum. Bear and pear provided closely related word alternatives. || **VOT**, the timing of voicing after consonant release, changed how ambiguous the input was. Competitor looking depended on the sound’s distance from the category boundary. A listener could look toward the rival picture and still make the correct final selection. || A completed click therefore does not describe the entire recognition process. Measuring where the eyes move identifies candidates considered while the input is being interpreted. This method can test competition from a word even when that word never becomes the final answer.
16|A **cohort competitor** shares the beginning of a spoken target. For **beaker**, **beetle** matches the early sounds, **speaker** overlaps at the ending, and **carriage** is unrelated. These alternatives receive useful matching information at different times. || In **Allopenna and colleagues’ (1994) task**, target and cohort looks rise early because the beginning fits both beaker and beetle. As later input distinguishes them, looks to the target continue increasing while cohort looks decline. **Rhyme competition** follows a different, later time course. || Spoken recognition therefore changes as matching and conflicting information arrives. Temporary support for a losing candidate can be visible before the final choice. The location of the sound overlap within the word helps determine when the competitor participates.
17|**Marian and Spivey** tested **cross-language cohort competition** in Russian–English bilinguals. English **speaker** begins similarly to Russian **спички, spichki**, meaning matches. During an English instruction, the picture of matches can receive support through its Russian name even though matches does not resemble speaker in English. || **Spear** provides an English within-language competitor. Displays can contain that competitor, the Russian-name competitor, or both. The arrangement separates competition within the task language from competition arising through the other language. || Other pairs are **boot with buben**, a tambourine, **chair with cherepaha**, a turtle, and **marker with marka**, a stamp. Looking toward one of those objects during English recognition connects the effect with its other-language form. The overlap is phonological, concerning the sound of the name, rather than similarity in the objects’ meanings.
18|During **L2 recognition**, a sole within-language competitor and a sole between-language competitor each attracted looks on about **18% of trials**. A Russian-name alternative could therefore compete while the participant was following English input. The final response language did not prevent all activation across languages. || When **both competitors** were present, within-language looks occurred on about **19% of trials** and between-language looks on about **13%**. The unrelated filler attracted looks on about **7%**. The two competitors had different strengths when directly available together. || Within-language competition was stronger in that combined display, but the cross-language object still attracted more looks than the control. The result concerns temporary activation during recognition. It does not require the participant to select the Russian-name object as the intended English target.
19|During **L1 Russian recognition**, a within-language competitor attracted looks on about **14% of trials when alone** and **13% when both competitors were present**. These rates exceeded looking to unrelated control objects. Native-language cohort competition was therefore present. || English-name competitors attracted looks on about **8%**, compared with a control rate near **5%**. That smaller difference was **not statistically significant**. The numerical trend alone did not establish the same reliable cross-language effect. || The reported interaction was **asymmetric**. L1 competitors clearly affected L2 recognition, while L2 competitors did not reliably produce the corresponding effect during L1 recognition. A model must account for the unequal directions rather than assume equally strong activation between the two vocabularies.
20|The **Revised Hierarchical Model**, associated with **Kroll and Stewart**, connects L1 words, L2 words, and shared concepts. **Spreading activation** means that activity in one representation can influence representations connected with it. A lexical connection joins words, while a conceptual connection links a word with meaning. || The **L1 lexicon** is larger and more strongly linked to concepts. A developing **L2 lexicon** has a stronger lexical route toward L1 than L1 has toward L2. Early learning emphasizes using an L1 counterpart to understand the new word. || Unequal connection strengths produce unequal cross-language effects. Activation can spread more readily from an L2 word to L1 than in the reverse direction. Both languages belong to one person, but the model does not assume their routes to words and meanings are identical in strength.
21|In the **Revised Hierarchical Model**, the visible **matches** object activates its concept. That concept partially activates the Russian word for matches. The Russian word can then send support back to the same conceptual representation, strengthening an unintended competitor during the English task. || The strengthened concept contributes to looking toward the matches picture. The target word is **speaker**, while the competing object is supported through **spichki**. Their relationship is sound overlap across languages, not a shared meaning of speakers and matches. || The model of **Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** explains the directional difference through unequal connections. In the L1 task, routes involving the weaker L2 system may not produce equally reliable interference. The mechanism concerns the strength and direction of lexical and conceptual activation, rather than deliberate translation into the other language.
22|A **balanced bilingual** has relatively strong proficiency in both languages. The model does not predict that this person becomes **“two monolinguals in one.”** Strong conceptual connections can keep both lexical systems involved rather than isolating them. || **Competitive dynamics** means that activated alternatives influence which representation wins. **Inhibition** helps reduce alternatives that are inappropriate for the current task. These control demands can remain when both languages are highly proficient because the systems share concepts and interacting pathways. || This prediction motivated work on **nonlinguistic cognitive-control tasks**. The immediate model claim concerns interacting language representations and their competition. Equal proficiency does not mean that one language can always be processed with no influence from the other.
23|One language history begins with **L1**, adds **L2**, and continues using both. The first language keeps receiving input and practice after second-language learning begins. Both systems have continuing opportunities to be activated. || Another history adds L2 and then **stops using L1**. The earlier language no longer receives the same ongoing support. Later performance can therefore depend on both the age at which a language was learned and what happened to its use afterward. || These histories differ in exposure after the second language begins. Knowing that a language was learned first does not establish that it stayed available in the environment. A comparison of current ability must distinguish an early start from continued maintenance of that early language.
24|**Pallier and colleagues** tested adults who had been adopted into French homes from Korean orphanages. They were **20–32 years old** when tested and had been adopted between **3 and 8 years of age**. Early Korean experience was followed by a French-dominant environment. || The research asked whether any **residual Korean knowledge** was detectable. Residual knowledge means an influence of the earlier experience that survives the later change in language use. It can be investigated even when the person no longer uses Korean in everyday life. || French speakers without that childhood history provided a comparison. A retained benefit could appear as better performance on Korean-related measures. The question concerns the persistence and accessibility of early learning after a major shift in the language environment.
25|Korean has a **three-way stop contrast** among **aspirated, plain, and tense** consonants. Stops involve a closure and release. The three categories can occur at the same place of articulation, so their distinction concerns how the stop is produced rather than only where the closure occurs. || The **bilabial series** includes forms meaning **grass, fire, and horn**. The **dental series** includes **mask, moon, and daughter**, while the **velar series** includes **nose, music, and exactly** in the materials. Different categories identify different words within each series. || The **laryngeal contrast** concerns the configuration and timing associated with the voice-producing system. Retaining Korean knowledge would require separating these categories, not just distinguishing a lip closure from a tongue closure. A first language without the same three-way division supplies a different starting organization for those sounds.
26|**Ventureyra and colleagues** compared Korean adoptees in France with French and Korean listeners. Tests included **plain–tense, plain–aspirated, and tense–aspirated** stop differences. Vowel differences and **same trials** also tested auditory judgments under other conditions. || **Native Korean listeners** detected the Korean consonant distinctions much more successfully. Adoptees were no better on those contrasts than monolingual French listeners, despite the adoptees’ early Korean exposure. The test did not detect a retained phonetic advantage. || Vowel and same-trial performance differed from the difficult consonant conditions. The poor stop-contrast result was therefore not simply inability to perform any auditory judgment. The finding concerns current performance on the tested distinctions after the language-environment change.
27|**Pallier and colleagues** looked for remaining Korean knowledge using several tasks. In **language identification**, adoptees judged whether sentences were Korean rather than **Japanese, Polish, Swedish, or Wolof**. Their performance did not reliably exceed that of monolingual French listeners. || **Word recognition** required selecting Korean translations of French words. **Speech-segment detection** required deciding whether a short excerpt had occurred inside a recorded sentence. Neither task produced a dependable adoptee advantage. || The tasks targeted different possible remnants, including language familiarity, vocabulary, and sensitivity to sound sequences. Across these tests, earlier Korean exposure did not provide a detectable current behavioral benefit. That statement concerns the measures used to access the earlier learning, rather than a direct observation of every connection left in the brain.
28|Hearing **French** activated the adoptees’ broad **language network**, including **superior temporal and inferior frontal regions**. These regions are associated with processing their current dominant language. Their brain responses were therefore not absent or generally unresponsive to speech. || Comparisons involved French alongside **Polish, Japanese, and Korean**. The analysis assessed the response to the familiar language relative to other speech. Subtracting the Korean response from the French response did not reveal the distinguishing group difference expected from a readily accessible Korean advantage. || The reported imaging result agreed with the absence of a detectable behavioral benefit under those conditions. The contrast concerns the measured neural response to current language processing. It does not directly count every possible remnant of earlier exposure.
29|A first language is represented through **connections within and between brain regions**. Those pathways are affected by experience and continued activation. The language is not a single object placed in a storage location independently of use. || If **L1 stops being activated**, L2-related connections can become dominant in the same broad system. **Competitive dynamics** describes this changing balance among pathways. Increasing support for one pattern can reduce the functional influence of another. || The proposal resembles **experience-dependent competition between visual inputs**. Reduced input can change which connections remain effective. Apparent language replacement can therefore reflect a changed balance of activation and support, rather than requiring a complete language object simply to be moved or discarded.
30|**Residual knowledge** is a surviving effect of earlier language experience. It can exist without an obvious advantage on ordinary current-performance tests. Being unable to use an old language readily and having no remaining influence from it are different claims. || **Relearning** gives the earlier forms new opportunities to be activated. That renewed experience can reveal an influence not apparent before training. A past language history may affect how readily the person acquires the forms again. || Current access and later learning therefore measure different consequences of the earlier experience. A test of present recognition asks what can be used now. A relearning comparison asks whether renewed input benefits from something retained from the past.
31|**Au and colleagues** studied undergraduates who often overheard **Spanish in childhood** but did not learn to speak it then. They began classroom Spanish around **age fourteen** and were tested after a **second-year college course**. Early listening and later active instruction were separate parts of their history. || Pronunciation was more native-like than in typical late learners. For initial voiceless stops, mean **VOT** was about **19.6 milliseconds for overhearers**, **19.3 for native speakers**, and **36.2 for typical late learners**. VOT measures the timing of voicing relative to stop release. || **Accent ratings and lenition**, the weakening of certain consonant articulations, also favored overhearers on several measures. Childhood slang knowledge remained well below native levels. Earlier hearing therefore benefited some aspects of pronunciation without creating uniformly native-like language ability. || In morphology and grammaticality tests, overhearers and typical late learners were much closer to each other than to native speakers. Different language components retained different benefits. The effect of early exposure has to be described by the particular ability measured, not only an overall label of knowing Spanish.
32|When **L1 disappears from the environment**, L2 can appear to replace it completely in current behavior. The earlier language may no longer produce an obvious advantage in recognition or identification. That observation concerns what the person can access under those testing conditions. || **Residual knowledge** can become available through renewed learning. Current inability does not settle whether earlier experience can affect a later training outcome. The measurement changes when practice is reintroduced. || Korean-adoptee tests concern accessibility after a major language shift. Childhood-overhearer tests include performance after renewed formal instruction. The histories therefore provide different opportunities for early knowledge to contribute. Their outcomes should not be treated as the same test of an identical exposure pattern.
33|**Korean is rarely encountered in France**, whereas **Spanish is widely available in Los Angeles**. A childhood language can remain socially present through family and community even when the learner does not speak it. Incidental exposure therefore differs between these histories. || The language pairs also differ in **cognates and shared writing systems**. Cognates provide related forms that can support learning, while shared writing conventions create another kind of familiarity. Later success can reflect these influences as well as the original childhood exposure. || Comparing residual knowledge requires distinguishing the **duration, form, and social context of experience**. Continued hearing, renewed instruction, and cross-language similarities can all affect performance. The fact that two groups encountered a language early is not enough to make all of their later learning conditions equivalent.
