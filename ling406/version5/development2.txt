2|**Word segmentation** is finding words within continuous speech. There are no physical boundaries that consistently separate every word. Listeners recover familiar word forms from a changing **“sea of sounds”** without receiving a space-like marker at each division. || A brief silence can occur inside a word because the speaker closes part of the vocal tract to produce a consonant. Silence therefore cannot always be treated as the end of a word. The same acoustic event can occur at different kinds of position. || Recognizing a word requires organizing the sound stream into units despite that uncertainty. Segmentation concerns where the sound form begins and ends. Finding a recurring unit is a distinct problem from already knowing what that unit means.
3|Some words occur **in isolation**, such as **“Hello,” “No,” “Please,” “Go,” and “Stop.”** Those utterances make a word-sized form relatively clear. Many other words are heard mainly in connected speech, so isolated exposure cannot explain all word segmentation. || **Phonotactics** concerns which sound combinations are permitted or common in a language. The joins in **wordboundary** and **speechcode** can contain sequences unusual inside one word. **Movement** cautions that an apparent internal join does not necessarily separate two whole words. || **Stress** is another grouping cue. Many English words begin with a strong syllable. **Jusczyk and colleagues (1999)** familiarized **7.5-month-olds** with **kingdom**, stressed initially, or **device**, stressed on the second syllable. Infants preferred passages containing kingdom but did not show the same preference for device. || The passages included **“Your kingdom is in a faraway place”** and **“sail to that kingdom,”** with device likewise embedded in sentences. The comparison concerns detecting a familiar word in running speech. The position of stress can make one candidate boundary easier to use than another.
4|In **pretty baby**, the first syllable of pretty strongly predicts its second syllable. The transition from the end of pretty to the beginning of baby crosses a word boundary and is less predictable. Words can therefore contain more reliable syllable transitions than the sequences joining neighboring words. || **Transitional probability** is the chance of a particular next syllable given the syllable just heard. It compares how often the pair occurs with how often the first syllable occurs. A frequent pair is not necessarily highly predictable if its first syllable also has many other continuations. || **f** means frequency of occurrence. The comparison is \[\frac{f(\mathrm{pretty})}{f(\mathrm{pre})}\gg\frac{f(\mathrm{tyba})}{f(\mathrm{ty})}\] The left ratio asks how reliably pre leads to the rest of pretty. The right ratio asks how reliably ty leads into ba. The much stronger within-word relationship supplies evidence that pretty belongs together as a unit.
5|The **likelihood of syllable B following syllable A** tends to be greater within a word than across a word boundary. This makes predictability a useful cue for grouping. It does not put a physical separator into the sound or guarantee one interpretation in every case. || **“What’s that in the road a/head?”** allows competing divisions around a and head. **“I scream, you scream, we all scream for ice cream”** contains similar sound sequences grouped into different words. A listener must decide how the sounds belong together. || **Low transitional probability** can support a possible boundary, while stronger internal transitions support a recurring unit. These are probabilistic relationships. The acoustic sequence and learned patterns can support more than one segmentation, so a cue changes the evidence rather than replacing the entire interpretation process.
6|**Saffran and colleagues (1996)** used an **artificial language** to isolate statistical information in word segmentation. The language contained three-syllable units such as **tokibu, gikoba, and gopila**. Its construction allowed sequence regularities to be tested without the usual mix of other cues in natural speech. || The stream was **monotone**, with no systematic pitch, duration, or amplitude changes marking words. Its simple **consonant–vowel syllables** also prevented unusual sound combinations from identifying the intended boundaries. These controls reduced alternative reasons to group the syllables. || The syllables occurred continuously with **no pauses between words**. A listener therefore received an uninterrupted sequence rather than pre-separated items. Sensitivity to recurring groups had to depend on the organization of the syllable sequence rather than stress, silence, or a distinctive boundary sound.
7|The continuous stream **tokibugikobagopila** includes recurring units such as **tokibu, gikoba, gopila, and tipolu**. In **tokibugikobagopilatipolu**, several units occur back to back. There are no heard spaces announcing where one stops and the next begins. || The learner receives the syllables in their actual sequence. Repetition makes particular transitions reliable, even though the input has not already been divided into words. Learning a unit therefore requires detecting a relationship among adjacent syllables. || **Stable internal sequences** can provide evidence about which sounds belong together. Their recurrence differs from simply hearing a pause after each group. Segmentation has to recover the organization from a continuous input rather than read off an explicit boundary marker.
8|**Tokibu, gikoba, gopila, and tipolu** preserve their internal syllable order across repetitions. Their neighbors can change because different whole units can follow one another. The regularity inside a unit is therefore stronger than the regularity across its boundary. || A word’s first syllable reliably predicts its next syllable within this artificial language. The final syllable can instead lead into different words, so the next syllable is less predictable there. These local differences provide evidence for grouping a recurring sequence. || Identifying that statistical organization does not mean that gaps were present in the recording. The sound remains continuous. The learner has inferred units from the different predictability of within-word and between-word transitions.
9|Testing **transitional probability** requires separating predictability from raw frequency. Half the intended words are repeated more frequently than the other half. A **part-word** spans an intended boundary. Test part-words can be chosen to occur as often as real words, so simply preferring a familiar three-syllable string cannot explain the distinction. || In **tokibu gikoba gopila gikoba tokibu gopila tokibu gikoba gikoba tokibu**, **GOPILA and BU-GIKO each occur twice**. Their full-string counts match, but their syllables do not predict each other equally reliably. The relevant question is how often a particular next syllable follows its predecessor, not simply how often three syllables occur somewhere in the stream. >> **GO–PI and PI–LA** each have probability \[\frac{2}{2}=1\] Every relevant occurrence of the first syllable is followed by that next syllable. >> **BU–GI** has probability \[\frac{2}{4}=0.5\] BU occurs four times, but only two instances lead into GI. || A boundary-spanning part-word therefore contains a weaker internal transition despite matching the word in total frequency. Distinguishing them requires using the relationship between syllables, not only how often the complete test item occurred.
10|Infants **looked longer toward part-words** than toward words from the artificial language. The test sequences differed in statistical organization even though their total occurrence frequencies were matched. The result is therefore not explained simply by having heard one whole test string less often. || The preference is a **novelty effect**. A sequence crossing the learned boundary is treated differently from a unit whose syllables consistently belong together. Looking provides a response measure without asking the infant to state a boundary. || The relevant learning concerns how reliably one syllable predicts another. Infants used structure in the continuous stream to distinguish a word from a frequency-matched part-word. Segmentation can therefore be sensitive to conditional relationships rather than raw repetition alone.
11|**Identifying a word’s referent** means learning what the word names. Finding the sound pattern **cat** in speech and knowing which entity it refers to are different accomplishments. The first concerns the spoken form, while the second concerns its relationship to meaning. || **Segmentation** can identify a recurring unit without establishing its referent. The same surrounding situation can contain several objects, properties, and actions. Hearing a word while those things are present does not directly label which one is intended. || Word learning must connect the recognized sound form with the speaker’s intended referent. Detecting a reliable syllable sequence therefore solves part of acquisition without automatically solving the meaning problem. A child must also use evidence about what the expression is being used to name.
12|The **inductive problem of early word learning** is that one encounter can support many possible meanings. Near a cat, a new word could name **CAT, ANIMAL, PET, or FELIX**. These alternatives differ in whether they pick out a category, a broader class, a role, or one individual. || The same event also supports **FURRY, ALIVE, WALKING, WHISKERS, or LEG**. Those are properties, a state, an action, and parts of the animal. Simply having a word and a visible cat together does not uniquely select one of them. || The **hypothesis space** is the set of candidate meanings consistent with the experience. **Quine’s gavagai problem** describes this uncertainty about reference. Word learning requires evidence or constraints that narrow the alternatives, rather than assuming that a single word–scene pairing makes the intended meaning obvious.
13|**Joint attention** means sharing an attentional focus with another person. An adult and child coordinate which object or event the interaction concerns. This provides information about reference beyond whichever object happens to be most noticeable to the child alone. || **Gaze following** emerges around **nine months**. Infants can follow the direction of another person’s eyes toward something that person is attending to. The speaker’s gaze provides a cue about what a label may be intended to name. || Coordinating attention can narrow the possible referents of a new word. The child can connect the spoken expression with the adult’s focus, rather than pairing it indiscriminately with everything in the scene. Shared attention concerns the relationship between two people and a referent, not just looking at an object independently.
14|In **Baldwin and colleagues’ joint-attention condition**, an adult interacted with an infant and labeled the object the infant was playing with. The spoken word occurred within a visibly shared focus. The adult’s behavior made the referential relationship part of an interaction. || In the **no-joint-attention condition**, a voice behind a screen supplied the label while the object was present. A word and an object were available in both conditions, but the visible coordination with a speaker differed. || **Eighteen-month-olds** learned the novel word in the joint-attention condition but not in the no-joint-attention condition. Temporal co-occurrence alone did not produce the same outcome. The social organization of reference affected which association the child learned.
15|**Intentional cues** provide evidence about what a speaker is trying to refer to. **Eye gaze** can identify an object different from the one the child is currently handling. The speaker’s intended label does not have to match the child’s own immediate focus. || Baldwin tested **16–17-month-olds and 18–19-month-olds**. In **follow-in labeling**, the adult looked at the toy already occupying the child’s attention. In **discrepant labeling**, the adult looked toward a toy in a bucket instead. || The comparison separates the infant’s current interest from the adult’s indicated referent. Using gaze allows the child to connect the word with what the speaker means, rather than automatically assigning every new label to the toy already in hand. The same available objects can support different word mappings depending on the speaker’s attention.
16|**Gaze direction** provides evidence about the intended referent of a word. When an adult looks toward an object while labeling, the child can relate the sound to that object. The adult’s attention helps distinguish the intended candidate from other things available in the interaction. || The child’s own current focus and the speaker’s focus can differ. Successful reference tracking therefore involves using another person’s behavior, not just pairing the label with whatever the child was already watching. || Coordinating the heard label with the indicated object narrows the word-learning problem. The gaze cue does not supply the sound’s meaning by itself. It supplies information about which available referent the speaker intends the child to connect with that sound.
17|**Affective and behavioral cues** are information supplied by a speaker’s emotional expressions and actions. A reaction can indicate that an object satisfies a goal or is not the item being sought. The child can use that reaction to distinguish possible referents of a new word. || In **Akhtar and colleagues’ study**, infants used adult facial expressions to identify the intended object. The expression supplied evidence about the adult’s relation to the object, rather than merely adding another visible event. || A word can occur while several objects are present. Emotional and behavioral evidence helps determine which object the label concerns. Word learning therefore includes interpreting the adult’s intention, instead of treating every item near the utterance as an equally likely association.
18|An adult’s **emotional reaction** can make one object a better candidate for a new word than another. The relevant information concerns which candidate the adult treats as fulfilling the intended goal. The child can relate the reaction to what the label is meant to identify. || **Intentional cues** therefore extend beyond the direction of the eyes. The expression accompanying an interaction can provide evidence about reference even when several objects could be associated with the sound. || Using that evidence narrows the hypothesis space for the word. The child is learning from the relationship between the speaker, the reaction, and the candidate object. Mere simultaneous presence of a word and an object does not capture the full information available in the exchange.
19|A **default word-learning strategy** favors an interpretation unless other evidence supports an alternative. It reduces the number of candidate meanings considered initially. A default is a starting preference rather than a claim that every word must have that kind of meaning. || The **whole-object assumption** favors treating a new label as naming an entire object. Other possible meanings include one of its parts, a property, or an action involving it. The preference makes the whole object a stronger initial candidate. || The strategy narrows the **hypothesis space**, the possible meanings consistent with the scene. The child does not have to test every visible property and part equally before learning can proceed. Further evidence can still establish a different intended kind of reference.
20|**Mutual exclusivity**, associated with **Markman (1989)**, is the default that an object has only one name. If a child already knows a label for one candidate, a new label is more likely to be assigned to an object without a known name. Existing vocabulary changes which referent seems plausible. || This strategy emerges during the **second half of the second year**. The child uses what has already been learned about one object to interpret an unfamiliar expression in the same scene. The new sound alone does not determine the choice. || Mutual exclusivity is a **default**, so its role is to constrain the initial inference. A familiar object with an established name becomes a less likely candidate for the novel label. An unnamed alternative becomes more likely because it does not create that competition between names.
21|Under **mutual exclusivity**, a child tends to connect a new label with an object lacking a known name. The available alternatives matter because the child can use the established name of one object to reduce its suitability for a different unfamiliar label. || This inference depends on the child’s **existing vocabulary**. An object counts as already named only if the child knows its label. The same word–object scene can therefore support different inferences for learners with different prior knowledge. || The strategy reduces uncertainty about reference without requiring the new word’s meaning to be transparent from its sound. It combines the unfamiliar label with what the learner already knows about the candidates. That combination favors an otherwise unnamed object as the intended referent.
22|Children producing **one, two, or three words** can be using different kinds of linguistic organization. **Menn** distinguishes a set of relevant words, a memorized **formula**, a productive combination, and a **stable construction**. These are different possible sources of a short utterance. || **“Up!”**, **“Pick you up!”**, and **“Mommy up!”** differ in length, but length does not establish how they were generated. A longer expression can be stored and repeated as a fixed unit. A shorter one can involve choosing and combining separate elements. || The interaction helps establish what the child means and how the expression is used. A stable child construction can also differ from an adult-like sentence. Even detailed context does not always settle whether an utterance is memorized or productively assembled, so output length alone is an incomplete measure of grammatical knowledge.
23|**Comprehension can exceed production**. A child who usually produces one word at a time can already use basic **transitive word order** to understand who acts on whom. A transitive event involves an actor and a participant affected by that action. || In **“The boy is tickling the girl,”** the first noun identifies the actor and the second identifies the recipient of tickling. The scene can also contain giggling and playing, but the sentence’s order selects the particular role relationship. Knowing the nouns alone does not supply that ordered relationship. || The **structure-mapping account** links a partial sentence representation with a conceptual event representation. Even incomplete knowledge of the sentence can map the noun positions onto event roles. Understanding that relationship can develop before the child regularly produces a complete adult-like sentence with the same structure.
24|**Gertner and colleagues (2006)** tested whether two-year-olds could generalize **transitive structure to a novel verb**. Children heard **“The duck is gorping the bunny”** while viewing unfamiliar actions that reversed which character acted on the other. Gorping had no previously learned action meaning to supply the roles. || Children looked longer at the event in which the **duck acted on the rabbit**. The noun order supported that choice. Their interpretation therefore depended on the construction’s role relationship rather than knowing what gorping meant beforehand. || Instructions occurred in blank-screen intervals, and the test events repeated the sentence. **Preferential looking** measured understanding without requiring a spoken response. The task could therefore reveal knowledge that the child was not yet expressing in full sentences. || Children at that age typically have a **mean length of utterance, or MLU, near two morphemes**. A morpheme is a meaningful word part or whole word. An output such as **“Duck gorp bunny”** can be shorter and less complete than the adult sentence the child can interpret.
