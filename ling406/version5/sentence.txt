2|**“The horse raced past the barn fell”** is grammatical, but its beginning encourages an interpretation that makes the ending hard to understand. At first, **raced** can seem to be the main action performed by the horse. The words then appear to form a complete event before **fell** arrives. || The intended meaning is that **the horse that was raced past the barn fell**. “Raced past the barn” identifies which horse is being discussed, and “fell” states what happened to that horse. The shorter sentence leaves out “that was,” making this structure less immediately apparent. || The difficulty comes from assigning an early structure that the final word cannot fit. Recognizing each word is not enough to understand the sentence. Their grammatical relationships determine which action identifies the horse and which action is the main event.
3|The **garden path effect** occurs when an initially plausible sentence structure leads processing in the wrong direction. In **“The horse raced past the barn fell,”** a reader can first treat raced as the main verb. The final verb fell then conflicts with that interpretation. || Understanding requires treating **raced past the barn** as a modifier meaning “that was raced past the barn.” Fell becomes the main verb of the sentence. The early interpretation has to be revised rather than simply extended with another word. || A sentence can therefore be **grammatical but difficult to process**. The available correct structure does not guarantee that it is the structure selected first. Garden-path difficulty concerns the route taken while interpreting the sentence, not proof that the sentence has no possible grammatical meaning.
4|A **parser** works out a sentence’s grammatical structure as words are heard or read. It identifies which words belong together and how their groups relate. A **syntactic tree** represents these relationships, rather than merely listing the meanings of the individual words. || A **noun phrase, or NP**, groups a noun with associated material. A **verb phrase, or VP**, groups a verb with its complements. A **prepositional phrase, or PP**, includes a preposition and its object. These groupings determine how a modifier or action belongs in the sentence. || The parser works with **limited memory and limited time**. In the reduced-relative interpretation, the horse contains a modifier equivalent to “that was raced past the barn,” while fell remains the main verb. Maintaining that embedded structure is more complex than initially treating raced as the main verb. A processing account has to explain how a real cognitive system handles such alternatives as the input arrives.
5|**Minimal attachment** is a preference for initially building the **smaller syntactic tree**. A tree’s nodes represent grammatical groupings, so an analysis with fewer nodes requires less structure to be constructed at that point. The parser does not begin by fully building every possible interpretation. || Treating **raced** as the main verb gives a simpler early analysis of the horse sentence. Treating “raced past the barn” as a modifier requires an embedded clause inside the noun phrase. Under that more complex analysis, fell is the main predicate outside the modifier. || Choosing the simpler structure can reduce the initial demands on memory. It can also cause difficulty when a later word requires the more complex alternative. The garden path occurs because the initially preferred structure cannot incorporate the continuation, not because the correct structure is unavailable in the grammar.
6|**Late closure** is a tendency to attach new material to the clause or phrase currently being processed, keeping that structure open. In **“The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite,”** “with the dynamite” is preferentially linked to **blew open**. The phrase then describes how the safe was opened. || Under this **verb-phrase attachment**, the dynamite is the instrument used in the action. Under the alternative **noun-phrase attachment**, the words form “the safe with the dynamite,” so the phrase identifies or describes the safe instead. || The same words allow both grammatical arrangements. The attachment determines whether the dynamite belongs to the action as its instrument or to the object as a property. A preference for one arrangement affects which event interpretation becomes available first.
7|In **“The burglar blew open the safe with the jewels,”** the preferred action-related attachment would make the jewels the instrument used to open the safe. That event is implausible. The sensible interpretation instead takes **with the jewels** as identifying the safe that contains them. || The parser has to use the **unpreferred noun-phrase attachment** to obtain that meaning. The difference is not that jewels is an unfamiliar word. It concerns which part of the sentence the phrase modifies. || **Reading times and eye movements** indicate slower processing when the sensible meaning requires this alternative attachment. The listener or reader can briefly adopt the wrong relationship and then revise it. A familiar grammatical preference can therefore create a processing cost even when the final meaning is straightforward.
8|An **autonomous parser** is proposed to make its earliest decisions using **abstract syntactic structure** rather than meaning. Autonomous means that its initial structural choices follow its own operating preferences. Semantic information is not used to choose the first parse in this account. || **Minimal attachment** favors a smaller tree, while **late closure** favors attaching incoming words to the structure already being processed. These preferences reduce the need to maintain every alternative fully. A preferred interpretation is selected before the continuation has necessarily resolved the ambiguity. || When a later word cannot fit, the parser revises that interpretation. Difficulty is expected when the initial structural preference has to be abandoned. This account therefore separates the earliest choice of structure from later correction based on the developing sentence.
9|A **reduced relative clause** modifies a noun without spelling out words such as “that was.” In **“Recent research reported by Ferreira and Clifton,”** reported means that someone reported the research. It is not the main verb describing an action performed by research. || Similar forms include **“referential information provided by the discourse,” “results obtained with global reading times,”** and **“examples cited here.”** The verbs introduce modifiers of information, results, and examples. These phrases can be understood readily despite using a structure associated with garden paths. || **MacDonald, Perlmutter, and Seidenberg (1994)** propose that **meaning and learned statistical regularities** affect parsing early. A structure’s formal complexity alone does not determine how difficult every use will be. The words involved, their usual grammatical patterns, and their plausible meanings can support the required interpretation.
10|In **“a noun phrase followed by a morphologically ambiguous verb,”** followed modifies noun phrase. In **“the initial syntactic analysis assigned to a sentence,”** assigned modifies analysis. In **“differences located on the critical disambiguating phrase,”** located modifies differences. Each verb belongs to a reduced-relative modifier rather than automatically becoming a new main verb. || **“A person presented with the evidence examined by the lawyer”** contains two such relationships. Presented modifies person, while examined modifies evidence. Understanding depends on keeping track of which noun each piece of information describes. || These constructions occur in readable scientific prose, including writing that argues for an autonomous parser. The presence of a reduced relative therefore does not make a sentence uniformly difficult. Lexical and semantic context can support the correct grouping even when its formal structure is complex.
11|In **“Recent research reported by…”**, research is **inanimate**, so it is unlikely to be the entity performing the reporting. Its meaning supports the interpretation that someone reported the research. The reduced-relative structure fits the expected relationship between the event and its participant. || **Reported** also occurs frequently in reduced relatives, particularly after research in scientific writing. **Lexical statistics** means these learned word-specific patterns of use. Familiarity with the construction and knowledge of the topic both favor the same analysis. || In **“The burglar arrested in the parking lot was charged today,”** the burglar is a plausible recipient of arrest. In the version beginning **“The policeman arrested…”**, policeman is also a plausible agent doing the arresting. More than one interpretation receives support. The grammatical structure remains alike, while the nouns’ likely roles change the competition between parses.
12|**Spivey and colleagues (1995)** asked participants to follow spoken instructions while viewing objects. The one-apple display contained **a pencil, a box, an empty towel, and an apple on a towel**. The instruction was **“Put the apple on the towel in the box.”** || **On the towel** can first sound like the destination of put, encouraging a plan to move the apple to the empty towel. In the intended meaning, it identifies the apple’s current location. **In the box** is the actual destination. || Participants often looked toward the empty towel before turning toward the box. The wording **“Put the apple that’s on the towel in the box”** makes the modifier explicit and reduces this temporary destination error. The eyes record an interpretation that can be considered before the final movement is completed.
13|In the **two-apple display**, one apple is on a towel and another is on a different support. “The apple” no longer identifies a unique object. The words **on the towel** can supply the information needed to select the intended apple. || With that visual context, eye movements proceed toward the target apple and then toward the box, rather than making the same detour toward the empty towel. The ambiguous wording and the explicit “that’s on the towel” wording have similar sequences and timing. || The local situation changes what the phrase is useful for. When there are two apples, a location modifier answers which apple is meant. Sentence interpretation can therefore depend on the objects currently available, not only on the sentence’s grammatical form in isolation.
14|With **one apple**, listeners hearing the ambiguous instruction make more looks toward the empty towel as a possible destination. With **two apples**, these incorrect-destination looks are substantially reduced. The same words can receive different early interpretations in the two contexts. || The **unambiguous instruction** produces relatively few destination errors in either display. Its wording already marks the towel phrase as a modifier. The largest cost of ambiguity therefore occurs when only one apple is present. || Two possible apples make extra identifying information useful. **On the towel** can then select the correct object before **in the box** gives its destination. The referential context, meaning which objects a phrase could refer to, helps determine the attachment of the location phrase.
15|**Semantic context** concerns meaning, while **pragmatic context** concerns how an expression is used in a particular situation. The same phrase can be interpreted differently depending on what a speaker must identify. With two apples, the location can specify which apple is intended. || With one apple, that identifying job is unnecessary, so the same location phrase can temporarily be taken as a movement destination. The comparison changes the local visual context while keeping the ambiguous sentence the same. || Incorrect-destination looks change during sentence processing, before the final action is completed. Context therefore affects the developing interpretation early. A purely syntactic account that chooses the same initial structure independently of context has difficulty explaining this difference between the displays.
16|**Syntactic ambiguity** can influence an actual arm movement, not only where the eyes look. Mouse-tracking measurements record the developing path of an action while the participant is interpreting the instruction. A competing destination can affect the route before the final target is selected. || In a **one-referent context**, ambiguous and unambiguous instructions produce more separated movement trajectories. In a **two-referent context**, their paths are more alike. A referent is the object that a phrase identifies, so the distinction concerns whether one or two apples could be meant. || The second apple makes a location modifier more appropriate and reduces interference from the competing destination interpretation. Sentence interpretation and action planning can therefore interact while both are still underway. The eventual correct movement does not erase the competition that affected its earlier path.

18|**The** is a **determiner**, a word that introduces a noun phrase. It supports an expectation for a noun or words modifying a noun. The word does not yet identify the referent by itself, but it constrains the kinds of material that can complete the phrase.
19|**Pizza** is a **noun** naming the referent. It can serve as the head of a noun phrase, meaning the main word that the phrase is organized around. Its grammatical category identifies its role, while its meaning identifies a kind of food.
20|**Was** is a past-tense form of **be** and can function as an auxiliary. It supplies tense while allowing more than one grammatical continuation. A following phrase can describe a location, or a following participle can contribute to a passive construction.
21|**In** is a **preposition** expressing a relationship such as location. It begins a prepositional phrase that requires additional material to complete that relationship. A noun phrase can identify the place or container that the preposition relates to the rest of the sentence.
22|**The** is a **determiner**, so it introduces a noun phrase rather than naming a place on its own. After a preposition, this supports an expectation for a noun or a modifier leading to a noun. The construction is waiting for a phrase that can complete the preposition’s relationship.
23|**Eaten** is the **participial form of eat**, not a noun or the simple past-tense form. In the intended construction **“in the eaten,”** it appears where a noun is expected. This is a **word-category violation** because the type of word conflicts with the grammatical requirement, rather than merely producing an unusual food-related meaning.
24|The **early left anterior negativity**, or **ELAN**, is a brain response associated with **word-class violations**. It is an **event-related potential**, meaning a measured brain response aligned to an event such as the onset of a word. It appears about **100–200 milliseconds** after the critical word and is strongest at left/anterior recording locations. || **“The pizza was in the eaten”** places a participial form where the intended phrase expects a noun. **“The scientist criticized Max’s of proof the theorem”** places the preposition of where it does not fit the expected noun phrase. The mismatch concerns grammatical category. || The early timing prompted an account based on rapid **syntactic analysis**. The measurement concerns how the system responds when the arriving word is of an unexpected type. An unexpected category differs from a grammatically appropriate word with an implausible meaning.
25|For **visually presented words**, the early category-related response was localized to **visual cortex**. Visual cortex processes information about what is seen. In **Dikker and colleagues’ (2010)** account, an expected grammatical category generates predictions about the physical forms of upcoming words. || **“The beautiful princess”** and **“The beautifully princess”** differ in a visible ending that changes which kind of continuation is expected. The mismatch can involve an available form cue before the whole sentence has been interpreted. || **Form typicality** means how characteristic a word’s form is of a grammatical category. An early response can therefore concern a mismatch between a predicted word form and the form received. A category-sensitive effect in a visual task does not have to come entirely from a separate, abstract syntax mechanism.
26|**Perches** can belong to different grammatical categories. In **“Chris and Ben are glad that the bird perches seem easy to install,”** it names supports for birds and is a noun. In **“…the bird perches comfortably in the cage,”** it describes the bird’s action and is a verb. || **Needs** is also ambiguous. In **“The teacher told the principal that the student needs were not being met,”** it names requirements. In **“…the student needs to be more focused,”** it is a verb. The continuation resolves the category required in each sentence. || **Phonological form typicality** concerns how much a word’s sound form resembles noun forms or verb forms. A corpus analysis of **3,158 words** compared each form’s average distance from nouns and verbs. A word’s current grammatical role can differ from the category that its form usually resembles.
27|**Perches is noun-like and needs is verb-like** in their form-based tendencies. These descriptions concern statistical resemblance to word categories, not a rule restricting how either word can be used. Both words can function as nouns or verbs. || In **“the bird perches seem easy to install,”** the noun-like form is used as a noun. In **“the bird perches comfortably in the cage,”** the continuation requires a verb. The same visible form can therefore either match or conflict with its typical category. || **“The student needs were not being met”** uses the verb-like form as a noun, while **“the student needs to be more focused”** uses it as a verb. Comparing these versions separates the category required by the sentence from the category suggested by learned form similarities.
28|Reading slows when a word’s required category conflicts with its **form typicality**. A noun-like form such as **perches** produces a larger cost when the continuation resolves it as a verb. A verb-like form produces a larger cost when it must instead be understood as a noun. || The measure compares length-adjusted reading time at **disambiguation** with reading time at the earlier **point of ambiguity**. Disambiguation is where the continuation makes the grammatical role clear. A positive difference means that processing takes longer at that resolution. || The critical form can stay the same while the continuation changes its role. The larger cost for a mismatching role connects parsing with learned form statistics. A reader uses information about what a word looks or sounds like as well as the grammatical relationship eventually required.
29|The early **ELAN** response to a noun where a verb is expected depends on available form information. An effect occurs for nouns with informative **morphological marking**, meaning meaningful word parts, and for forms strongly associated with nouns. Neutral forms such as **infant** do not produce the same effect. || The comparisons include **“The beautiful princess” versus “The beautifully princess,” “The tasteless soda” versus “The tastelessly soda,”** and **“The cute infant” versus “The cutely infant.”** Princess supplies informative morphology, soda has a statistically noun-like form, and infant has weaker category-form evidence. || An abstract category violation alone therefore does not determine this earliest visual response. The expected grammatical category can predict a physical word form, and the observed response depends partly on whether the arriving form provides evidence of that mismatch.
30|**“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously”** has an acceptable grammatical organization but an implausible combined meaning. **Syntax and semantics are partly independent** because words can fit a grammatical structure without describing a sensible event. || **“The horse raced past the barn fell”** is difficult when the preferred early analysis cannot accommodate the final verb. Faster processing for preferred structures is consistent with shortcuts that reduce memory demands instead of fully maintaining every possible parse. || **“Put the apple on the towel in the box”** receives different early interpretations in different visual contexts. Meaning and communicative circumstances can affect the structure being selected before the sentence is finished. || **“The bird perches were comfortable”** uses a noun-like form as a noun. Very early category-related brain responses depend partly on learned form typicality and predictions about physical word patterns. They are not simply a pure readout of abstract grammatical structure.
