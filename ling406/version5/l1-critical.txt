2|A strict **critical period** is a learning window with a discrete, programmed opening and closing. The relevant environmental input can change the system during that interval. Under the strict definition, the same kind of sensitivity is absent before the window opens and after it closes. || A **sensitive period**, or optimal period, means that learning remains possible across the lifespan but is easier or more effective during a particular interval. Its onset and offset can be gradual. Experience can also affect how that sensitivity develops. || The distinction concerns **impossibility versus reduced effectiveness**. An early advantage together with some later capacity to change fits a sensitive period. Evidence that ordinary late learning is difficult is not, by itself, evidence that all later change is impossible.
3|**Amblyopia** is poor vision in one eye despite normal peripheral function. **Peripheral function** concerns the eye and its incoming visual apparatus, rather than only the brain’s interpretation of the signal. A developmental disturbance can leave a lasting visual deficit even after the original eye problem has been corrected. || **Strabismus** misaligns the eyes. A **cataract** obstructs light transmission through the lens. **Anisometropia** gives the eyes different refractive properties, so they do not supply equally focused input. Each condition can deprive one eye of normal coordinated experience. || Correcting the optical problem does not automatically reverse the organization learned by the visual system. The eye can be physically capable of supplying input while the brain still makes inadequate use of it. The long-term impairment and its original peripheral cause are therefore different parts of the developmental process.
4|In amblyopia, the two eyes’ inputs **compete for influence in visual cortex**. If one eye provides abnormal information, the other can gain influence over representations that would otherwise support the disadvantaged eye. The inputs develop in interaction rather than complete independence. || During **monocular deprivation**, abnormal or reduced input affects one eye. After about **2–3 days**, dendritic spines become more mobile. Around **3–4 days**, spine loss and degeneration occur. After **more than a week**, axon sprouting and new spine formation reorganize connections. || **Dendritic spines** receive input at connections between nerve cells, while **axon sprouting** adds branches through which cells can communicate. These changes alter the routes carrying the eyes’ influence. When normal input returns, the deprived eye can remain at a competitive disadvantage. Ordinary use of both eyes can maintain the imbalance rather than immediately restoring normal function.
5|**Intermittently patching the stronger eye** reduces its opportunity to dominate visual input. The weaker eye then has more opportunity to drive the visual system. Treatment uses the competition between the inputs to encourage the brain’s own plasticity. || **Plasticity** is the capacity to change with experience. Temporarily altering the balance of input can help the weaker eye regain function instead of leaving the stronger pathway continuously advantaged. The patch is applied to the better eye so that the disadvantaged input gets more use. || The treatment works best early in development. Its effectiveness declines with age and can be very limited by late childhood, around **ten years** in the comparison. The outcome depends not only on the manipulation but also on the developmental state in which it occurs.
6|**Levi and Polat (1996)** reported improvements in adult amblyopia after **long-term, intensive perceptual training** using the affected eye. The psychophysical tasks required controlled visual judgments. Some participants were in their **fifties**, well beyond the usual childhood treatment window. || Performance changed across **tens of thousands of trials**. Improvement included faster-changing periods and flatter periods, so progress was not uniform from one practice block to another. Benefits extended to the trained task and broader visual function. || Adult visual plasticity therefore remains possible under particular conditions of experience. Poor results from an ordinary treatment late in life do not establish that the adult system cannot change at all. The intensity and kind of training matter when assessing the extent of remaining plasticity.
7|**Li, Ngo, Nguyen, Lam, and Levi (2008)** reported that playing video games improved **visual acuity and visual attention** in adults with amblyopia. Visual acuity concerns resolving fine detail. Visual attention concerns selecting and responding to relevant visual information. || The participants already had a developmental visual deficit. Improvement therefore involved a visual system whose earlier experience had left a persistent difficulty, rather than simply normal childhood visual development. || Intensive visual experience was associated with change beyond the early optimal period. These adult improvements support remaining **experience-dependent plasticity**. The learning window can become less favorable without eliminating every possibility of functional improvement afterward.
8|An early **peripheral disturbance** can produce lasting changes in the brain’s visual organization. A temporary problem with one eye changes the input available while the system is developing. The later functional deficit can persist after the original physical problem has ended. || **Experience-dependent organization** means that the pattern of input helps shape how neural connections are used. Competitive differences between the eyes can become established during deprivation. Restoring the eye does not automatically undo those accumulated changes. || Treatment is most effective during a restricted developmental window, yet specialized adult experience can still produce improvements. Amblyopia therefore combines strong early sensitivity with some later plasticity. That combination fits a **sensitive period** more readily than an absolute claim that no later learning is possible.
9|**Lenneberg** connected developmental neurology with the proposal of a **language acquisition device**. The device refers to a biologically supported capacity for acquiring language. The developmental question is whether changes in the brain alter the conditions under which that capacity operates. || Evidence from **early language deprivation** concerns people who encounter a first language late. These histories include neglected children and Deaf children without early access to sign. The timing of accessible language differs from the typical pattern of input from early life. || Delayed first-language experience can therefore be compared with early acquisition. The comparison concerns when language was available, not simply how much vocabulary a person knows at the time of testing. A biological account has to connect developmental state with the later capacity to learn from that input.
10|In **Biological Foundations of Language (1967)**, **Lenneberg** related language development to maturational changes. Development follows a broadly ordered pattern, with words preceding telegraphic phrases and longer spontaneous sentences emerging later. **Telegraphic speech** contains important content words with reduced grammatical material. || Repeated sound patterns and early vocal play occur before a clear vocabulary. Around **twelve months**, recurring sequences and emerging words such as mama and dada accompany expanding motor abilities. By about **eighteen months**, a more definite vocabulary coexists with complex babbling and growing understanding. || Lenneberg interpreted these regularities as compatible with an unfolding **biological program**. The proposal is that the state of the developing organism contributes to acquisition. Language milestones were connected with maturation rather than explained only by the accumulated quantity of input.
11|Lenneberg’s evidence about typical brain development came mostly from **post-mortem tissue**, measured after death. It was not a continuous series of measurements from the same living children. The neuron-size comparison had no observations between roughly **two and eighteen years**. || **Neuron size** increased rapidly early in development, alongside changes in **packing density**, how closely the cells occupy tissue. Lenneberg proposed that changes before about two years made the brain ready for language acquisition. The favorable window would open after that early rapid growth. || The account connects a proposed learning transition with measured tissue development. The ages actually represented matter because gaps in sampling do not provide direct observations of a continuous switch. The biological proposal and the available developmental measurements therefore have to be distinguished.
12|Children can have much better language outcomes than adults after severe injury to language-related tissue. In extreme cases, **hemispherectomy** removes one cerebral hemisphere to treat severe seizures. Substantial language development can follow early removal even of the **left hemisphere**. || Historical cases included many operations before the teenage years with speech unaffected or improved. A puberty case improved slowly. Adult left-hemisphere operations were associated with permanent aphasia, while adult right-hemisphere operations had a different language outcome. || **Cerebral dominance** means specialization of the hemispheres for particular functions. Lenneberg proposed that earlier organization is less fixed and permits greater reassignment of language. More adult-like dominance would reduce that flexibility. || This hypothesis connects age with the consequences of extensive injury. The same broad loss of tissue can have different outcomes depending on when it occurs. The proposed closing of the favorable window concerns the changing organization of the system, rather than damage being harmless in childhood.
13|A **perinatal stroke** occurs before birth or within **28 days after birth**. It can damage the brain while the systems supporting language are still developing. Outcomes are usually better than those following comparable adult injury, but they are not uniformly unaffected. || Early language deficits occur after **both left- and right-hemisphere damage**. Some early difficulties can be greater after right-hemisphere injury. The pattern does not simply reproduce the usual adult division of language functions. || Later performance often remains in the **low-normal range**. A better outcome than adult aphasia does not mean complete catch-up or absence of a developmental cost. The trajectory can involve a lasting delay while still revealing substantial capacity for reorganization.
14|**Curtiss and colleagues (1974)** studied **Genie** after severe early neglect and restricted language access. She was first studied at **thirteen years and seven months**. Her confinement had begun at about **twenty months**, making the period without ordinary language and social experience extensive. || She had been physically restrained, minimally cared for, and punished for producing sounds. Accessible spoken language and normal interaction were severely limited. Her history therefore involved deprivation in several developmental domains at once. || On admission, she was malnourished, did not speak, and had major difficulties in everyday physical functioning. Her later learning began after this history of abuse and isolation. The case concerns delayed language acquisition within that broader developmental context, not an otherwise typical childhood with only one experience removed.
15|During **four years of observation**, Genie learned to understand **hundreds of words**, some **morphology**, and some **prepositions**. Morphology concerns meaningful word parts, and plural forms were a relative strength. She could use some changes in form to distinguish one item from more than one. || Preposition tests required actions with buttons and boxes. **“Put the red box behind the blue box”** tested a spatial relationship without requiring her to define behind aloud. The response measured how she understood the instruction. || Correct responses exceeded errors for **on, in, over, under, in front of, in back of, next to, and behind**. Behind received **12 correct and 5 incorrect** responses, while in received **37 correct and 4 incorrect**. Learning therefore extended beyond object names to some grammatical forms and relations between objects.
16|**Genie’s production developed much more slowly than comprehension**. She could understand more than she could express through speech. Some receptive advantage is typical, but her punishment history could also affect how readily knowledge appeared in vocal behavior. || **Grammar lagged behind vocabulary**. Tests of conjunctions included **“Show me the fork and pencil,” “Show me the crayon or the knife,”** and **“Show me either the spoon or the crayon.”** These required understanding how the connecting word changed which response satisfied the instruction. || Genie initially treated **or** much like **and**. She later distinguished the words without reliably understanding the either/or relationship. And sentences received **100 correct and 3 incorrect** responses. Or sentences received **7 correct, 51 incorrect, and 6 unanalyzable** responses. The contrast identifies a difficulty with the relationship expressed by grammar despite substantial word learning.
17|Genie made much more progress in **vocabulary than grammar**. That difference has been interpreted as evidence that late first-language learning especially affects grammatical development. Her ability to learn many words did not bring equally rapid progress in the relationships required by grammatical tests. || Her history also involved possible learning difficulties and the effects of neglect and abuse. Those factors accompanied delayed language exposure. The observed outcome therefore cannot be attributed to age of first language alone. || Funding ended after **four years**, so the research program did not measure what she might have learned over a longer interval. Claims about her development concern the recorded period. Both the uneven learning pattern and the circumstances limiting its interpretation are part of the evidence.
18|**Mayberry, Lock, and Kazmi (2002)** compared delayed first-language learning with later learning after an early language. Deaf children first encountering **American Sign Language, or ASL, at 9–15 years** had persistent difficulty despite otherwise more typical upbringing. They were learning a first language, not just a new vocabulary for an established language system. || Children who became deaf **after first learning a spoken language** did better with later ASL. Both groups encountered sign later, but only one had early linguistic experience. The difference separates the timing of a first language from the timing of a second one. || In adulthood, the no-early-language group was poorer at recalling complex ASL sentences. In English grammaticality judgments, groups with an early signed or spoken language also performed better than the group lacking early language. Early linguistic experience therefore mattered across modalities, rather than requiring the first and later languages to be both spoken or both signed.
19|Language outcomes after **stroke and hemispherectomy** differ greatly between childhood and adulthood. The developing system can support language after injuries that would produce more severe and lasting adult impairment. That difference supports a developmental contribution to the capacity for recovery and reorganization. || **Lack of input does not keep a first-language window open indefinitely**. When accessible language becomes available only later, persistent difficulty can remain. Delaying experience is therefore different from postponing the whole maturational course until input arrives. || The human evidence comes from unusual histories of injury or deprivation, often with other influences present. There is also no equivalent experimental animal model of human language in which the relevant capacity can simply be removed and retested. Biological explanations must account for the patterns and qualifications in these observed human cases.
