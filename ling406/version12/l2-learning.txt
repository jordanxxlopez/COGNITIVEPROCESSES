2|**Babies are overrated** as language learners. When **sensitive periods** for a second language, or **L2**, are taken to mean that infants, toddlers and children are “better” at learning language than adults, it is important not to get carried away. >> A typical child **by 18 months** understands the concepts of “in and out” and “off and on,” points to several body parts when asked, **uses at least 20 words**, responds with words or gestures to simple questions such as “Where’s teddy?” and “What’s that?”, shows some pretend play, such as giving teddy a drink, makes at least four different consonant sounds, such as b, n, d, g, w and h, enjoys being read to, and points to pictures using one finger. >> An adult who **only knew about 20 words after 18 months of language instruction** would be considered a very poor learner. || **Adults initially learn more quickly than children**. This is due largely to two advantages. >> **Transfer from L1**, the first language. Adults can build on what they already know about how language works. >> **Better general cognitive skills**, such as memory, attention and reasoning. || **Where adults do worse is ultimate attainment**, meaning the final level of skill they eventually reach. >> Adults **rarely become native-like** in a second language. >> This may also be **due in part to transfer**. The first language that helps adults start quickly may also hold them back from reaching native-like skill. >> Speed of early learning and final level of success are different measures, and children and adults compare differently on each.
3|Some of the **“language milestones”** in a toddler’s development are actually **general conceptual abilities**. >> Understanding **“in and out”** and **“off and on”** depends on understanding **spatial relations between objects**. >> **Pretend play**, such as giving teddy a drink, depends on **event representations**, which are mental representations of who does what in an event. >> These are achievements in thinking about the world as well as in language. || **Later learners of a second language already have much of this in place**. >> An adult learning Spanish already understands what “in” and “on” mean as concepts. >> Adults can **focus on learning how these concepts are represented in the target language**, meaning the language being learned. >> A toddler has to build the concepts and the words at the same time. || Later learners **also already know another language**, which affects **how the second language is learned**. >> The first language provides ready-made ways of expressing concepts. >> Some of these fit the second language and help. Others clash with it and cause errors. || The highlighted milestones, understanding in and out and off and on and pretend play, are conceptual achievements. A toddler learning them is building new ideas about the world as well as new words. >> An adult learner already has these ideas. >> The adult’s task is mainly to learn how the new language expresses them, which is a smaller job.
4|**Transfer** is **the use of native-language, or L1, processes and representations for a second language, or L2**. >> A learner brings L1 habits for hearing sounds, building sentences and finding words into L2. >> The learner approaches L2 with a fully working language system already in place. || **Transfer can have both positive and negative effects on second-language learning and performance**. >> **Positive transfer** happens when an L1 pattern matches what L2 requires, so the learner gets a head start. >> **Negative transfer** happens when an L1 pattern conflicts with L2, so the learner makes errors or finds the L2 pattern hard to learn. || A cartoon of two men at a bar has one asking **“Do you not be happy with me as the translator of the books of you?”** >> The sentence uses English words, but its structure follows the grammar of another language. >> Word-by-word translation carries over the word order and phrasing of the first language, which is negative transfer in grammar. || Transfer is the reason a first language shapes second-language learning in both directions. Every habit built for the first language comes along into the second, whether it helps or hurts. >> Shared sounds, words and structures give the learner a head start. >> Differences between the languages are where errors appear, because the learner’s habits point the wrong way.
5|Transfer affects **phonetic and phonological learning**, the learning of speech sounds. **Categorical perception** leads to **acquired similarity** for sounds in the same category. >> **Acquired similarity** means that sounds a language groups into one category come to sound more alike through experience with that language. || **Minimal pairs in Hindi** reveal the problem. A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in only one sound. >> One pair of words both sound like “tap” to English ears. One means **hot**, and the other means **a kind of beat**. >> One pair both sound like “dab.” One means **pressure**, and the other means **a kind of fruit**. >> One pair both sound like “ari.” One means **saw**, the tool, and the other means **crooked**. >> In each pair, one word has a **dental** consonant, made with the tongue against the teeth, and the other has a **retroflex** consonant, made with the tongue curled back toward the roof of the mouth. || **Native English speakers tend to use the English “alveolar” category to interpret both dental and retroflex consonants**. An **alveolar** consonant, like English /t/ or /d/, is made with the tongue on the ridge just behind the teeth. >> English has only the alveolar category, so both Hindi sounds get mapped onto it. >> The two Hindi sounds then seem the same to English listeners. || **In this case, transfer makes learning the L2 contrast more difficult**. >> The English category was learned successfully, but it now hides a difference that Hindi uses to distinguish words. >> The learner has to learn to separate sounds that the first language taught them to treat as one.
6|Transfer also affects **grammar learning**. **Classifiers** are a **somewhat unusual grammatical feature across languages**. A **classifier** is a word that must appear between a number or demonstrative and a noun, chosen according to the kind of thing the noun names. >> The structure is **demonstrative pronoun, then nominal classifier, then noun**, as in **yí gè rén**, “one person,” where **gè** is the classifier. || Chinese has many classifiers, each used with a particular kind of noun. >> **Gè** is the **general classifier**. >> **Bǎ** is used with **objects that have a handle**. >> **Běn** is used with **books and similar objects**. >> **Kuài** is used with **objects that are divided into pieces**. >> **Liàng** is used with **wheeled vehicles**. >> **Tái** is used with **devices and gadgets**. >> **Xiē** is used to indicate an **indeterminate amount**, or with the **plural** of pronouns and demonstratives. >> **Zhāng** is used with **flat objects**. >> **Zhī** is used with **long, thin objects**. || **Native English speakers learning Chinese have difficulty learning these**. >> In English, to say **“a chair,” “that dog,” “the car”** or **“one book,”** we do not pay attention to what kind of thing the noun names when picking a determiner or pronoun. >> **In Chinese, different classifiers are required by different nouns**, so the learner must notice properties such as shape and function. || One way to think about this is that **English speakers have learned to ignore the different cues for classifiers** as part of learning English grammar. >> The features of objects that predict classifiers are irrelevant to English grammar, so English speakers stopped attending to them. >> Learning Chinese requires paying attention again to cues that English taught them to ignore.
7|**Grammatical gender** is another area where **transfer causes problems**. **Grammatical gender is different from social or cultural gender**. Grammatical gender is a system that sorts nouns into classes, often called masculine and feminine. >> **There is nothing “male” about a book or “female” about liberty**. In French, **le livre**, the book, is masculine, and **la liberté**, liberty, is feminine. >> Other French examples include masculine **le cheval** (the horse), **le chien** (the dog) and **le bruit** (the noise), and feminine **la colombe** (the dove), **la chemise** (the shirt) and **la maison** (the house). || **Grammatical gender has implications for agreement**. **Agreement** means that related words must match the noun’s gender. **Pronouns, adjectives, some forms of verbs** and other words **all need to be coordinated with noun gender**. >> A learner has to know each noun’s gender in order to use the right forms of all these related words. >> English has no grammatical gender on nouns, so English speakers have no habit of tracking it. || French is famous for having **synonyms with different genders**. >> **Auto** and **voiture**, both meaning car, are masculine and feminine. >> **Rivière** and **fleuve**, both meaning river, are feminine and masculine. >> **Vélo** and **bicyclette**, both meaning bicycle, are masculine and feminine. >> Words with the same meaning can have different genders, so gender has to be learned word by word. || Common **word endings** give clues to gender. >> Endings common with **masculine nouns** include **-age**, as in le fromage (the cheese), **-r**, as in le professeur (the teacher), **-t**, as in le chat (the cat), and **-isme**, as in le capitalisme. >> Endings common with **feminine nouns** include **-ie**, as in la boulangerie (the bakery), **-ion**, as in la nation, **-ité** or **-tié**, as in la fraternité (brotherhood), **-ance**, as in la balance (the scales), and **-nne, -mme** and **-lle**, as in la fille (the girl) and l’indienne. >> These are statistical patterns in word form that a learner can use.
8|**Transfer is not all bad**. **Vocabulary learning in some L1–L2 situations is aided by the similarity of words across languages**. >> French **auto** resembles English “auto” and refers to a car. >> French **rivière** resembles English “river.” >> French **bicyclette** resembles English “bicycle.” >> Japanese **コロッケ, korokke**, refers to a croquette, and Japanese **モノレール, monoreru**, refers to a monorail. || Sometimes the similarity is **due to shared etymology**. **Etymology** is the history of where a word comes from. >> Words with a shared origin are called **cognates**. French rivière and English river both come from the same older word. || Sometimes the similarity is **due to more recent “borrowing.”** >> A **loan word** is a word one language takes from another. >> Japanese korokke and monoreru are borrowed from European languages, with their sounds adapted to Japanese. || Cognates and loan words are **positive transfer** for vocabulary. >> The learner already knows a similar form with a similar meaning. >> The new word comes with a head start from the familiar one. || Cognates and loan words make vocabulary learning faster and easier. A learner who knows river can quickly link it to rivière, because the two words share sounds and meaning. >> Shared etymology comes from languages descending from a common ancestor. >> Borrowing comes from one language adopting words from another, often with changes to fit its own sounds. || In both cases, the learner gets a familiar anchor for the new word, which makes it easier to store and to recall.
9|**Not all similar-sounding words are cognates**. **The bias to interpret L2 words as related to similar-sounding L1 words can be very strong**. **Sometimes the result is embarrassing**. >> A word that looks or sounds like a word in another language but means something different is called a **false friend**. || Spanish **“Arnold está embarazado”** does not mean that Arnold is embarrassed. >> Spanish **embarazado** means **pregnant**. >> An English speaker relying on the similar sound of embarrassed would get the meaning badly wrong. || The same tendency to link L2 words to similar L1 words drives both helpful and harmful transfer. >> When the meanings match, the link speeds up learning, as with cognates. >> When the meanings differ, the link leads to errors. || False friends reveal how automatically learners link L2 words to similar L1 words. The link happens before the learner checks the meaning, so a familiar-sounding word is assumed to mean the familiar thing. >> Embarazado sounds like embarrassed, so English speakers assume it means embarrassed. >> Its real meaning, pregnant, leads to an embarrassing mistake. || The same process that makes cognates helpful makes false friends misleading. The difference lies only in whether the meanings actually match.
10|**De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** tested in the lab whether people **use cognates in “second language” learning**. >> They used **paired-associate learning of made-up words**. In **paired-associate learning**, participants study pairs of items and later have to recall one item when given the other. >> The made-up words **varied in their similarity to their Dutch partners**, so some were cognate-like and some were not. >> The words **also varied in “concreteness.”** A **concrete** word names something that can be seen or touched, while an **abstract** word names an idea. || There were four kinds of pairs. >> **Gitaar–gettar**, “guitar,” is **concrete and cognate-like**. >> **Tijger–duunze**, “tiger,” is **concrete and non-cognate**. >> **Domein–domaan**, “domain,” is **abstract and cognate-like**. >> **Herstel–groop**, “repair,” is **abstract and non-cognate**. || **Participants were simply presented with pairs of Dutch words and their nonword partners and asked to learn the pairs**. >> They were Dutch speakers, so the Dutch words stood in for their first language, and the made-up words stood in for a new second language. >> **They were then tested a week later on retention**, meaning how well they remembered the pairs after a delay without further study. || De Groot and Keijzer designed their materials to cross two factors. Each pair was either concrete or abstract and either cognate-like or not, giving four kinds of pairs. >> Crossing the factors lets the study measure the effect of each one separately. >> It also reveals whether cognate status helps both concrete and abstract words.
11|The results show that **cognates are learned more rapidly and also remembered more easily**. A table gives **recall scores** and **response times** across four sessions, with **session 4** coming **after one week with no training**. || **Recall scores** in percent increased across the three training sessions. >> For **concrete cognates**, recall was 69.7% in session 1, rising to 91.7% and then 93.4%. **Concrete non-cognates** started at only 43.7% and rose to 92.0% by session 3. >> For **abstract cognates**, recall was 42.7% in session 1, rising to 86.0% by session 3. **Abstract non-cognates** started at just 19.0% and reached 72.7%. >> After a week without training, in session 4, concrete cognates dropped to 78.4% and concrete non-cognates to 69.0%. Abstract cognates dropped to 68.4%, while abstract non-cognates fell sharply to **36.0%**. || **Response times**, in milliseconds, were faster for cognates in every session. >> In session 1, concrete cognates took about 1,880 milliseconds, while abstract non-cognates took about 3,641. >> In session 3, concrete cognates took about 1,080 milliseconds and concrete non-cognates about 1,643. >> In session 4, concrete cognates took about 1,833 milliseconds and abstract non-cognates about 3,412. || This is evidence that **cognate status can contribute to the very earliest stages of word learning in a second language**. >> The cognate advantage was largest in the first session, when learning had just begun. >> It reappeared after a week, when non-cognates were forgotten more. >> Concreteness also had a large effect, with concrete words learned and remembered better than abstract words. One explanation for concreteness effects is called **context availability**, the idea that concrete words are easier to link with a supporting context. || Concreteness had a large effect alongside cognate status. Concrete words were learned faster and forgotten less than abstract words, and abstract non-cognates were the hardest of all. >> Abstract non-cognates started at only 19.0% recall and fell to 36.0% after a week. >> Concrete cognates started at 69.7% and stayed at 78.4% after a week. || The two factors add together. A word that is both concrete and cognate-like has two sources of support, while a word that is abstract and unrelated to any known word has neither.
12|The experiment needs a closer look. **If de Groot and Keijzer had only tested “receptive” vocabulary**, meaning **how easily participants could produce the Dutch word given its made-up partner**, **this would not be very impressive**. >> In receptive testing, the learner sees the new word and has to supply the familiar Dutch word. >> For cognate-like pairs, the new word already looks like the Dutch word, so it can give away the answer. || **Many of the meanings, such as gitaar, tijger and domein, could be guessed without any Dutch training**, because they resemble English guitar, tiger and domain. The **concrete cognates** in the study show how close the resemblance is. >> **Broer–breur**, **brood–brodde**, **gitaar–gettar**, **jongen–jange** and **lepel–lippel**. >> **Lichaam–lechem**, **naald–nadel**, **paraplu–parredu**, **rivier–riffer** and **schaar–skare**. >> **Stier–stire**, **trein–trinen**, **varken–vark**, **viool–vialo** and **vrouw–vrauk**. >> In each pair, the made-up word is a slightly changed version of the Dutch word. || The study **also tested “expressive” vocabulary**, which the authors call **“productive” testing**. >> In productive testing, the learner sees the Dutch word and has to produce the new made-up word. >> The learner has to remember the exact new form, gettar, out of many similar possibilities. >> A cognate advantage in productive testing therefore reflects real learning of new forms. || The criticism of receptive testing concerns what the test can reveal. If the new word looks like the Dutch word, a participant could answer correctly simply by noticing the resemblance, without having learned the pair. >> Productive testing removes this shortcut. >> The participant has to produce the exact new form, which requires memory of the pairing.
13|A second table compares **productive testing** and **receptive testing** **immediately after learning, in session 3**, and **at retest a week later, in session 4**. The **effect** row gives the drop in recall over the week. || In **productive testing**, learners had to produce the new word. >> **Concrete** words fell from 92.7% to 73.7%, an effect of 19.0 points. **Abstract** words fell from 79.3% to 52.2%, an effect of 27.1 points. >> **Cognates** fell from 89.7% to 73.3%, an effect of 16.4 points. **Non-cognates** fell from 82.3% to 52.5%, an effect of 29.8 points. || In **receptive testing**, learners had to give the Dutch word. >> **Concrete** words fell from 97.7% to 88.9%, an effect of 8.8 points. **Abstract** words fell from 91.5% to 64.9%, an effect of 26.6 points. >> **Cognates** fell from 98.5% to 90.7%, an effect of 7.8 points. **Non-cognates** fell from 90.7% to 63.0%, an effect of 27.7 points. || The table reveals what participants actually learned. >> Productive scores were lower than receptive scores, because generating a new form is harder than recognizing a familiar one. >> Cognates and concrete words were forgotten less in **both** directions. >> The cognate advantage held up in productive testing, where the learner had to recall the new form. Similarity to a known word helped learners form a lasting memory of the new word. || The difference between the two testing directions tells us about the nature of the cognate advantage. Receptive testing gave high scores overall because the familiar Dutch word only had to be recognized. Productive testing gave lower scores because the new word had to be generated. >> The cognate advantage held in both directions. >> In productive testing, it reflects real memory for the new form. || The week-long delay adds a measure of durability. Cognates and concrete words lost much less over the week, so their advantage extends from initial learning to long-term retention.
14|A key **research question** is whether **similar L1 words are always activated when processing L2**. **There have been many, many studies of this**. >> If a bilingual person hears a word in their second language, words in their first language that sound similar might also become active. >> This activation could happen automatically, even when the person is trying to use only the second language. || **One clear example** comes from a **“visual world” experiment** by **Marian and Spivey (2003)**. >> In a visual world experiment, participants hear spoken instructions while viewing a display of objects, and their eye movements are recorded. >> Looks toward an object whose L1 name sounds like the spoken L2 word would show that the L1 word was activated. >> Eye movements reveal this activation while the participant goes on to choose the correct object. || The research question asks how automatic cross-language activation is. If L1 words become active even when a person is using only L2, the two languages are never fully separate in the mind. >> Eye tracking can reveal this activation without asking the person to report it. >> Looks toward an object with a similar L1 name would reveal that the L1 word was active.
15|This kind of experiment parallels **McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)**, a study of **gradient effects of within-category VOT on lexical access**. >> In that study, participants saw a display with a **lamp**, a **ship**, a **bear** and a **pear**, and heard a word from a continuum between “bear” and “pear.” >> The bear and pear differ only in their first consonant, and the lamp and ship are unrelated fillers. || **Eye movements reveal competition between possible responses that participants are considering**. >> A participant could click on the bear while having looked briefly at the pear. >> Those looks show that “pear” was active as a competitor. || In the McMurray study, **the amount of competition between BEAR and PEAR depended on distance from the category boundary**. >> Sounds closer to the /b/–/p/ boundary produced more looks to the competitor. >> Sounds farther from the boundary produced fewer. >> The eye-tracking method can therefore measure how strongly an alternative word is active along the way to the final choice. || The McMurray study provides the logic that Marian and Spivey borrowed. In that study, looks to a competitor picture measured how strongly a similar word was active, even when the participant chose correctly. >> Bear and pear differ by one sound, so they compete during recognition. >> The amount of competition varied with how close each sound was to the category boundary. || Applying the same method across languages lets researchers ask whether a word in one language competes with a similar word in another.
16|**Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** demonstrated the **“cohort effect.”** A **cohort** is the set of words that share the same beginning sounds. >> Participants viewed a grid containing pictures and shapes and followed spoken instructions. >> The display included a **beaker**, the target, a **beetle**, which shares its beginning with beaker, a **speaker**, which rhymes with beaker, and a **carriage**, which is unrelated. || The graph plots the **probability of fixating** each item over the first 1,000 milliseconds after the target word begins. >> Looks to the **beaker** and the **beetle** rise together early, because both fit the first sounds of the word. >> Looks to the beetle peak near 0.2 at around 500 milliseconds and then fall once the sounds that distinguish the words arrive. >> Looks to the beaker keep rising, approaching 1 by about 1,000 milliseconds. >> Looks to the **speaker** rise later and stay smaller, peaking near 0.1. Looks to the carriage stay near zero. || The **cohort effect** is this early competition from words that share a beginning with the target. >> Words compete as soon as they match the incoming sounds. >> They drop out when the input stops matching them. Eye movements capture this competition even when listeners end up choosing correctly. || The cohort effect captures how quickly recognition begins. Words that share a beginning with the target become active as soon as the first sounds arrive. >> Beetle competes with beaker because both begin with the same sounds. >> Speaker competes later and less, because its overlap comes at the end of the word. || Competition fades as soon as the input rules out a candidate. The eye movements track this rise and fall moment by moment.
17|**Marian and Spivey** examined the cohort effect with **“cross-language competitors.”** A cross-language competitor is an object whose name in the **other language** shares its beginning with the spoken target word. || The **hypothesis** was that **if L1 words are always activated, native Russian speakers should look at a book of matches when the target is “speaker” in English**. >> English **speaker** begins with the same sounds as Russian **спички, spichki**, meaning **matches**. >> If Russian is active while the participant listens to English, the picture of matches should attract looks. || For **English targets**, each target was paired with an **English competitor** and a **Russian competitor**. >> **Speaker** was paired with **spear** in English and **spichki**, matches, in Russian. >> **Boot** was paired with **book** and **buben**, tambourine. >> **Shark** was paired with **shovel** and **sharik**, balloon. >> **Chair** was paired with **chess set** and **cherepaha**, turtle. >> **Marker** was paired with **marbles** and **marka**, stamp. >> **Barbed wire** was paired with **bark** and **barhat**, velvet. >> **Plug** was paired with **plum** and **platye**, dress. >> **Gun** was paired with **gum** and **gaika**, nut. >> **Lock** was paired with **lobster** and **lak dlea nogtei**, nail polish. || For **Russian targets**, the pairing was reversed. >> Russian **spichki**, matches, was paired with English **spear** and Russian **spitsi**, knitting needles. >> Russian **marka**, stamp, was paired with English **marker** and Russian **marlya**, cheese cloth. >> Russian **platye**, dress, was paired with English **plum** and Russian **plashch**, raincoat. || The connection between the target and the cross-language competitor is purely in **sound**. >> Speakers and matches have nothing to do with each other in meaning. >> Any looks to the matches must come from the activation of the Russian word spichki. || The word lists were built so that each target had a competitor in each language. For an English target such as speaker, the English competitor spear shares its beginning, and the Russian competitor spichki also shares its beginning. >> Both competitors overlap with the target in sound. >> Only the Russian competitor’s overlap depends on knowing Russian. || Looks to the Russian competitor during an English task can only come from activation of Russian words. The design makes these looks a direct measure of cross-language activation.
18|The results for **competition during L2 spoken word recognition**, when Russian–English bilinguals followed instructions in **English**, come from a table giving the **percent of trials** in which participants looked at each competitor. >> When only the **between-language competitor** was present, participants looked at it on **18%** of trials. >> When only the **within-language competitor** was present, participants looked at it on **18%** of trials. >> When **both competitors** were present, participants looked at the between-language competitor on **13%** of trials and the within-language competitor on **19%**. >> When **no competitor** was present, participants looked at a control filler object on only **7%** of trials. || **When only one competitor was present, participants looked at within- and between-language competitors to the same extent**. >> The Russian name of the matches pulled the eyes just as strongly as a similar English word did. || **When both competitors were present, participants looked at the within-language competitor more**, **but still looked at the between-language competitor a lot**, about **twice as often as the control condition**. >> The English competitor won out when the two were side by side. >> The Russian competitor still attracted far more looks than an unrelated object. || The first language stays active during second-language listening. >> Bilinguals keep their L1 active while they process their L2. >> They still select the correct object, but L1 words compete along the way. || The results reveal that bilinguals keep both languages active. When listening in English, they looked at objects whose Russian names sounded like the English target as often as at objects whose English names did. >> With one competitor present, both kinds drew looks on 18% of trials. >> With both present, the English competitor drew a little more, but the Russian one still drew far more than a control object.
19|The results for **competition during L1 spoken word recognition**, when the same bilinguals followed instructions in **Russian**, come from a second table. >> When only the **between-language competitor**, an object with a similar **English** name, was present, participants looked at it on **8%** of trials. >> When only the **within-language competitor** was present, participants looked at it on **14%** of trials. >> When **both** were present, they looked at the between-language competitor on **8%** of trials and the within-language competitor on **13%**. >> With **no competitor**, looks to the control filler occurred on **5%** of trials. || **English words do not seem to be activated when native Russian speakers do this experiment in Russian**. >> The **small numerical difference between the between-language and control conditions**, 8% versus 5%, is **not statistically significant**. >> A difference that is not statistically significant is within the range of chance variation. || The within-language competitor still produced a reliable cohort effect in Russian. >> The pattern across the two experiments is **asymmetric**. >> The first language interferes with the second, but the second language does not reliably interfere with the first. || The asymmetry between the two experiments is the key result. The first language reliably interfered with the second, but the second did not reliably interfere with the first. >> Russian words became active during English tasks. >> English words did not become reliably active during Russian tasks. || This asymmetry fits a picture in which the first language is more strongly established and more easily activated than the second.
20|**The Revised Hierarchical Model** of **Kroll and Stewart** explains **L1–L2 interactions in terms of spreading activation**. **Spreading activation** is the idea that activating one representation partly activates the representations connected to it. >> The model has three boxes. An **L1** box holds the first-language vocabulary, an **L2** box holds the second-language vocabulary, and a **concepts** box holds meanings. >> **Lexical links** connect the L1 and L2 words directly. **Conceptual links** connect each vocabulary to the concepts. || **The L1 lexicon is assumed to be larger and more strongly connected to relevant concepts**. >> In the model diagram, the L1 box is drawn bigger than the L2 box. >> The conceptual link from L1 to concepts is drawn as a thick, solid arrow, while the link from L2 to concepts is a thin, dashed line. || **The L2 lexicon is assumed to have strong forward connections to the L1 lexicon**. >> The lexical link from L2 to L1 is drawn as a solid arrow, while the link from L1 to L2 is dashed. >> **Activation can spread more readily from an L2 word to an L1 word than the other way**. || **This occurs because this direction is emphasized in learning**. >> When people learn a second language, they usually learn each new word by connecting it to its L1 translation. >> That practice builds a strong route from L2 words to L1 words, while the reverse route stays weaker. || The model’s arrows encode the history of learning. A second language is typically learned through translation, so each L2 word is first linked to its L1 equivalent before it is linked directly to a concept. >> This builds a strong link from L2 words to L1 words. >> The link from L2 words directly to concepts stays weaker, at least early in learning. || As proficiency grows, the L2 conceptual links can strengthen. The model therefore predicts that the balance between the two languages changes with experience.
21|The **Revised Hierarchical Model of Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** can explain the Marian and Spivey results. Consider what happens in the model during the task in **L2**, with the target “speaker.” >> **The concept for “matches” partially activates the Russian word for matches**, spichki, because L1 words are strongly connected to their concepts. >> Spichki is also partly activated by the sounds of the English word **speaker**, since the two words begin alike. >> **This activity feeds back to the conceptual activation for “matches.”** >> **Conceptual activation for matches leads to eye movements toward the L1 competitor**, the picture of matches. || The link between speaker and spichki is only **sound overlap**. >> Speakers and matches share no meaning. >> The extra activation of spichki strengthens the concept of matches, which draws the eyes to that picture. || **When the task is done in L1**, **the conceptual links with L2 might be too weak to create this kind of interference reliably**. >> In the Russian version, an English competitor would need strong links from the English word to its concept. >> In the model, L2 words have only weak conceptual links, too weak to drive looks to their objects. >> The unequal strength of the links explains why the effect appears in one direction and not the other. || The model explains the Marian and Spivey asymmetry through the strength of its links. Strong L1 conceptual links let a Russian word activate its object, which drives the eyes toward the matches. >> Weak L2 conceptual links mean an English word cannot do the same in a Russian task. >> The asymmetry in looks follows directly from the asymmetry in links.
22|The Revised Hierarchical Model makes a **counter-intuitive prediction** even for **“balanced bilinguals.”** A **balanced bilingual** is a person who is about equally skilled in both languages. >> One might expect balanced bilinguals to work like **“two monolinguals in one,”** with each language running separately. >> The model predicts the opposite. || In a balanced bilingual, **the conceptual links are similarly strong for both languages**. >> Every concept strongly activates its word in both languages. >> **Competitive dynamics and inhibition will be engaged all the time**. **Competitive dynamics** means that the two languages’ words constantly compete for selection. **Inhibition** is the suppression of the word from the language not being used. || This prediction **has led to a lot of work on bilinguals’ advantages in nonlinguistic cognitive control tasks**. >> **Cognitive control** is the ability to manage attention and suppress irrelevant responses. >> If bilinguals constantly practice suppressing one language, they might become better at controlling attention in general, including in tasks that have nothing to do with language. || Balanced bilinguals are especially interesting for the model. With strong links in both languages, both vocabularies become active whenever either is used. >> The bilingual must constantly select the right language and suppress the other. >> This constant practice in control could strengthen general cognitive control abilities.
23|The usual sequence of second-language learning runs along a timeline. >> A person **begins learning L1**. >> Later, the person **learns L2**. >> At the same time, the person **continues learning and using L1**. Both languages are kept in use from then on. || **What happens when someone stops using L1 after L2 is introduced?** >> In the second timeline, the person begins learning L1 and later learns L2, but “continue learning/using L1” is crossed out. >> L1 is no longer heard or used, while L2 takes over. || This situation separates the effect of learning a language early from the effect of continuing to use it. >> If early learning leaves a permanent foundation, traces of L1 should remain even without use. >> If continued use is needed, L1 might fade or disappear. || The two timelines separate two factors that usually go together. In the first timeline, the first language is learned early and kept in use. In the second, it is learned early but then dropped. >> Comparing the two reveals whether early learning alone leaves a lasting mark. >> Or whether continued use is needed to keep the first language alive.
24|**Pallier, Dehaene, Poline, LeBihan, Argenti, Dupoux and Mehler** studied **brain imaging of language plasticity in adopted adults**, asking **whether a second language can replace the first**. >> **Adults aged 20 to 32** who **had been adopted into French homes from Korean orphanages between the ages of 3 and 8** were tested. >> These adults had learned Korean as young children but then moved into homes where only French was spoken. >> They stopped using Korean entirely after adoption. || The researchers tested whether the adoptees **had any residual knowledge of Korean**. **Residual knowledge** is any remaining trace of the earlier language. >> Monolingual French speakers with no Korean experience served as a comparison group. >> If the adoptees did better than the French speakers on Korean-related tasks, some Korean knowledge would have survived. || The Korean adoptees are a natural test of the second timeline. They learned Korean as young children, then lost all contact with it after moving to France. >> By adulthood, they had spoken only French for many years. >> Any surviving Korean knowledge would reveal what early learning leaves behind when the language is no longer used.
25|Korean has a **three-way stop contrast**. A **stop** is a consonant made by fully blocking the airflow and then releasing it, such as /p/, /t/ or /k/. >> English and French distinguish two kinds of stops at each place, such as /b/ and /p/. >> Korean distinguishes three kinds at each place, which are **aspirated**, **plain** and **tense**. An **aspirated** stop is released with a strong puff of air. A **plain** stop has a lighter release. A **tense** stop is produced with a stiffened, tightened throat. || The contrast appears in three series, recorded in the UCLA Phonetics Archive. >> The **bilabial** series, made with the lips, has **pʰul**, “grass,” **pul**, “fire,” and **p’ul**, “horn.” >> The **dental** series, made with the tongue at the teeth, has **tʰal**, “mask,” **tal**, “moon,” and **t’al**, “daughter.” >> The **velar** series, made with the back of the tongue, has **kʰo**, “nose,” **kok**, “music,” and **k’ok**, “exactly.” || In each series, the three words are made at the same place in the mouth. >> They differ only in the type of stop, so a listener must hear the three-way difference to tell the words apart. >> French speakers do not have this three-way distinction, which makes it a good test of retained Korean knowledge. || The three-way stop contrast makes a sensitive test of retained Korean. French speakers have no such contrast, so they tend to hear the three Korean sounds as two. >> A Korean speaker hears all three as different. >> Adoptees who kept any Korean sound knowledge should hear the differences better than French speakers.
26|**Ventureyra and colleagues** found that **Korean adoptees in France were no better than monolingual French speakers** at discriminating the Korean contrasts. The graph plots the percentage of **“different” responses** for **adoptees**, **French native speakers** and **Korean native speakers**. >> **DC1** is the **plain–tense** contrast for p and k. Korean speakers responded “different” on about 96% of trials, while adoptees scored about 8% and French speakers about 3%. >> **DC2** is the **plain–aspirated** contrast for p and k. Korean speakers scored about 90%, adoptees about 19% and French speakers about 26%. >> **DC3** is the **tense–aspirated** contrast for p and k. Korean speakers scored about 95%, adoptees about 23% and French speakers about 20%. >> **DS** is the **plain–tense** contrast for s. Korean speakers scored about 79%, adoptees about 26% and French speakers about 23%. || Two control conditions checked general performance. >> **DV** is a **vowel** contrast. All three groups said “different” on about 97 to 99% of trials. >> **P** is the **same** condition, where the two sounds were identical. All groups rarely said “different,” at about 3 to 13%. || The adoptees could do the task, as shown by their near-perfect vowel discrimination and their correct “same” responses. >> They heard the Korean stop contrasts at the same level as people who had never heard Korean. >> Their early Korean experience left no measurable advantage for these sounds. || The control conditions confirm that the adoptees could do the task. All groups heard vowel differences easily and correctly judged identical sounds as the same. >> The difficulty was specific to the Korean stop contrasts. >> On those contrasts, the adoptees’ scores matched French speakers, far below Korean speakers.
27|In **a more extensive study**, **Pallier and colleagues** found that **adoptees were no better than monolingual French speakers** on three tasks. >> **(a) Distinguishing Korean from a collection of largely unrelated languages**. Participants rated on a scale of 1 to 7 how much each sentence sounded like Korean. Both groups rated Korean sentences around 4, but they also rated **Japanese** sentences around 4 to 5. **Polish** sentences got about 2, and **Swedish** and **Wolof** sentences got about 2.5 to 3. Adoptees did not pick out Korean any better than French speakers. >> **(b) Identifying the correct Korean translation of French words**. Adoptees scored about 55% correct and French speakers about 50%, which is close to chance for both. >> **(c) Determining whether a short excerpt was repeated from a recorded sentence they had just heard**. Both groups detected segments in French sentences about 95% of the time. For **Korean**, **Japanese** and **Polish** sentences, both groups scored about 80%, with no adoptee advantage for Korean. || **These are not standard psycholinguistic tasks**. >> Pallier and colleagues **were looking for any remaining trace of Korean**, using tasks designed to catch even weak knowledge. >> They **did not find any**. On every measure, the adoptees looked like people who had never learned Korean. || The three tasks were chosen to catch any remaining Korean knowledge, even weak or unconscious. Each task tapped a different kind of knowledge. >> Language identification tapped familiarity with the overall sound of Korean. >> Word recognition tapped memory for Korean vocabulary. >> Segment detection tapped sensitivity to Korean sound sequences. || The adoptees performed like French speakers on all three. Across this wide range of measures, no trace of Korean remained.
28|Pallier and colleagues also used brain imaging. **Listening to French activated the classical language network, including superior temporal and inferior frontal regions**, in both groups. >> The **superior temporal** region is the upper part of the temporal lobe, involved in processing speech sounds. >> The **inferior frontal** region is the lower part of the frontal lobe, including Broca’s area. || The comparisons subtracted brain activity for other languages from activity for French. >> **(A) French minus Polish**, **(B) French minus Japanese** and **(C) French minus Korean** were computed for the **French group** and the **Korean group**, the adoptees. >> In every comparison, French produced more activity in the language network than the foreign language did. || **Subtracting Korean from French might have revealed some difference between groups**, if there were **any residual processing ability for Korean left**. >> If adoptees still processed Korean as a language, Korean would activate their language network more than it did for French speakers. >> The French-minus-Korean difference would then be smaller in adoptees. >> **It was not**. The adoptees’ brains responded to Korean like a foreign language, much as they responded to Polish and Japanese. || The brain imaging results agree with the behavioral results. Adoptees processed French as their language and Korean as a foreign language, just as French speakers did. >> French activated the language network more than any of the foreign languages in both groups. >> The French-minus-Korean comparison looked the same in adoptees as in French speakers.
29|**L2–L1 interactions** can also be understood in terms of **competitive dynamics**. >> **L1 can be thought of as a set of connections within and between different brain regions**. >> These connections are built and maintained through use. || The same process that occurs in **monocular deprivation** in vision could apply to language. >> In monocular deprivation in mice, after **2–3 days** the deprived eye’s connections show **increased spine motility**. After **3–4 days**, there is **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration**, the breakdown of cut-off nerve fibers. After **more than a week**, the other eye’s fibers show **axon sprouting and new spine birth**, taking over the lost space. >> The eye that keeps receiving input gains control of connections that the deprived eye loses. || **If the L1 connections are never activated again, they could be “taken over” by L2**. >> L2 receives constant use, so its connections strengthen and spread. >> L1 receives no use, so its connections weaken and can be claimed by L2. >> On this view, L2 replaces L1 through competition for the same neural resources. || The competition idea from vision gives a possible mechanism for losing a first language. Connections that are used get stronger, while connections that are not used can be taken over by competitors. >> In vision, the open eye takes over the deprived eye’s connections. >> In language, the actively used L2 could take over the unused L1’s connections.
30|The answer to whether **a second language can replace the first** appears to be **“Apparently yes!”** The adoptees showed no behavioral or brain evidence of their childhood Korean. >> As far as these tests could measure, French had completely replaced Korean. || A remaining question is whether **traces of L1 are left behind that can be “salvaged.”** >> **Salvaging** a trace would mean recovering or using some leftover effect of the earlier language. >> A trace might not show up on a test of current ability but could still make relearning easier. || Testing current recognition and testing later learning ask different questions. >> One asks what the person can do with the language now. >> The other asks whether new learning benefits from something left over from the past. >> Relearning speed is the measure that reveals such a trace. || The phrase “apparently yes” sums up the adoptee findings. No test, behavioral or brain-based, found any trace of Korean. >> By every measure used, French had replaced Korean. >> The remaining question is whether anything survived that these tests could not detect.
31|**Terry Au and colleagues** at UCLA studied this question in **“Overhearing a language during childhood,”** by **Au, Knightly, Jun and Oh**. >> They studied **undergraduate students who overheard Spanish frequently as children but never learned to speak it**. >> **All participants started classroom instruction of Spanish at around age 14** and **were tested at the end of a second-year college Spanish course**. || The table compares **native speakers**, **childhood overhearers** and **typical late L2 learners** on several measures. >> **Childhood slang**, in percent correct. For **production**, native speakers scored 79, overhearers 17 and late learners 3. For **comprehension**, native speakers scored 88, overhearers 22 and late learners 3. >> **Voice onset time** in milliseconds. For **word-initial voiceless stops**, native speakers had 19.3, overhearers 19.6 and late learners 36.2. For **word-medial voiceless stops**, the values were 22.4, 23.4 and 31.2. Overhearers matched native speakers, while late learners produced the longer, English-like VOTs. >> **Percentage lenited**. **Lenition** is a softening of a consonant, which Spanish applies to voiced stops between vowels. For **word-initial voiced stops**, native speakers lenited 43%, overhearers 24% and late learners 1%. For **word-medial voiced stops**, the values were 72%, 63% and 23%. >> **Accent rating** on a 5-point scale, where higher means more native-like. For **voiceless stops**, native speakers scored 4.3, overhearers 3.5 and late learners 2.2. For **voiced stops**, 4.4, 3.3 and 2.7. || Other measures showed little or no overhearer advantage. >> **Morphology production**. For **gender agreement**, native speakers scored 93%, overhearers 70% and late learners 68%. For **number agreement**, 92%, 88% and 85%. >> **Grammaticality judgment**. **Accuracy** was 94% for native speakers, 64% for overhearers and 63% for late learners. **Reaction time** was 1,042 milliseconds for native speakers, 2,475 for overhearers and 2,209 for late learners. || Childhood overhearing gave a clear advantage in **pronunciation**. >> Overhearers produced Spanish sounds much more like native speakers than typical late learners did. >> Their grammar was no better than that of typical late learners. >> Early listening left traces in the sound system that were unmasked when the students later studied Spanish. || The overhearers’ pattern divides language into parts that early exposure helped and parts it did not. Pronunciation benefited strongly, while grammar did not. >> Their voice onset times for voiceless stops matched native speakers almost exactly. >> Their accent ratings fell between native speakers and typical late learners. >> Their grammaticality judgments matched typical late learners. || Early listening appears to leave lasting traces in the sound system, which can be unmasked when the language is later studied, while grammar seems to need more than overheard input.
32|Taking **the data from Korean adoptees together with the data from overhearers**, a picture emerges. >> **If L1 is removed from the environment entirely, L2 can appear to completely replace it**. The adoptees showed no trace of Korean on any test. >> **But this replacement is not necessarily complete**. **There may be some residual knowledge that can be “unmasked” with later learning**, as with the Spanish overhearers’ pronunciation. || The two populations also differ in **potentially important ways**. >> The adoptees were tested on their current knowledge without any new Korean instruction. >> The overhearers were tested after two years of Spanish classes, which gave hidden knowledge a chance to show itself. >> These and other differences between the groups make the two sets of results hard to compare directly. || Putting the two populations together suggests a nuanced answer. Complete loss of exposure can make a first language seem to vanish, while traces may still remain that show up when the language is learned again. >> The adoptees showed no trace on tests of current ability. >> The overhearers showed a clear advantage once they began learning Spanish.
33|Several **potentially important differences between these populations** could explain why one group showed traces of early language and the other did not. >> **Korean is rarely heard or encountered in France**. **Spanish is common in Los Angeles**, so overhearers may have kept hearing it throughout their lives. >> A **“heritage language”** spoken by other family members differs from **a language not spoken by anyone you know**. The overhearers often heard Spanish from relatives, while the adoptees lost all contact with Korean speakers. >> **Cognates** are common between Spanish and English but rare between Korean and French. Shared vocabulary could help overhearers relearn. >> **Shared writing system**. Spanish and English use the same alphabet, while Korean uses a different script from French. || **This is a general issue** with research on **second language acquisition and bilingualism**. >> **Languages and learning situations differ in many, many ways that are hard, perhaps impossible, to control experimentally**. >> Researchers cannot assign people to grow up hearing particular languages in particular settings. >> Conclusions about early language experience must take these uncontrolled differences into account. || The differences between the populations are large enough to explain the different results. The overhearers kept hearing Spanish throughout their lives, often from family, while the adoptees had no further contact with Korean. >> Continued exposure could have kept the overhearers’ Spanish sound knowledge active. >> Similarities between Spanish and English could also have helped them. || Untangling these factors would require comparing populations that differ in only one way, which is rarely possible with real language histories.
