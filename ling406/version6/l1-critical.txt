2|A strict **critical period** is a developmental window with a programmed opening and closing. The relevant experience can shape the system during that interval. Under the strict definition, the same sensitivity is unavailable before the window opens or after it closes. || A **sensitive period**, also called an optimal period, is an interval when learning is easier or more effective. Its boundaries can be gradual, and learning remains possible outside it. Experience can also influence how sensitivity develops. || The distinction is between a loss of possibility and a reduction in effectiveness. Early advantages followed by some capacity for later improvement fit a sensitive-period account. Poor ordinary learning at a later age does not by itself establish that all later change is impossible.
3|**Amblyopia** is poor vision in one eye despite normal peripheral function. A developmental problem can leave a lasting impairment even after the original optical disturbance is corrected. The eye’s ability to supply input and the brain’s ability to use it are therefore different parts of the condition. || **Strabismus** misaligns the eyes, while a **cataract** blocks light through the lens. **Anisometropia** gives the eyes different refractive properties, so their input is not equally focused. Each can disrupt the coordinated visual experience available during development. || Correcting the physical problem does not automatically restore the organization of visual processing. The system may continue using one eye less effectively. A temporary disturbance in the incoming signal can therefore have consequences that outlast the disturbance itself.
4|The two eyes’ inputs **compete for influence in visual cortex**. When one supplies abnormal information, the other can gain influence over connections that would otherwise support the disadvantaged eye. The pathways develop in relation to one another rather than independently. || During **monocular deprivation**, input from one eye is reduced or abnormal. After **2–3 days**, dendritic spines become more mobile. Around **3–4 days**, spine loss and degeneration occur. After **more than a week**, axon sprouting and new spine formation contribute to reorganization. || **Dendritic spines** receive input at connections between neurons, while **axon sprouting** adds branches through which neurons communicate. These changes alter which input effectively drives the system. Restoring normal input can leave the previously deprived eye at a competitive disadvantage, so ordinary binocular experience does not immediately undo the imbalance. || The stronger eye can continue driving the reorganized system when both eyes are open again. That competition explains why repairing the input does not necessarily repair the later function. The lasting disadvantage concerns how cortical connections now use the restored signal, rather than an ongoing absence of light at the eye.
5|**Patching the stronger eye intermittently** gives the weaker eye more opportunity to drive visual processing. The patch reduces the stronger input’s ongoing advantage. Treatment therefore uses competition between the pathways rather than simply correcting the original optical disturbance. || **Plasticity** is the capacity to change with experience. Increasing use of the weaker input can encourage functional improvement because the system can reorganize how it responds. Patching the better eye creates conditions favoring the disadvantaged pathway. || This treatment is most effective early and becomes less effective with age. By late childhood, around **ten years** in the comparison, the benefit can be very limited. The same manipulation therefore has different effects depending on the developmental state of the visual system.
6|**Levi and Polat (1996)** found improvement in adults with amblyopia after **long-term, intensive perceptual training**. Participants used the affected eye to perform controlled visual judgments. Some were in their **fifties**, beyond the usual childhood treatment window. || Learning occurred over **tens of thousands of trials**. Progress included periods of faster change and periods with little improvement. Benefits extended beyond performance on the practiced task to broader visual function. || The adult system therefore retained some **experience-dependent plasticity**. Limited success with an ordinary late treatment does not mean that no kind of experience can produce change. The type and intensity of training matter when assessing how much learning remains possible.
7|**Li, Ngo, Nguyen, Lam, and Levi (2008)** reported benefits from video-game experience in adults with amblyopia. Improvement involved **visual acuity**, the ability to resolve fine detail, and **visual attention**, the selection of relevant visual information. These were changes in an already impaired adult system. || The participants’ earlier visual development had left a persistent deficit. Improvement therefore did not simply reflect normal childhood growth. Intensive experience was associated with better function after the age when ordinary treatment is usually most effective. || These findings support remaining adult **plasticity**. An early optimal period can become less favorable without eliminating every possibility of improvement. The result concerns functional change under particular conditions, rather than an absence of developmental limits.
8|An early **peripheral disturbance** can alter how the brain organizes visual input. One eye may supply reduced or abnormal information while connections are developing. The resulting imbalance can remain even after that eye becomes physically capable of providing normal input. || This is **experience-dependent organization**. The pattern of input changes which connections are effective, and competition can give the stronger eye a lasting advantage. Removing the original disturbance does not automatically reverse the history of neural change. || Treatment works best within an early developmental window, but specialized adult training can still help. Amblyopia therefore combines strong early sensitivity with some later capacity for learning. That pattern fits a **sensitive period** more closely than an absolute claim that change becomes impossible after childhood.
9|**Lenneberg** connected neurological development with a proposed **language acquisition device**. This refers to a biologically supported capacity for acquiring language. The developmental question concerns whether changes in the brain alter how effectively that capacity can use experience. || **Early language deprivation** provides evidence about people first encountering an accessible language unusually late. Relevant histories include neglected children and Deaf children without early access to sign language. These differ from ordinary acquisition beginning early in life. || The issue is the timing of a **first language**, not simply the size of someone’s later vocabulary. Comparing early and delayed exposure tests how developmental state relates to the eventual outcome. A biological account must explain how age and available language interact during learning.
10|In **Biological Foundations of Language (1967)**, **Lenneberg** related language acquisition to maturation. Development follows a broadly ordered sequence, with early words preceding telegraphic combinations and longer spontaneous sentences. **Telegraphic speech** preserves important content words while leaving out some grammatical material. || Repeated vocal patterns and early play with sounds occur before a clear vocabulary. Around **twelve months**, recurring forms and emerging words such as mama and dada appear. By roughly **eighteen months**, vocabulary is clearer while complex babbling and comprehension continue to expand. || Lenneberg interpreted this ordered progression as compatible with an unfolding **biological program**. Language changes occurred alongside wider developmental changes, rather than being attributed only to the accumulated quantity of input. The proposal assigns a role to the changing state of the child as well as to experience.
11|Lenneberg’s evidence about typical brain development came largely from **post-mortem tissue**, examined after death. The measurements did not follow the same children continuously. In the neuron-size comparison, observations were absent across a long interval from roughly two to eighteen years. || **Neuron size** increased rapidly early in development, together with changes in **packing density**, meaning how closely cells occupy the tissue. Lenneberg proposed that these early changes helped prepare the brain for language acquisition. The favorable window would open after the initial rapid growth. || The proposal relates learning to maturation, but the measurements and the interpretation are different kinds of evidence. Sampling gaps do not establish that a continuous biological switch was directly observed. The age pattern must be interpreted in light of which developmental stages were actually measured.
12|Language outcomes after major brain injury can be much better in children than adults. **Hemispherectomy** removes one cerebral hemisphere to treat severe seizures. Substantial language development can follow early removal even when the removed hemisphere is the **left** one. || Historical childhood cases often had speech that was unaffected or improved after the operation. A case around puberty improved more slowly, while adult left-hemisphere removal was associated with permanent aphasia. Adult right-hemisphere removal had a different language outcome. || **Cerebral dominance** refers to specialization of the hemispheres for particular functions. Lenneberg proposed that language organization is less fixed early, permitting more reassignment after damage. The age difference concerns changing capacity for reorganization, not a claim that extensive childhood injury has no consequences.
13|A **perinatal stroke** occurs before birth or within **28 days after birth**. It damages tissue while the systems supporting language are still developing. Outcomes are generally better than after comparable adult injury, but development is not always unaffected. || Early language difficulties follow damage to **either hemisphere**. Some initial problems can be greater after right-hemisphere injury. This differs from simply applying the usual adult pattern of language specialization to a newborn brain. || Later performance often remains in the **low-normal range**. Better recovery than in adult aphasia does not necessarily mean complete catch-up. The trajectory combines substantial developmental flexibility with possible lasting costs, so reorganization and absence of impairment are different claims.
14|**Curtiss and colleagues (1974)** studied **Genie** after severe early abuse, isolation, and restricted language access. She was first studied at **thirteen years and seven months**. Her confinement began around **twenty months**, leaving a long period without ordinary linguistic and social experience. || She was physically restrained, minimally cared for, and punished for making sounds. Language deprivation occurred alongside many other developmental hardships. Accessible speech and normal interaction were both severely limited. || On admission, she was malnourished, did not speak, and had major physical difficulties. Her later acquisition therefore began after a history affecting more than language input alone. The case provides evidence about delayed learning within those circumstances rather than isolating age of exposure as the only variable.
15|Across **four years of observation**, Genie learned to understand **hundreds of words**, some **morphology**, and several **prepositions**. Morphology concerns meaningful word parts. Plurals were a relative strength, indicating some ability to use changes in form to distinguish number. || Preposition tests used actions with objects rather than requiring spoken definitions. **“Put the red box behind the blue box”** tested understanding of a spatial relationship. Her placement of the boxes supplied the response. || Correct answers exceeded errors for relations including **on, in, over, under, in front of, in back of, next to, and behind**. Learning therefore extended beyond object labels. Genie acquired some ways of expressing relationships among objects as well as vocabulary and grammatical forms.
16|**Genie’s comprehension developed more than her spoken production**. She understood information she could not readily express. Her history of punishment for vocalizing could also affect how much knowledge appeared in speech, so output alone did not capture all learning. || **Grammar remained weaker than vocabulary**. Instructions such as **“Show me the fork and pencil”** and **“Show me the crayon or the knife”** tested how conjunctions changed the required response. Knowing the individual object names was insufficient to distinguish those instructions. || Genie initially treated **or** much like **and**. She later distinguished the words without consistently understanding the either/or relationship. Her strong performance with and contrasted with frequent errors on or, identifying a difficulty with the relation expressed by the connecting word despite substantial vocabulary growth.
17|Genie learned much more **vocabulary than grammar**. This uneven development has been interpreted as evidence that delayed first-language acquisition particularly affects grammatical learning. Learning many words did not bring equally rapid improvement in the relationships tested by grammatical instructions. || Her history also included abuse, neglect, and possible learning difficulties. These accompanied delayed exposure and could contribute to the outcome. The observed pattern therefore does not isolate age of first language from all other developmental influences. || Funding ended after **four years**, limiting the period of systematic observation. The evidence concerns what she learned within that interval rather than proving what any longer period would have produced. The uneven outcome and the qualifications attached to this unusual history both belong to the scientific account.
18|**Mayberry, Lock, and Kazmi (2002)** compared late first-language learning with later acquisition after an early language. Deaf children first encountering **American Sign Language, or ASL, at 9–15 years** showed persistent difficulty. They were acquiring a first language rather than adding another one to an established linguistic system. || Children who became deaf **after acquiring a spoken language** did better when they learned ASL later. Both groups encountered sign relatively late, but only one had early language. Their comparison separates timing of first-language experience from timing of sign-language exposure itself. || Adults without early language were poorer at recalling complex ASL sentences. English grammaticality judgments also favored groups with an early signed or spoken language. The advantage therefore concerned accessible early linguistic experience across modalities, not a requirement that the early and later languages both be spoken or both signed. || The comparison group had early language but did not have to retain normal hearing throughout childhood. Their better later sign learning connects the benefit with prior linguistic experience rather than continued access to spoken sound. An accessible early signed or spoken language can provide a foundation for later acquisition.
19|Outcomes after **stroke and hemispherectomy** differ between childhood and adulthood. A developing system can support language after damage that would produce more severe adult impairment. This supports a developmental role in the capacity for recovery and reorganization. || **Absence of input does not indefinitely preserve an unused first-language window**. Delayed access can leave persistent difficulties even after language becomes available. Maturation does not simply wait until the relevant experience begins. || The evidence comes from unusual human histories involving deprivation or injury, often with additional influences. There is no equivalent experimental animal model that reproduces human language acquisition. An explanation of biological constraints must account for both the developmental patterns and the qualifications attached to these cases.
