2|**Lenneberg (1967)** linked language-learning conditions to the development of **cerebral dominance**. This means increasing specialization of the hemispheres. **Language laterality** describes how strongly language functions depend on one side rather than the other. || His proposal combined typical language milestones with observations about neural development and injury. Children often achieved better language outcomes than adults after extensive damage or surgery. Such outcomes suggested that the organization supporting language changes with age. || The hypothesis concerns the brain’s **maturational state**. Greater early flexibility could allow functions to develop through different tissue after injury, while a more specialized adult system has fewer possibilities for reassignment. This is a proposed change in organization, rather than simply an effect of how many words a learner has heard.
3|Evidence about **first-language sensitive periods** often comes from rare injuries or unusual deprivation histories. Such cases differ in many individual circumstances. Delayed language can occur alongside changes in health, social experience, or other developmental conditions. || **Second-language acquisition** is more common and begins across a broad range of ages. Larger groups provide variation in starting age and eventual attainment. Researchers can compare these patterns without relying entirely on a few exceptional first-language cases. || The question is whether age at the beginning of learning predicts later proficiency. L2 learners already possess a first language, so the comparison is not identical to delayed first-language acquisition. Both prior linguistic knowledge and later experience must be considered when interpreting an age relationship.
4|**Johnson and Newport** tested **46 Korean- or Chinese-speaking immigrants** who arrived in the United States between **ages 3 and 39**. They had substantial subsequent English experience. The study compared eventual proficiency rather than only progress during the first weeks of exposure. || Participants listened to English sentences and judged their grammaticality across several constructions. Earlier arrivals generally performed better. Before puberty, increasing arrival age strongly predicted lower accuracy, while adult-arrival scores were lower but much more variable. || Measured differences in English experience, motivation, self-consciousness, and American identification did not explain the reported pattern. Johnson and Newport interpreted it as evidence for an effect of **maturational state**. The observed age–performance relationship and that proposed biological explanation are related but distinct parts of the argument.
5|A **grammaticality judgment task, or GJT**, asks whether a sentence follows the language’s grammar. Johnson and Newport used an **untimed** task with recorded sentences. A participant judged the form rather than simply deciding whether the described event was sensible. || **“Two pigs” versus “two pig”** tests plural marking. **“Is speaking” versus “is speak”** tests the required verb form. **“Reading a book” versus “reading book”** tests the article, while **“The boys” versus “A boys”** tests another determiner relationship. || **“Larry went home” versus “went the home”** tests a context in which an article is inappropriate. **“When will Sam fix his car?”** and **“What do they sell at the corner store?”** test English question structure against versions lacking the required auxiliary arrangement. The score combines several grammatical demands rather than one repeated rule.
6|**Age of arrival, or AOA**, related differently to accuracy in early and late arrivals. For the **3–15-year** range, the correlation was about **−0.87**. Later arrival strongly accompanied lower grammaticality scores within that group. || For **17–39-year** arrivals, the correlation was about **−0.16**. Scores were lower overall but widely dispersed, with only a weak ordering by arrival age. An older adult arrival did not reliably score below every younger adult arrival. || The pattern is therefore not one equally strong decline throughout life. It combines a pronounced childhood gradient with substantial variation among later learners. Keeping those ranges separate preserves what the data actually contribute to the developmental argument.
7|Learners arriving **by about age eight** often performed near the native level on the grammaticality task. Later childhood arrival was associated with progressively lower scores. The early sample therefore contained both a very high-performing youngest range and a decline further into childhood. || Adult-arrival scores varied much more without the same strong age ordering. The description **“nearly random distribution”** refers to that weak relationship between adult arrival age and score. It does not mean that these learners answered the test at chance. || Adults with similar starting ages could differ substantially. Early learning had a population advantage, while later performance left more individual variation to explain. A description based only on average early and late scores would miss the different relationships within those groups.
8|**Johnson and Newport** interpreted the childhood decline as a reduction in **language-learning capacity with development**. Early exposure occurred in a more favorable maturational state. Later learners encountered English after those conditions had changed. || The timing did not reproduce every feature of **Lenneberg’s** proposal. His account relied on sparse neural evidence across much of childhood. The grammaticality study supplied a behavioral pattern rather than directly measuring the proposed change in the brain. || They related the wide spread among late learners to greater reliance on **individual learning abilities**. Under this interpretation, successful later acquisition depends less uniformly on the processes supporting ordinary early acquisition. The account connects a regular early advantage with more varied routes to later success.
9|The **exercise hypothesis** proposes an early superior capacity for acquiring languages. If it is unused during the favorable period, it declines with maturation. If exercised through early language acquisition, it remains available for learning further languages throughout life. || The **maturational-state hypothesis** also proposes an early advantage, but its decline occurs even after successful childhood language learning. Using the ability does not permanently preserve the same developmental state. Early L1 acquisition can therefore coexist with reduced later L2-learning capacity. || The disagreement concerns what early use protects. Exercise predicts continued availability after a normal first language. Maturational state predicts that the learning conditions still change with development. Later learners who already possess L1 provide an important comparison between these predictions.
10|**Implicit knowledge** supports language use without conscious rule explanation. In **Ellis’s (2005)** distinction, it is procedural and accessed automatically during fluent performance. A person can use a construction appropriately without being able to state the rule governing it. || **Explicit knowledge** consists of consciously available grammatical information. It can be verbalized and used through deliberate, controlled processing. In this theoretical comparison, learning such information remains possible at any age. || An **untimed GJT** can permit **metalinguistic knowledge**, meaning conscious knowledge about language. A participant may inspect the form and recall a taught rule. The proposed developmental restriction concerns implicit acquisition, so success on a task drawing on both kinds of knowledge is not a pure measure of automatic language use. || A grammatical answer can be reached by automatically recognizing the form or by consciously applying a rule. The identical response does not identify which route produced it. Test timing and emphasis on meaning or form affect the opportunity to use those routes.
11|Different tasks draw on different kinds of linguistic knowledge. An **untimed GJT** allows deliberate attention to form and consultation of explicit rules. Imitation and oral narrative emphasize meaning under more pressured conditions, leaving different opportunities for conscious checking. || In **Ellis (2005)**, formal instruction was related to untimed judgments of ungrammatical items, with a correlation around **0.27**. Starting age had a significant relationship with the **timed GJT**, around **−0.23**, but little relationship with the relevant untimed judgments. || The pattern connects test design with the knowledge available for answering. Unlimited time can make a task more sensitive to taught rules than fluent performance is. Interpreting an age effect therefore requires identifying whether the measure favors automatic use, deliberate analysis, or a mixture.
12|**Typological similarity** concerns how similarly languages organize their forms. An L1 structure resembling an L2 structure can provide positive transfer. A different starting system can instead leave the learner acquiring distinctions that prior language use did not require. || Johnson and Newport tested **Chinese- and Korean-speaking learners of English**. Those pairings do not offer the same transfer opportunities as every other first language. An observed age pattern belongs to learners bringing particular linguistic systems to English. || **Cognates, loan words, and grammatical similarities** can each supply familiar information. Language pairing therefore contributes to what must be learned and what is already partly supported. Age cannot be interpreted independently of these differences in the learning problem.
13|English constructions differ in difficulty for later learners. **Progressive -ing and word order** are relatively easy in the reported comparison. **Determiners and plural marking** produce larger costs for some later-arriving groups. Age does not reduce every grammatical ability by the same amount. || The tested categories also include **verb subcategorization**, meaning which complements a verb permits, along with past tense, pronouns, particles, auxiliaries, third-person singular forms, and questions. Each introduces requirements that can differ from the learner’s L1. || Some patterns resemble child development, while others fit **transfer**. Chinese lacks the same determiner system as English, creating a particular learning demand. Questions that develop relatively late in children can be easier for older L2 learners. The required construction and existing knowledge both affect the outcome.
14|**Birdsong and Molis (2001)** tested **Spanish-speaking learners of English**. Their age-of-arrival pattern differed from Johnson and Newport’s Korean- and Chinese-speaking sample. The relationship changed when the language pairing changed. || Spanish-speaking early arrivals remained high-performing without the same strong childhood decline. Their early correlation was about **−0.23**. Among late arrivals, the negative relationship was stronger, about **−0.69**, unlike the weak adult relationship in the earlier study. || A single fixed maturational cutoff does not explain both patterns by itself. Transfer opportunities and learning histories must also be considered. The comparison concerns differences in the shape of the age–attainment relationship, beyond a general claim that starting earlier is advantageous.
15|**Later L2 learners generally have lower ultimate attainment**, but the measured outcome depends on the constructions, task, and language pairing. A grammaticality score describes performance on particular items under particular conditions. It does not directly represent every component of language ability. || **Age of acquisition** is related to the amount and quality of experience. **Transfer** changes which new distinctions are supported by existing knowledge. Learners sharing an arrival age can differ in both their linguistic starting point and their subsequent opportunities to use the language. || **Competence and performance** must also be distinguished. Deliberate rule checking and rapid fluent use can produce different outcomes. Explaining age-related success therefore requires identifying which knowledge and demands contribute to the observed score, rather than treating all tests as one uniform measure of maturation.
