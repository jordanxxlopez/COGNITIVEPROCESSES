2|**Word segmentation** is the process of finding words in continuous speech. Spoken language does not contain a reliable equivalent of written spaces. Listeners recover recurring forms from a **“sea of sounds”** even when the acoustic signal does not clearly mark each beginning and ending. || Silence is not a dependable word boundary. A brief closure of the vocal tract can create silence inside a word during consonant production. Treating every silent interval as a boundary would therefore divide some words incorrectly. || Segmentation requires using evidence about how sounds group together. Identifying a recurring sound unit is also different from knowing its meaning. A learner can begin detecting a word-shaped sequence before establishing what object, action, or property it names.
3|Some words are heard **in isolation**, including **“Hello,” “No,” “Please,” “Go,” and “Stop.”** Their boundaries are relatively accessible because the whole utterance contains one word. Many words occur mainly within longer utterances, so isolated examples cannot explain all segmentation. || **Phonotactics** concerns which sound combinations are common or permitted in a language. Unusual combinations in wordboundary or speechcode can support a boundary, although movement cautions against treating every apparent join as two whole words. These cues concern learned sound patterns rather than visible spaces. || **Stress** supplies another cue because many English words begin with a strong syllable. **Jusczyk and colleagues (1999)** familiarized **7.5-month-olds** with kingdom or device. Infants preferred passages containing the initially stressed kingdom but not the same way for device, stressed on its second syllable. Detecting a familiar form in running speech depended on how stress aligned with its boundary. || Stress is not a universal solution to segmentation. Different languages organize stress differently, and not every English word begins with a strong syllable. The kingdom–device contrast identifies how a useful regularity can favor one word pattern while leaving another harder to recognize in connected speech.
4|In **pretty baby**, pre strongly predicts the following ty, while ty can be followed by the beginning of many different words. The within-word sequence is therefore more reliable than the sequence crossing into baby. Differences in predictability can supply evidence about where words belong. || **Transitional probability** is the probability of a next syllable given the syllable just heard. The frequency of the pair is divided by the frequency of its first member. A pair occurring often is not necessarily highly predictable if its first syllable also has many other continuations. || **f** means frequency of occurrence. The relationship is \[\frac{f(\mathrm{pretty})}{f(\mathrm{pre})}\gg\frac{f(\mathrm{tyba})}{f(\mathrm{ty})}\] Within pretty, pre predicts ty much more reliably than ty predicts ba across the word boundary. The stronger internal relationship supports treating pretty as one unit within the continuous sequence.
5|One syllable tends to predict the next more reliably **within a word** than across a word boundary. A learner can use this difference to group sounds. It is a statistical cue rather than a physical gap that fixes every possible interpretation. || **“What’s that in the road a/head?”** permits competing divisions involving a and head. **“I scream, you scream, we all scream for ice cream”** similarly contains overlapping sound sequences grouped into different words. The same acoustic material can support more than one segmentation. || **Lower transitional probability** can suggest a boundary, while stronger internal transitions favor a unit. A cue changes the relative support for those possibilities. It does not eliminate the need to interpret how the sequence fits the utterance.
6|**Saffran and colleagues (1996)** created an **artificial language** to test statistical segmentation without the usual mixture of natural-language cues. It contained repeated three-syllable forms such as **tokibu, gikoba, and gopila**. The input was constructed so that sequence regularities supplied the relevant grouping information. || The stream was **monotone**. Pitch, duration, and amplitude did not systematically mark the intended boundaries. Simple **consonant–vowel syllables** also prevented unusual boundary combinations from providing an alternative cue. || There were **no pauses between words**. Listeners heard one continuous syllable stream rather than separately presented items. Detecting the intended groups therefore required using relationships within the sequence, rather than relying on silence, stress, or a special boundary sound.
7|In **tokibugikobagopila**, recurring syllables occur without heard spaces between the intended units. A longer sequence such as **tokibugikobagopilatipolu** places several groups directly next to one another. The learner receives the stream, not an already separated list of words. || Repetition makes particular syllable transitions reliable. The syllables within tokibu or gopila recur together even when the surrounding units change. Their relationship can be learned without an explicit signal announcing each boundary. || Segmentation therefore involves recovering organization from the sequence itself. The evidence lies in which syllables regularly follow one another. A recurring group can become recognizable even though no pause marks where it begins or ends.
8|The forms **tokibu, gikoba, gopila, and tipolu** preserve their internal order across repetitions. The words next to them can change. This makes the relationships within each form more reliable than many relationships across its boundaries. || A first syllable strongly predicts its next syllable within an intended word. At the end, several different words can follow, making the next syllable less predictable. This contrast supplies evidence about which adjacent sounds form a stable group. || The signal still contains no actual spaces. The learner uses differences in predictability to infer units within it. Knowing that a sequence belongs together is therefore an outcome of learning its statistical structure, rather than detecting gaps that were already present.
9|**Transitional probability must be distinguished from raw frequency**. A whole sequence can occur often because its syllables reliably belong together or because it repeatedly spans a boundary. A **part-word** is a test sequence crossing an intended word boundary. || In the given stream, **GOPILA and BU-GIKO each occur twice**. Their full-string frequencies match, but the transitions inside them differ. **GO–PI and PI–LA** each have probability \[\frac{2}{2}=1\] Every relevant first syllable is followed by the specified next syllable. || **BU–GI** has probability \[\frac{2}{4}=0.5\] BU occurs four times but leads to GI only twice. The part-word therefore contains a weaker transition despite matching GOPILA’s total count. Discriminating the two kinds of sequence requires sensitivity to conditional predictability, not merely familiarity with how often the whole string occurred. || Half of the intended words occurred more frequently than the others. Choosing frequency-matched part-words prevented simple whole-string repetition from distinguishing every test item. The learner had to be sensitive to how the syllables predicted each other within those equally frequent strings.
10|Infants **looked longer toward part-words** than toward words from the artificial language. The test sequences were matched in total occurrence frequency. Their different treatment therefore cannot be explained simply by hearing one full sequence fewer times. || This was a **novelty preference**. A boundary-spanning sequence was treated as different from a unit whose syllables reliably belonged together. Looking supplied a response without requiring infants to report the word boundaries. || The learned distinction depended on how reliably one syllable predicted the next. Infants extracted organization from a continuous stream and used it when hearing the test items. Statistical segmentation therefore involves relationships among occurrences, beyond a count of repeated whole strings.
11|Learning a word’s **referent** means learning what it names. Recognizing the sound pattern cat and knowing which entity cat refers to are different accomplishments. One concerns the form in speech, and the other concerns the connection between that form and meaning. || **Segmentation** can recover a recurring unit without identifying its referent. A scene contains many possible objects, parts, properties, and actions. The presence of a word during that scene does not itself specify which candidate the speaker intends. || Word learning therefore requires additional evidence about reference. The child must connect the recognized form with the speaker’s intended meaning. A reliable syllable sequence solves a problem of grouping sounds, but not automatically the problem of what that group names.
12|The **inductive problem of early word learning** arises because an encounter supports many possible meanings. A word heard near a cat could mean **CAT, ANIMAL, PET, or FELIX**. These candidates refer to different levels of category or to one particular individual. || The word could instead refer to **FURRY, ALIVE, WALKING, WHISKERS, or LEG**. Those candidates concern a property, a state, an action, or part of the animal. Seeing the same event does not uniquely select one interpretation. || The **hypothesis space** is the set of possible meanings compatible with the experience. **Quine’s gavagai problem** captures this uncertainty about reference. Learning requires evidence or constraints that narrow the possibilities instead of assuming that a single word and scene make the intended meaning obvious.
13|**Joint attention** occurs when two people share a focus on an object or event. An adult and child coordinate what the interaction concerns. This provides information about reference beyond whatever happens to attract the child’s attention independently. || **Gaze following** develops around **nine months**. Following the adult’s eyes can reveal which object the adult is attending to. When a label is spoken, that focus provides evidence about the object it may name. || Shared attention narrows the possible referents of the word. The child can relate the expression to the adult’s focus rather than associating it equally with everything nearby. The useful information concerns a relationship among the speaker, the learner, and the object.
14|In **Baldwin and colleagues’ joint-attention condition**, an adult interacted with an infant and labeled the object occupying the infant’s attention. The label occurred within an exchange involving a shared object. The adult’s behavior provided a visible connection between the sound and its intended referent. || In the **no-joint-attention condition**, a voice behind a screen supplied the label while the object remained present. Both conditions contained a word and an object, but only one included visible coordination with the speaker. || **Eighteen-month-olds** learned the word in the joint-attention condition but not in the other condition. Temporal co-occurrence did not produce the same result by itself. How the speaker organized the interaction affected the mapping learned from the available experience. || A visible speaker gives the learner evidence that the sound is being used to refer to that object. The screened voice supplies temporal overlap without the same coordinated exchange. Their different outcomes distinguish a social relationship supporting reference from the mere presence of the relevant sound and object.
15|**Intentional cues** indicate what a speaker is trying to refer to. The adult’s gaze can identify a different object from the one currently occupying the child’s attention. A new word therefore should not automatically be assigned to whatever the child happens to be handling. || Baldwin tested **16–17-month-olds and 18–19-month-olds** with two labeling conditions. In **follow-in labeling**, the adult looked at the child’s current toy. In **discrepant labeling**, the adult looked toward a toy in a bucket instead. || The contrast separates the learner’s own interest from the adult’s indicated referent. Gaze can supply evidence about which of the available objects the speaker means. The intended mapping depends on the social information accompanying the word, not merely on which objects are present.
16|**Gaze direction** helps identify the intended referent when several objects are available. The adult can look at an object while labeling it, linking the spoken form with a particular focus. The child can use that behavior to distinguish the candidate from other items nearby. || The speaker’s focus can differ from the child’s. Following it therefore requires using information about another person rather than simply continuing one’s own attention. A label can concern an object the child was not initially watching. || This cue reduces uncertainty without making the word’s meaning transparent from its sound. The learner combines the expression with evidence about the speaker’s intention. The resulting choice is more specific than associating the new form with everything occurring during the utterance.
17|**Affective and behavioral cues** include the speaker’s emotional expressions and actions. A reaction can indicate whether a candidate object satisfies what the speaker is trying to do. The child can use that information when deciding which object a new word names. || In **Akhtar and colleagues’ study**, infants used adults’ facial expressions to identify the intended object. The relevant information was the adult’s response to the candidate, rather than its mere presence near the spoken word. || Several objects can coincide with an utterance, but the adult may treat only one as the intended target. Interpreting that reaction narrows the alternatives. Word learning therefore includes tracking the purpose of the exchange as well as forming associations between sounds and objects.
18|An adult’s **emotional reaction** can distinguish the intended object from other candidates. A response indicating success or satisfaction can connect one object with the goal of the interaction. The learner can use that connection to interpret a new label. || **Intention is conveyed through more than gaze direction**. Facial expressions and behavior also supply evidence about what the speaker means. The word can occur near several objects without all of them receiving equal support as its referent. || The useful information lies in how the adult responds to each candidate. Relating the label to that response reduces the word’s **hypothesis space**, meaning its possible interpretations. The child learns from the organization of the exchange rather than from co-occurrence alone.
19|A **default word-learning strategy** favors one interpretation unless other evidence supports an alternative. It narrows the set of meanings considered initially. A default is a useful starting preference, not an absolute rule governing every word. || The **whole-object assumption** favors assigning a new label to an entire object. A scene also contains parts, properties, and actions, any of which could in principle be named. The assumption gives the whole object greater initial support than those alternatives. || This reduces the **hypothesis space** without requiring the child to test every candidate equally. Further evidence can establish that a word refers to a part or property instead. The strategy supports an early inference while leaving room for learning a different kind of meaning.
20|**Mutual exclusivity**, associated with **Markman (1989)**, is a default expectation that an object has one name. If one candidate already has a known label, an unfamiliar label is more likely to refer to another object. Existing vocabulary helps constrain the new mapping. || The strategy develops during the **second half of the second year**. Its operation depends on what the child already knows, because a familiar object is excluded only if its name is available to that learner. The sound of the new word alone does not select the referent. || An unnamed object therefore becomes a stronger candidate when contrasted with an already named one. This is a default inference rather than a claim that an object can never receive another expression. Its role is to reduce uncertainty during the initial interpretation.
21|Under **mutual exclusivity**, an unfamiliar label is preferentially assigned to an object lacking a known name. The known name of another object makes that object less likely to be the intended target. The choice depends on a comparison among the available candidates. || **Prior vocabulary** determines which candidates count as already named. Two children could therefore make different inferences from the same scene if they know different words. The strategy uses learned information rather than treating every new encounter independently. || Combining the novel label with existing names narrows its possible referents. The learner does not have to infer meaning directly from the word’s sound. A relationship among vocabulary, objects, and the speaker’s expression supplies evidence for the otherwise unnamed candidate.
22|The length of a child’s utterance does not directly establish how it was generated. **Menn** distinguishes selecting relevant words, repeating a memorized **formula**, making a productive combination, and using a **stable construction**. These can produce similarly short expressions through different kinds of organization. || **“Up!”**, **“Pick you up!”**, and **“Mommy up!”** differ in length, but the longer expression might be stored as a unit. The shorter one could involve selecting and combining separate elements. Counting words therefore cannot by itself distinguish memorization from productive grammar. || Context helps identify what the child intends and how the expression functions. A stable construction can also differ from adult grammar. Even with detailed observation, a particular utterance may leave uncertainty about its source, so production length is an incomplete measure of linguistic knowledge.
23|**Comprehension can develop beyond what a child regularly produces**. A one-word speaker can use basic **transitive word order** to interpret an event involving an actor and someone affected by the action. Limited spoken output therefore does not establish equally limited understanding of structure. || In **“The boy is tickling the girl,”** the first noun identifies the actor and the second identifies the recipient. Knowing boy and girl alone does not specify that relationship. Their positions help distinguish who performs the tickling from who receives it. || The **structure-mapping account** relates a partial sentence representation to an event representation. A child can map noun positions onto event roles without yet producing a fully adult-like sentence. Understanding these relationships can therefore precede fluent expression of the same grammatical construction.
24|**Gertner and colleagues (2006)** tested whether two-year-olds apply **transitive structure to a novel verb**. Children heard **“The duck is gorping the bunny”** while viewing unfamiliar actions with reversed actor roles. The word gorping had no learned action meaning that could identify who did what. || Children looked longer at the event in which the **duck acted on the rabbit**. Noun order supplied the relationship even without prior knowledge of the verb. The task therefore tested generalization of a construction rather than recall of a familiar sentence or event. || Instructions occurred during blank-screen intervals, and the sentence accompanied the test events. **Preferential looking** measured interpretation without requiring spoken production. Children’s **mean length of utterance, or MLU**, was typically near two **morphemes**, meaning meaningful words or word parts. Their output could be less complete than the adult structure they could already understand. || Reversing the characters’ roles makes knowing duck and bunny insufficient. Both possible events contain those participants and unfamiliar actions. The child must use the sentence’s ordered relationship to prefer the duck acting on the rabbit, even though the verb cannot be matched to a previously learned action.
