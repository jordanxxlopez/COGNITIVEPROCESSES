2|**Adults often begin learning a second language faster than children**. They already have a first language and more developed cognitive abilities. Those resources provide a starting point that an infant acquiring a first language does not yet have. || An **eighteen-month-old with about twenty words** is still developing pointing, pretend play, spatial understanding, questions, and vocal patterns. Adult learners have many of these capacities available already. Comparing ages therefore involves different starting knowledge, not simply the same learner receiving input at different dates. || **Ultimate attainment** means the level eventually reached. Adults are less likely to become fully native-like despite their initial speed. Their first language can support new learning while also affecting it through transfer, so early progress and final success address different aspects of acquisition.
3|Language development includes learning concepts as well as learning how a language expresses them. Understanding that an object is inside or outside another is different from knowing the words for that relationship. A later learner can already possess the concept without knowing its L2 expression. || This gives **second-language learners** an advantage for some kinds of learning. They can connect new forms with familiar events, objects, and spatial relationships. They are not acquiring every part of the conceptual system from the beginning. || Their **first language** also provides established patterns for expressing those relationships. Some transfer effectively, while others conflict with the new language. Adult acquisition therefore combines conceptual resources with the continuing influence of an already organized linguistic system.
4|**Transfer** occurs when processes or representations from a **first language, L1**, influence a **second language, L2**. A familiar way of hearing a sound, arranging a sentence, or retrieving a word remains available during new learning. The learner does not approach L2 without prior linguistic organization. || **Positive transfer** occurs when that organization fits the new requirement. An established distinction or form provides useful support. **Negative transfer** occurs when the old pattern favors an interpretation or response that does not fit L2. || Transfer is therefore not inherently a benefit or an error. Its effect depends on the relationship between the languages. A category useful in L1 can make L2 easier when the distinction matches, but harder when the new language requires separating sounds that L1 groups together.
5|Hindi distinguishes **dental and retroflex consonants**, while English listeners often assimilate both to an **alveolar** category. Dental articulation involves the teeth. Alveolar articulation is near the ridge behind them, while retroflex articulation involves a tongue configuration farther back. || In Hindi **minimal pairs**, changing only that consonant changes word identity. The examples distinguish meanings such as pressure from a kind of fruit, or a saw from crooked. A listener who hears both consonants as equivalent loses a distinction the language uses for meaning. || English experience produces **acquired similarity** among sounds grouped within its category. Applying that grouping to Hindi reduces their perceived difference. Negative transfer can therefore arise from successful earlier learning, because the learner must now distinguish inputs previously treated as versions of the same sound.
6|A **classifier** is a grammatical form chosen in relation to a noun. Chinese requires choices in expressions for objects such as chairs, dogs, cars, and books where English does not make the same grammatical selection. The learner must use properties that English allowed to remain irrelevant to this operation. || **Gè** is a general classifier. **Bǎ** applies to handled objects, **běn** to books and related objects, and **kuài** to pieces. **Liàng** applies to wheeled vehicles and **tái** to devices or gadgets. These choices connect forms with properties of the noun’s referent. || **Xiē** expresses an indeterminate plural quantity, **zhāng** is associated with flat objects, and **zhī** with long thin objects. Acquiring the system requires learning the appropriate grammatical category as well as the object name. Understanding what the object is does not automatically supply the classifier required with its noun.
7|**Grammatical gender** divides nouns into classes and affects related forms. It does not mean that an object has a social or cultural gender. French **livre** is grammatically masculine and **liberté** feminine without making a book socially male or liberty socially female. || **Agreement** requires forms such as adjectives and pronouns to fit the noun’s class. The learner must retrieve that class even when the object offers no obvious cue. Similar meanings can have different genders, as in **auto/voiture, rivière/fleuve, and vélo/bicyclette**. || Word endings provide useful statistical patterns. The examples associate forms such as **-age, -r, -t, and -sme** with masculine nouns, and **-ie, -ion, -ité/-tié, -ance, -me, -mme, and -lle** with feminine nouns. Learning therefore involves form and lexical knowledge as well as meaning. A meaning-only strategy cannot supply every gender and agreement choice.
8|**Cognates** are similar words sharing a historical origin. **Loan words** enter one language by borrowing from another. Both can make an L2 form partly familiar because the learner already knows a related form or meaning. || French **auto, rivière, and bicyclette** resemble forms associated with a car, river, and bicycle. Japanese **コロッケ, korokke**, refers to a croquette, while **モノレール, monoreru**, refers to a monorail. Borrowing can preserve recognizable overlap while adapting spelling and pronunciation. || These existing connections provide **positive vocabulary transfer**. The learner has information to connect with the new expression instead of starting with a wholly unfamiliar association. The advantage depends on the relationship actually supporting the intended form and meaning.
9|A **false friend** is a similar form that encourages an incorrect meaning across languages. Familiarity with its appearance or sound can create confidence without supplying the right interpretation. Similarity alone does not establish equivalent meanings. || Spanish **embarazado** concerns pregnancy rather than ordinary embarrassment. **“Arnold esta embarazado”** therefore does not convey the intended English idea that Arnold is embarrassed. The error comes from relying on a familiar-looking form whose meaning differs. || An L2 word can activate a similar L1 word during recognition or learning. Matching meanings can make that activation helpful, while different meanings make it misleading. The same general reliance on overlap can therefore produce positive transfer or a false interpretation.
10|**De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** used **paired-associate learning** to examine how known vocabulary supports new forms. Participants learned Dutch words paired with invented associates and were tested again after **one week**. This separated immediate learning from retention after a delay. || **Gitaar–gettar** is concrete and cognate-like, while **tijger–duunze** is concrete without the resemblance. **Domein–domaan** is abstract and cognate-like, while **herstel–groop** is abstract without the form overlap. The meanings include guitar, tiger, domain, and repair. || **Concreteness** concerns how readily the referent can be imagined, while cognate-like status concerns form similarity. Crossing those factors tests whether resemblance helps within both kinds of meaning. Participants must still learn the correct association rather than merely notice that two strings look alike.
11|**Cognate-like forms were learned faster and retained better** than dissimilar forms. The difference was especially visible early in learning. Repeated practice allowed some non-cognate conditions to approach cognates in immediate recall, but matching immediate accuracy did not make the learned associations equivalent. || After **one week without training**, cognate advantages remained. Abstract non-cognates showed a particularly large loss. The delayed result distinguishes being able to answer after recent practice from retaining that information when practice stops. || **Response times** also favored cognates. Their benefit therefore included more successful retrieval and faster access. Accuracy and speed capture different aspects of learning, and form overlap supported both rather than only making the first encounter feel familiar. || The delay separates a practiced response from a durable association. Two conditions can approach similar accuracy immediately after repeated training while differing in how readily the information is recovered later. Measuring response speed as well as correctness makes that difference visible even when both groups can often supply an answer.
12|A **receptive test** presents the unfamiliar associate and asks for its known Dutch partner. For cognate-like pairs such as **gitaar–gettar, trein–trien, and viool–vialo**, resemblance can help identify the familiar word. Correct responses in that direction alone could partly reflect recognition or guessing from the input. || A **productive or expressive test** reverses the direction. The Dutch word is supplied, and the learner must retrieve the unfamiliar form. Knowing gitaar does not automatically provide the exact learned associate gettar without some memory of the new pairing. || Testing both directions distinguishes access to a familiar meaning from the ability to generate a new form. A resemblance advantage in productive recall provides evidence that similarity supported learning itself. It cannot be explained entirely by recognizing the old word when a new look-alike is presented.
13|Both **productive and receptive recall** decreased after a week. Producing the unfamiliar form was harder overall than identifying its familiar partner. The two directions impose different demands because one requires generating the newly learned expression. || **Concrete forms** were better retained than abstract forms, and **cognates** were better retained than non-cognates. These advantages appeared in both directions. Practice had not made every condition equally resistant to forgetting. || The productive advantage matters for interpreting the cognate effect. Learners had to supply the new form rather than merely guess a known word from resemblance. Similarity therefore supported a retrievable association, although the level of success still depended on the type of material, direction of testing, and delay.
14|**Cross-language competition** occurs when a word from one language receives activation during use of another. That candidate may never become an intended or permitted answer. Partial activation can still influence processing before the correct word is selected. || **Marian and Spivey (2003)** tested whether similar **L1 words** become active during **L2 recognition**. Their visual-world task included objects whose names overlapped with the target across languages. Eye movements measured temporary attraction to those alternatives. || Looking at an object with a competing L1 name provides evidence beyond the final answer. Participants do not have to deliberately translate the target for its sounds to activate another language. The question is which candidates receive support during recognition in the designated task language.
15|**Eye tracking** records temporary competition during spoken-word recognition. In **McMurray and colleagues’ task**, listeners viewed **bear, pear, lamp, and ship** while hearing a /b/–/p/ continuum. Bear and pear supplied close lexical alternatives, while the other objects did not. || Changing **voice onset time**, the timing of voicing relative to release, changed the ambiguity of the input. Sounds nearer the category boundary produced more competitor looking. A listener could look at the alternative and still click the correct target. || The final click therefore identifies the winner without recording every candidate involved beforehand. The eyes provide a time-sensitive measure of support for those candidates. This makes the method useful for testing an alternative that never becomes the participant’s overt answer.
16|A **cohort competitor** shares the beginning of a target word. **Beetle** competes early with **beaker**, while **speaker** shares its ending and is a rhyme competitor. **Carriage** provides an unrelated comparison. The position of overlap affects when a candidate receives matching evidence. || In **Allopenna and colleagues’ (1994) task**, looks toward the target and cohort rise early. Later sounds fit beaker but not beetle, so target looks continue increasing while cohort looks decline. The initial match does not guarantee continued support. || Rhyme competition occurs later because its overlap becomes useful at a different point. Recognition therefore changes as the signal unfolds. Eye movements can record those temporary candidates even when the participant ultimately identifies the word correctly.
17|**Marian and Spivey** tested Russian–English bilinguals for **cross-language cohort competition**. English **speaker** begins similarly to Russian **спички, spichki**, meaning matches. The picture of matches can therefore become a competitor through its Russian name during an English instruction. || **Spear** provides a within-English competitor. A display can contain that object, the Russian-name competitor, or both. Comparing these displays separates competition within the selected language from competition supplied by the other vocabulary. || Other pairs include **boot–buben**, referring to a tambourine, **chair–cherepaha**, referring to a turtle, and **marker–marka**, referring to a stamp. Their relationship is **phonological**, meaning sound-based. Speaker and matches do not share a meaning, so looks toward matches must be related to its other-language name rather than semantic similarity between the objects.
18|During **L2 English recognition**, both within-language and between-language competitors attracted more looks than unrelated objects. A Russian-name alternative could therefore receive support while the listener was following English. Selecting a task language did not prevent all activation of the other vocabulary. || When both kinds of competitor appeared together, the **within-language competitor was stronger**. The Russian-name competitor nevertheless attracted more attention than the unrelated control. Competition across languages remained present even when another plausible alternative was available. || These effects concern temporary activation rather than a requirement to choose the wrong object. A participant can complete the English instruction correctly after looking at an L1-supported candidate. Recognition therefore includes alternatives beyond the final intended response. || When just one competitor was present, within-language and between-language alternatives attracted looking to a similar extent. Their unequal strength became clearer when both were available together. The presence of other candidates therefore changed the competition, without eliminating the influence of the other-language name.
19|During **L1 Russian recognition**, within-language competitors reliably attracted more looks than controls. The native-language cohort effect remained whether that competitor appeared alone or alongside the other-language alternative. Similar beginnings therefore affected recognition within Russian. || English-name competitors produced a smaller numerical increase over controls, but the difference was **not statistically significant**. The observed trend did not establish a reliable reverse cross-language effect. A numerical difference and a supported effect are not interchangeable. || The reported pattern was **asymmetric**. L1 words affected L2 recognition more clearly than L2 words affected L1 recognition. An explanation must account for unequal influence in the two directions rather than assuming that both vocabularies activate one another with identical strength.
20|The **Revised Hierarchical Model**, associated with **Kroll and Stewart**, connects L1 words, L2 words, and concepts. **Spreading activation** means activity at one representation influences connected representations. A lexical connection joins words, while a conceptual connection links a word with its meaning. || The **L1 lexicon** is larger and more strongly connected to concepts. A developing **L2 lexicon** has stronger links toward L1 than the reverse direction. Early use of L2 often relies on the established L1 counterpart to support access to meaning. || Unequal connection strengths predict unequal effects. Activity can spread from an L2 word toward L1 more readily than in reverse. The model allows both languages to interact without assuming equivalent routes to their word forms and shared concepts.
21|In the **Revised Hierarchical Model**, the matches picture activates the concept of matches. This can activate its Russian name, **spichki**, which partly overlaps in sound with the English target **speaker**. The competing word can then reinforce its associated concept. || Stronger support for that concept contributes to looking at the matches picture. The relationship between speaker and spichki is **sound overlap**, not shared meaning. Activating the competing word strengthens the representation of its own object rather than making speakers and matches semantically equivalent. || **Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** explain directional differences through unequal lexical and conceptual connections. Weaker routes involving L2 can make reverse interference less reliable during an L1 task. The mechanism involves the strength and direction of activation without requiring deliberate translation.
22|A **balanced bilingual** has relatively strong proficiency in both languages. In this model, increasing proficiency does not create **“two monolinguals in one.”** Both vocabularies can remain linked through concepts and interacting pathways. || **Competitive dynamics** means that active alternatives affect which representation is selected. **Inhibition** reduces candidates inappropriate for the current task. Strong knowledge of both languages can preserve control demands because both systems contain available alternatives. || These predictions motivated work on **nonlinguistic cognitive control**. The immediate language-model claim is that proficiency does not remove interaction. A balanced bilingual can recognize a word correctly while still managing competition from representations belonging to the other language.
23|One language history involves learning **L1**, adding **L2**, and continuing to use both. The first language keeps receiving practice and input. Its early acquisition is followed by maintenance rather than disappearance from the environment. || Another history involves adding L2 and **stopping L1 use**. The first language no longer receives the same repeated activation. A language learned early can therefore have a very different subsequent history from another equally early language. || Current ability depends on more than the original starting age. Continued exposure, reduced use, and later dominance change the opportunities for earlier learning to remain accessible. Comparing bilingual outcomes requires distinguishing an early start from ongoing support for the first language.
24|**Pallier and colleagues** studied adults adopted from Korean orphanages into French homes. They were adopted between **three and eight years** and tested as adults. Their early Korean experience was followed by a major shift to a French-speaking environment. || The question concerned **residual Korean knowledge**, meaning a surviving influence of the earlier language. An individual might no longer use Korean while retaining some advantage on tasks sensitive to its sounds or words. Current fluency and detectable traces are different outcomes. || French speakers without that early history provided a comparison. Better Korean-related performance among adoptees would indicate a retained effect of childhood exposure. The study tested the persistence and accessibility of learning after continued use of the original language had ended.
25|Korean distinguishes **aspirated, plain, and tense stops**. Stops involve closure followed by release, but these three categories can occur at the same place in the mouth. Their difference therefore cannot be reduced to where the closure is made. || The contrast occurs in **bilabial, dental, and velar series**. The examples include grass, fire, and horn in one series, and mask, moon, and daughter in another. Changing the laryngeal category can distinguish words even when the place of articulation remains the same. || **Laryngeal** refers to the voice-producing system and its configuration or timing. Retaining the Korean contrast requires sensitivity to that three-way organization. A language without the same categories supplies a different starting pattern, even if its speakers can distinguish lip-produced from tongue-produced stops.
26|**Ventureyra and colleagues** tested Korean adoptees in France alongside French and Korean listeners. Comparisons included **plain–tense, plain–aspirated, and tense–aspirated stops**. Vowel differences and identical pairs provided additional auditory judgments. || **Native Korean listeners** discriminated the stop distinctions more successfully. Adoptees performed no better than monolingual French listeners on those contrasts. The experiment therefore did not detect a retained phonetic advantage from their childhood Korean exposure. || Their performance on vowel and same trials differed from the difficult consonant comparisons. The result was not simply a failure to perform any auditory task. It specifically concerned accessible sensitivity to the tested Korean contrasts after the language environment had changed.
27|**Pallier and colleagues** used several tasks to search for remaining Korean knowledge. In **language identification**, adoptees distinguished Korean sentences from **Japanese, Polish, Swedish, and Wolof**. They did not reliably outperform monolingual French comparison listeners. || **Word recognition** tested selection of Korean translations for French words. **Speech-segment detection** tested whether a brief excerpt had occurred in a recorded sentence. Neither provided a dependable adoptee advantage. || The tasks examined different possible remnants, including familiarity with the language, vocabulary, and sound sequences. Earlier exposure did not produce a detectable current benefit on these measures. This describes what the tests could access, rather than directly establishing that every possible influence of early learning had disappeared.
28|Hearing **French** activated the adoptees’ broad **language network**, including **superior temporal and inferior frontal regions**. Their current dominant language therefore produced a clear neural response. The system was not generally unresponsive to speech. || Comparisons involved French alongside **Polish, Japanese, and Korean**. The analysis tested whether Korean produced a distinctive retained effect relative to the other speech. The French-minus-Korean comparison did not reveal the expected group difference associated with an accessible childhood-language advantage. || The imaging findings agreed with the absence of a detectable behavioral advantage under the tested conditions. These are measurements of current responses to speech. They do not directly count every connection or establish what renewed training might make available.
29|A language is supported by **connections within and between brain regions**. Repeated use affects those connections and their influence. L1 is therefore not a single stored object remaining unchanged independently of experience. || If **L1 stops receiving activation**, pathways associated with L2 can become dominant. **Competitive dynamics** describes this changing balance. Increasing use and support for one pattern can reduce the effective influence of another within the same broad system. || The proposal resembles **experience-dependent competition in vision**. Reduced input changes which pathways remain effective. Apparent replacement of a language can therefore result from changes in support and accessibility, rather than requiring a complete language object to be physically removed or transferred elsewhere.
30|**Residual knowledge** is a surviving influence of earlier language experience. It may not provide an advantage on an ordinary current-performance test. Being unable to use the language readily is different from having no remaining effect of that learning. || **Relearning** supplies renewed opportunities to activate earlier forms. Training can expose an influence that was not accessible before practice. A past language history might therefore affect how readily a person learns the forms again. || Current recognition and later learning test different consequences of experience. One asks what can be used now, while the other asks whether new exposure benefits from something retained. A lack of present fluency does not by itself answer the relearning question.
31|**Au and colleagues** studied students who had often overheard **Spanish in childhood** without learning to speak it. They began classroom Spanish around **age fourteen** and were tested after a second-year college course. Early listening was therefore followed much later by active instruction. || Their pronunciation was more native-like than that of typical late learners. **Voice onset time, or VOT,** for initial voiceless stops was closer to the native pattern, and accent ratings also favored overhearers. **Lenition**, the weakening of particular consonant articulations, provided further pronunciation measures. || The benefit was not uniform across language. Childhood slang knowledge remained well below native levels, and morphology and grammaticality judgments were closer to typical late learners than native speakers. Early hearing supported some sound patterns without producing native-like mastery of every component. || The students were not fluent childhood Spanish speakers simply retaining a practiced skill. They had frequently heard Spanish but began active classroom learning much later. Their later pronunciation advantage concerns a benefit of that earlier listening history after renewed instruction, alongside continuing weaknesses on other language measures.
32|When **L1 disappears from the environment**, L2 can appear to replace it in current behavior. Recognition or language identification may reveal no obvious advantage from childhood exposure. That outcome concerns access under the conditions being tested. || **Renewed learning** offers a different opportunity for residual knowledge to contribute. Earlier experience can affect practice even when it does not produce a clear untrained response. Present inability and absence of all learning effects are therefore distinct claims. || Korean-adoptee studies test accessibility after a major language shift. Childhood-overhearer studies include performance after renewed formal instruction. Their histories and tests provide different opportunities for early learning to matter, so the results should not be treated as identical measures of the same exposure pattern.
33|**Korean is rarely encountered in France**, while **Spanish is widely available in Los Angeles**. A person may continue hearing a childhood language through family or community without speaking it. Continued incidental exposure therefore differs across the histories being compared. || The language pairs also differ in **cognates and writing systems**. Familiar forms can support vocabulary learning, and shared writing conventions provide another kind of overlap. Later outcomes can reflect these advantages alongside the original childhood experience. || Comparing residual knowledge requires considering the **duration, form, and social setting of exposure**. Continued hearing, renewed instruction, and language similarity can each change performance. Having encountered a language early does not make all subsequent opportunities for maintaining or recovering it equivalent.
