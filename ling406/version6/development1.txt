2|The **nature-and-nurture question** concerns how built-in capacities and experience contribute to language acquisition. **Nativist positions** developed from **Chomsky’s critique of Skinner**. Skinner relied on general learning mechanisms such as reward and punishment, while Chomsky argued that these mechanisms could not explain the grammar children acquire. || For Chomsky, language’s important property is **generativity**. People understand and produce new sentences they have never heard. Acquiring a language therefore means identifying the **abstract grammar that produces its sentences**, rather than memorizing a collection of earlier utterances. || Grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are not **similar in any straightforward way**. A small change can make a sentence unacceptable, while two acceptable sentences can look very different. Grouping expressions by surface resemblance cannot simply separate everything permitted by the grammar from everything excluded. || **Operant conditioning** changes behavior through rewards and punishments. Children are not consistently rewarded for grammatical sentences and punished for ungrammatical ones, so those required contingencies do not reliably occur. Nativist accounts instead propose **Universal Grammar** and language-specific acquisition processes. Innate constraints limit the grammars considered, while a specially equipped **“data-handling” or “hypothesis-formulating” ability** helps identify the surrounding language. || An abstract grammar captures relationships that extend beyond the particular sentences a child has heard. Generativity creates the learning problem because memorized utterances cannot supply every novel sentence. The proposed innate constraints concern the kinds of grammar considered, while experience still determines which language the child acquires.
3|Early accounts treated **categorical perception** as both **language-specific and species-specific**. The claim was that its organization reflected a specialized human-language ability. Language-specific means restricted to language, while species-specific means restricted to one species. || In a synthetic consonant continuum, formant transitions change by equal amounts while listeners assign **/b/, /d/, and /g/** labels in relatively distinct regions. Acoustic distance changes continuously, but perceived identity switches more sharply. The categories organize how the physical differences are experienced. || **Discrimination is better near category boundaries** than within a category. This pattern appears with one-step, two-step, and three-step comparisons. Physical separation matters, but an equal separation can also be easier to hear when it crosses a phoneme boundary.
4|The **conditioned head turn procedure** tests whether an infant detects a sound change. The infant learns to turn toward a sound associated with a rewarding visual event. An observable orienting response replaces the spoken judgment that an adult could give. || **High-amplitude sucking** uses a different behavior. Sucking often declines as the same sound is repeated. If a changed sound renews sucking, the change has affected behavior and provides evidence that the infant distinguished the input. || Neither response requires naming or producing the sounds. The experimenter controls the acoustic comparison and measures whether the infant reacts differently. These methods make speech discrimination available for study before the child can perform a verbal identification task.
5|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** tested infants with synthetic syllables differing in **voice onset time**, or VOT. VOT describes when vocal-fold vibration begins relative to the stop release. Sucking declined during repetition, after which a controlled change tested whether responding recovered. || In **20D**, VOT changed from **+20 to +40 milliseconds**, crossing an adult phoneme boundary. Sucking increased. In **20S**, an equally large change stayed within a category, either **−20 to 0** or **+60 to +80 milliseconds**, and produced much less recovery. || The **0 condition** kept the sound unchanged, and sucking continued to decline. The equal 20-millisecond differences in the changed conditions are essential to the comparison. Their different effects indicate sensitivity to the category relationship rather than only the amount of physical change, before infants can provide phoneme labels.
6|**Categorical perception** was proposed as a way to convert variable sound into a **symbolic representation**. A symbolic unit preserves a recognizable identity despite acoustic changes. Finding such organization in very young infants contributed to an interpretation based on an innate speech-processing ability. || At roughly **four months**, infants are still developing basic motor and visual abilities. They are beginning to manipulate objects with both hands and support their head and chest while prone. Vocal production is also limited, with more vowel-like sounds only beginning to replace earlier vocalizations. || Category-related perception therefore occurs well before mature speech production. That timing motivated the claim that perception is specially prepared for language. The comparison concerns the early availability of organized responses to speech, rather than an ability to articulate the same sounds accurately. || Color vision and general visual acuity are also still developing at this age. The contrast with these broad developmental changes contributed to the interpretation of speech categorization as an unusually early ability. Early timing motivated the innateness claim without itself specifying every mechanism producing the response.
7|**Perceptual narrowing** is a reduction in sensitivity to some sound distinctions as perception becomes adapted to the surrounding language. Young English-learning infants can distinguish certain contrasts not used in English. Some become harder to hear toward the end of the first year. || **Werker and Tees** tested **Hindi and Salish contrasts**. Discrimination was relatively strong at **6–8 months**, lower at **8–10 months**, and much lower at **10–12 months** for English-learning infants. Infants with relevant language experience retained stronger discrimination. || The change depends on the sound system being experienced. A distinction available early does not necessarily remain equally easy to perceive. Learning a native system can improve adaptation to its useful categories while reducing sensitivity to distinctions outside them.
8|The **“citizens of the world” account** proposes broad, innate sensitivity to speech contrasts. **Language-general** means that this starting ability is not restricted to one native language. The proposal treats categorical organization as part of the infant’s initial language capacity. || Experience then **selects among innately specified contrasts**. Exposure establishes which distinctions matter in the language being learned. The child becomes tuned to those differences instead of remaining equally sensitive to every available contrast. || Under this account, development mainly narrows a broad initial inventory. Learning preserves or emphasizes existing distinctions rather than building every category from the beginning. It is therefore a claim about the infant’s starting knowledge as well as the role that experience plays afterward.
9|**Categorical perception is not restricted to speech or to humans**. Category-related patterns also occur with nonspeech sounds and in animals without human language. A categorical result alone therefore cannot establish a mechanism devoted only to human phonemes. || Performance also depends on the **task**. Naming a sound and deciding whether two sounds differ place different demands on the participant. A task requiring one label can produce a sharply categorical answer without recording all the uncertainty present during processing. || Infants also do not begin equally sensitive to every possible contrast. Some distinctions are weak initially and strengthen with relevant experience. An account of development must therefore explain gains in sensitivity as well as narrowing, rather than only removing contrasts from a complete starting inventory.
10|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** investigated categorical perception with **nonspeech sounds**. They varied **rise time**, the speed with which a sound grows stronger after onset. A faster rise sounds more plucked, while a slower rise sounds more bowed. || The materials included **sawtooth and sine waves**. As rise time increased, listeners changed from pluck labels to bow labels. The input differed in an auditory property rather than in which phoneme a speaker produced. || Discrimination was strongest around the labeling transition instead of remaining constant for equal rise-time changes. The relationship between category assignment and sensitivity can therefore occur outside speech. The result concerns the organization of auditory input without requiring linguistic categories as the explanation.
11|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** tested infant responses to **nonspeech sound changes**. Sucking declined as a repeated sound became familiar. The sound then either remained unchanged, changed within a category, or crossed a category boundary. || No change and a within-category change produced relatively little renewed sucking. A **between-category change** increased responding, which then declined again as the new sound became familiar. The category relationship affected the infant’s response to the difference. || This pattern appears early without speech as the stimulus. It is therefore compatible with a broader auditory ability. Early categorical organization and language-specific organization are different claims, and the nonspeech result separates evidence for the former from evidence for the latter.
12|Chinchillas learned responses to speech sounds through **operant conditioning**. Food rewarded the trained behavior after a **0-millisecond-lag or 80-millisecond-lag** stimulus. The rewarded signal is called the **SD**, or discriminative stimulus, because it indicates when the response will have that consequence. || Testing intermediate values measured how the learned response generalized across a **/ga/–/ka/ VOT continuum**. Responses changed sharply within part of the range, broadly resembling the human pattern. The animals responded systematically to the acoustic distinction without possessing human language. || **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and **Kuhl and Miller (1975)** are associated with these comparisons. Changing burst and aspiration intensity could shift the boundary or alter its steepness. Acoustic properties and training affected the resulting categorical behavior.
13|**Narayan and colleagues (2010)** compared infants’ responses to **nasal place contrasts**. **/ma/–/na/** changes from a nasal made with the lips to one made near the ridge behind the teeth. **/na/–/ŋa/** changes from that location to one farther back in the mouth. || At **4–5 months**, English-hearing infants looked longer after a /ma/–/na/ change. Their same-versus-change responses differed much less for /na/–/ŋa/. The first contrast was more **salient**, meaning easier to notice. || Broad early speech sensitivity therefore does not imply equal sensitivity to every contrast. An infant can detect one place change more readily than another. The initial system has uneven sensitivities, leaving some distinctions especially dependent on subsequent learning.
14|A **Filipino language environment** is associated with improving sensitivity to the native **/na/–/ŋa/** distinction. At **6–8 months**, Filipino-learning infants have similar looking times for same and change trials. At **10–12 months**, looking is longer after the sound changes. || The distinction begins weak rather than simply remaining strong from birth. English-hearing infants also have weak early sensitivity to it, despite clearer responses to /ma/–/na/. Relevant experience is associated with a gain over the first year. || **Learning can strengthen a contrast**, not just preserve it. Development therefore includes constructing more effective distinctions from the input as well as losing sensitivity to unused ones. A model based only on narrowing an initially complete set cannot account for this improvement by itself.
15|**Emergentist approaches** propose that language patterns develop through learning from structured experience. **Associative learning** detects relationships among events that repeatedly occur together or predict one another. Experience changes connections so that a familiar pattern becomes more likely to produce a particular response. || In conditioning, food produces salivation. Repeatedly pairing a bell with food can make the bell produce the response as well. The learned effect depends on the experienced relationship between the two events rather than on the bell’s physical resemblance to food. || Applied to **pattern recognition**, this means learning which stimulus properties tend to occur together. Repeated relationships in acoustic input can shape speech categories. The proposal does not require every category to be fully specified before the learner encounters the distribution of sounds.
16|A **vowel space** describes vowels through combinations of **F1 and F2**, the first two vocal-tract resonances. Natural tokens vary, so a vowel forms a distribution rather than occupying one exact location. Different vowel distributions can overlap even though listeners usually identify them successfully. || In the account associated with **Kuhl and others**, experience teaches regularities within these distributions. Common combinations receive repeated support. Learning concerns the pattern across many productions rather than memorizing a single ideal recording. || A **prototypical /a/** can develop around a central or especially frequent part of its distribution. The **mean** is an average position, while the **mode** is the most frequent value or region. The prototype can therefore reflect what the listener has experienced instead of one universal acoustic value fixed in advance.
17|**Maye and colleagues** exposed infants to different distributions along a **VOT continuum**. A **bimodal distribution** concentrates examples in two regions, supporting a distinction between two groups. A **unimodal distribution** concentrates examples near the middle, supporting one group. || At test, the same **stimuli 3 and 6** either alternated or repeated without alternation. Both exposure groups heard the same test contrast, so any difference depended on the preceding distribution rather than a larger acoustic separation at test. || The **bimodal-exposure groups** distinguished the test conditions, while the **unimodal-exposure groups** did not reliably do so. The distribution of heard sounds therefore affected how later input was organized. Experience with two clusters supported treating the tokens differently, while one central cluster supported grouping them together. || Alternating trials repeatedly switch between the two test tokens, while non-alternating trials do not. The relevant comparison is therefore the infants’ different treatment of those arrangements after exposure. It does not require a spoken statement that there are two categories or an adult-style decision naming the sounds.
18|**Social interaction affects language learning from exposure**. Nine-month-old American infants received **twelve 25-minute sessions** before a head-turn test of a Mandarin contrast. Live Mandarin interaction improved performance compared with English exposure. Television and audio-only Mandarin did not produce the same benefit. || The interactive setting changed the effect of hearing another language. Exposure through a recording was not equivalent to exchanging behavior with a live speaker. The result connects learning to how the acoustic input is encountered, not only to whether it is available. || Maternal responses also differed in whether they were **contingent**, timed to an infant’s vocalization, or **noncontingent**, delivered at unrelated times. Contingent feedback more strongly increased syllable-like vocalizations. Proposed explanations include an early attraction to social interaction and the ability to use another person’s cues. The timing of a response can affect the learner’s own vocal behavior. || Contingent and noncontingent responses differ in their relationship to the infant’s behavior. A contingent response follows the vocalization at the relevant time, making the exchange responsive. The contrast tests that relationship rather than merely whether an adult is present or whether the infant hears speech at all.
19|**Statistical learning is constrained** by which properties the learner treats as relevant. A **contrastive property** distinguishes linguistic units. Speaker identity and speaking rate also alter acoustic input, but they do not necessarily identify different phonemes. Learning must handle variation without creating a new category for every difference. || **Earlier learning changes later learning**. An established pattern affects which new distinctions are easy to notice and acquire. The learner’s response to a new distribution therefore depends partly on the organization already developed through experience. || **Social context** also changes how input is used. The same sounds can occur during responsive interaction or in an unresponsive recording. Learning depends on the information encountered, its relationship to prior knowledge, and the circumstances in which the learner experiences it.
