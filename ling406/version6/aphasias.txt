2|**Aphasia** is impaired language function following brain damage. In the **1870s, Lichtheim and Wernicke** proposed that different symptoms could arise from damage to different processing centers or their connections. A task could fail because a representation was damaged or because information could no longer travel along a required route. || In **Lichtheim’s House**, **A** contains sound images, **M** contains speech-movement images, and **B** represents concepts. Acoustic input enters through a, while output from M travels through m toward articulation. Understanding requires a connection from the sound representation to concepts. Repetition requires sound information to reach the movement representation. || The numbered sites distinguish these possible disruptions. **1 and 2** affect M and A, while **3, 4, and 6** interrupt connections among the centers. **5 and 7** interrupt motor output and acoustic input. The proposal explains different task failures through different combinations of representations and pathways.
3|The **Geschwind–Wernicke model** links language functions to brain regions and connecting pathways. **Norman Geschwind revived this approach in the 1970s**. Broca’s area lies toward the front in the frontal region, while Wernicke’s area lies farther back in the temporal region. The **arcuate fasciculus** is a fiber pathway connecting them. || The **central sulcus**, or **fissure of Rolando**, and **lateral sulcus**, or **fissure of Sylvius**, are major brain grooves used as anatomical landmarks. The lateral sulcus helps distinguish the temporal region from regions above it. These locations provide a way to relate damaged tissue to observed language problems. || A **dissociation** occurs when one ability is impaired while another is relatively preserved. A **double dissociation** includes a reverse pattern in another patient. Distinguishing syntax, word knowledge, and meaning makes those comparisons more specific than simply classifying language as present or absent.
4|In **Fromkin’s (1997)** description, **Broca’s aphasia** includes effortful word finding, missing grammatical material, and disrupted word order. **Grammatical morphemes** are small words or word parts expressing grammatical relationships. Content words can remain available even when the material connecting them into sentences is reduced. || Everyday comprehension can appear fairly good while controlled tests reveal difficulty. **Agrammatism** refers to problems producing or interpreting grammatical structure, but it does not correspond perfectly to a Broca-area lesion or diagnosis. Some patients with such lesions are not agrammatic, and some agrammatic patients do not fit that neurological classification. || **Wernicke’s aphasia** often involves fluent speech with relatively good pronunciation and intonation. Word selection and comprehension are impaired. Substitutions may be related or unrelated in meaning, and **neologisms** are invented word forms. Fluency describes the delivery of speech, not whether its words communicate a coherent message.
5|**Wernicke’s aphasia** can involve fluent **“word salad” or “Jabberwocky.”** A patient describing a visual problem said **“My wires don’t hire right.”** The expression has a sentence-like arrangement, but the selected words fail to communicate the intended problem with vision. || Other responses continue with grammatical-sounding phrases that do not form a clear message. **“I felt worse because I can no longer keep in mind from the mind of the minds…”** retains fluency without a coherent explanation. Invented forms such as **“demaploze” and “tubaboys”** are neologisms. Calling a fork “a need for a schedule” similarly fails to identify the object despite producing familiar words. || **Broca’s aphasia** can instead produce **telegraphic speech**. A hospital account retains words about Dad, the hospital, doctors, appointments, and teeth, but pauses and missing grammatical connections make the event hard to express. The surviving content words convey parts of the experience even though sentence production is restricted.
6|A traditional account assigns **lexical activation or inhibition problems** to Wernicke’s aphasia and **grammatical problems** to Broca’s aphasia. The **lexicon** contains stored word knowledge. Activation makes a candidate available, while inhibition reduces competing words that should not be selected. || Broca’s original patient **Leborgne**, known as **Tan**, could produce essentially one syllable while appearing to retain comprehension and other abilities. This extremely restricted output differs from the partly informative, agrammatic speech associated with the broader later syndrome. || **Apraxia of speech** is a problem organizing the movements needed to speak. That could account for restricted output without requiring the same loss of grammatical knowledge. The amount a person says is therefore insufficient to distinguish a problem selecting or arranging language from a problem planning its articulation.
7|A distinction between **grammar and the lexicon** separates rules for combining words from stored word knowledge. Grammar allows a sentence to be understood even when that complete sentence has never been encountered. Its structure can be constructed rather than retrieved as one memorized unit. || **“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously”** has a grammatical organization despite its implausible meaning. **“Furiously sleep ideas green colorless”** contains the same words in an unacceptable order. Familiarity with the words alone does not explain the difference between the two sequences. || The traditional aphasia account links Wernicke’s aphasia to disrupted lexical processing and Broca’s aphasia to disrupted grammar. This proposes distinct impaired operations rather than simply different levels of fluency. It predicts that patients will differ in what they can do with words and grammatical relationships across controlled tasks.
8|The **message level** organizes the event, concepts, referents, and perspective that a speaker intends to communicate. A **referent** is the person, object, or event being discussed. These intentions must be connected with words and arranged into a form that can be spoken. || At the **functional level**, lexical meanings and semantic roles are selected. **Lemmas** connect concepts with lexical items. At the **positional level**, forms are ordered and grammatical operations add the required structure and affixes. **Phonological encoding** supplies the sounds, and speech gestures organize their production. || Problems can arise at any of these operations rather than only at a single language center. **HOUSEBOAT and DOGHOUSE** involve meaningful combinations of forms. The error **“easy enoughly”** supplies evidence for an operation adding morphology, rather than selection of an already stored whole word. A production account must distinguish choosing a message from constructing its linguistic form. || A lemma’s connection with its referent is generally arbitrary, meaning that the concept does not determine the word’s form by resemblance alone. Positional operations have a different role. They combine and arrange forms according to grammatical patterns, including meaningful combinations whose parts already have established lexical representations.
9|An **autonomous parsing account** treats early grammatical structure as separate from meaning. In conversation, familiar events and surrounding context can support comprehension even when a listener struggles to compute every grammatical relationship. Successful everyday communication therefore does not test syntax in isolation. || In **“The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite,”** the final phrase can attach to the action or describe the safe. The words and their individual meanings stay the same. The attachment determines the relationship expressed. || Testing a **specific parsing deficit** requires sentences in which grammatical relationships cannot be replaced by guessing the most familiar event. A listener must identify which words belong together and who plays each role. That demand is more specific than understanding a conversation in which many cues point toward the same message.
10|Broca distinguished the ability to produce **articulate speech** from the ability to comprehend language. A person might understand what others say but struggle to produce intelligible speech. Poor output alone does not establish an equivalent loss of comprehension. || A **syntactic impairment** concerns the organization of grammatical relationships. Unlike a problem only with speech movements, it could affect both production and sentence understanding. Comprehension can therefore be tested without requiring the patient to produce a complete spoken answer. || This separates available knowledge from the ability to express it. Restricted speech might reflect an articulation problem while leaving more grammatical knowledge intact than the output suggests. A sentence-interpretation task provides evidence about that knowledge through a different response.
11|**Broca’s aphasia** is usually accompanied by some motor dysfunction. Speaking can become effortful, encouraging the use of informative content words with less grammatical material. Such speech may reflect an attempt to communicate under difficult conditions rather than directly expressing the full extent of retained grammar. || **Competence** refers to linguistic knowledge, while **performance** concerns using that knowledge in a task. Chomsky’s distinction allows poor performance to coexist with knowledge that cannot be expressed effectively. Hughlings Jackson likewise emphasized what a patient manages despite impairment. || **Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** investigated comprehension to test whether the difficulty extended beyond articulation. Their task required interpreting grammatical relationships without producing a full sentence. Difficulty under those conditions would concern more than the physical effort of speaking.
12|Ordinary conversation provides **redundant cues** to meaning. In **“The dog bit the man,”** knowledge of dogs and biting favors the dog as the actor. English **subject–verb–object order** supports the same interpretation, so meaning and word order point in the same direction. || A **heuristic** is a shortcut based on a regular pattern. A listener might select the most plausible actor or assume that the first noun performs the action. These strategies can support an answer without requiring every grammatical relationship to be analyzed. || In **“The dog that the man is biting is angry,”** the man performs the biting even though dog occurs first. The embedded clause and the main clause assign different roles. Correct interpretation requires tracking those relationships because familiar-event knowledge and a first-noun strategy favor the wrong answer.
13|**Caramazza and Zurif** compared ordinary control sentences with **center-embedded sentences**, which contain a clause inside another clause. A control such as **“The girl is kicking a green ball”** aligns familiar event knowledge with ordinary word order. The actor is easy to identify through several available cues. || In **semantically constrained** items, an embedded clause still contains a highly plausible actor. **“The apple that the boy is eating is red”** makes the boy the likely eater. Meaning can support the grammatical relationship even when the structure is more complex. || **Reversible** items, such as **“The horse that the bear is kicking is brown,”** allow either participant to act plausibly. **Improbable** items, such as **“The bird that the worm is eating is blue,”** make ordinary expectations favor the wrong relationship. Grammar must identify who acts on whom when plausibility either fails to distinguish them or actively misleads. || The embedded items place the actor inside the modifying clause rather than making the first noun perform its action. In the apple sentence, the boy eats the apple. In the worm sentence, the worm eats the bird. The grammatical relationship stays comparable while plausibility changes dramatically.
14|In **sentence–picture matching**, a participant hears a sentence and selects the picture expressing its meaning. The conditions differ in whether familiar event knowledge helps identify the actor. Accuracy measures whether the listener recovered the correct relationship, without requiring a spoken explanation. || **Healthy controls** perform similarly well across the conditions. **Wernicke’s patients** perform poorly overall. **Broca’s patients** do better when plausible event roles support the sentence and worse when the relationship must be recovered without that support. || **Caramazza and Zurif** interpreted the Broca pattern as a **syntactic comprehension impairment**. The deficit appeared in understanding, not just producing speech. Its dependence on the sentence conditions suggested particular difficulty assigning grammatical roles when ordinary expectations could not supply the answer.
15|Distinguishing **grammar from the lexicon** makes an impairment claim more precise. Grammar concerns relationships among words, while lexical processing concerns stored words and access to the appropriate entries. Problems with either operation can disrupt communication, but they predict different behavior. || A person might know the meanings of content words while assigning them the wrong roles in a complex sentence. Another person might produce a fluent grammatical frame containing words that fail to express the intended message. A single measure of overall fluency would conceal those differences. || A **structure–function hypothesis** links a particular operation to the system supporting it. Controlled tasks test whether the predicted operation is impaired. Explaining the pattern across those tasks requires more detail than describing someone as fluent, nonfluent, or generally poor at comprehension.
16|In **Dick and colleagues’ task**, listeners identify **which animal performs the action**. The event is a dog biting a cat, but the sentence structure changes. Knowing the animal names is insufficient because the answer depends on assigning their roles. || **“The dog is biting the cat”** is active, and **“It’s the dog that is biting the cat”** is a subject cleft. Both place the actor first. A strategy of treating the first animal as the actor gives the correct answer in these conditions. || **“It’s the cat that the dog is biting”** is an object cleft, and **“The cat is bitten by the dog”** is passive. Both begin with the animal receiving the action. Correctly selecting the dog now requires the grammatical relationship rather than a first-noun shortcut.
17|**Healthy aging controls** identify the actor accurately across active sentences, subject clefts, object clefts, and passives. Aphasia groups differ in both their overall accuracy and the additional cost of harder structures. They do not produce one uniform pattern of impaired comprehension. || **Anomic aphasia**, associated with word-finding difficulty, has relatively better performance, although harder structures still reduce accuracy. **Broca’s and conduction aphasia** involve a marked decline for object clefts and poorer performance for passives. **Wernicke’s patients** have low accuracy across all four forms. || Broca’s patients are especially vulnerable to **noncanonical structures**, which depart from the usual actor-first arrangement. Their active-sentence accuracy is also below the healthy level. The pattern combines a broader performance reduction with a disproportionate difficulty assigning roles in the less usual structures.
18|Comprehension also becomes less accurate in **healthy undergraduates** when processing is stressed. Manipulations include compressed speech, noise, **low-pass filtering**, and remembering digit lists while interpreting sentences. Low-pass filtering removes higher-frequency information, while a concurrent memory task uses resources during comprehension. || **Object clefts and passives** are particularly vulnerable under these conditions. Active sentences and subject clefts remain relatively easier. Combining manipulations, such as filtering and compression, produces greater losses than ordinary listening. || These participants have no aphasic injury, but difficult conditions produce a pattern resembling some aphasia findings. A disproportionate cost for complex grammar can therefore reflect **performance demands**, not only loss of a grammatical rule. The comparison concerns how effectively a system can use its knowledge when processing becomes difficult.
19|**Saygin and colleagues (2004)** asked participants to match **actions with objects**. A nonverbal version conveyed an action such as licking without spoken language and required choosing a fitting object, such as an ice cream. A linguistic version supplied language-based information for the same general decision. || Aphasic participants were less accurate than controls in both versions. The difficulty was therefore not confined to understanding grammatical sentences. The nonverbal task still required identifying the relevant action meaning and connecting it to an appropriate object. || **Semantically related distractors** caused more difficulty than unrelated choices. An object related in meaning competed more strongly with the correct match. Selecting the intended meaning could therefore be disrupted even when no linguistic sentence had to be interpreted. || The nonverbal comparison removes a linguistic expression without removing meaning from the task. The patient still has to identify what the action calls for and select the corresponding object. Similar difficulty in that condition therefore concerns a demand shared with language rather than grammar alone.
20|Poorer **nonverbal action matching** was significantly associated with **damage to Broca’s area**. Participants interpreted a pantomimed action and chose an appropriate object. They did not have to produce a sentence or extract the grammar of spoken instructions to identify the action. || This relationship extends the affected behavior beyond grammatical processing. A patient can have difficulty connecting an action with its meaning even when language is removed from the input. The nonverbal task still requires successful comprehension and selection. || The result challenges a description of every Broca-related difficulty as exclusively syntactic. The task establishes which operation is required, while the lesion analysis relates failure to damaged tissue. A proposed explanation must account for both the grammatical findings and the nonlinguistic action difficulty.
21|**Saygin and colleagues** compared matching **environmental sounds to pictures** with matching spoken descriptions. A cow’s moo supplies nonverbal auditory information, while **“cow mooing”** supplies words. Both versions require selecting the appropriate picture from alternatives. || **Wernicke’s patients** were particularly impaired, with accuracy around **90% in both versions**. Their verbal and nonverbal performance did not differ significantly. Controls, right-hemisphere-damage patients, and the other aphasia groups performed more accurately in these comparisons. || The impairment therefore extended beyond recognizing spoken word forms. Connecting an environmental sound with the event or object it represents was also difficult. Meaning-related alternatives could compete with the target regardless of whether the auditory information consisted of language or an ordinary sound.
22|Impaired **verbal and nonverbal sound–picture matching** was associated with damage involving **Wernicke’s area**. Lesion overlap occurred in posterior language-related regions for patients impaired on either task. The input differed, but both tasks required connecting auditory information with meaning. || A spoken description conveys that information through words. An environmental sound, such as an animal call, conveys it without a linguistic expression. Difficulty in both conditions cannot be described only as a failure to identify word forms. || The common demand is recovering what meaningful auditory input represents and selecting the matching object or event. Relating that performance to lesion location extends the anatomical account beyond a simple language-versus-nonlanguage division. The result concerns an association between damaged regions and measured comprehension difficulty.
23|The **language specificity of aphasia** remains disputed. Some patients are especially vulnerable to particular grammatical structures. Others also struggle with action meanings or environmental sounds in tasks that do not require language. An explanation must accommodate both kinds of finding. || **Controlled behavioral tests** separate demands such as selecting a word, assigning grammatical roles, matching an action, and identifying an auditory event. Measuring those tasks makes it possible to describe what is impaired more precisely than a general comprehension score permits. || **Lesion analyses** relate the resulting patterns to damaged tissue. A structure–function explanation must connect the task’s required operation with the observed performance and lesion distribution. Fluency and diagnostic labels describe broad behavior, but they do not by themselves establish which underlying operations are language-specific.

25|**Voxel-based lesion–symptom mapping** relates the location of brain damage to behavioral impairment across patients. A **voxel** is a small three-dimensional unit of an MRI image. Comparing damaged and spared locations against symptom measures identifies regions associated with reduced performance. || **Fluency deficits** have stronger associations with anterior, or front, regions. **Comprehension deficits** have stronger associations with posterior, or back, regions. These measures concern broad speaking and understanding abilities rather than direct tests of one grammatical operation. || The distributions support a rough **double dissociation** between production-related and comprehension-related difficulties. Different patterns of damage have different behavioral associations. Mapping across patients provides a quantitative relationship between tissue location and symptoms rather than relying only on a single case description.
