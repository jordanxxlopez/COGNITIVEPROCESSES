2|In **Klatt’s model of speech perception (1979)**, the auditory system begins by analyzing the incoming sound. The **cochlea**, in the inner ear, responds to its frequency components. Acoustic property detectors supply information that phonetic feature detectors use to estimate properties such as voicing. || **Segmental analysis** organizes these features into speech segments. A segmental feature matrix represents the resulting sequence, and **lexical search** uses that sequence to activate possible words. These possible words are lexical hypotheses, meaning candidates that might match what was heard. || A strict staged account completes a phoneme decision before passing it to word recognition. **Cascaded processing** instead passes partial information forward while a decision is developing. **Feedback** sends influence in the other direction, allowing an activated word to affect the interpretation of its sounds. These describe different ways that the levels can communicate. || A phoneme sequence is more abstract than the incoming waveform. Reducing different recordings to shared segments makes them usable for lexical search. The strict staged claim adds a timing requirement, however, by making word identification wait until those component identities have been selected.
3|The **beach–peach continuum** varies **voice onset time**, or VOT. VOT measures when vocal-fold vibration begins relative to the release of a stop consonant. A shorter delay supports the /b/ in beach, while a longer delay supports the /p/ in peach. Much of the remaining word stays similar. || In **identification**, the listener selects a word. Early continuum steps receive one label, responses become mixed near the middle, and later steps receive the other. The **category boundary** is the region where the preferred label changes and the input is most uncertain between the alternatives. || In **discrimination**, the listener decides whether two sounds differ. Performance is strongest for pairs near the identification boundary, including pairs 4–6 and 5–7. Equal physical changes are less noticeable within a category than across categories. The acoustic distance alone therefore does not determine how easily a difference is heard.
4|A **threshold model** requires activity to reach a particular amount before it can affect the next level. Evidence below that threshold can fluctuate without producing a response at the higher level. The higher stage waits until the lower stage has enough support to deliver its selected output. || Feature evidence varies between **+voiced and −voiced**. Early fluctuations remain below the relevant thresholds, so the phoneme level receives no completed decision. When +voiced activity crosses its threshold, support for **/b/** appears abruptly at that level. || Under this account, word recognition receives the selected phoneme rather than the entire history of uncertainty. Two acoustically different inputs that both produce /b/ can therefore become identical for the word stage. The transformation preserves the category but discards differences that existed before the threshold was crossed.
5|In **cascaded processing**, developing activity affects the next level before a decision is complete. Changing feature evidence can therefore change phoneme activity throughout recognition. The system does not wait for a single feature to cross a threshold before passing any information onward. || Stronger voicing evidence increases support for **/b/**, while weaker voicing favors **/p/**. A sound can activate both to different degrees. Two sounds eventually labeled /b/ can still differ in how much support they temporarily give /p/. || A **categorical final response** does not establish that all preceding processing was categorical. A listener must click either beach or peach, even if both words were active. The selected answer identifies the winner, while cascading preserves differences in the evidence that contributed to the competition.
6|A **lumber mill** changes trees into lumber, and a **furniture factory** changes lumber into furniture parts. An **IKEA customer** assembles those parts into usable furniture. Each operation depends on receiving a product in a form it can use. || A factory cannot treat an unprocessed tree as finished lumber, and the customer cannot assemble the intended furniture directly from a tree. Work at one operation does not automatically become usable input for the next. The required transformation must be completed first. || **Thresholded processing** has this dependence on a completed product. A higher level waits for the lower level to supply the required representation. Unfinished information is not continually passed forward merely because some processing has already occurred.
7|**Thresholded activity** means that one level begins only when the preceding level supplies the required kind of input. The stages carry out separate transformations. Activity at the lower stage can change while the higher stage still waits for a finished representation. || In speech recognition, a strict threshold account makes word processing depend on an identified phoneme. A partially resolved acoustic signal is not yet that phoneme. The word level receives the completed segment rather than all the evidence that competed during its identification. || This arrangement also determines which information is lost. If different sounds are converted into the same phoneme, their earlier differences no longer reach word recognition through that pathway. The output is useful for treating varied sounds alike, but later processing cannot recover uncertainty that was never passed forward.
8|In a **food web**, a population can respond while its food supply is still changing. A drought reduces desert plants, which affects kangaroo rats and then animals that feed on them. A snake population does not wait for the rat population to reach a final, completed state before being affected. || **Cascaded processing** has a similar continuous dependence. Changing feature evidence alters phoneme activity, and changing phoneme activity alters support for words. Each level can respond to partial information instead of waiting for the previous level to finish. || The food web also contains reciprocal influences among predators and prey. In a recognition model, **feedback** means influence from a higher level back to a lower one. Cascading and feedback are distinct. One passes developing evidence forward, while the other allows activity farther along the system to affect an earlier representation.
9|A **thresholded account** begins word recognition with a completed phoneme decision. A **cascaded account** allows a word to receive support while the phoneme is still uncertain. The difference concerns how much of the developing acoustic evidence remains available to possible words. || Two sounds can both receive a final **/b/** label while differing in their resemblance to **/p/**. If both are reduced to the same completed /b/, a strict threshold system supplies the word level with the same input. Cascading can preserve greater /p/ support for the more ambiguous sound. || The accounts can therefore agree about the word eventually selected while predicting different competition before that selection. A final answer alone does not separate them. Evidence about temporary support for a competing word tests whether uncertainty continues to influence recognition.
10|**McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin, and Spivey (2003)** tested spoken-word recognition using a **visual-world eye-tracking task**. Participants listened to sounds varying between /b/ and /p/ while viewing pictures. The sound manipulation changed the evidence for the initial consonant without replacing the whole word on every trial. || The pictures included **bear and pear**, which differ in that initial sound, along with unrelated objects such as **a lamp and a ship**. Participants chose the picture matching what they heard. Their eye movements were measured while recognition was still developing. || A participant could eventually select bear after briefly looking at pear. Those competitor looks provided evidence that the alternative had received some support. The method therefore measured competition preceding the final click, including activity for a word that was ultimately rejected.
11|Participants **clicked the picture** matching the spoken word. Increasing VOT made the onset more /p/-like, and pear responses rose sharply. The identification boundary occurred near **17.5 milliseconds**, where the preferred interpretation changed between the two words. || The final choices formed a **step-like pattern**. Several different sounds on the /b/ side produced bear responses, and several on the /p/ side produced pear responses. The final label grouped acoustically different inputs together. || A mouse click records the word selected at the end of recognition. It does not record every candidate that received support beforehand. The sharp identification change is therefore compatible with gradual differences in competition among sounds that receive the same final label.
12|**Gradient ambiguity** means that uncertainty varies by degree. Sounds near the bear–pear boundary provide more support for both alternatives than clear sounds farther away. This uncertainty affects looking even when the listener eventually chooses the same word. || Among trials ending in **bear**, sounds closer to the boundary produce more looks toward **pear**. Among trials ending in pear, approaching the boundary increases looks toward bear. Each comparison holds the final answer constant while changing how ambiguous the input is. || Competitor looking rises after the spoken word begins and declines as the target gains enough support. More ambiguous inputs keep the alternative involved for longer. The final answer is categorical, but the strength and duration of competition depend on the remaining acoustic evidence. || The comparison does not merely contrast correct bear trials with correct pear trials. It asks whether two sounds both accepted as bear can nevertheless produce different looking toward pear. That difference is evidence that the recognition system retained information beyond the final shared label.
13|A listener’s own **category boundary** provides a reference for measuring ambiguity. Approaching that boundary increases looks toward the other member of the bear–pear pair. This happens even when all trials in the comparison end with the same selected word. || The analysis separates an **early interval, 300–1100 milliseconds**, from a **late interval, 1100–1900 milliseconds** after onset. Increased competitor looking near the boundary occurs in both. Uncertainty therefore affects more than a brief initial response. || A sound can support the eventual winner while continuing to support its rival. Using each listener’s boundary relates this continuing competition to the ambiguity that person experiences. The effect concerns differences within a final response category, rather than only the obvious difference between choosing bear and choosing pear.
14|In a **cascade**, changing acoustic features affect phonemes, and changing phoneme activity affects words. A sound eventually identified as /b/ can still vary in how strongly it supports voicing. That partial evidence can reach the word level while recognition continues. || **/b/ and /p/** are both **bilabial stops**. Bilabial means that the lips form the closure, and a stop includes a closure followed by release. Their shared properties support both candidates, while voicing helps distinguish them. || Greater support for /p/ can temporarily strengthen pear even when bear eventually wins. Looks toward pear can therefore increase without a change in the final word. **TRACE** represents this developing activity with feature, phoneme, and word units at possible times in the input, allowing candidates to gain support as the signal unfolds.
15|**Andruski and colleagues (1994)** tested whether an imperfect sound match can activate a word’s meaning. **Semantic priming** occurs when one item makes a meaning-related target easier to process. A priming benefit after an altered spoken form indicates that some information has reached the corresponding word and its meaning. || The nonword **beace** facilitated responding to **war** through partial activation of **peace**. Similarly, **ging** facilitated **queen** through **king**. The input did not have to be accepted as a correctly pronounced word for its partly matching lexical candidate to affect a related meaning. || Related word primes, including **bill before drug**, were compared with unrelated primes. Altering an initial sound could weaken the benefit rather than eliminating all access. The amount of sound overlap influenced the strength of lexical and semantic activation, instead of acting only as an all-or-none gate. || Beace resembles peace closely enough to provide partial support, even though its beginning is altered. War is related to the meaning of peace rather than to the altered input as a valid dictionary word. The priming relationship therefore reaches beyond phonetic similarity to the activated word’s semantic associations.
16|A **minimal pair** contains two words distinguished by one speech sound. As the input approaches the boundary between the pair, looking toward the competitor increases gradually. The effect remains when the final response is held constant, so it cannot be explained only by different words being selected. || A **cascaded account** allows the remaining acoustic uncertainty to influence possible words. More support for the competing phoneme supplies more support for its word. Recognition can therefore retain information about how close the sound was to the alternative category. || A strict **threshold-only account** passes the same finished phoneme for every token assigned to that category. Once the earlier differences have been removed, the word level has no varying input to produce the changing competitor looks. The problem is the loss of useful distinctions before lexical competition begins.
17|**Lexical feedback** occurs when an activated word sends support back to its component phonemes. An uncertain sound can partly activate a word, and the word can then strengthen the sound interpretation compatible with its full form. Word knowledge contributes while phoneme identification is still developing. || Influence is therefore possible in both directions. Acoustic evidence changes which words are active, while those words change the balance between possible phonemes. The higher level contributes information instead of simply waiting for a finished lower-level decision. || **Feedback differs from cascading**. Cascading passes incomplete evidence forward from features to phonemes and words. Feedback sends influence from words toward phonemes. An interactive account can include both, allowing developing representations to affect one another during recognition.
18|**Ganong (1980)** tested whether **word knowledge changes phoneme identification**. Participants heard a continuum whose endpoints formed a real word and a nonword. In **corpse–gorpse**, one interpretation of the initial consonant produces a familiar word, while the other does not. || The onset changed in **5-millisecond steps**, varying the acoustic evidence near the consonant boundary. The remaining sounds supplied information about whether each possible beginning would complete a word. The manipulation therefore combined uncertain phonetic evidence with a difference in lexical status. || If lexical knowledge affects identification, an ambiguous consonant should be more likely to receive the interpretation forming the real word. The question concerns the uncertain part of the input. Recognizing an unmistakable word endpoint would not establish the same influence on phoneme categorization.
19|A **late-correction account** leaves initial phoneme perception unchanged and adds a word preference when the listener reports an answer. In corpse–gorpse, the listener would favor saying corpse after the sound has already been categorized. Lexical knowledge affects the response rather than changing the earlier perceptual boundary. || This account predicts a **general reporting bias**. Favoring a real word should adjust responses across the continuum, rather than producing its main effect only where the sound is maximally ambiguous. The bias is added after the phoneme decision. || The **word–nonword, neutral, and nonword–word conditions** vary which consonant interpretation forms a word. A reporting adjustment can favor the lexical option in these conditions without relocating the perceptual boundary. That distinguishes the proposal from one in which word knowledge helps resolve uncertainty during identification itself.
20|A **boundary-shift account** proposes that word knowledge changes where the listener switches phoneme categories. Near an ambiguous onset, the interpretation forming a real word receives extra support. The effect changes categorization rather than merely correcting a report afterward. || The largest difference is expected **near the category boundary**. At clear endpoints, the acoustic evidence strongly favors one consonant. Near the boundary, neither interpretation has that advantage, leaving more room for lexical information to affect the choice. || Identification differences between lexical conditions should therefore be large in the uncertain region and small at the endpoints. A uniform reporting bias predicts a different distribution of effects. Where the lexical effect occurs is as important as whether listeners favor a word overall. || The word ending provides the same kind of lexical advantage at every continuum position, but the onset does not remain equally uncertain. Near an endpoint, strong acoustic evidence limits the effect of that knowledge. Near the boundary, competing phoneme interpretations leave more uncertainty for the lexical information to resolve.
21|**Ganong’s results** contain the largest lexical differences near the **phoneme boundary**. The curves shift according to which consonant interpretation forms a real word. Differences are smaller near clear endpoints, where acoustic evidence already strongly supports one category. || This pattern is compatible with word knowledge affecting phoneme labeling. It differs from adding an equal preference across the entire continuum. The lexical influence depends on how much uncertainty remains in the sound. || Ganong considered possible guessing strategies, while **interactive models** implement the effect through **lexical-to-phoneme feedback**. An activated word supports the phonemes that compose it. The whole-word representation can therefore affect how an ambiguous part is interpreted during recognition.
22|A **TRACE simulation of the Ganong effect** begins with a sound ambiguous between **/b/ and /p/**. The following input contains /l/, the vowel in plug, and a final /g/. Both initial phonemes can receive support before the rest of the word resolves the uncertainty. || Partial /b/ evidence activates possible **bl-** words, while /p/ evidence activates **pl-** words. Candidates gain support when new sounds fit them and lose support when the input conflicts. The word is processed as it arrives rather than only after its ending is complete. || **Plug, blood, and blush** can all receive some early support. The final **/g/** fits plug but not the endings of blood or blush. Plug becomes stronger as the alternatives weaken, and feedback from the winning word favors its compatible phonemes.
23|The word **plug** gains support as its sounds arrive, especially when the **final /g/** becomes available. **Blood and blush** fit some earlier information, but their endings do not match /g/. A candidate can therefore be plausible initially and lose support once the input continues. || Stronger activation of plug sends feedback to **/p/**. The beginning becomes less ambiguous because /p/ belongs to the word favored by the full sequence. Information near the end of the word can thus influence interpretation of an earlier sound. || This is **top-down processing** in TRACE. A representation of the whole word affects a representation of a component phoneme. The effect operates through interactions during recognition, rather than only as a final preference for reporting a familiar word.
24|**TRACE** adapts the **Interactive Activation model** of written-word recognition to speech. Compatible representations at different levels support one another, while alternatives compete. A candidate can influence other levels before it becomes the final response. || In written-word recognition, visual features support letters and letters support words such as **WORK, WORD, WEAR, and WORM**. Activated words also support compatible letters. Information about a whole word can therefore help resolve incomplete evidence about one of its components. || Spoken input unfolds over time. TRACE represents features, phonemes, and words at possible positions in that unfolding signal. A candidate can fit the beginning, gain activation, and then weaken when the ending conflicts. The model represents the changing evidence rather than treating recognition as one decision over a fully completed string.
25|**Categorical perception** combines relatively poor discrimination within a category with better discrimination across its boundary. Equally sized physical differences can therefore have different perceptual effects. A listener’s final labels divide a continuous acoustic range into distinct identities. || That categorical answer can coexist with **graded word competition**. Sounds assigned the same label still vary in how much they activate an alternative word. The word level retains information about uncertainty instead of receiving only an identical completed category for every token. || **Lexical knowledge can also affect phoneme categorization**. Partial information passes forward, and activated words can send support back. Speech recognition can therefore involve interaction among levels while uncertainty is being resolved, even though the task ultimately requires one word or phoneme as the answer.

27|In **Allopenna and colleagues’ (1994) task**, listeners hear a word while viewing possible referents. For **beaker**, **beetle** shares the beginning and is called a **cohort competitor**. **Speaker** shares the ending and is a **rhyme competitor**, while **carriage** is unrelated. || The initial sounds support both beaker and beetle, so looks toward both increase early. Once the sounds distinguishing the words arrive, target looking continues to rise while beetle looking declines. The competitor loses support because the later input no longer matches. || Speaker overlaps at a later point, so its competition has a different time course. Eye movements record when each candidate attracts attention before the final choice. Correctly selecting beaker does not mean that beaker was the only word considered throughout recognition. || Cohort and rhyme overlap are not interchangeable. Beetle is supported while only the beginning has been heard, whereas speaker has a mismatching beginning and gains overlap later. The time at which evidence becomes available determines when each candidate can plausibly compete.
28|**Eye movements** provide a record of changing word competition. Looks toward the target and its cohort competitor rise early because both fit the beginning. Rhyme competition is later and smaller because the matching information occurs toward the end of the spoken word. || A **TRACE simulation** predicts changing activation for these same kinds of candidates. Early input can support more than one word, and later input can remove support from an initially plausible alternative. A rhyme competitor receives matching evidence at a different time from a cohort competitor. || The timing of fixations can therefore be compared with the timing of model activity. A correct final answer alone cannot distinguish candidates that were never active from candidates that became active and then lost. The developing pattern supplies information about how recognition reaches its final result.
