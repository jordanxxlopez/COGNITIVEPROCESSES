2|**“The horse raced past the barn fell”** is grammatical, but the beginning encourages an interpretation that does not fit the ending. A reader can initially take **raced** as the horse’s main action. That makes the words before fell seem to describe a complete event, leaving the final verb difficult to incorporate. || The intended meaning is **“The horse that was raced past the barn fell.”** The phrase raced past the barn identifies the horse. Fell then states what happened to it. Omitting “that was” leaves this modifying relationship less explicit. || Sentence comprehension depends on assigning relationships among words, not only recognizing their individual meanings. Here, one action belongs inside the description of the horse and the other is the main event. Difficulty arises when the early interpretation assigns those roles incorrectly. || The horse is the one that fell, not a second participant introduced by the last word. Raced belongs within the description of that horse. Keeping those relationships distinct supplies a complete interpretation without deleting either verb or treating the original sentence as an ungrammatical word list.
3|A **garden path effect** occurs when a plausible early interpretation leads the reader toward the wrong grammatical structure. In **“The horse raced past the barn fell,”** raced initially appears to be the main verb. Fell then conflicts with the structure already being built. || The sentence becomes understandable when raced past the barn is interpreted as meaning **“that was raced past the barn.”** That phrase modifies horse, leaving fell as the main verb. The reader must revise the earlier relationship rather than simply add the last word to it. || **Grammaticality and processing difficulty are different**. A sentence can have an acceptable structure while being difficult to interpret as it unfolds. The garden path concerns which analysis is selected first and how hard it is to replace, rather than whether a correct analysis exists.
4|A **parser** assigns grammatical structure while a person reads or listens. It determines which words form groups and how those groups relate. A **syntactic tree** represents these relationships, including which phrase modifies a noun and which verb states the main event. || A **noun phrase, or NP**, groups a noun with related words. A **verb phrase, or VP**, contains a verb and the material associated with it. A **prepositional phrase, or PP**, combines a preposition with its object. Grouping the words differently can change how the same sentence is understood. || Parsing operates with **limited time and memory**. Treating raced as the main verb initially requires less structure than treating raced past the barn as an embedded modifier. The latter analysis contains a clause inside the noun phrase while keeping fell available as the main verb. A processing account must explain how those alternatives are handled during comprehension.
5|**Minimal attachment** is a preference for building the smaller available syntactic structure first. The nodes in a syntactic tree represent grammatical groupings. Fewer nodes mean less structure to construct and maintain at that moment. || In the horse sentence, treating **raced as the main verb** produces the simpler initial analysis. The alternative makes raced past the barn a modifier inside the noun phrase. It requires an embedded relationship equivalent to “that was raced past the barn.” || Choosing the smaller structure can reduce the immediate processing demand, but it can also create a garden path. The final word fell requires the analysis that was not initially preferred. Difficulty then comes from replacing the earlier structure, even though the grammar permits the correct alternative.
6|**Late closure** is a tendency to attach new words to the phrase or clause currently being processed. In **“The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite,”** the phrase with the dynamite is preferentially connected to blew open. It describes how the action was carried out. || This **verb-phrase attachment** makes the dynamite the instrument used to open the safe. An alternative **noun-phrase attachment** groups the safe with the dynamite together. In that version, the phrase describes or identifies the safe instead of describing the action. || Both attachments are grammatically available, but they express different relationships. A preference for attaching the phrase to the action makes one interpretation easier to reach initially. The words alone do not fix whether the dynamite belongs to the event as an instrument or to the object as a property.
7|In **“The burglar blew open the safe with the jewels,”** attaching with the jewels to the action would make jewels the instrument used to open the safe. That is an unlikely event. The intended interpretation instead identifies **the safe containing the jewels**. || Reaching that meaning requires **noun-phrase attachment**, connecting with the jewels to safe. The difficult part is not recognizing the word jewels. It is assigning the phrase to the object rather than to the action. || Longer reading times and changes in eye movements occur when the sensible meaning requires this unpreferred attachment. A reader can briefly adopt the action-related interpretation and then revise it. The eventual meaning is straightforward, but the route to it can still impose a processing cost.
8|An **autonomous parser** is proposed to select its earliest grammatical structure independently of meaning. Its initial choices follow abstract syntactic preferences. Semantic information can contribute afterward, but it does not choose the first analysis in this account. || **Minimal attachment** favors a smaller tree, and **late closure** favors attaching incoming material to the structure already being processed. These preferences limit how much structure must be maintained at once. The parser selects a likely route rather than fully building every possible alternative. || When the continuation does not fit, the selected structure must be revised. Garden-path difficulty is attributed to that revision. The account therefore separates an initial structure-based choice from a later adjustment when the developing meaning or grammar conflicts with it.
9|A **reduced relative clause** modifies a noun while omitting words such as “that was.” In **“Recent research reported by Ferreira and Clifton,”** reported describes research that someone reported. Research is not itself performing the reporting as the main action of the sentence. || **“Referential information provided by the discourse,” “results obtained with global reading times,”** and **“examples cited here”** have the same kind of modifier. Provided, obtained, and cited relate to their preceding nouns. These forms can be understood readily despite the complexity associated with reduced relatives. || **MacDonald, Perlmutter, and Seidenberg (1994)** argue that meaning and learned usage patterns contribute early to parsing. The formal structure alone does not determine difficulty. Particular words and their familiar relationships can support a reduced-relative interpretation without producing a strong garden path.
10|In **“a noun phrase followed by a morphologically ambiguous verb,”** followed modifies noun phrase. In **“the initial syntactic analysis assigned to a sentence,”** assigned modifies analysis. In each case, the verb form introduces information about a noun rather than automatically serving as a new main verb. || **“Differences located on the critical disambiguating phrase”** uses located in the same way. **“A person presented with the evidence examined by the lawyer”** contains two such relationships. Presented modifies person, while examined modifies evidence. || Understanding these expressions requires assigning each modifier to the appropriate noun. The constructions occur in readable scientific prose, including arguments for an autonomous parser. Their occurrence supports a distinction between having a formally complex structure and necessarily being difficult to understand in every context.
11|In **“Recent research reported by…”**, the meaning of research favors a reduced relative. Research is **inanimate**, so it is unlikely to be the entity doing the reporting. Interpreting it as something reported by another person fits the relationship among the words. || **Reported** also occurs often in reduced relatives, particularly with research in scientific writing. These **lexical statistics** are learned patterns associated with particular words. Familiar usage and plausible meaning both favor treating reported as a modifier. || **“The burglar arrested in the parking lot was charged today”** similarly makes the burglar a plausible person being arrested. **“The policeman arrested…”** also supports an agent who performs an arrest, creating more competition. The grammatical options remain similar, but the nouns’ likely roles change which analysis is easiest to select. || World knowledge and word-specific frequency provide different sources of support. Research is an unlikely reporter because of its meaning. Reported is also familiar in this modifying construction because of its usage. Both favor the same parse without being the same kind of information.
12|**Spivey and colleagues (1995)** asked people to follow spoken instructions while viewing objects. The one-apple display contained **a pencil, a box, an empty towel, and an apple on a towel**. Participants heard **“Put the apple on the towel in the box.”** || The phrase **on the towel** can initially sound like a destination, prompting a plan to put the apple on the empty towel. In the intended interpretation, it identifies where the apple already is. **In the box** supplies the actual destination. || Participants often looked toward the empty towel before turning toward the box. With **“Put the apple that’s on the towel in the box,”** the modifier is explicit and this temporary error is reduced. Eye movements reveal a competing interpretation considered before the final action is completed. || The two towels play different roles. One already supports the apple, while the empty towel is the competing destination. Confusing the current-location modifier with the destination changes the planned action, even though the listener recognizes all the object names correctly.
13|A **two-apple display** changes what information is needed to identify the intended object. One apple rests on a towel and another rests elsewhere. Saying the apple does not yet distinguish them, so **on the towel** can specify which apple should be moved. || In that setting, participants look toward the intended apple and then toward the box. They make fewer detours toward the empty towel. The ambiguous wording produces a sequence more like the explicit instruction containing “that’s on the towel.” || The phrase’s usefulness changes with the objects available. With two apples, a location modifier solves a real identification problem. Context can therefore affect the developing grammatical interpretation, even though the ambiguous sentence itself has not changed.
14|With **one apple**, the ambiguous instruction often produces looks toward the empty towel as a possible destination. With **two apples**, those looks are reduced. The same wording therefore creates different early interpretations depending on the visual context. || The **unambiguous instruction** produces relatively few destination errors in either setting. Its grammar already identifies the towel phrase as a modifier. The major ambiguity cost occurs when the display contains only one apple and extra identifying information is less useful. || When two apples are available, **on the towel** distinguishes the target before **in the box** gives its destination. This is an effect of **referential context**, meaning the set of objects a phrase could identify. It influences which relationship the listener assigns while the sentence is still being understood.
15|**Semantic context** concerns meaning, while **pragmatic context** concerns how an expression is used in a particular situation. If two apples are present, describing one as on the towel helps identify the intended object. The phrase has a useful modifying role in that situation. || With one apple, the object is already identifiable. The same phrase can then be taken temporarily as the destination of put. Changing the display changes the developing interpretation without changing the ambiguous instruction. || The difference occurs in eye movements before the final action. Context therefore affects processing early rather than merely correcting a completed interpretation afterward. An account that always selects the same initial structure independently of context has difficulty explaining this contrast.
16|A developing sentence interpretation can affect **arm movement** as well as eye movement. **Mouse tracking** records the path of an action while the instruction is being processed. A competing interpretation can pull the movement toward another destination before the participant reaches the correct target. || Ambiguous and unambiguous instructions produce more different trajectories in a **one-referent context**. Their trajectories are more similar when two referents are available. A referent is the object identified by the phrase, so the comparison concerns whether one or two apples could be intended. || The second apple makes the location phrase useful for identification and reduces interference from the destination interpretation. Comprehension and action planning can therefore interact while both are underway. A correct endpoint does not mean the competing interpretation had no influence on the movement.

18|**The** is a **determiner**, a word that introduces a noun phrase. It creates an expectation for a noun or for words that modify a noun. It does not name the object by itself, but it limits which kinds of continuation can complete the phrase. || That expectation is grammatical rather than a prediction of one exact word. Many nouns could follow, and an adjective could occur before the noun. The category information helps the parser anticipate the form of the phrase while its specific meaning remains incomplete.
19|**Pizza** is a **noun** that names what the phrase refers to. It can be the **head of a noun phrase**, meaning the central word around which the rest of that phrase is organized. A determiner such as the combines with it to form the pizza. || The word supplies both category information and meaning. Its noun status determines how it can combine grammatically, while its meaning identifies a kind of food. Recognizing a word therefore makes more than its sound or spelling available to sentence processing.
20|**Was** is a past-tense form of **be**. It can act as an **auxiliary**, helping another verb express the sentence’s grammatical structure. Its arrival does not yet determine a single completed continuation. || A location phrase can follow, or a participle can contribute to a passive construction. The reader must retain the developing relationship while more words arrive. The same word can support several structures, so its grammatical role depends partly on the continuation.
21|**In** is a **preposition** that expresses a relationship such as location. It begins a prepositional phrase rather than naming the location on its own. More material is required to identify what the relation connects to. || A following noun phrase can supply that location or container. The parser therefore uses in to establish a grammatical expectation. Understanding the phrase requires combining the preposition with the words that complete its relationship.
22|**The** introduces a noun phrase within the developing prepositional phrase. It does not itself supply the location expected after in. A noun, potentially preceded by modifiers, is still needed to complete that part of the structure. || The expectation concerns a **word category**, not simply a sensible topic. A continuation must function grammatically as the required phrase. Knowing that the sentence concerns pizza does not remove the need for the next words to fit that structure.
23|**Eaten** is the **participial form of eat**. It is not the noun expected to complete the intended phrase **“in the eaten.”** Its category conflicts with the developing structure, producing a **word-category violation**. || The problem differs from an unusual but grammatical meaning. The words cannot be combined in the intended construction simply by imagining an uncommon event. The mismatch concerns the grammatical type of the arriving word and the type required at that position.
24|The **early left anterior negativity**, or **ELAN**, is an **event-related potential** associated with word-class violations. An event-related potential is a measured brain response aligned to an event such as a word’s onset. This response occurs roughly **100–200 milliseconds** after the critical word, with a left/anterior distribution. || In **“The pizza was in the eaten,”** a participle appears where the intended phrase expects a noun. In **“The scientist criticized Max’s of proof the theorem,”** the preposition of appears where it does not fit the developing noun phrase. Both involve a mismatch in grammatical category. || The early response motivated an account involving rapid syntactic analysis. The critical distinction is between a word of an unexpected grammatical type and a correctly positioned word with an implausible meaning. The measurement concerns a response very soon after the relevant form becomes available.
25|For **visually presented words**, an early category-related response was localized to **visual cortex**, which processes what is seen. **Dikker and colleagues (2010)** proposed that grammatical expectations also generate predictions about the physical forms of upcoming words. An early response can therefore depend on visible form cues. || **“The beautiful princess”** and **“The beautifully princess”** differ in an ending that changes the expected continuation. The form beautifully supports a different grammatical relationship from beautiful. The arriving noun can conflict with that expectation before the complete sentence is interpreted. || **Form typicality** describes how characteristic a word’s form is of a category. A predicted category can therefore imply likely visual properties. An early category-sensitive response in a reading task is not necessarily produced only by a separate mechanism operating on abstract syntax. || The predicted form does not have to identify one exact upcoming word. A category can favor a set of likely forms. When the arriving word has properties incompatible with that expectation, the visual system can respond to the mismatch very early in processing.
26|**Perches** can function as a noun or a verb. In **“the bird perches seem easy to install,”** it names supports for birds. In **“the bird perches comfortably in the cage,”** it describes what the bird does. The continuation resolves how the same word must be interpreted. || **Needs** is similarly ambiguous. **“The student needs were not being met”** uses needs as a noun naming requirements. **“The student needs to be more focused”** uses it as a verb. Recognizing the word form alone does not settle its grammatical role. || **Phonological form typicality** concerns how much a word’s sound pattern resembles other nouns or verbs. An analysis of **3,158 words** compared those similarities. A form can resemble one category statistically while being used as another in a particular sentence.
27|**Perches is relatively noun-like, while needs is relatively verb-like** in their form-based patterns. These are learned statistical tendencies rather than rules restricting grammatical use. Both forms remain available as nouns and verbs. || **“The bird perches seem easy to install”** uses the noun-like form as a noun. **“The bird perches comfortably in the cage”** requires a verb. The same form can therefore agree with its usual category pattern in one sentence and conflict with it in another. || The pattern reverses for needs. **“The student needs were not being met”** requires a noun, while **“The student needs to be more focused”** requires a verb. Comparing these sentences separates what the form tends to suggest from what the grammatical continuation actually requires.
28|Reading becomes slower when a word’s required category conflicts with its **form typicality**. A noun-like form such as **perches** is harder to resolve as a verb. A verb-like form is harder to resolve as a noun. The cost depends on the relationship between the form’s tendency and its use in the sentence. || The analysis compares length-adjusted reading time at the **point of ambiguity** with time at **disambiguation**. Disambiguation is where the continuation makes the intended category clear. Extra time there indicates difficulty resolving the word’s grammatical role. || The ambiguous form itself can remain unchanged while the continuation determines a different use. Greater cost for the mismatching continuation connects parsing to learned form statistics. Grammatical interpretation uses information associated with word forms as well as the structure eventually required.
29|The early **ELAN** response depends partly on how much a word’s form reveals about its category. It occurs for nouns with informative **morphological marking**, meaning meaningful word parts, and for forms strongly associated with nouns. A neutral form such as **infant** does not produce the same early pattern. || The comparisons include **beautiful princess versus beautifully princess**, **tasteless soda versus tastelessly soda**, and **cute infant versus cutely infant**. Princess provides informative morphology, soda has a statistically noun-like form, and infant supplies weaker form-based category evidence. || The abstract category mismatch is therefore not sufficient to determine this earliest visual response. An expected grammatical category can generate predictions about word form. The response depends partly on whether the word that arrives contains physical evidence conflicting with that prediction.
30|**“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously”** has an acceptable grammatical organization but an implausible combined meaning. **Syntax and semantics are partly independent** because words can fit a structure without forming a sensible event. Grammatical organization is not simply the same property as plausibility. || **“The horse raced past the barn fell”** becomes difficult when an early structural preference cannot accommodate the final verb. Such preferences can reduce memory demands while leaving some sentences vulnerable to revision. In the apple instruction, however, the available objects affect early interpretation of the ambiguous phrase. || Word forms also carry learned category information. **“The bird perches were comfortable”** uses a noun-like form as a noun, while other continuations require a different use. Early category-related brain responses depend partly on such form-based expectations. Parsing therefore involves grammatical relationships together with meaning, context, and learned regularities in the available input.
