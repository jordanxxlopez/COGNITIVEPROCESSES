2|**Speech perception** allows a listener to recognize words in a changing sound signal. Somehow, the sound of “I can see you” becomes a message with recognizable words, even though the recording contains no separate acoustic pieces corresponding to the written letters. The movements used to produce neighboring sounds overlap, so their acoustic effects overlap as well. || A speaker’s voice also changes the physical form of an utterance. Different speakers can produce the same vowel with different frequencies, and some frequencies can overlap across vowels. Matching a recording to one fixed sound pattern would therefore leave many ordinary productions unrecognized. || Recognizing speech requires finding a linguistic identity within this physical variation. The words can remain the same when the voice changes, because the message depends on how the sounds function in the language rather than on an exact repetition of every acoustic detail.
3|Somehow we have to turn a **highly variable, analog signal into something more abstract**, so the language system can operate on it. An **analog signal** changes continuously. Frequency, loudness, and timing can each vary by small amounts, but every small change does not create a different speech sound. || A **symbolic representation** identifies a language unit rather than preserving every physical detail of the recording. A **phoneme** is a sound category that helps distinguish words. Different acoustic productions can count as the same phoneme, allowing different recordings to supply the same linguistic unit. || **Categorical perception** is one proposed way of making this translation. Listeners become less sensitive to some differences within a category and more sensitive to similar differences that cross its boundary. A change in the sound is especially noticeable when it changes the identity assigned to it. This organization can preserve useful distinctions while allowing variation within one identity.
4|The **vocal tract** is the passage through which sound travels during speech. Its shape changes as the tongue, lips, and mouth move. Those changes affect which frequencies receive the strongest amplification, so the sound reaching the listener depends partly on the shape of this passage. || **Formants** are the resonant frequencies emphasized by the vocal tract. Vowels such as those in heed, hid, head, and had differ in their combinations of formants. The remaining examples, including hod, hawed, hood, and who’d, involve other combinations. A vowel is characterized by the relationship among resonances rather than by one frequency that stays identical across speakers. || A **formant transition** is a change in one of these resonances over time. The transition occurs because the vocal tract is moving into or out of a sound. A steady portion supplies information about a vowel, while the movement toward that portion also carries information about how the sound is produced.
5|A **spectrogram** represents acoustic energy across time and frequency. Time describes when parts of the sound occur, and frequency describes how rapidly the air pressure is vibrating. Stronger energy appears darker. The prominent bands associated with a vowel include its resonances, called **formants**. || The vowels in **bead, bid, bade, bed, bad, bod, baud, bode, “bood,” and booed** differ even though their surrounding consonants are similar. Their resonance patterns change with vowel quality. Bead combines a relatively low first formant with a high second formant, while the second formant is much lower for rounded back vowels such as the vowel in booed. || **F1** is the lowest formant, and **F2** is the second-lowest. These are two resonances occurring within one vowel, rather than successive sounds in a word. Comparing their frequencies provides a way to describe how vowels differ without treating every acoustic component as a separate speech sound.
6|The vowel **/u/** contains several resonances within one continuous sound. In this recording, **F1 is near 300 Hz and F2 is near 900 Hz**. These values identify two frequency regions with strong energy. They do not mean that the speaker produces one sound at 300 Hz and then another at 900 Hz. || A **waveform** records changes in air pressure over time. A **spectrogram** separates the recording into frequency components, allowing its resonance pattern to be examined. Both representations describe the same spoken vowel, but they make different properties available for analysis. || **Praat** is software used to analyze speech sounds and estimate their formant frequencies. In **source–filter synthesis**, the sound energy supplied by the voice is separated from the filtering associated with the vocal tract. This separation allows the resonance pattern to be changed while the underlying sound source is kept constant.
7|**Praat can automatically identify formants** and track how their frequencies change during a recording. The analysis of /u/ includes five resonances. Each track follows one resonance through time, so several tracks can describe different frequency components of the same spoken sound. || **F1, F2, and F3** are usually most relevant to identifying phonemes. Their numbers refer to their order from low to high frequency. F1 is the lowest resonance, F2 is the next, and F3 is the third. The labels do not describe the order in which separate sounds are spoken. || The manipulation focuses on **F1 and F2**, which are near 300 Hz and 900 Hz in this /u/. Holding the remaining formants constant allows the effect of changing these lower resonances to be examined. A difference in the resulting vowel can then be connected to a particular part of its frequency pattern.
8|The voice supplies a **source** of sound energy, and the vocal tract acts as a **filter**. A filter changes how strongly different frequencies contribute to the final sound. The formants describe the resonances created by this filtering, so a recorded vowel contains effects of both the source and the filter. || Estimating the filter makes it possible to remove much of its contribution from the recording. The remaining signal approximates an **unfiltered voice** and sounds like a buzz. It still contains acoustic energy, but the vowel’s characteristic pattern of amplification has been reduced. || This estimation is comparable to separating a contribution within a combined signal. A filter can then be applied to the extracted source to produce a vowel again. Keeping that source unchanged while replacing the filter makes the role of the resonance pattern available for controlled testing. || The filter estimate comes from the formant frequencies in the recording. Removing that estimated pattern is comparable to separating one contribution from a combined measurement. The extracted source is approximate, so it is described as more-or-less unfiltered rather than a perfect direct recording of the source alone.
9|A **filter** specifies which frequencies in a source receive more or less emphasis. The original /u/ filter has low first and second formants. Applying that pattern to the source preserves the combination of resonances associated with the original vowel. || A second filter **raises F1** while keeping the higher formants approximately unchanged. A third filter **raises F2 substantially** instead. These manipulations change different parts of the resonance pattern, rather than moving every frequency together. || The **sound source remains the same** across the three filters. Any resulting difference in vowel quality therefore occurs without requiring a different voice or a new natural recording. The comparison isolates how a change in the lowest resonance differs from a change in the second resonance. || The first filter uses the first four formants from the original recording. Raising one of the lower formants changes the pattern without replacing the remaining resonances. This distinction matters because a vowel can change when only part of its spectral organization changes.
10|**Combining a source with different filters produces different vowels** because each filter emphasizes a different pattern of frequencies. The source supplies the available sound energy. The filter shapes how much of that energy remains prominent in each frequency region. || With the original filter, the low-formant pattern of /u/ is retained. Raising **F1** changes the lowest strong resonance, while raising **F2** changes the second. The resulting sounds can have different vowel qualities even though they come from the same underlying source. || **Source–filter synthesis** therefore separates two contributions that are combined in ordinary speech. The voice supplies the sound, and the vocal tract’s resonances shape its quality. A researcher can change that quality by replacing the filter without asking a speaker to produce a completely new utterance.
11|**Vowel space** describes relationships among vowels through their formant frequencies. F1 and F2 are useful because different vowel qualities involve different combinations of these resonances. A vowel occupies a range of values rather than one exact point that every speaker must reproduce. || **Anatomy, speaking rate, and surrounding sounds** affect these values. Different vocal tracts have different resonances, and the movements required for neighboring sounds affect the vowel as it is produced. The measured frequencies of different vowels can therefore overlap. || Formants still provide a useful description, especially for **one talker speaking carefully**. Changing the **steady-state formants**, meaning the resonances during a relatively stable part of the vowel, can produce recognizable vowel approximations. Natural speech also contains changes over time, so these stable values describe an important part of the signal without accounting for all its variation.
12|The **pattern playback synthesizer** turns a painted spectrographic pattern into sound. The pattern specifies where acoustic energy should occur across time and frequency. Changing the pattern changes the sound produced, allowing researchers to create controlled speech-like signals without relying on repeated natural utterances. || A **cylindrical lens** and rotating **tone wheel** produce changing light. A lens and mirror direct it toward the painted pattern, which allows different amounts of light through at different positions. The pattern therefore controls the fluctuations that continue through the device. || A **light collector and photocell** convert those fluctuations into an electrical signal. An amplifier strengthens the signal, and a loudspeaker produces the audible output. The researcher can alter a frequency band or transition in the pattern and hear the corresponding change in the synthesized sound.
13|The stop consonants **/b/, /d/, and /g/** differ partly in their **formant transitions**. These transitions are movements in resonance as the vocal tract opens into the following vowel. The audible part of a stop is produced during movement, so consonant information is carried by how the frequencies change rather than only by a stable portion of sound. || **Liberman and colleagues (1957)** created synthetic syllables with a similar lower formant and different starting frequencies for the upper formant. The upper transition could rise, remain relatively flat, or fall before reaching a similar vowel region. The following vowel could remain alike while its onset changed. || The **pattern playback synthesizer** allowed these properties to be controlled. Listeners heard speech-like sounds whose transitions differed in a specified way. Their responses could then be related to the transition itself, rather than to an uncontrolled collection of differences between natural utterances.
14|A **speech continuum** contains sounds that change gradually along a controlled acoustic property. Here, **F1 stays constant while F2 onset frequency changes linearly** across thirteen stimuli. A linear change means that each neighboring pair differs by the same programmed amount. || When F2 begins below the stable vowel frequency, its transition rises. Higher starting frequencies make the transition flatter and then produce a falling transition. The acoustic property changes gradually throughout this sequence, even if the listener begins assigning a different consonant label at particular positions. || Equally spaced pairs can fall within one category or cross a category boundary. Comparing those pairs holds their physical separation constant. If a boundary-crossing pair is easier to distinguish, its advantage cannot be explained simply by a larger change in the synthesized signal.
15|An **ideal observer model** describes responses expected under a particular account of perception. In a **noncategorical** account, uncertainty grows gradually as a sound moves away from a clear category example. That clear example is called a **prototype**. A sound between prototypes can resemble more than one category to different degrees. || The continuum extends from **/b/** through **/d/** to **/g/**. Moving away from the /b/ prototype gradually reduces support for /b/ and increases support for /d/. The proposed response to stimulus 2 is about **75% /b/ and 25% /d/**, representing partial uncertainty rather than an immediate change in identity. || The same gradual exchange occurs between /d/ and /g/. Labeling becomes blurry between prototypes because two labels are plausible. This account predicts smooth changes across the continuum, rather than sharp switches confined to narrow category boundaries.
16|A **labeling task** asks a listener to identify a sound as /b/, /d/, or /g/. A **discrimination task** asks whether two sounds are the same or different. Naming a category and detecting an acoustic difference are separate responses, even when both tasks use the same continuum. || If sensitivity to F2 onset is **linear**, equal acoustic differences should be equally noticeable. Sounds 1 and 3 should be about as easy to distinguish as sounds 5 and 7 because each pair has the same physical separation. Their position along the continuum should not by itself change discrimination. || This account predicts **discrimination independent of labeling**. Choosing among three names produces rises and falls in labeling responses near the prototypes. Detecting equally sized physical differences should remain approximately constant, rather than becoming better specifically where a label changes. || A listener can be uncertain which of two names to choose while still hearing that two sounds differ. The model therefore makes separate predictions for the two tasks. A labeling peak is not automatically a prediction of a discrimination peak at the same position.
17|**Liberman and colleagues (1957)** found sharp changes in consonant identification across an evenly changing acoustic continuum. Early stimuli mainly received **/b/** labels, middle stimuli **/d/** labels, and later stimuli **/g/** labels. The changes near category boundaries were more abrupt than the gradual uncertainty predicted by a noncategorical account. || Listeners were **best at discriminating sounds near the category boundaries**. Equally spaced sounds were easier to tell apart when they received different consonant labels. This relationship appeared with one-step, two-step, and three-step comparisons, so it was not limited to one chosen pair size. || Increasing physical separation generally improved discrimination, but it did not explain why performance peaked near the boundaries. Predictions derived from labeling captured much of that pattern. Some acoustic sensitivity remained beyond the simplest categorical prediction, so category identification and discrimination were related without being identical measures.

19|In **beach–peach identification**, one listener keeps the same label through the early continuum and gives mixed responses near the boundary. Further along, the other word becomes the consistent answer. The acoustic input changes gradually, while the chosen word changes within a narrower region. || That listener is better at distinguishing pairs **4–6 and 5–7**, which cross the identification boundary. Pairs well inside one category are harder to tell apart despite a similar physical separation. Pair 6–8 falls between these patterns because stimulus 6 itself receives mixed labels. || Another listener changes word labels later and has stronger discrimination closer to that later boundary. People can therefore differ in exactly where they switch categories. The shared relationship is stronger discrimination around the region where their own word identification changes.
20|**Category boundaries differ across listeners**. The same sound can lie near the beach–peach boundary for one person but farther inside a category for another. A pair that crosses one person’s boundary might therefore remain within the other person’s category. || **Discrimination is generally better across categories** than within them. A listener may fail to detect a physical difference between two versions of the same phoneme. A similarly sized difference becomes more noticeable when the sounds are assigned different linguistic identities. || The shortened task used only **physically different pairs**. Every trial therefore had “different” as its correct physical answer. A “same” response indicated that the listener had not detected the difference, not that the recording contained identical sounds. The responses concern what was perceived, while the construction of the stimuli establishes what physically changed.
21|The **bear–pear** identification results contain a fairly clear change in word label, while discrimination varies irregularly across pairs. The **beach–peach** results also include fluctuations. A carefully constructed continuum does not guarantee that every measured response will form a smooth curve. || Identification records the word a listener chooses, whereas discrimination records whether a difference is detected. These behaviors can vary differently across trials and people. An isolated rise in discrimination does not establish that an additional phoneme category exists at that location. || The scientific comparison concerns whether easier discrimination tends to occur where identification changes. Class responses can contain that relationship alongside substantial variability. Evaluating the account requires relating the two measurements, rather than treating every fluctuation as a new categorical distinction.
22|**Reduced sensitivity to some sound differences can support stable word recognition**. Different productions of the same phoneme are physically different. Treating every small acoustic change as a new identity would make ordinary versions of one word seem unrelated. || **Categorical perception** allows different acoustic versions to count as members of the same category. Differences inside that category become less noticeable, while differences separating categories remain useful. The listener can recognize the same language unit across changes that do not alter the word. || This advantage does not require equal sensitivity to every detail of the sound. What matters is preserving differences that separate linguistic identities while tolerating variation within them. Being worse at detecting some physical changes can therefore be compatible with being better at recognizing a stable message.

24|The **conditioned head turn procedure** measures whether an infant detects a change in sound. The infant learns an association between the change and a visual reward. Turning toward the sound provides an observable response without requiring the infant to name what was heard. || **High-amplitude sucking** measures a different behavior. Sucking can decline as a repeated sound becomes familiar. If a new sound causes sucking to increase again, the change has affected the infant’s behavior and provides evidence that the sounds were distinguished. || Both methods make perception testable before spoken responses are available. The experimenter controls the sound difference and measures a learned turn or renewed sucking. These responses establish sensitivity to the change, rather than requiring an infant to perform an adult labeling task.
25|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** used **high-amplitude sucking** to test infant speech discrimination. Infants first heard a repeated synthetic syllable until sucking declined. The sound then changed across an adult phoneme boundary, changed within a category, or remained unchanged. || **Voice onset time**, or **VOT**, describes when voicing begins relative to the consonant release. In **20D**, a change from **+20 to +40 milliseconds** crossed the adult boundary and sucking recovered. In **20S**, an equally large change remained within a category, either **−20 to 0** or **+60 to +80 milliseconds**, and did not produce comparable recovery. || In the **0 condition**, the sound stayed the same and sucking continued to decline. The equal size of the two changed conditions matters because acoustic distance alone cannot explain their different effects. Infants responded more strongly when the change crossed the adult category boundary, before they could provide verbal labels. || A recovery in sucking is the measured behavior, while discrimination is the inference drawn from it. The unchanged control establishes what continued repetition does. The within-category comparison then tests whether any equally sized acoustic change is enough to produce the recovery seen across the boundary.
26|**Perceptual narrowing** occurs when experience tunes perception to the surrounding language while sensitivity to some other distinctions decreases. English-learning infants initially distinguish the tested **Hindi and Salish contrasts** relatively well. Their performance becomes poorer as the first year progresses. || Many infants meet the discrimination criterion at **6–8 months**. Fewer do so at **8–10 months**, and performance is much lower at **10–12 months**. Infants learning Hindi or Salish retain stronger discrimination for the contrast used in their language. || **Werker and Tees** relate this developmental change to experience with a particular sound system. The infant becomes adapted to distinctions that occur in that system. The same development can leave distinctions from other languages harder to detect, rather than preserving equally strong sensitivity to every speech contrast.
27|One proposal treats **categorical perception as a special property of the language system**. Under this account, infants begin life with category-organizing abilities that support speech perception. Their early responses are attributed to a starting system prepared for linguistic distinctions. || The description **“citizens of the world”** captures the claim that young infants can initially detect contrasts used across languages. Experience rapidly tunes this broad sensitivity to the language heard around them. Distinctions used in that language remain available, while sensitivity to others declines. || This is an account of both the initial state and developmental change. It proposes broad early abilities followed mainly by narrowing. The claim is stronger than saying that adults recognize native speech well, because it also specifies what infants can distinguish before extensive language experience.
28|**Categorical perception also occurs outside human language**. Category-related responses have been observed with nonspeech sounds and in other animals. A categorical pattern therefore does not by itself establish that the mechanism is unique to human speech. || The strength of the pattern also depends on the **task**. Naming a sound, detecting a difference, and making a learned response place different demands on a listener. A categorical final answer can be especially pronounced when the task requires a choice between labels. || **Infant perception is not completely universal**. Some contrasts are already harder to detect than others early in life. Development includes initial differences in sensitivity as well as changes caused by experience. These observations challenge the stronger claim that every speech distinction begins equally available in a system devoted only to language.
29|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** tested categorical perception with **nonspeech sounds** that differed in **rise time**. Rise time is how quickly a sound becomes stronger after it begins. A rapid onset sounds plucked, while a slower onset sounds bowed. || The materials included **sawtooth and sine waves**, with speech syllables included for comparison. Listeners could assign pluck or bow labels even though the manipulated sounds were not different phonemes. The relevant property was the rate of increase in amplitude. || For sawtooth waves, increasing rise time changed the preferred label from pluck to bow. Discrimination was strongest near that labeling change rather than remaining constant for equally spaced sounds. The relationship between a category boundary and improved discrimination can therefore occur with a nonlinguistic auditory distinction. || Pluck and bow are perceptual labels for different onset qualities, not names of phonemes. The physical continuum remains gradual across the change in preferred label. Improved discrimination near that change links the result to categorical organization without requiring the listener to interpret the sounds as speech.
30|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** tested infants’ responses to **nonspeech sounds**. After a sound was repeated, the experiment compared no change, a change within a category, and a change between categories. Sucking behavior provided the measure of whether the change was detected. || Sucking declined as the repeated sound became familiar in all conditions. It stayed relatively low after no change and after a within-category change. A between-category change caused sucking to increase again, followed by another decline as that sound became familiar. || The boundary-crossing change affected behavior more strongly than a change within the category. Infants can therefore display category-related discrimination for sounds that are not speech. An ability’s early appearance does not establish that it operates only on phonemes or only within language.
31|Chinchillas learned to respond to speech sounds through **operant conditioning**, in which consequences change the likelihood of a behavior. A stimulus with **0-millisecond or 80-millisecond lag** signaled that a response would produce food. This rewarded signal is called the **SD**, or discriminative stimulus. || Tests with intermediate **voice onset times** measured how the response extended across a **/ga/–/ka/** continuum. Chinchillas and human comparison listeners changed their responses sharply within part of that continuum. The animals could categorize the acoustic distinction without possessing human language. || **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and **Kuhl and Miller (1975)** are associated with these comparisons. Making the initial burst and aspiration noise stronger could shift the boundary toward shorter VOTs or change how sharply responses switched. The categorization depended on acoustic properties in both the animal and human responses.
32|Some speech distinctions are **less salient**, meaning harder to notice, than others early in development. At **4–5 months**, English-learning infants respond more clearly to **[ma]–[na]** than to the nonnative **[na]–[ŋa]** contrast. Increased looking after a change provides evidence that the infant detected it. || These contrasts involve different **places of articulation**. The nasal in [ma] is made with the lips, the nasal in [na] near the ridge behind the teeth, and the nasal in [ŋa] farther back in the mouth. The comparison concerns whether changing that place changes the infant’s response. || Filipino-learning infants respond weakly to their native [na]–[ŋa] contrast at **6–8 months**, but more clearly at **10–12 months**. Experience can therefore strengthen a distinction that begins difficult to hear. Speech development includes increased sensitivity to some contrasts as well as declining sensitivity to others.
