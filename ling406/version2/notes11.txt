Section 1: A Very Old and Very General Model of Speech Perception
Categorical perception is the tendency to hear a continuously varying speech signal as a
small number of discrete sound categories. The analog signal comes in through the
cochlea, and by the time it activates phonemes, a lot of information has been lost. The
listener no longer keeps track of exactly how a sound was produced. The listener keeps
only which category the sound belongs to.
This loss of information is helpful. When the system throws away acoustic detail that
does not separate one category from another, it can make contact with higher levels of
representation, such as words. A natural assumption follows from this: in order to
identify words, we first need to identify their constituent phonemes.
Klatt (1979), published in the Journal of Phonetics, described speech perception as a
fixed series of stages. Each stage takes in one kind of representation and passes a
transformed version to the next stage.
1. The speech waveform enters the peripheral auditory system, which performs
a spectral analysis and breaks the sound into the frequencies it contains.
2. The spectral analysis feeds a set of acoustic property detectors. Their output is
detector outputs versus time, a record of which acoustic properties are present
at each moment.
3. Those detector outputs feed phonetic feature detectors. Their output is
feature probabilities versus time, an estimate of how likely each phonetic
feature, such as voicing, is at each moment.
4. The feature probabilities feed segmental analysis, which produces a
segmental feature matrix. This is a sequence of segments, or phonemes, each
described by its set of features.
5. The segmental feature matrix feeds lexical search, which produces lexical
hypotheses, the candidate words that match the sequence of segments.
In this model, each stage hands a finished product to the next stage, and word recognition
starts only after the phonemes have been identified. Two problems challenge this simple
view: cascaded processing and feedback.
Cascaded processing challenges the idea that each stage has to finish its work
before the next stage can start.
Feedback challenges the idea that information only flows upward, from sound
to phonemes to words, and never back down.
Section 2: A Voicing Continuum from "Peach" to "Beach"
A voicing continuum is a series of synthetic speech sounds that changes in small, equal
steps from one word to another along a single acoustic dimension. This continuum has
nine steps running between "peach" and "beach." The dimension that changes is voice
onset time (VOT), which is the delay between the release of a stop consonant and the
moment the vocal folds start vibrating. A long VOT is heard as the voiceless /p/, and a
short VOT is heard as the voiced /b/. Making the VOT shorter moves the sound from
"peach" toward "beach."


===== PAGE 2 =====
Each step can be displayed as a spectrogram, with frequency from 0 to 5000 Hz on the
vertical axis, time from 0 to 0.5 seconds on the horizontal axis, and darkness representing
how much energy is present.
The dark horizontal bands are formants, the resonant frequencies of the vocal
tract, and they mark the vowel.
The fine vertical stripes inside those bands are individual pulses of the vocal
folds, so they mark voicing.
After the vowel there is a short blank gap of silence followed by a patch of
scattered noise, and that noise is the "ch" sound at the end of both words.
The spectrograms for "peach," for the middle steps, and for "beach" look nearly
identical. The only thing that changes across the continuum is the timing of
voicing at the very start of the word.
In the identification task, listeners hear one step at a time and label it as one word or the
other. The identification proportion stays at 1 for steps 1 through 5, falls to about 0.33 at
step 6, and drops to 0 for steps 7 through 9. Steps 1 through 5 receive the same label on
every trial, and steps 7 through 9 receive the other label on every trial.
Step 6 is circled in red because it is the only ambiguous step. It sits right at the
category boundary, and listeners give it one label on about a third of trials and
the other label on the rest.
In the discrimination task, listeners hear two steps that are two positions apart and
judge whether they are the same or different. The pairs 1–3, 2–4, and 3–5 are
discriminated at 0. The pairs 4–6 and 5–7 are discriminated at 1. The pair 6–8 is
discriminated at about 0.33, and the pair 7–9 drops back to 0.
The physical difference is exactly the same for every pair, since every pair is two
steps apart.
Pairs inside one category cannot be told apart at all, while pairs that straddle the
boundary are told apart perfectly.
The 6–8 pair is discriminated at about 0.33, which matches the roughly one-
third of trials on which step 6 is labeled differently from step 8.
This pattern is the core of categorical perception. How well two sounds can be
distinguished depends on whether they get different category labels, not on how
different they are acoustically.
Section 3: How Categorical Perception Could Happen: The Threshold Model
In a threshold model, activity is only passed forward from one level to the next when an
activity threshold is crossed. A lower level can be partly active for a long time, but the
level above it receives nothing until that activity reaches a fixed cutoff. In this kind of
model, categorical perception arises at the stage of phoneme identification.
At the feature level, the activation of the voicing feature rises and falls like a wave across
100 time steps. Two dashed lines mark the thresholds, one at about +1.25 for +voiced and
one at about −1.25 for −voiced.
The wave first peaks at about 1.05 and then at about 1.2, so both peaks stay
below the +voiced threshold.
The wave dips to about −0.85 and then about −0.7, so both dips stay above the
−voiced threshold.
Only at the very end does the wave climb to about 1.4 and cross the +voiced
threshold.
At the phoneme level, activation stays completely flat at 0 for almost the entire time, even
though the feature level is swinging up and down. Right around time step 97, when the
feature level finally crosses the +voiced threshold, activation for /b/ jumps suddenly to
about 1.5. The /p/ threshold at the bottom is never reached.


===== PAGE 3 =====
The phoneme level receives no information at all while the feature level is
undecided.
Once the threshold is crossed, the phoneme is either fully on or fully off, so the
output is all-or-none.
Graded differences at the feature level never reach the phoneme level, which is
why the phoneme level behaves categorically.
In this model, two tokens that are both eventually labeled /b/ look identical to every level
above the phoneme, even if one of them had much weaker evidence for voicing than the
other.
Section 4: How Categorical Perception Could Happen: The Cascaded Model
In a cascaded model, activity is passed forward continuously over time from one level to
the next. A lower level does not have to reach a cutoff before the level above it starts
responding. In this kind of model, categorical perception might happen later on in
processing, or it might even be an artifact of the task structure.
With the same feature-level input as before, phoneme activation now follows the feature
activation moment by moment. It rises to about 1.0, dips to about −0.9, rises to about 1.1,
dips to about −0.85, and ends at about 1.2, just under the /b/ threshold. The phoneme-
level curve is a slightly weaker copy of the feature-level curve.
Partial evidence for voicing produces partial activation of /b/.
Partial evidence against voicing produces partial activation in the direction of
/p/.
The levels above the phoneme therefore receive graded information, including
how good an example of /b/ a particular sound is.
The idea that categorical perception could be an artifact of the task structure comes
from the way identification tasks are set up. The listener is forced to choose one of two
labels on every trial. Even if the internal representation is graded, the required answer is
discrete, so the response curve comes out step-shaped. A step-shaped identification
function could reflect the decision the task demands rather than the form of the
representation underneath.
Section 5: A Metaphor for Thresholded Activity: Making Furniture
Thresholded processing works like a chain of production in which each step takes a
finished product from the step before.
1. A lumber mill takes trees as input and produces lumber as output.
2. A furniture factory takes lumber as input and produces unassembled
furniture as output, meaning flat wooden pieces, legs, round tabletops, screws,
and hardware.
3. An IKEA customer takes unassembled furniture as input and produces
finished furniture, such as a round table, as output.
Each "layer" of this system can only take a specific input that has been transformed
completely by the previous layer. The factory has to wait for the shipment of lumber
before it can start manufacturing. The customer cannot start assembling until the boxed
parts arrive, and a half-cut tree is of no use to the factory.
In thresholded speech perception, the phoneme level waits for the feature level to deliver
a finished decision, and the word level waits for finished phonemes. Nothing partial is
ever passed along.
Many computer programs function this way. One routine runs to completion and then
hands its output to the next routine.
Section 6: A Metaphor for Cascaded Processing: A Food Web


===== PAGE 4 =====
Cascaded processing works more like a desert food web, where arrows run from each
food source to the animals that eat it.
Desert plants are eaten by insects, small lizards, and kangaroo rats.
Insects are eaten by scorpions and large lizards, and small lizards are also
eaten by large lizards.
Large lizards, snakes, and kangaroo rats in turn feed the top predators, hawks
and desert foxes.
The "levels" of a food web continuously interact with one another. A drought that
reduces the number of plants immediately affects the health of the kangaroo rats, which
then affects the snakes, and so on up the web. No level waits for another level to finish
anything, and change spreads across the system continuously.
A food web also contains clear feedback loops. Adding more hawks changes the
populations of snakes, lizards, and rats, so influence also travels from the top of the web
back down.
The central question is whether phoneme identification for word recognition is more
like thresholded processing (the lumber mill) or cascaded processing (the food web).
Section 7: The McMurray et al. (2003) Visual World Experiment
McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin, and Spivey (2003) published "Probabilistic constraint
satisfaction at the lexical/phonetic interface: Evidence for gradient effects of within-
category VOT on lexical access" in the Journal of Psycholinguistic Research, volume 32,
pages 77–97. They tested identification of a /b/–/p/ continuum in a "visual world" display
designed for eye-tracking.
In the visual world paradigm, participants look at a screen of pictures while hearing a
spoken word, and an eye-tracker records where they look from moment to moment. Eye
movements are fast and largely automatic, so they reveal which candidate words a
listener is considering while the word is still unfolding.
Each display contained four pictures. The target was a "pear," the competitor was a
"bear," and the two filler items were a "lamp" and a "ship."
"Bear" and "pear" form a minimal pair, two words that differ in only one sound,
here the voicing of the first consonant.
The fillers are unrelated to the target, so looks to them give a baseline for
random looking.
The spoken words came from a continuum that varied VOT from 0 to 40 ms in 5 ms steps.
Participants used the mouse to click the picture most consistent with what they heard,
such as the bear or the pear.
The mouse responses produced a typical, step-like identification function. The
proportion of /p/ responses is near 0 at 0, 5, and 10 ms, rises only slightly at 15 ms, jumps
to about 0.95 at 20 ms, sits at about 0.92 at 25 ms, and stays near 1.0 from 30 to 40 ms.
The category boundary falls at
 ms of VOT.
Judged by the clicks alone, these data match exactly what a threshold model
predicts: a sharp jump from one category to the other.
Section 8: Eye Movements Reveal Gradient Ambiguity
The eye movements reveal that ambiguity is gradient, meaning it changes smoothly with
distance from the boundary rather than being all-or-none. Listeners make more looks to
the competitor when the VOT is close to the category boundary.
To isolate this effect, trials were sorted by which picture the participant finally clicked.
Then the proportion of fixations to the competitor picture was tracked over time, from 0
to 2000 ms after word onset.
On trials where the participant clicked the bear, the VOTs were 0, 5, 10, and 15
ms, and the competitor was the pear. Looks to the pear start at about 0.04, peak

ms. The 15 ms tokens produce the highest peak, about 0.15, and keep looks to

On trials where the participant clicked the pear, the VOTs were 20, 25, 30, 35,

According to McMurray and colleagues, the effect of VOT falls mainly on the duration of
activation. Near-boundary tokens do not just raise competitor looks briefly. They keep
the competitor active for a longer stretch of time.

Early-window looks are higher overall, since listeners are still considering both pictures
at that point. Late-window looks are much lower, since listeners have mostly settled on an
answer. The gradient is still present late, which fits the idea that near-boundary tokens
keep the competitor active for longer.
Section 10: Cascaded Activation from Features to Phonemes in the TRACE Architecture
The gradient competitor effect fits the idea that activation flows in a cascaded way from
features to phonemes. The TRACE model of spoken word recognition is built around
three layers of processing units.
The feature layer receives a pseudo-spectral input representation, a
simplified, spectrogram-like coding of the speech signal, across time slices
numbered 0 to 44. It has seven feature dimensions labeled POW, VOC, DIFF,
ACU, GRD, VOI, and BUR, where VOI stands for voicing and BUR stands for the
burst of air at the release of a stop. Horizontal bars mark which values of each
feature are active at each moment, and the cross-hatched region from about 36
to 44 marks the end of the input.


===== PAGE 6 =====
The phoneme layer contains units for phonemes such as /^/ (the vowel in
"but"), /b/, /r/, and /t/. Each phoneme unit is copied many times in overlapping
rows, so there is a separate detector for that phoneme starting at each point in
time. The boxed units are the most active copies.
The word layer contains units for whole words, such as "abrupt," written ^ b r ^
p t, and a word written b r i d. Each word is also copied across time, and the
bolded, boxed copy of "abrupt" is the one lined up with the input.
Arrows run upward from the feature layer to the phoneme layer, and both
upward and downward between the phoneme layer and the word layer.
The curved connectors with filled circles beside each layer stand for lateral
inhibition. Units within the same layer compete, and an active unit suppresses
its rivals.
Even stimuli that are identified as /b/ on 100% of trials can vary in how strongly and
unambiguously the "voiced" feature is activated. A token with a 0 ms VOT activates
voicing very strongly, while a token with a 15 ms VOT activates it more weakly, even
though both are labeled /b/.
If a stimulus is obviously +bilabial, meaning it is made with both lips, and obviously
+stop, meaning airflow is completely blocked and then released, the only open question
is voicing. This creates some momentary ambiguity between /b/ and /p/, the two bilabial
stops.
In a cascaded system, the weak voicing activation passes partly to /b/ and partly
to /p/.
Those partially active phonemes pass activation on to the words "bear" and
"pear."
That ambiguity shows up in eye movements while the participant is deciding
where to click.
Section 11: Partial Activation Reaches the Lexical Level: Andruski et al. (1994)
Andruski et al. (1994), published in Cognition, volume 52, pages 163–187, found
evidence that partial activation can reach all the way to the lexical level, the level where
words are stored. They found that you can even get semantic priming with nonwords.
Semantic priming happens when hearing one word speeds up processing of a word
related in meaning. Andruski and colleagues measured it with a lexical decision task,
where participants decide as quickly as possible whether a target string is a real word. A
faster reaction time to the target means the prime had already partly activated it.
The primes began with voiceless stops. The initial consonant was either unaltered or had
its VOT shortened by one third (−1/3) or two thirds (−2/3), which pushed it toward its
voiced counterpart. The unaltered, −1/3, and −2/3 primes were all semantically related to
the target, and a set of unrelated primes served as the baseline.
In word-to-word items, the voiced counterpart of the prime is a real word, as
when "pet" is pushed toward "bet." For example, "pill" pushed toward "bill" still
primes "drug."
In word-to-nonword items, the voiced counterpart of the prime is a nonword, as
when "king" is pushed toward "ging." For example, "beace" primes "war," and
"ging" primes "queen."
Mean reaction times to the targets in Experiment 1 were approximately:
Unaltered primes: about 858 ms for word-to-word items and about 808 ms for
word-to-nonword items.
−1/3 primes: about 857 ms for word-to-word items and about 820 ms for word-
to-nonword items.
−2/3 primes: about 888 ms for word-to-word items and about 816 ms for word-
to-nonword items.


===== PAGE 7 =====
Unrelated primes: about 965 ms for word-to-word items and about 945 ms for
word-to-nonword items.
Every related condition is faster than its unrelated baseline, by roughly 75 to 140 ms. In
the word-to-nonword items, priming survives even at −2/3, where the target is about 130
ms faster than baseline. In the word-to-word items at −2/3, priming is smaller, about 77
ms, so the advantage shrinks when the altered prime is pushed toward another real word.
Lexical representations can be activated even by incomplete phonetic information. A
token of "peace" with a shortened VOT, heard as something like "beace," is not a clean /p/,
yet it still activates "peace" strongly enough to speed recognition of "war."
Section 12: Graded Competitor Looks Are Evidence Against a Threshold Model
The graded eye-movement pattern is one piece of evidence for cascaded processing in
speech perception. In a thresholded model, it would be very hard to explain why the
number of looks to the other member of the minimal pair increases gradually as the VOT
approaches the category boundary.
In the threshold model, the connection between the feature level and the phoneme level
is effectively blocked, marked with a red X, until the feature level crosses its threshold.
Graded voicing activation never reaches the phonemes, and the phoneme level stays flat
until it commits all at once.
Under that model, every token categorized as /b/ would produce the same /b/
activation, the same word activation, and the same number of looks to the pear.
The data contradict this prediction. In the late window, looks to the pear rise
from about 0.018 at 0 ms to about 0.043 at 15 ms, even though every one of
those tokens was clicked as "bear."
In a cascaded model, graded feature activation flows continuously into the phoneme and
word levels. A 15 ms token partly activates /p/ and therefore partly activates "pear," which
produces more looks to the pear than a 0 ms token does.

Section 13: Feedback from the Lexical Layer to the Phoneme Layer
The TRACE model also includes feedback from the lexical layer to the phoneme layer.
The arrows between these two layers run in both directions. Phonemes send activation
up to the words that contain them, and active words send activation back down to the
phonemes they contain.
Bottom-up connections carry evidence from the signal upward, from features
to phonemes to words.
Top-down connections carry knowledge of words downward, so an active word
strengthens the phonemes that belong to it.
With feedback, word knowledge can change how a phoneme is perceived. A sound that is
acoustically ambiguous between two phonemes can be pushed toward the phoneme that
completes a real word. Several experiments provide evidence for this kind of feedback.
Section 14: Gisses and Kifts for Valentine's Day: The Ganong Effect
Ganong (1980) studied how phoneme identification is affected by word knowledge.
The question was whether knowing which sound sequences are real words changes
where listeners place a phoneme category boundary.
Ganong built word/nonword pairs in which one end of a voicing continuum is a real word
and the other end is not, such as "corpse/gorpse." "Corpse" begins with the voiceless /k/
and is a word, while "gorpse" begins with the voiced /g/ and is not a word.
"Gisses and kifts" is built from the same kind of pairs. In "kiss/giss," the word is
at the voiceless end, so a "giss" is the nonword version. In "gift/kift," the word is
at the voiced end, so a "kift" is the nonword version.


===== PAGE 8 =====
Some continua have the word at the voiced end, labeled Word–Nonword, and
others have the word at the voiceless end, labeled Nonword–Word.
For each pair, Ganong produced a continuum spanning the relevant phoneme boundary
in 5 ms steps of VOT. Listeners reported whether each token began with the voiced or the
voiceless consonant.
Responses are plotted as the proportion of voiced responses at six positions along the
continuum: the short-VOT endpoint, −7.5 ms, −2.5 ms, +2.5 ms, +7.5 ms, and the long-VOT
endpoint, with the boundary falling between −2.5 and +2.5 ms. The lexical effect is the
difference in voiced responses between Word–Nonword and Nonword–Word continua at
each position. A larger lexical effect means word knowledge pulled responses further
toward the word.
Section 15: Prediction One: A Late "Correction" Process
One possibility is a late "correction" process. Categorical perception works as usual, but
afterward there is a bias to report having heard a word. The bias operates on the
response, not on perception itself.
Under this model, the lexical effect is flat at about 0.25 across the whole continuum. The
bias adds the same amount everywhere, no matter how clear or ambiguous the sound is.
The Word–Nonword continuum gives voiced responses of about 1.00 at the
short-VOT end, 0.97 at −7.5 ms, 0.90 at −2.5 ms, 0.45 at +2.5 ms, 0.28 at +7.5 ms,
and 0.25 at the long-VOT end.
The neutral baseline, where neither end has a lexical advantage, gives about
1.00, 0.96, 0.73, 0.27, 0.04, and 0.00.
The Nonword–Word continuum gives about 0.75, 0.72, 0.65, 0.20, 0.03, and 0.00.
Subtracting the Nonword–Word value from the Word–Nonword value gives the lexical
effect. At the short-VOT end it is 1.00 minus 0.75, which is 0.25. At −2.5 ms it is 0.90 minus
0.65, which is 0.25. At the long-VOT end it is 0.25 minus 0.00, which is 0.25.
A flat lexical effect means that even a perfectly clear endpoint token gets reported as the
word on an extra quarter of trials. A clear "gorpse" would sometimes be reported as
"corpse" simply out of a preference for words.
Section 16: Prediction Two: Word Knowledge Shifts the Category Boundary
A second possibility is that word knowledge works to shift the categorical boundary.
Under this model, the word changes where the boundary falls rather than adding a fixed
bias to every response.
Under this model, the lexical effect is near 0 at both endpoints, about 0.1 at −7.5 and +7.5
ms, and peaks at about 0.38 at −2.5 and +2.5 ms.
The Word–Nonword continuum gives voiced responses of about 1.00, 0.99,
0.88, 0.50, 0.12, and 0.01.
The neutral baseline is the same as before: about 1.00, 0.96, 0.73, 0.27, 0.04, and
0.00.
The Nonword–Word continuum gives about 0.99, 0.88, 0.50, 0.12, 0.01, and 0.00.
The two lexical curves are copies of the neutral curve slid sideways in opposite directions.
At −2.5 ms the lexical effect is 0.88 minus 0.50, which is 0.38. At −7.5 ms it is 0.99 minus
0.88, which is 0.11. At the endpoints it is essentially 0.
The lexical effect is strongest for the items closest to the category boundary. Those are
the stimuli for which lexical information can help resolve ambiguity about the initial
phoneme. A clear endpoint token needs no help from word knowledge, so the word has
almost no effect there.

the word level flows back down and strengthens the phoneme that completes the word,
which moves the boundary at the level of perception itself.
Section 18: A Simulation of the Ganong Effect in the TRACE Model
TRACE simulates the Ganong effect with an input whose first segment is ambiguous
between /b/ and /p/, followed by /l/, /^/, and /g/. The only real word this input can match is
"plug," while the /b/ reading leads only to nonwords. Activation at the phoneme level and
the word level is tracked at four points as the input unfolds, with silence marked by a
dash at each end of the input.
Frame 1, at the ambiguous first segment:
At the phoneme level, the unit for the preceding silence is still high at about 0.6,
/b/ and /p/ are nearly tied at about 0.2, and a weak /d/ unit sits near 0.1.
At the word level, only the silence unit is active, at about 0.17.
Frame 2, as /l/ arrives:
At the phoneme level, /b/ and /p/ are still nearly tied at about 0.42, /l/ is at about
0.45, /r/ is at about 0.37, and /^/ is starting to rise at about 0.25.
At the word level, the silence unit is at about 0.33, and a large cluster of
candidate words starting with /b/ or /p/ sits between about 0.05 and 0.12.
Frame 3, as /^/ arrives:
At the phoneme level, /l/ and /^/ are at about 0.5, /g/ is at about 0.45, /p/ is just
above /b/ at about 0.43, /r/ is at about 0.37, /k/ is at about 0.25, /a/ is at about 0.17,
and /d/ is at about 0.08.
At the word level, "plug" has risen to about 0.28. Words such as "blood" and
"blush" are also active at about 0.12 to 0.17, since they match the beginning "bl-"
plus the vowel.
Frame 4, as the final /g/ arrives:
At the phoneme level, /^/ is at about 0.63, /g/ is at about 0.6, /l/ is at about 0.57,
and /p/ has pulled ahead of /b/, about 0.47 versus about 0.40.
At the word level, "plug" is above 0.45 and is now the most active word, while
the other candidates have dropped to about 0.07 to 0.1.
As the input unfolds over time, activity accumulates for "plug," especially when the final
/g/ arrives. The /g/ is highly inconsistent with "blood" and "blush," so those competitors
fall away and "plug" wins.
TRACE explains word effects on phoneme category boundaries in terms of top-down
processing. Activity at the lexical level produces feedback to the phoneme level, which

The acoustic evidence for /b/ and /p/ is the same throughout.
The growing activation of "plug" sends activation back down to /p/.
That added activation pushes /p/ above /b/, so the ambiguous sound is

Section 19: The Interactive Activation Model
The TRACE model is a version of the Interactive Activation model for written word
recognition, developed by McClelland and Rumelhart (1981).
The Interactive Activation network has three levels of units.
At the feature level, units detect simple line segments, such as horizontal,
vertical, and diagonal strokes.
At the letter level, units stand for letters in a given position, such as D, K, and R
in the fourth position of a word.
At the word level, units stand for whole words, such as WEAR, WORK, WORD,
and WEAK.
Excitatory connections link consistent units across levels, so a K unit supports
WORK and WEAK. Inhibitory connections link rival units within a level, so
word units compete with each other and letter units compete with each other.
A hypothetical set of features extracted on one trial of a word perception experiment
consists of the letters W, O, and R, followed by a fourth letter that is partly covered by ink
blots. The visible strokes of the fourth letter fit either an R or a K.
The letter level alone cannot decide between R and K.
The word level settles the question: WORK is a word and WORR is not, so the
WORK unit becomes active and sends activation back down to K.
The fourth letter is then perceived as K, which is the written version of the same
top-down effect seen in the Ganong effect.
Many of the principles are the same in both models, including excitation between levels,
competition within levels, and feedback from words to smaller units. A key issue in
speech perception is the way the stimuli unfold over time. A written word is available all
at once, while a spoken word arrives one sound after another. TRACE handles this by
copying every feature, phoneme, and word unit across time positions.
Section 20: Categorical Perception and the "Lexical-Phonetic Interface"
Perception appears to be shaped by category boundaries. Given the same physical
difference, it is more difficult to distinguish two sounds from the same category than two
sounds from different categories.
The findings on cascaded processing and feedback do not undermine that basic finding.
They add two further facts about how phonemes and words relate.
Ambiguity about phoneme identity affects word recognition. Near-boundary
VOT values produce more looks to the competitor word, and altered primes still
activate the meanings of words.
Word-level information affects phoneme categorization. In the Ganong
effect, the boundary shifts toward whichever end of the continuum forms a real
word.
The word recognition system therefore cannot just sit and wait for the phoneme
recognition system to finish its job, the way a furniture factory waits for lumber from the
mill. Spoken word recognition is more interactive than that. Information flows
continuously between levels, both upward and downward.
Section 21: Spoken Word Recognition Is Fast: Allopenna et al. (1994)


===== PAGE 11 =====
Allopenna et al. (1994), published in the Journal of Memory and Language, used eye-
tracking to measure how quickly spoken words are recognized. Spoken word
recognition is also very fast.
Participants saw a 5-by-5 grid with a fixation cross in the center. Four pictures
surrounded the cross: a beaker, a beetle, a speaker, and a baby carriage. Four geometric
shapes, a triangle, a square, a circle, and a diamond, filled other cells.
The referent is the word actually spoken, "beaker."
The cohort competitor, "beetle," shares the beginning of the word, the sounds
"bea-."
The rhyme competitor, "speaker," shares the end of the word, "-eaker."
The unrelated item, "carriage," shares no sounds with the referent.
The probability of fixating each picture type was tracked from 0 to 1000 ms after the
spoken word began, in the full competitor condition of Experiment 1. The data are
averaged over all stimulus sets, and beaker, beetle, speaker, and carriage are the
examples from one set.
Referent looks start rising at about 200 ms, reach about 0.2 at about 400 ms,
about 0.5 at about 600 ms, and level off near 0.95 from about 850 ms onward.
Cohort looks also start rising at about 200 ms and climb right alongside the
referent at first. They peak at about 0.18 around 500 to 550 ms and then fall to
near 0 by about 800 ms.
Rhyme looks start rising a bit later, at about 250 to 300 ms, peak lower at about
0.1 around 450 to 700 ms, and fall away by about 850 ms.
Unrelated looks stay near 0 the whole time, with only a slight rise to about 0.03
around 600 to 750 ms.
The average target offset, the point where the spoken word ends, falls just under 400 ms.
Referent looks are already climbing before the word has finished, so recognition begins
while the word is still arriving.
Early on, "beaker" and "beetle" match the input equally well, so the referent and
cohort rise together.
Once the later sounds rule out "beetle," cohort looks drop and referent looks
keep climbing.
The rhyme is also activated, though less strongly, even though it mismatches at
the very first sound, so partial matches anywhere in the word attract some
activation.
Section 22: TRACE Simulations of the Eye-Movement Data
TRACE's predicted activations can be converted into predicted fixation probabilities
and placed alongside the actual fixation data from 0 to 1000 ms.
For the referent, TRACE rises slightly earlier than the real fixations. At about
600 ms, TRACE predicts about 0.6, while actual fixations are about 0.5. Both
curves converge near 0.95 by about 800 ms.
For the cohort, TRACE peaks at about 0.17 around 480 ms, and actual fixations
peak at about 0.18 around 500 to 550 ms. Actual cohort looks stay elevated a
little longer, at about 0.14 until about 650 ms, before both curves fall to near 0
by about 800 ms.
For the rhyme, TRACE and the actual fixations both peak at about 0.1 between
about 450 and 600 ms, and both decline slowly to about 0.03 by 1000 ms.
The simulation matches the data at a fine granularity, tracking the rise and fall of each
competitor over tens of milliseconds. Eye movements provide a moment-by-moment
estimate of the changes in "activity" for different competitors over time. They make
cognitive processing visible before a final response is issued.
