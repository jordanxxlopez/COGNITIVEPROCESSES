Section 1: Nativist Positions in Child Language
The question of nature and nurture in child language asks how much of language comes
built into the child and how much is learned from experience. The "nativist" positions
grew out of Chomsky's critique of Skinner, where Chomsky argued that the behaviorist
account of learning could not explain how children acquire language.
Skinner was a behaviorist, and behaviorists studied general learning
mechanisms such as reward and punishment. Chomsky's critique was aimed at
the idea that these general mechanisms are enough to produce language.
For Chomsky, the most important feature of language is its generativity. Generativity
means that a language can produce an unlimited number of new sentences, including
sentences that no one has ever said or heard before.
From this point of view, acquiring a language means identifying the abstract
grammar that produces the sentences of that language. The child is not just
memorizing the sentences heard so far. The child has to work out the hidden
system of rules that could generate all of the possible sentences.
According to the nativist argument, the general learning mechanisms studied by
behaviorists were not up to this task, and there are two reasons for that claim.
The first reason is that grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are not
"similar" in any straightforward way. A learning mechanism that groups
things by how much they resemble each other cannot easily separate the good
sentences from the bad ones, since a grammatical sentence and an
ungrammatical one can look and sound very much alike.
The second reason is that the context for language acquisition does not look
like operant conditioning. In operant conditioning, a behavior becomes more
or less frequent based on whether it is followed by a reward or a punishment,
and in language acquisition these reward and punishment contingencies
don't hold. Children are not systematically rewarded for grammatical
sentences and punished for ungrammatical ones, so this kind of learning
cannot be what drives grammar acquisition.
Instead of general learning, nativist approaches assert two things that the child brings to
the task.
The first is innate knowledge of what language is like, which is called
Universal Grammar. This is knowledge about the general shape of human
languages that the child has before any experience with a particular language.
The second is language-specific acquisition processes. These are learning
processes that exist specifically for language rather than being the same
general mechanisms used for learning everything else.
The nativist position is captured in the following quotation: "The fact that all normal
children acquire essentially comparable grammars of great complexity with remarkable
rapidity suggests that human beings are somehow specially designed to do this, with
data-handling or 'hypothesis-formulating' ability of unknown character and complexity."
The argument in this quotation rests on three observations. All normal children
end up with essentially comparable grammars, those grammars are of great


===== PAGE 2 =====
complexity, and children acquire them with remarkable rapidity.
From those observations, the conclusion is that humans are specially designed
for language acquisition. The quotation describes this special design as a "data-
handling" or "hypothesis-formulating" ability, meaning a built-in capacity for
processing language input and forming guesses about its grammar, while
admitting that the exact character and complexity of that ability are unknown.
Section 2: Categorical Perception Revisited
Categorical perception is the pattern in which listeners hear a smoothly changing series
of sounds as falling into sharp, separate categories. In the classic demonstration, a set of
14 synthetic speech sounds varies in equal physical steps, and listeners hear each sound
as b, d, or g.
The 14 stimuli are simplified speech patterns made of two bands of energy
called formants, plotted with frequency in cycles per second (CPS, from 0 to
2400) against time in milliseconds, with each sound lasting about 300
milliseconds. The lower formant is the same in all 14 stimuli, rising quickly at
the start to a steady level near the bottom of the frequency range.
What changes from stimulus to stimulus is the starting portion of the upper
formant, called the transition. In stimulus 1, the upper formant starts low and
rises steeply to its steady level near 2000 CPS. Across stimuli 2 through 7, that
rise becomes smaller and smaller until the transition is nearly flat, and across
stimuli 8 through 14, the transition starts higher than the steady level and falls
down to it, with the fall getting steeper until stimulus 14 has the sharpest drop.
The labelling data record the percentage of times listeners identified each stimulus as b,
d, or g, with stimulus value from 1 to 14 on the horizontal axis and percent identification
from 0 to 100 on the vertical axis.
Stimuli 1 through 3 are identified as b close to 100% of the time. At stimulus 4, b
responses drop to about 30%, and by stimulus 5 they reach zero.
Over that same short stretch, d responses climb from almost zero at stimulus 3
to about 70% at stimulus 4 and to 100% at stimulus 5. Stimuli 5 through 8 are
heard as d essentially all of the time, then d responses fall to about 85% at
stimulus 9, about 25% at stimulus 10, and close to zero by stimuli 11 and 12.
g responses are at zero through stimulus 8, rise to about 15% at stimulus 9 and
almost 80% at stimulus 10, and stay near 100% from stimulus 11 through 14.
The physical change from one stimulus to the next is the same size everywhere
along the series, but the labels do not change gradually. Each label holds steady
across a wide stretch of stimuli and then flips within one or two steps, which
places one category boundary between stimuli 3 and 5 and a second boundary
between stimuli 9 and 11.
Discrimination was tested by asking listeners to tell apart two stimuli separated by one,
two, or three steps along the series, and these are the one-step, two-step, and three-step
discrimination conditions. Each pair is plotted by the value of its first member, called the
"A" stimulus, with percent correct on the vertical axis, where 50% is the level of guessing.
Two curves appear in each discrimination condition. The obtained curve is how
well listeners actually performed, and the predicted curve is how well they
would perform if they could only tell two sounds apart when they gave them
different labels.
In the two-step condition, obtained accuracy reaches about 100% for the pair
starting at stimulus 3, which is the pair that crosses the b and d boundary, and
about 98% for the pair starting at stimulus 8, which crosses the d and g
boundary. For pairs starting at stimuli 5 and 6, both of which fall inside the d
category, accuracy drops to around 50%, which is chance. The predicted curve
follows the same peaks and valleys, although obtained accuracy also stays high,


===== PAGE 3 =====
between about 80% and 90%, for pairs starting at stimuli 10 through 12, where
the prediction stays near 50%.
In the one-step condition, accuracy is lower overall since the two sounds are
physically closer together. Obtained accuracy peaks at about 78% to 80% for
pairs starting at stimuli 3 and 4 and at about 73% for the pair starting at
stimulus 8, and it sits near 50% for most within-category pairs.
In the three-step condition, obtained accuracy is near 100% for the pair
starting at stimulus 2 and again near 100% for pairs starting around stimulus 7,
with high values near the second boundary as well. The predicted curve has
peaks at the same boundary locations and drops to 50% for within-category
pairs starting at stimuli 5 and 6 and at stimuli 10 and 11.
Across all three conditions, listeners discriminate pairs that cross a category
boundary much better than pairs of the same physical distance that fall inside
one category. Discrimination therefore follows the labels, which is the defining
feature of categorical perception.
Early theories of categorical perception treated it as both a language-specific and a
species-specific phenomenon. At that time, only humans were known to show
categorical perception, and they were known to show it only for speech sounds.
Categorical perception is a compelling example of how similarity between stimuli is
defined in linguistic, rather than physical, terms. Two sounds that are physically very
different can be heard as the same if they share a category label, while two sounds that
are physically very close can be heard as clearly different if they fall on opposite sides of a
category boundary.
Section 3: Methods for Testing Infant Speech Perception
Testing categorical perception in infants requires methods that do not depend on the
infant saying anything or pressing buttons. One such method is the conditioned head
turn procedure, used in Janet Werker's infant studies laboratory
(http://infantstudies.psych.ubc.ca/).
In this setup, the infant sits on a parent's lap facing an experimenter, who keeps
the infant's attention forward with toys on a small table. To the infant's side is a
display box with dark panels, and when one of its compartments lights up, a toy
inside becomes visible, which gives the infant a reason to turn toward it. Both
the parent and the experimenter wear headphones during testing.
The procedure is called a conditioned head turn because the infant's head turn
toward the display is a learned response, and that response is what researchers
use to measure whether the infant noticed something about the sounds.
A second method is "high amplitude sucking." In this method, the infant sucks on a
pacifier while the strength and timing of each suck are recorded.
The sucking record is a continuous trace of repeated peaks, where each peak is
one strong suck. Researchers count how many of these sucking responses the
infant makes per minute and track how that rate changes over the course of the
experiment.
The example of a sucking record comes from Rochat & Striano (2001),
published in Infant Behavior and Development (IBD), a paper unrelated to the
speech perception studies.
Section 4: Categorical Perception in Four-Month-Olds (Eimas et al., 1971)
Eimas et al. (1971), published in Science, used the high amplitude sucking procedure to
test whether very young infants perceive speech sounds categorically. The sounds varied
in voice onset time (VOT), which is the time between the moment a consonant is
released and the moment the vocal folds begin vibrating.


===== PAGE 4 =====
The stimuli were synthetic syllables built from three formants, labeled F-1, F-2,
and F-3, plotted with frequency in kilocycles from 0 to 3 against time. In a
syllable with a VOT of +10 msec, all three formants begin almost at once, just 10
milliseconds after the release.
In a syllable with a VOT of +100 msec, the upper two formants begin at the
release, but the regular vibration and the first formant, F-1, do not start until 100
milliseconds later. During that delay, F-2 and F-3 carry a noisier, weaker form of
energy before the full voiced portion of the syllable begins.
The main experiment had three variations in VOT differences at each of two age levels,
and in each condition the infant heard one stimulus for a period of time and then heard a
second stimulus after a shift.
In condition 20D, the two stimuli differed in VOT by 20 msec and came from
different adult phonemic categories, meaning adults would hear them as two
different consonants. The two stimuli in this condition had VOT values of +20
and +40 msec.
In condition 20S, the VOT difference was again 20 msec, but the two stimuli
came from the same phonemic category, meaning adults would hear them as
the same consonant. The stimuli in this condition had VOT values of −20 and 0
msec or +60 and +80 msec.
Condition 0 was a control condition. In the control group, there was no actual
change in the stimulus, and the time at which a shift would have occurred is
marked for comparison.
The key data come from the 4-month-old infants, plotted as the mean number of
sucking responses per minute, on a vertical axis from 15 to 75, as a function of time and
condition. The letter B marks the baseline rate, which is the sucking rate before any
sounds began, and time is measured relative to the moment of stimulus shift, covering
the 5 minutes before the shift and the 4 minutes after it, with a dashed line marking the
shift itself.
In every condition, sucking starts at a baseline of roughly 24 to 26 responses per
minute, then jumps well above baseline once the first stimulus begins, and then
declines over the minutes just before the shift. In condition 20D, sucking rises
to about 52 to 55 responses per minute and then falls to about 34 by the last
minute before the shift. In condition 20S, it rises as high as about 66 and falls to
about 35, and in the control condition it rises to about 58 and falls to about 30.
After the shift, the three conditions separate. In 20D, sucking jumps back up to
about 51 responses in the first minute after the shift and stays near 47 or 48 for
the remaining minutes, which means the infants responded to the new sound
as something new.
In 20S, sucking moves only slightly at the shift, to about 38 responses, and then
keeps falling to about 28. In the control condition, sucking keeps dropping
steadily, from about 27 down to about 16 by the fourth minute.
The 20D and 20S conditions involve exactly the same 20-millisecond physical
difference in VOT. The infants' sucking recovered only when that difference
crossed an adult category boundary, so 4-month-olds were discriminating
speech sounds categorically, in the same way adults do.
Section 5: Why Infant Categorical Perception Seemed Like Evidence for Innateness
Categorical perception was considered to be the first step in turning speech into a
symbolic format that could be used by later linguistic processes. Once a continuously
varying sound is converted into a category such as b or d, later stages of language
processing can work with those discrete symbols rather than with raw acoustic detail.
Four-month-olds are very young, and their general development makes this point clear.
Some gross motor milestones for that age include manipulating objects with two


===== PAGE 5 =====
hands and holding their head and chest up while prone, which means lying on the
stomach.
Their color vision and general visual acuity are still "in progress," meaning
these abilities are not yet fully developed.
In terms of their own vocal output, they are just starting to produce vowel-like
sounds instead of creaky whines.
Finding categorical perception at 4 months meant that an apparently language-specific
ability was emerging very, very early in development. Infants at this age have had very
little time to learn from experience, and they cannot yet produce speech themselves, so
the early appearance of categorical perception seemed like evidence for innateness.
Section 6: Perceptual Narrowing for Speech Categories
At around six months, English-learning infants show evidence of discriminating speech
contrasts they have never heard before. Over the course of development, the ability to
perceive these contrasts declines, and this decline is called "perceptual narrowing" for
speech categories.
Werker & Tees (1984a), in the paper "Cross-language speech perception: Evidence for
perceptual reorganization during the first year of life," published in Infant Behavior and
Development, volume 7, pages 49–63, tested the effects of age and experience on infants'
ability to discriminate two non-English contrasts.
The first contrast was the Hindi Ta/ta contrast, which is a distinction between
two different t-like sounds that Hindi treats as separate. The second was the
Salish ki/qi contrast, which is a distinction between two different k-like sounds
in Salish.
The measure was the percentage of infants at each age reaching a
discrimination criterion, meaning the proportion of infants whose responses
met the standard for showing that they could tell the two sounds apart.
The results changed sharply with age for English-learning infants.
Among English infants aged 6–8 months, about 95% reached criterion on the
Hindi contrast and about 80% on the Salish contrast. At this age, most English-
learning infants could discriminate both contrasts even though neither one
occurs in English.
Among English infants aged 8–10 months, the numbers fell to about 67% for
Hindi and about 52% for Salish.
Among English infants aged 10–12 months, only about 20% reached criterion
on the Hindi contrast and about 10% on the Salish contrast.
Hindi and/or Salish infants, who were learning a language that uses these
contrasts, reached criterion at close to 100% on both. Infants with experience of
the contrasts kept the ability, while English-learning infants lost most of it by
the end of the first year of life.
Section 7: The Nativist Narrative of Speech Perception
The findings on infant categorical perception and perceptual narrowing fit together into a
very compelling narrative. In this narrative, speech perception depends on categorical
perception abilities that are a special property of the language system.
These categorical perception abilities are present very early in development,
as the 4-month-olds in Eimas et al. (1971) indicated.
In fact, according to this account, children start off as "citizens of the world," able to hear
all contrasts. This means they have innate, language-general knowledge about speech,
which is knowledge of the full set of sound distinctions that any human language might
use.


===== PAGE 6 =====
Children then rapidly tune their perceptual systems to the specific language
in their environment, which is what the decline in English-learning infants'
discrimination of Hindi and Salish contrasts reflects.
Under this narrative, the role of learning is to select among the many possible innately
specified phonetic contrasts. Learning narrows the inventory down to just those
contrasts that are meaningful in the child's native language.
In this view, learning does not create new categories. All of the categories are
already provided, and experience only determines which ones are kept and
which ones are lost.
Section 8: Problems for the Nativist View
The nativist narrative faces several problems on the side of categorical perception itself.
Categorical perception is not limited to language. It also appears for sounds
that are not speech, which goes against the early claim that it is a language-
specific phenomenon.
Categorical perception is not limited to humans. Non-human animals can also
show it, which goes against the early claim that it is species-specific.
Categorical perception is task-dependent. Whether listeners appear to
perceive sounds categorically depends on the task used to measure their
perception.
The narrative also faces a problem on the side of infant speech perception. Infant speech
perception is not actually "universal," meaning infants do not start out able to hear every
possible contrast equally well, which goes against the idea that children begin as citizens
of the world with access to all contrasts.

Section 9: Categorical Perception of Non-Speech Sounds (Cutting & Rosner, 1974)
Cutting & Rosner (1974), published in Perception & Psychophysics, tested whether
categorical perception appears for sounds that are not speech. The sounds varied in rise
time, which is how long it takes a sound to grow from silence to its full loudness.
Their Experiments I and II used three types of sounds: sawtooth waves, speech
syllables, and sine waves. Each type was made with different rise times, and
sample versions at a 10 msec rise time and a 70 msec rise time are compared,
with each sound lasting roughly 400 msec.
At a 10 msec rise time, each sound reaches full strength almost instantly, so its
waveform begins abruptly and then tapers off. At a 70 msec rise time, the same
sound swells more gradually before reaching full strength.
For the sawtooth waves, the rise time determines whether the sound resembles
a plucked string or a bowed string. A short rise time sounds like a pluck, and a
long rise time sounds like a bow.
Listeners labeled sawtooth waves with rise times from 0 to 80 msec, in steps of 10 msec,
as either PLUCK or BOW, with percent correct identification on the left vertical axis.
PLUCK responses are near 98% at rise times of 0 and 10 msec, fall to about 88%
at 20 msec and about 80% at 30 msec, then drop to about 50% at 40 msec. They
continue falling to about 25% at 50 msec and reach only a few percent by 70 and
80 msec.
BOW responses follow the mirror image of that pattern. They are near zero at 0
and 10 msec, rise to about 10% at 20 msec and about 18% at 30 msec, reach
about 50% at 40 msec, and then climb to about 72% at 50 msec and above 90%
by 80 msec.
The two label curves cross at a rise time of about 40 msec, which places the
category boundary between pluck and bow at that point.


===== PAGE 7 =====
Listeners also tried to discriminate pairs of sawtooth waves whose rise times differed by
20 msec, with percent correct discrimination on the right vertical axis. The pairs were 0–
20, 10–30, 20–40, 30–50, 40–60, 50–70, and 60–80 msec.
Discrimination is around 58% for the 0–20 and 10–30 pairs, both of which fall
within the pluck category. It rises to about 72% for the 20–40 pair and peaks at
about 76% for the 30–50 pair, which are the pairs that span the boundary near
40 msec.
For the 40–60, 50–70, and 60–80 pairs, which fall within the bow category,
discrimination drops back to about 55%.
Every pair has the same 20 msec physical difference, yet listeners discriminate
best right at the pluck and bow boundary and poorly within each category.
Sharp labeling plus a discrimination peak at the boundary is the same
categorical pattern found for speech, which means categorical perception is not
limited to language.
Section 10: Categorical Perception of Non-Speech Sounds by Infants (Jusczyk et al., 1977)
Jusczyk et al. (1977), published in Perception & Psychophysics, used the high amplitude
sucking procedure to test whether infants also perceive non-speech sounds
categorically. The study compared three experimental sessions: No Change, Within
Categories, and Between Categories.
In the No Change session, the infant kept hearing the same sound throughout.
In the Within Categories session, the sound changed at the shift to a different
sound from the same category, and in the Between Categories session, the
sound changed to one from a different category.
The results are plotted as mean sucking rates, on a vertical axis from 3 to 30 responses
per minute, as a function of time and session. Time is measured relative to the moment of
the stimulus shift, which is marked by a vertical dashed line, with the 3 minutes before
the shift and the 4 minutes after it, and the letter B marks the baseline rate of sucking.
In all three sessions, sucking begins at a baseline of roughly 13 to 15 responses
per minute, rises to about 20 or 21 in the third minute before the shift, and then
falls to about 11.5 to 12.5 by the last minute before the shift.
In the No Change session, sucking stays flat at about 12 responses per minute
for all 4 minutes after the shift time. In the Within Categories session, sucking
also stays flat, at about 11 to 12.5 responses per minute, even though the sound
actually changed.
In the Between Categories session, sucking recovers to about 17.5 responses in
the first minute after the shift and about 16 in the second minute, before
returning to about 12.5.
Infants responded to a change in the sound only when the change crossed a
category boundary, and they treated a within-category change as if nothing had
changed. Infants therefore show categorical perception for non-speech sounds
too, so the early appearance of categorical perception in infancy is not specific
to speech.

Section 12: Some Contrasts Are Less Salient Than Others for Infants (Narayan et al., 2010)
Narayan et al. (2010) tested whether infants can perceive all speech contrasts equally
well from the start. They measured looking time in seconds on same trials, where the
infant hears one syllable repeated, and on change trials, where the syllable switches.
Longer looking on change trials than on same trials means the infant noticed the switch.
The first test used English-hearing infants at 4–5 months, who were tested on two
contrasts. The [ma]–[na] contrast is native to English, and the [na]–[ŋa] contrast is non-
native, with error bars representing standard error.
For [ma]–[na], looking time rises from about 3.4 seconds on same trials to
about 5.4 seconds on change trials. The infants clearly detected this contrast.
For [na]–[ŋa], looking time is about 4.5 seconds on same trials and about 4.2
seconds on change trials, so there is no increase when the sound changes. The
infants did not detect this contrast.


===== PAGE 9 =====
The [na]–[ŋa] pair is a place contrast for nasals in onset, meaning the two
sounds are both nasal consonants at the start of a syllable, and they differ in
where in the mouth the sound is made. This contrast, written /na/–/ŋa/, is not
readily perceived by four-month-olds, independent of language experience,
while /ma/–/na/ is relatively salient, meaning it is easy for infants to notice.
The second test used Filipino-learning infants at 6–8 months and 10–12 months, tested
on [na]–[ŋa], which is a native contrast in their language environment.
The 6–8-month-olds look for about 3.8 seconds on both same and change trials,
so even these infants, who hear the contrast in their own language, do not yet
detect it.
The 10–12-month-olds look for about 3.3 seconds on same trials and about 4.8
seconds on change trials. By this age, the Filipino-learning infants detect the
contrast.
Between 6 and 10 months, exposure to this contrast in a Filipino language
environment increases its salience.
These results mean that not all speech contrasts are available innately. Infants are not
born hearing every contrast, so infant speech perception is not actually universal, and
exposure to contrasts in the environment is necessary for acquisition in some cases. In
these cases, experience does more than select from a built-in set of contrasts, since the
ability to hear the contrast appears only after the infant has been exposed to it.
Section 13: Emergentist Approaches and Associative Learning
The alternative to the nativist approach is the emergentist approach. Emergentist
approaches assume that associative learning mechanisms are sensitive to statistical
regularities in the environment, which are the patterns in how often things occur and
how often they occur together.
Associative learning at the level of neurons involves three cells in a classical conditioning
setup: a Bell neuron, a Food detector neuron, and a Salivation neuron.
The food detector neuron is connected to the salivation neuron, so detecting
food already triggers salivation, and the signal flows in the direction from the
input neurons toward the salivation neuron.
The bell neuron also sends a connection to the salivation neuron, and this
connection is labeled the synapse to be strengthened. When the bell neuron
and the salivation neuron are active together, which happens when the bell
rings at the same time food is detected, this synapse grows stronger.
Once the synapse is strong enough, the bell alone can activate the salivation
neuron. Learning in this system consists of changing connection strengths
based on which events reliably occur together.
The challenge for the emergentist approach is how this kind of associative mechanism
can extend beyond conditioning, where one stimulus becomes linked to one response, to
support learning for pattern recognition, such as recognizing the sound categories of a
language.
Section 14: Vowel Distributions and Statistical Learning
When Kuhl and others talk about learning the statistics of distributions in the
environment, they mean measurements like those of American English vowels plotted
in a "vowel space." The vowel space is determined by the first formant (F1) and the
second formant (F2), which are two of the main frequency bands in a vowel.
The horizontal axis gives the first formant from 300 to 1,200 Hz, and the vertical
axis gives the second formant from 600 to about 3,400 Hz. Each point is one
spoken vowel, marked with the symbol for the vowel the speaker intended, and
each vowel category has an ellipse drawn around its cloud of points.


===== PAGE 10 =====
The vowel marked i sits in the upper left, with a low first formant of roughly 300
to 500 Hz and a very high second formant reaching above 3,000 Hz. The vowel
marked u sits in the lower left, with a similarly low first formant but a much
lower second formant of roughly 850 to 1,600 Hz.
The vowel marked a stretches furthest to the right, with first formant values
reaching up to about 1,200 Hz. Other categories, including those marked ae, I, 3,
U, ^, and c, fill the space between these extremes.
Each vowel category is not a single point but a distribution, which is a spread-out cloud
of many different productions. These clouds are wide, and many of them overlap, so a
single combination of F1 and F2 values can fall inside the ellipses of two or three different
vowels at once. This overlap is the reason speech categorization is difficult.
According to the statistical learning idea, we might learn the vowels of our language by
extracting the statistics of these distributions from the speech we hear.
Under this idea, a "prototypical" /a/ is just the mean of the distribution of all
the /a/ sounds we hear, or possibly the mode, which is the most frequent value.
The best example of a category is not stored innately but is computed from the
input.
Section 15: Distributional Learning in Infants (Maye et al., 2002)
Maye et al. (2002), published in Cognition (volume 82, pages B101–B111), tested whether
infants actually learn categories from the statistics of the sounds they hear. Infants
listened passively to one of two distributions of voice onset time along a continuum
from [da] to [ta].
The continuum had 8 tokens, with Token 1 being the endpoint [da] stimulus
and Token 8 being the endpoint [ta] stimulus. During the familiarization
phase, each token was played a certain number of times, and that number is the
familiarization frequency.
The two groups heard different distributions of these tokens.
The bimodal group heard a distribution with two peaks, which provided
evidence for two different categories. Tokens 1 and 8 were each played 4 times,
tokens 2 and 7 were each played 16 times, tokens 3 and 6 were each played 8
times, and tokens 4 and 5 were each played 4 times, so most of the input
clustered near the two ends of the continuum.
The unimodal group heard a distribution with one peak, which provided
evidence for a single category. Tokens 1, 2, 7, and 8 were each played 4 times,
tokens 3 and 6 were each played 8 times, and tokens 4 and 5 were each played
16 times, so most of the input clustered in the middle.
Tokens 3 and 6 were each played 8 times in both groups. The two groups heard
these particular tokens equally often and differed only in the overall shape of
the distribution around them.
At test, infants listened to stimuli 3 and 6 in either alternating trials, where the two
stimuli switched back and forth, or non-alternating trials, where one stimulus was
repeated. The measure was mean looking time in seconds, with standard error in
parentheses.
6-month-old unimodal infants looked 4.85 s (0.47) on alternating trials and
4.53 s (0.51) on non-alternating trials.
8-month-old unimodal infants looked 4.98 s (0.63) on alternating trials and
5.20 s (0.56) on non-alternating trials.
6-month-old bimodal infants looked 5.66 s (0.44) on alternating trials and 6.41
s (0.32) on non-alternating trials.
8-month-old bimodal infants looked 5.45 s (0.52) on alternating trials and 6.15
s (0.56) on non-alternating trials.

Only infants pre-exposed to the bimodal distribution were sensitive to the difference
between the alternating and non-alternating conditions, with looking times differing by
about 0.7 seconds at both ages. Unimodal infants had small differences in opposite
directions at the two ages.
The unimodal infants behaved as if they had learned to treat stimuli 3 and 6 as
members of the same category, while the bimodal infants behaved as if 3 and 6
belonged to different categories. A few minutes of passive listening to a
particular distribution changed how infants at 6 and 8 months grouped the
same pair of sounds.
Section 16: Social and Interactive Context for Learning
Experimental evidence indicates that social interaction is important for speech learning.
The same exposure to a Mandarin speaker is more effective for learning when it happens
in person and is interactive than when it is viewed as a video.
In the first experiment, nine-month-old American infants were exposed to Mandarin
Chinese in twelve 25-minute sessions.
One group received live exposure, with a Mandarin speaker playing and
reading with the infants in person. Other groups received auditory or
audiovisual exposure, meaning they heard the speaker through audio alone or
watched the speaker on a television screen.
After exposure, the Mandarin exposure groups and English control groups
were tested on a Mandarin phonetic contrast using the head-turn procedure.
The English control infants received the same kind of sessions in English rather
than Mandarin.
In the live condition, infants exposed to Mandarin reached about 66% correct,
while English control infants reached about 56%, with chance at 50%. Infants
in the TV and audio conditions each reached about 56%, the same as the
English controls.
Phonetic learning occurred in the live-exposure group, but there was no
learning in the TV or audio-only groups. Hearing the same Mandarin sounds
was not enough on its own, and learning occurred only when the speaker was
physically present.
Social interactions contingent on the production of different sounds seem to function
much like a reinforcing stimulus. Contingent means that the social response depends
on, and follows, the infant's own behavior.
In the second experiment, eight-month-old infants received either contingent
or non-contingent social feedback from their mothers in response to their
vocalizations.
In the contingent condition (CC), Visit 1 consisted of a 30-minute
familiarization period. Visit 2 consisted of a 10-minute baseline, then 10
minutes of contingent social response, in which the mother responded right
after the infant vocalized, and then a 10-minute extinction period, in which the
social responses stopped.
In the yoked-control condition (YC), the schedule was the same, except that
the middle 10 minutes involved non-contingent social response. These infants
received social responses that were not timed to their own vocalizations.
The measure was the proportion of syllables to vocalizations, meaning how many of the
infant's vocalizations contained syllables, which are more complex, speech-like sounds.
At baseline, the proportion is about 0.08 for the contingent group and about
0.04 for the non-contingent group.
During the social response period, the contingent group rises to about 0.32,
while the non-contingent group stays around 0.05.

During extinction, the contingent group falls to about 0.20, still well above its
baseline, and the non-contingent group is about 0.09.
Contingent social feedback increased the quantity and complexity of
infants' vocalizations. The social responses acted like reinforcement, since the
infants changed their vocal behavior only when the responses followed their
own sounds.
The phonetic learning results and the social feedback results both come from
studies published in 2003 by the National Academy of Sciences USA.
There are two ideas about why social interaction matters for learning.
One idea is that humans find social interaction with other humans innately
reinforcing. In this view, social contact works like a built-in reward that
strengthens whatever behavior it follows.
Another idea is that humans are innately good at using social cues to learn
from other people. In this view, social interaction helps by guiding the learner
toward the relevant information, such as what the speaker is attending to.
Section 17: Statistical Learning with Constraints
The emergentist view does not claim that learners absorb every statistical pattern in the
input equally. Instead, it involves statistical learning with constraints, and "constraints"
refers to a lot of things.
The first kind of constraint concerns what stimulus properties can be contrastive. Some
properties of speech vary a great deal from one utterance to the next but never
distinguish one word or sound category from another, such as who the speaker is and
speech rate. A learner has to track the statistics of the properties that can signal a
contrast while not forming categories around properties like these.
The second kind of constraint is based on earlier learning. What a learner has already
learned shapes what the learner can learn next, and constraints of this kind are
connected to sensitive periods in development.
The third kind of constraint is the social and/or interactive context for learning.
Statistical regularities in speech are learned more effectively when they come through
live, interactive, and contingent social exchanges, as in the live Mandarin exposure and
contingent maternal feedback experiments, than when the same input is presented
without that social context.
