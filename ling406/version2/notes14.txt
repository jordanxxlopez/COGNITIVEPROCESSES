Section 1: Nature and Nurture in Child Language
Nativist positions on language acquisition come from Chomsky's critique of Skinner.
Skinner's behaviorist approach explained behavior, including language, through general
learning mechanisms such as reward and punishment. Chomsky argued that this kind of
explanation cannot account for what children actually accomplish when they learn a
language.
For Chomsky, the most important feature of language is its generativity. A speaker can
produce and understand an unlimited number of sentences they have never heard
before, so a language cannot be a stored list of sentences. Under this view, acquiring a
language means identifying the abstract grammar that produces the sentences of that
language. The child's job is to work out the hidden system of rules, not to memorize the
sentences they happen to hear.
Chomsky argued that the general learning mechanisms studied by behaviorists were not
up to this task, and he gave two main reasons.
Grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are not "similar" in any
straightforward way. A grammatical sentence and an ungrammatical one can
differ by a single word, while two grammatical sentences can look completely
different from each other. A learner that groups things by surface similarity
therefore has no reliable way to sort sentences that follow the grammar from
sentences that break it.
The context for language acquisition does not look like operant
conditioning. In operant conditioning, a behavior becomes more or less
frequent depending on whether it is followed by reward or punishment.
Children are not consistently rewarded for grammatical sentences and
punished for ungrammatical ones, so the reward and punishment
contingencies that operant conditioning depends on do not hold.
Instead of general learning, nativist approaches assert two things that the child brings to
the task from birth.
The first is innate knowledge of what language is like, called Universal
Grammar. This is built-in knowledge of the general form that any human
language can take, which narrows down the possible grammars a child has to
consider.
The second is language-specific acquisition processes. These are learning
processes dedicated to language alone, separate from the general mechanisms
used to learn everything else.
Chomsky supported this view with an argument about speed and uniformity. All normal
children acquire essentially comparable grammars, and they do it with remarkable
rapidity. He took this as evidence that humans are somehow specially equipped with a
data-handling or "hypothesis-formulating" ability of unknown character, meaning a
built-in capacity for forming and testing guesses about the grammar of the language
around them.
Section 2: Categorical Perception


===== PAGE 2 =====
Categorical perception happens when listeners hear sounds that vary smoothly and
continuously in their physical properties as falling into a small number of separate
categories. Listeners are much better at telling apart two sounds from different categories
than two sounds from the same category, even when the physical difference between the
two pairs is exactly the same size.
The classic stimuli are a set of 14 synthetic speech syllables, numbered 1 through 14. Each
syllable is built from two bands of sound energy called formants, plotted as frequency in
cycles per second (cps) over time in milliseconds, with each syllable lasting roughly 300
ms.
The lower formant, the first formant, is the same in all 14 stimuli. It starts low
and rises quickly to a steady level of a few hundred cps.
The upper formant, the second formant, is the only thing that changes across
the continuum, and the change is in its opening movement, called the
transition. In stimulus 1 the second formant starts well below its steady level
and rises steeply up to about 2200 cps. Moving through the continuum, this rise
gets smaller, it becomes nearly flat around stimuli 6 to 8, and from stimulus 9
onward the transition starts above the steady level and falls, with stimulus 14
having the steepest fall.
The result is a single physical dimension, the direction and size of the second-
formant transition, that changes in equal steps from one stimulus to the next.
Labeling data come from asking listeners to identify each stimulus as b, d, or g.
Stimuli 1 through 3 are labeled b nearly 100% of the time, and b responses drop
sharply around stimulus 4.
Stimuli 5 through about 8 are labeled d nearly 100% of the time, and d
responses then drop sharply around stimuli 9 and 10.
Stimuli 11 through 14 are labeled g nearly 100% of the time.
The switch between categories is abrupt rather than gradual. Even though the
stimuli change in equal steps, listeners hear a long stretch of b, a sudden
change, a long stretch of d, another sudden change, and then g.
Discrimination data come from asking listeners to tell apart two stimuli that are a fixed
number of steps apart on the continuum: one step, two steps, or three steps. Performance
is plotted as percent correct against the value of the "A" stimulus, which is the first
member of each pair, with 50% correct being chance.
In one-step discrimination, listeners are near chance for most pairs but rise to
about 80% correct for pairs near stimuli 4 and 5 and to about 75% for pairs near
stimulus 8. These are exactly the places where the labeling curves cross from b
to d and from d to g.
In two-step discrimination, the peaks are higher, reaching close to 100% for
pairs starting around stimulus 3 and around stimuli 7 and 8, while pairs that fall
inside a category, such as those starting at stimuli 5 and 6, drop to about 50%.
In three-step discrimination, performance is again near 100% for pairs that
cross a category boundary and drops toward 50% to 75% for pairs inside a
category.
Larger step sizes make every pair physically more different, which raises
discrimination overall, but the peaks still sit at the category boundaries and the
dips still sit inside the categories.
Each discrimination graph compares obtained performance with predicted
performance. The predicted values are calculated from the labeling data, under the
assumption that listeners can only tell two sounds apart if they give them different labels.
The obtained curves follow the same pattern of peaks and dips as the predicted curves,
sometimes running somewhat higher, which means listeners' ability to discriminate the
sounds is closely tied to how they categorize them.


===== PAGE 3 =====
Early theories treated categorical perception as both language-specific and species-
specific. At the time, only humans were known to show categorical perception, and they
were only known to show it for speech sounds. This made it look like a special property of
a uniquely human language system.
Categorical perception is a compelling example of similarity between stimuli being
defined in linguistic rather than physical terms. Physically, stimulus 3 and stimulus 5
are just as far apart as stimulus 6 and stimulus 8. Listeners treat the first pair as clearly
different and the second pair as nearly identical, since 3 and 5 belong to different speech
categories while 6 and 8 belong to the same one.
Section 3: Methods for Testing Categorical Perception in Infants
Infants cannot label sounds or say whether two sounds are the same or different, so
researchers measure discrimination through behaviors infants already produce. Two
methods are central here.
The conditioned head turn procedure, used in Janet Werker's infant studies lab
(infantstudies.psych.ubc.ca), relies on training an infant to turn toward a reward when a
sound changes.
The infant sits on a parent's lap facing an experimenter across a small table, and
the experimenter keeps the infant's attention forward with toys. Beside the
infant is a box that can light up and reveal a moving toy, such as a stuffed rabbit.
The infant hears a repeating sound, and when the sound changes to a different
one, the toy lights up. Over time the infant learns that a change in sound
predicts the toy, so the infant starts turning toward the box as soon as the sound
changes, even before the toy appears.
Once this conditioning is in place, a head turn after a sound change indicates
that the infant detected the change. Both the parent and the experimenter wear
headphones, so they cannot hear the sounds and cannot accidentally signal the
infant when a change happens.
High amplitude sucking measures how strongly and how often an infant sucks on a
pacifier. The pacifier is connected to a device that records each suck as a pressure peak
over time, producing a trace of sucking activity. The apparatus appears in Rochat &
Striano (2001, IBD), a paper unrelated to speech perception, but the recording method is
the same.
Section 4: Eimas et al. (1971): Categorical Perception in Infants
Eimas et al. (1971, Science) used high amplitude sucking to test whether infants
perceive speech categorically. Each strong suck caused a speech syllable to play, so
infants controlled how often they heard the sound.
When a sound is new, infants suck more to keep hearing it. As they get used to
the sound, their interest fades and their sucking rate drops, a decline called
habituation.
After the sucking rate dropped, the experimenters switched to a new syllable, a
moment called the stimulus shift. If the infant can tell the new syllable apart
from the old one, sucking should increase again. If the infant hears the new
syllable as the same as the old one, sucking should keep declining.
The syllables differed in voice onset time (VOT), which is the time between the release
of a consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration, or voicing. VOT is measured in
milliseconds, with positive values meaning voicing starts after the release and negative
values meaning voicing starts before it.

Section 5: Why Infant Categorical Perception Seemed Like Evidence for Innateness
Categorical perception was considered the first step in turning speech into a symbolic
format that later linguistic processes could use. Speech arrives as a continuously varying
sound signal, but processes such as recognizing words and building sentences need
discrete units. Categorical perception sorts the continuous signal into those discrete
categories.
Four-month-olds are very young, and their other abilities are still limited.
Their gross motor milestones at this age include manipulating objects with two
hands and holding their head and chest up while lying on their stomach, a
position called prone.
Their color vision and general visual acuity are still developing.
They are just starting to produce vowel-like sounds instead of creaky whines.
An apparently language-specific ability appearing this early in development, before
infants can even produce speech-like vowels, seemed like strong evidence for
innateness. The infants had very little time to learn speech categories from experience,
so it seemed the categories must have been built in from the start.

Perceptual narrowing is the decline, over the first year of life, in infants' ability to
discriminate speech contrasts that are not used in their own language. The key evidence
comes from Werker & Tees (1984a, "Cross-language speech perception: Evidence for
perceptual reorganization during the first year of life," Infant Behavior and
Development, 7, 49–63).
English-learning infants were tested on two non-English contrasts, which are pairs of
sounds that are distinct speech sounds in another language but not in English.
The Hindi Ta/ta contrast consists of two t-like sounds that Hindi treats as
different, produced with the tongue touching different places in the mouth.
The Salish ki/qi contrast consists of two k-like sounds produced at slightly
different places at the back of the mouth, which Salish treats as different.
English speakers hear each pair as a single English sound, a t in the first case
and a k in the second.
Performance was measured as the percentage of infants at each age who reached a
discrimination criterion, meaning a set level of reliable performance.
Among English-learning infants aged 6 to 8 months, about 95% reached
criterion on the Hindi contrast and about 80% on the Salish contrast.
Among English-learning infants aged 8 to 10 months, these numbers dropped
to about 67% for Hindi and about 52% for Salish.
Among English-learning infants aged 10 to 12 months, only about 20% reached
criterion for Hindi and about 10% for Salish.
Hindi-learning and Salish-learning infants, who hear these contrasts in their
own languages, reached criterion at close to 100% on their native contrasts.
At around six months, English-learning infants show evidence of discriminating speech
contrasts they have never heard before. Over the course of development, the ability to
perceive these contrasts declines, and by the end of the first year most English-learning
infants no longer discriminate them, while infants exposed to the languages that use
these contrasts keep the ability.
Perceptual narrowing connects directly to categorical perception. For an English-
learning infant at 10 to 12 months, the two Hindi t sounds have come to fall inside a single
English category. A difference inside one category is exactly the kind of difference that
categorical perception makes hard to detect, so the once-discriminable contrast now
behaves like a within-category difference.
Section 7: The Nativist Narrative of Speech Development
The findings on infant categorical perception and perceptual narrowing fit together into a
very compelling nativist narrative.
First, speech perception depends on categorical perception abilities that are a
special property of the language system, not part of general hearing.
Second, these abilities are present very early in development, already in place
by 4 months.
Third, children start off as "citizens of the world," able to discriminate all
speech contrasts used in any language. Under this view, infants have innate,
language-general knowledge about speech, and they then rapidly tune their
perceptual systems to the specific language in their environment.
In this narrative, the role of learning is selection rather than construction. The full set
of possible phonetic contrasts is innately specified, and experience simply selects among
them, narrowing the inventory down to just the contrasts that are meaningful in the
child's language. Contrasts that the child's language does not use are lost, as in the
Werker & Tees data.
Section 8: Problems for the Nativist View


===== PAGE 6 =====
Several findings create problems for this narrative, some about categorical perception
itself and some about infant speech perception.
Categorical perception is not limited to language. Listeners also show categorical
perception for sounds that are not speech, which weakens the claim that it is a special
property of the language system.
Categorical perception is not limited to humans. Non-human animals can also show
categorical perception of speech sounds, which weakens the claim that it is a uniquely
human, species-specific ability.
Categorical perception is task-dependent. Whether listeners show sharp category
boundaries depends on the details of how they are tested, which suggests it is not a fixed,
built-in property of perception.
Infant speech perception is not actually "universal." Infants do not start out able to
discriminate every possible speech contrast, which contradicts the idea that they begin
as "citizens of the world" with all contrasts innately available.
Section 9: Categorical Perception of Non-Speech Sounds in Adults
Cutting & Rosner (1974, Perception & Psychophysics) tested whether adults show
categorical perception for sounds that are not speech. The stimuli varied in rise time,
which is how long a sound takes to grow from silence to its full loudness.
Three types of stimuli were used across Experiments I and II, each at rise times ranging
from short to long, with the sounds plotted as oscillograms, records of the sound wave's
amplitude over time, each lasting about 400 ms.
Sawtooth waves are buzzy, musical-sounding non-speech tones. At a 10 msec
rise time the wave reaches full amplitude almost instantly, and at a 70 msec rise
time it swells gradually before reaching full amplitude.
Speech syllables were also made with short and long rise times, with the 10
msec version starting abruptly and the 70 msec version starting more gradually.
Sine waves are pure tones, also made in 10 msec and 70 msec versions, with the
same contrast between an abrupt and a gradual onset.
For the sawtooth waves, rise time varied from 0 to 80 ms in 10 ms steps. Listeners labeled
each sound as either a pluck, like a plucked string, or a bow, like a string played with a
bow.
Pluck responses were near 100% at rise times of 0 and 10 ms, about 88% at 20
ms, and about 80% at 30 ms. They then dropped to about 50% at 40 ms, about
25% at 50 ms, and down to only a few percent at 80 ms.
Bow responses mirrored this pattern, staying near 0% at 0 and 10 ms, rising to
about 50% at 40 ms, and reaching about 93% at 80 ms.
The category boundary between pluck and bow therefore sits at a rise time of
about 40 ms, where listeners give each label about half the time.
Discrimination was tested on pairs of sawtooth waves that differed by 20 ms in rise time,
such as 0 versus 20 ms, 10 versus 30 ms, and so on up to 60 versus 80 ms.
Pairs inside a category, such as 0 versus 20 ms or 50 versus 70 ms, were
discriminated at only about 55% to 59% correct.
Pairs near the boundary were discriminated much better, with 20 versus 40 ms
at about 72% and 30 versus 50 ms, the pair that straddles the 40 ms boundary
most evenly, peaking at about 77% correct.
This is the full categorical perception pattern, with an abrupt shift in labeling and a
discrimination peak at the category boundary, produced by sounds that are not speech at
all. Categorical perception in adults is therefore not limited to language.

Section 10: Categorical Perception of Non-Speech Sounds in Infants


===== PAGE 7 =====
Jusczyk et al. (1977, Perception & Psychophysics) used the high amplitude sucking
method from Eimas et al. to test whether infants also perceive non-speech sounds
categorically. Infants sucked to hear a sound, their sucking declined as they habituated,
and then the sound changed at the stimulus shift.
There were three conditions.
In the no change condition, the sound never changed, so this group served as
the control.
In the within categories condition, the sound changed to a different stimulus
from the same category.
In the between categories condition, the sound changed to a stimulus from a
different category.
Sucking was measured as the mean number of sucking responses per minute, starting
from a baseline rate labeled B, then over the 3 minutes before the shift and the 4 minutes
after it.
In the no change condition, sucking started at a baseline of about 15, rose to
about 20, and fell to about 13 and 12 before the shift. After the shift, sucking
stayed flat at about 12 for all 4 minutes.
In the within categories condition, sucking started at about 14, rose to about 20,
and fell to about 12 before the shift. After the shift, it stayed at about 11 to 12,
matching the no change group.
In the between categories condition, sucking started at about 13, rose to about
20, and fell to about 13 and 11 before the shift. After the shift, sucking jumped to
about 18 and then 16 before settling back to about 12.
Infants recovered their sucking only when the non-speech sound changed across a
category boundary, and a within-category change produced no more sucking than no
change at all. Infants therefore show the same categorical pattern for non-speech sounds
that Eimas et al. found for speech, which undermines the claim that infant categorical
perception is a special property of the language system.

rather than a fixed property of perception.
Section 12: Some Contrasts Are Less Salient Than Others for Infants
Narayan et al. (2010) tested whether infants really start out able to discriminate every
speech contrast. They compared two contrasts between nasal consonants, sounds made
with air flowing through the nose, such as m, n, and the ng sound written .
The [ma]–[na] contrast is native to English, meaning English uses it to
distinguish words.
The [na]–[ŋa] contrast is non-native for English-learning infants. Both are a
place contrast, a difference in where in the mouth the consonant is made, and
in [na]–[ŋa] the nasal appears in the onset, the beginning of the syllable, a
position where English never uses .
English-hearing infants at 4 to 5 months were tested on same trials, where one syllable
repeated, and change trials, where the syllables switched. Looking time was measured in
seconds, with longer looking on change trials than on same trials indicating that infants
noticed the change. Error bars represent standard error, a measure of how much the
averages would be expected to vary.
For [ma]–[na], looking time rose from about 3.4 seconds on same trials to about
5.4 seconds on change trials, so infants discriminated this contrast.
For [na]–[ŋa], looking time was about 4.5 seconds on same trials and about 4.2
seconds on change trials, so infants did not look longer when the sound
changed and showed no evidence of discrimination.
The /na/–/ŋa/ place contrast for nasals in onset is not readily perceived by four-month-
olds, independent of language experience, meaning young infants have trouble with it
even when their language uses it. In contrast, /ma/–/na/ is relatively salient, meaning it
stands out and is easy for infants to detect. Infants therefore do not start out with equal
access to all speech contrasts.

Section 13: Experience Increases the Salience of a Hard Contrast
Narayan et al. (2010) also tested Filipino-learning infants on the [na]–[ŋa] contrast,
which is native to Filipino, at two ages.
At 6 to 8 months, Filipino-learning infants looked about 3.8 seconds on both
same trials and change trials, so they did not yet discriminate the contrast even
though it occurs in their language.
At 10 to 12 months, looking time rose from about 3.3 seconds on same trials to
about 4.8 seconds on change trials, so these older infants did discriminate it.
Between 6 and 10 months, exposure to this contrast in a Filipino language
environment increases its salience. Infants in this environment become able to detect a
contrast that they could not detect earlier.
Not all speech contrasts are available innately. The "citizens of the world" narrative says
infants begin with every contrast and experience only removes the ones their language
does not use. The [na]–[ŋa] contrast follows the opposite pattern, starting out hard to
perceive and becoming perceivable only after months of hearing it. Exposure to
contrasts in the environment is necessary for acquisition in some cases, so learning
has to build some contrasts, not just select among innate ones.
Section 14: The Emergentist Alternative
Emergentist approaches are the main alternative to nativism. They assume that
associative learning mechanisms are sensitive to statistical regularities in the
environment, so language structure emerges from general learning applied to the
patterns in the input, rather than from innate language-specific knowledge.
The basic associative mechanism is the strengthening of a connection between neurons
that are active together, as in classical conditioning of salivation to a bell.
A food detector neuron connects to a salivation neuron, so food on its own
triggers salivation from the start.
A bell neuron also connects to the salivation neuron, but at first this connection
is too weak to trigger salivation by itself.
When the bell sounds at the same time food is presented, the bell neuron is
active at the same moment the salivation neuron is being driven by the food
detector. This joint activity strengthens the synapse between the bell neuron
and the salivation neuron.
After repeated pairings, that synapse becomes strong enough that the bell
alone activates the salivation neuron, and the animal salivates to the bell.
The emergentist challenge is to extend this kind of associative learning beyond simple
conditioning to support learning for pattern recognition, such as recognizing the speech
categories of a language from the sounds a child hears.

Section 16: Maye et al. (2002): Infants Learn from Distributions
Maye et al. (2002, Cognition, 82, B101–B111) tested whether infants actually learn
categories from the statistics of what they hear. Infants listened passively, with no
reward or feedback, to one of two distributions of voice onset time along a continuum of
eight [da]–[ta] stimuli. Token 1 was the endpoint [da] and Token 8 was the endpoint [ta].
During the familiarization phase, the two groups heard the same eight tokens, but with
different frequencies.
The bimodal group heard Tokens 1 through 8 presented 4, 16, 8, 4, 4, 8, 16, and 4
times. The frequencies form two peaks, at Token 2 and Token 7, which provides
evidence for two different categories, one near the [da] end and one near the
[ta] end.
The unimodal group heard Tokens 1 through 8 presented 4, 4, 8, 16, 16, 8, 4, and
4 times. The frequencies form a single peak in the middle, at Tokens 4 and 5,
which provides evidence for a single category.
Both groups heard 64 tokens in total, and both heard Token 3 and Token 6
exactly 8 times each. The only difference between the groups was the overall
shape of the distribution.
At test, all infants heard stimuli 3 and 6 in two kinds of trials. In alternating trials the two
stimuli switched back and forth, and in non-alternating trials a single stimulus repeated.
Infants who hear 3 and 6 as different sounds should look differently on the two trial
types, while infants who hear them as the same sound should treat both trial types alike.
Mean looking times in seconds, with standard errors in parentheses, were as follows.
6-month unimodal: 4.85 (0.47) on alternating trials and 4.53 (0.51) on non-
alternating trials.
8-month unimodal: 4.98 (0.63) on alternating trials and 5.20 (0.56) on non-
alternating trials.
6-month bimodal: 5.66 (0.44) on alternating trials and 6.41 (0.32) on non-
alternating trials.
8-month bimodal: 5.45 (0.52) on alternating trials and 6.15 (0.56) on non-
alternating trials.
Only infants pre-exposed to the bimodal distribution were sensitive to the difference
between conditions. Bimodal infants at both ages looked about 0.7 seconds longer on
non-alternating trials than on alternating trials. Unimodal infants showed only small
differences that went in opposite directions at the two ages.

Section 17: The Social and Interactive Context for Learning
Experimental evidence indicates that social interaction is important for learning
speech sounds, beyond simple exposure to the sounds themselves.
In a foreign-language exposure study, nine-month-old American infants were exposed
to Mandarin Chinese in twelve 25-minute sessions.
One group received live exposure, with a Mandarin speaker playing and
reading books with the infants in person. Other groups received the same
Mandarin material through television, as audiovisual recordings, or through
audio only.
A control group received live sessions in English instead of Mandarin.
After the exposure period, all infants were tested on a Mandarin phonetic
contrast using the head-turn procedure, with 50% correct as chance.
Infants learned the Mandarin contrast only from live exposure.
Infants with live Mandarin exposure scored about 65% to 66% correct,
compared with about 56% for the English control group.
Infants with TV exposure and audio-only exposure each scored about 56%, no
better than the English controls.
The same exposure to a Mandarin speaker is more effective for learning
when it happens in person and is interactive than when it is viewed as a
video.
In a social response manipulation study, eight-month-old infants received either
contingent or non-contingent social feedback from their mothers in response to their
vocalizations.
In the contingent condition (CC), Visit 1 was a 30-minute familiarization
session. Visit 2 had a 10-minute baseline, then 10 minutes in which the mother
responded socially right after the infant's vocalizations, then a 10-minute
extinction period in which these responses were removed.
In the yoked-control condition (YC), the schedule was the same, but the
mother's social responses during the middle 10 minutes were non-contingent.
The infant received the same kind of social attention, but its timing was not
linked to the infant's own vocalizations.
Learning was measured as the proportion of syllables to vocalizations, which captures
how often the infant's vocalizations contained more mature, syllable-like sounds.
At baseline, the proportion was about 0.08 in the contingent group and about
0.04 in the non-contingent group.
During the social response period, the contingent group rose to about 0.32,
while the non-contingent group stayed at about 0.05.
During extinction, the contingent group dropped to about 0.20, still well above
its baseline, while the non-contingent group was about 0.09.
Contingent social feedback increased the quantity and complexity of
infants' vocalizations, and non-contingent feedback of the same amount did
not.
Social interactions contingent on the production of different sounds seem to function
much like a reinforcing stimulus. The mother's response, delivered right after a
vocalization, increased the infant's production of syllable-like sounds the way a reward

responses stopped.
There are two ideas about why social interaction matters.
One idea is that humans find social interaction with other humans innately
reinforcing, so social responses act as a built-in reward that drives learning.
Another idea is that humans are innately good at using social cues to learn
from other people, such as following where another person is attending.
Section 18: Statistical Learning with Constraints
The emergentist view does not claim that infants learn from raw statistics with no limits.
Statistical learning operates with constraints, and "constraints" refers to several different
things.
1. What stimulus properties can be contrastive. Some properties of speech
never distinguish one speech category from another, such as who is speaking or
how fast they are speaking. A /da/ is still a /da/ whether a child or an adult says
it, quickly or slowly, so the learner must not form categories along these
dimensions even though they vary a great deal in the input.
2. Constraints based on earlier learning. What an infant has already learned
shapes what it can learn next, so later statistical learning is limited and guided
by the categories and patterns acquired earlier.
3. Social and interactive contexts for learning. The live Mandarin exposure and
contingent feedback findings indicate that the same input is learned more
effectively, or only learned at all, when it comes through social interaction with
another person.
