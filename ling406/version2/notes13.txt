Section 1: Reasoning About Aphasia Before Chomsky
Aphasia is a loss or disruption of language ability caused by brain damage. In the 1870s, a
group of German neurologists, including Lichtheim and Wernicke, began making more
systematic observations of aphasic patients. Instead of only recording that a patient had
"lost language," they tried to explain each specific pattern of loss using a model of how
language is organized in the brain.
The core idea of these neurologists was that the brain contains separate "centers" for
different language functions, and that these centers are linked to one another by
pathways. A specific loss of function could then be explained as a "disconnection"
between two centers. In a disconnection, each center might still work on its own, but
information can no longer travel from one to the other, so any task that depends on that
route breaks down.
Lichtheim's "House" model (described in Eling & Whitaker, 2009, History of aphasia:
from brain to language, in the Handbook of Clinical Neurology, Vol. 95, pp. 571–582) is the
classic version of this approach. It gets its name from its shape, which looks like a house
with a pointed roof. The model contains three centers and two outside pathways.
A is the center for sound images, which stores the sound patterns of words.
When we hear a word, its sound pattern is recognized here.
M is the center for movement images, which stores the movement patterns
needed to say words out loud.
B is the center for concepts, sitting at the top of the "roof." It holds meanings,
and it connects to both A and M.
a is the acoustic pathway, which carries incoming sound up into A. m is the
motor pathway, which carries commands down from M to produce speech.
The model connects these parts with lines: A connects to M along the floor of the house,
A connects up to B, and M connects up to B. The numbers 1 through 7 mark places where
damage would produce a different form of aphasia, depending on whether a center or a
pathway is destroyed.
Damage at 1 destroys the movement center M itself, and damage at 2 destroys
the sound center A itself.
Damage at 3 cuts the direct line between A and M. Damage at 4 cuts the line
between M and B, and damage at 6 cuts the line between A and B.
Damage at 5 cuts the motor pathway leaving M, and damage at 7 cuts the
acoustic pathway entering A.
The logic of the model lets us predict a specific symptom from a specific break. A loss of
the ability to understand spoken words might be due to a disconnection between A
and B. In that case, the sound of a word still arrives through the acoustic pathway and is
still recognized as a sound pattern in A, but that pattern can no longer reach the concept
center, so the word is heard without being understood.
In the same way, a loss of the ability to do verbatim repetition may be due to a
disconnection between A and M. Repeating a word exactly requires the sound pattern
recognized in A to be passed directly to the movement patterns in M, which then send it
out through the motor pathway. If that direct line is cut, a patient could still understand


===== PAGE 2 =====
words and still speak from their own ideas, since the routes through B remain intact, but
could fail at simply saying back what they just heard.
This kind of "box and arrow" model is foundational, not only in aphasiology (the study
of aphasia) but also in modeling typical language function in healthy people. The
interactive activation model is another example of this style of modeling, in which
language is described as a set of processing units connected by pathways that pass
information between them.
Section 2: The Geschwind-Wernicke Model
In the 1970s, Norman Geschwind revived this centers-and-connections approach. The
resulting account, known as the "Geschwind-Wernicke model" of brain and language,
places the language centers on real anatomical regions of the left side of the brain and
links them with a real bundle of nerve fibers.
Broca's area sits in the lower part of the frontal lobe, toward the front of the
brain.
Wernicke's area sits farther back, in the temporal lobe.
The arcuate fasciculus is a curved bundle of fibers that arches from Wernicke's
area forward to Broca's area, connecting the two.
Two major grooves serve as landmarks. The central sulcus, also called the
fissure of Rolando, runs down from the top of the brain and separates the
frontal lobe from the region behind it. The lateral sulcus, also called the fissure
of Sylvius, runs along the side of the brain and separates the temporal lobe
below it. A sulcus is a groove in the brain's surface, and fissure is an older name
for a large groove.
In the century between Wernicke and Geschwind, the field of linguistics evolved
dramatically. The most important change was the development of a theoretical model in
which syntax, the system of rules for how words combine into sentences, was
independent of other aspects of language. In this model, syntax had its own
computational principles, meaning its own rules for building structure that did not
depend on what the words meant or how they sounded.
This new view of syntax opened the door for theorizing about the (double)
dissociations of language functions in more sophisticated ways. A dissociation occurs
when brain damage impairs one function while leaving another intact. A double
dissociation occurs when one patient has function X impaired and function Y spared,
while another patient has the reverse pattern. Double dissociations are strong evidence
that two functions rely on separate systems, and once linguists described syntax as its
own system, researchers could ask whether syntax specifically could be lost on its own.
Section 3: Fromkin's Description of Broca's and Wernicke's Aphasia
Victoria A. Fromkin, in her 1997 paper Some thoughts about the brain/mind/language
interface (Lingua, Vol. 100, pp. 3–27), gives a standard description of the two best-known
aphasia types.
The speech output of Broca's aphasia patients is characterized by three features. There
are word-finding pauses, where the speaker stops while searching for a word. There is a
loss of grammatical morphemes, which are the small words and word parts that carry
grammatical information, such as "the," "is," or the endings "-ed" and "-s." Quite often
there is also disturbed word order, so words come out in an arrangement that does not
follow the rules of the language.
Auditory comprehension in Broca's aphasia, meaning the understanding of heard
speech, gives the impression of being generally good in colloquial conversation, which is
ordinary everyday talk. However, controlled testing reveals considerable impairments.
Casual conversation can hide comprehension problems that become visible only when a
test is carefully designed to measure understanding.


===== PAGE 3 =====
The term agrammatism, which refers to speech that lacks normal grammatical structure,
is almost synonymous with Broca's aphasia. The overlap is not perfect in either
direction, though. Some patients with lesions in Broca's area are not agrammatic, and
some agrammatic patients would not be classified neurologically as Broca's aphasics.
Wernicke's aphasia patients, on the other hand, produce fluent speech with good
intonation, meaning the natural rise and fall of the voice, and good pronunciation. Their
speech contains many word substitutions, where one word is replaced by another, and
these substitutions can be either semantically similar (close in meaning to the intended
word) or semantically dissimilar (unrelated in meaning). They also produce
neologisms, which are made-up words that do not exist in the language, as well as
phonological errors, which are errors in the sounds of words. Unlike the description of
Broca's patients in casual conversation, Wernicke's patients also show clear
comprehension difficulties.
Section 4: An Attractive Dichotomy: Word Salad Versus Telegraphic Speech
These descriptions suggest an attractive dichotomy, a clean two-way split between the
two aphasia types. Wernicke's aphasia is described as "word salad" or "Jabberwocky,"
often with neologisms, while syntax remains roughly intact. "Word salad" captures the
way words are tossed together without a coherent meaning, and "Jabberwocky" refers to
speech that has the sound and shape of real sentences but does not make sense.
One Wernicke's patient, asked about his health, replied: "I felt worse because I can no
longer keep in mind from the mind of the minds to keep me from mind and up to the ear
which can be to find among ourselves" (Kriendler et al., 1971).
The reply is built from pieces that are grammatical on their own, such as "I felt
worse," "keep in mind," and "up to the ear," and it flows like one long sentence.
Despite this grammatical surface, the pieces do not combine into any coherent
meaning about his health, which is exactly the pattern of intact syntax with
disrupted word selection.
Another patient, asked about his poor vision, said: "My wires don't hire right." The
sentence has a perfectly normal grammatical frame, with a subject, a negated verb, and
an adverb at the end. The words placed into that frame are wrong, and "hire" rhymes with
"wires," so the error has a sound-based relationship to the surrounding words rather than
a meaningful one.
An aphasic physician, asked if he was a doctor, replied: "Me? Yes, sir, I'm, a male
demaploze on my own. I still know my tubaboys what for I have that's gone hell and some
of them go."
"Demaploze" and "tubaboys" are neologisms, invented words that fill noun
positions in otherwise normal phrase frames such as "a male ___" and "I still
know my ___."
The speaker produces fluent, grammatical-sounding phrases while the
meaning breaks down, which matches the description of Wernicke's aphasia as
fluent but uninterpretable.
One aphasic patient described a fork as "a need for a schedule" and a spoon as "How
many schemes on your throat." These descriptions are fluent strings of real words, but
they do not describe the objects. The word "throat" in the spoon description has a loose
connection to eating, which fits the description of substitutions that range from
semantically related to unrelated.
Broca's aphasia, on the other side of the dichotomy, is described as "telegraphic"
speech, with difficulty with word order and grammatical particles and a relative
sparing of important content words. Grammatical particles are the small function
words, such as articles, prepositions, and helping verbs. Content words are the words that
carry the main meaning, such as nouns and main verbs. The term "telegraphic" comes


===== PAGE 4 =====
from old telegrams, where people paid by the word and so left out everything except the
essential words.
A Broca's aphasic, asked what brought him to the hospital, answered: "Yes – ah – Monday
– ah – Dad and P.H. (the patient's name) and Dad ... hospital. Two ... ah doctors ..., and ah ...
thirty minutes ... and yes ... ah ... hospital. And, er Wednesday ... nine o'clock and eh
Thursday, ten o'clock ... doctors. Two doctors ... and ah ... teeth. Yeah ... fine" (Badecker
and Caramazza, 1985).
The content words survive: "Monday," "Dad," "hospital," "two doctors," "thirty
minutes," "Wednesday," "nine o'clock," "Thursday," "ten o'clock," and "teeth."
From these alone, a listener can piece together a rough story about going to the
hospital with his father, seeing doctors, and having something done involving
teeth.
Almost all of the grammatical glue is missing. There are almost no verbs, no
tense markers, no articles, and no prepositions linking the words, and the
speech is broken up by long pauses and filler sounds such as "ah" and "er."
These halting but partly interpretable utterances are very different from the
fluent but uninterpretable utterances of Wernicke's aphasia, and this contrast is
what makes the dichotomy so attractive.
Section 5: Lexicon Versus Grammar, and Broca's Original Patient
The dichotomy can be stated in more theoretical terms. Wernicke's aphasia is described
as problems with activation (or perhaps inhibition) in the lexicon, and Broca's aphasia
is described as problems with grammar.
The lexicon is the mental store of words. A problem with activation means the
wrong words become active and get selected, which fits the word substitutions
and neologisms of Wernicke's aphasia.
The question mark next to inhibition reflects an alternative possibility: the
problem may be a failure to suppress competing words, so that many words stay
active and the wrong one wins.
A problem with grammar means difficulty with the rules that combine words
into structured sentences, which fits the missing grammatical morphemes and
disturbed word order of Broca's aphasia.
This grammar-based description is fairly distant from how Broca described his original
cases. Broca's most famous patient was Leborgne, who was nicknamed "Tan." Broca's
behavioral description of Tan had three parts.
Tan could only produce one jargon syllable, the syllable "tan" that gave him his
nickname.
He seemed otherwise to be in possession of his senses, meaning his other
mental and sensory abilities appeared normal.
He seemed to understand what was being said to him.
Broca's presentation of Tan raises three considerations about the reasoning behind the
case.
The first concerns the role of dissociations in the argument. It is critical that
Leborgne had his other senses intact, since only then can the loss of speech be
separated from a general loss of mental function. If he had also been unable to
hear, think, or understand, his silence could have been one part of an overall
decline rather than evidence for a separate speech ability.
The second asks why a "capacity for articulate speech" sounds like a more
plausible function of a particular brain region than "criminality" or
"philoprogenitiveness," which means love of one's offspring. Articulate speech
is a specific, observable ability that can be lost while other abilities remain, as
Tan's case demonstrates, whereas criminality and love of offspring are broad


===== PAGE 5 =====
social and personality traits that are much harder to define as a single function
with its own location.
The third asks what measurements we would want to make if we could examine
Tan, in order to specify his deficits in terms of the levels of description used in
modern linguistics. These levels include the phonetic level (the physical
production of speech sounds), the phonological level (the sound system and
sound patterns of the language), the lexical level (words), and the syntactic
level (sentence structure).
The mismatch here is genuinely confusing: Broca's actual patients would not
necessarily be classified as Broca's aphasics today. They were arguably unable to
produce speech at all, apparently due to motor control issues, rather than producing
speech with missing grammar. Today they would be classified as suffering from apraxia
of speech, a disorder in planning and coordinating the movements needed to produce
speech, even though the muscles themselves can move.
Section 6: Grammar and Lexicon in Formal Linguistics
The Wernicke's-lexicon and Broca's-grammar dichotomy is interesting in part because it
seems well aligned with a sharp distinction drawn in late 20th century formal
linguistics between the grammar and the lexicon. If the brain respects this same
division, then damage to one region should impair the grammar while damage to another
should impair the lexicon.
This distinction starts with Chomsky's formulation of the computational problem of
language learning, along with his compelling demonstrations of the independence of
syntax and semantics. Semantics is the meaning of words and sentences, so the
independence claim is that the rules of sentence structure can operate separately from
meaning.
Chomsky's view holds that we recognize a new string of words as a sentence not because
it matches some familiar item in any simple way, but because it is generated by the
grammar that each individual has somehow internalized. We understand a new
sentence, in part, because we are capable of determining the process by which that
sentence is derived in this grammar.
The important consequence is that a speaker's knowledge of language cannot
simply be a memorized list of sentences heard before, since people constantly
recognize and understand sentences they have never encountered.
Instead, each speaker has an internalized grammar, a set of rules that can
produce an unlimited number of sentences, and understanding a sentence
involves working out how those rules built it.
Chomsky's famous example (Chomsky, 1955) contrasts two word strings: (a) "colorless
green ideas sleep furiously" and (b) "furiously sleep ideas green colorless." Neither (a)
nor (b), nor any of their parts, has ever occurred in the past linguistic experience of an
English speaker, yet (a) is grammatical while (b) is not.
Sentence (a) is meaningless: ideas cannot be green, something cannot be both
colorless and green, and ideas do not sleep. Even so, it follows a normal English
pattern of adjective, adjective, noun, verb, adverb, and English speakers judge it
to be a proper sentence.
Sentence (b) uses exactly the same words in reverse order, and speakers
immediately judge it to be ungrammatical.
The two strings are equally meaningless and equally unfamiliar, so the
difference in judgment must come from syntax alone. This is the evidence that
syntax operates independently of semantics.
Section 7: Functional and Positional Levels in Speech Production


===== PAGE 6 =====
The same grammar-lexicon split appears in the difference between the Functional and
Positional levels of the speech production model. This model describes speaking as a
series of processing levels, each building on the one above it.
The Message Level is conceptual, not yet linguistic. Here the speaker chooses
the story angle, arouses concepts of the referents (the things being talked
about), and sets up the event structure, meaning who is doing what in the
situation.
The Functional Level is the first linguistic level. Here the speaker arouses
word meanings and assigns semantic roles, such as which participant is the
doer of the action and which is acted upon.
The Positional Level is the second linguistic level. Here the speaker gets word
forms into grammatically appropriate order, building the actual sentence
structure.
The Phonological Encoding Level fills in the sounds of the words.
The Speech Gesture Level is articulation, the physical movements of the
mouth, tongue, and vocal cords that produce speech.
Lemmas, the word entries selected at the functional level, have an arbitrary relationship
to their referents. Nothing about the word "dog" resembles an actual dog, so the
connection between the word and its meaning must simply be learned and stored. This
makes lemmas the lexicon side of the dichotomy.
In contrast, the syntactic trees, affixes, and other structures at the positional level are the
product of abstract syntactic processes. An affix is a word part attached to a stem, such
as "-ly" or "-ed." These structures are built by rules rather than stored one by one, which
makes the positional level the grammar side of the dichotomy.
A natural objection concerns words like HOUSEBOAT and DOGHOUSE, where the
meaning is clearly not arbitrary. A houseboat is a boat that serves as a house, and a
doghouse is a house for a dog, so the meanings follow from the parts. The answer comes
from speech error evidence that derivational morphology is done at the positional
level.
Derivational morphology is the process of building new words from parts, such
as adding "-ly" to an adjective to form an adverb.
The speech error "easy enoughly" comes from an intended "easily enough."
The "-ly" affix, which belonged on "easy," ended up attached to "enough"
instead.
For an affix to land on the wrong word, it must be handled as a separate piece
and placed into position at the stage where words are being ordered. This places
word-building at the positional level, so words whose meanings come from
their parts are assembled by grammatical processes rather than stored as
arbitrary lemmas.
Section 8: Sentence Processing and Parsing
There are also popular theories of sentence processing in which the syntactic structure
of sentences is computed independently of meaning as we listen or read. On these
theories, the comprehension system first builds a grammatical structure for the incoming
words, and meaning is fitted to that structure.
These structure-building mechanisms are called "parsing" mechanisms, and isolating
them requires carefully constructing stimulus materials. Ordinary sentences give
many clues to their meaning besides syntax, so researchers must design sentences in
which syntax alone determines the interpretation.
The sentence "The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite" has two possible
structures, each represented as a syntactic tree. In these trees, S stands for sentence, NP
for noun phrase, VP for verb phrase, det for determiner (a word like "the"), N for noun, V
for verb, PP for prepositional phrase, and prep for preposition.


===== PAGE 7 =====
In the preferred structure (A), S splits into an NP, "The burglar," and a VP. The
VP contains three parts side by side: the verb "blew open," the NP "the safe,"
and the PP "with the dynamite." The prepositional phrase is attached to the
verb phrase, so it describes an aspect of the action, namely the instrument: the
burglar used the dynamite to blow the safe open.
In the unpreferred structure (B), the VP contains the verb "blew open" and one
larger NP. That larger NP contains "the safe" together with the PP "with the
dynamite." The prepositional phrase is now attached to the noun phrase, so it
describes a property of the safe, namely what was in it: the burglar blew open
the safe that contained dynamite.
The words and their order are identical in both versions. The only difference is
where the prepositional phrase attaches in the tree, and that single structural
difference produces two different meanings.
Under these theories, one possibility is that Broca's patients generally understand
what's being said even though they have a core grammatical deficit. Their
comprehension in conversation could look good if they rely on cues other than syntax,
while the grammatical system itself is damaged.
Section 9: Broca in the 1860s and the Problem of Testing Syntax
None of this theory was available to Broca back in the 1860s. For Broca, it was interesting
enough to dissociate "articulate speech" from comprehension, which is why the
observation that Tan seemed to understand what was being said carried so much
weight in his argument.
Demonstrating that some patients have a difficulty that is more generally with syntax is
a harder problem. It requires the kind of measurement Broca could not make: specifying
a patient's deficit at the syntactic level in particular, separate from the phonetic,
phonological, and lexical levels. A patient who speaks with great effort or barely speaks at
all cannot reveal much about syntax through production, so the evidence has to come
from a task that measures grammar in some other way.
Section 10: Why Comprehension Deficits in Broca's Aphasia Matter
Showing that Broca's patients have comprehension deficits was important for a specific
reason. Broca's aphasia is almost always accompanied by some level of motor
dysfunction. If agrammatic speech merely reflects an attempt to communicate
despite motor limitations, then the missing grammar in their speech tells us about their
muscles and movement planning, not about their knowledge of grammar.
Hughlings Jackson made this point long ago: when we study a patient, we are observing
what the individual can do despite the impairment. Just because a brain injury leads to
agrammatic speech does not literally mean that the grammar itself is injured. The
speech we hear is the output of a damaged system working as well as it can, and the
patient may be producing the best message possible under difficult conditions.
Chomsky's distinction between competence and performance applies directly here.
Competence is a speaker's underlying knowledge of the language, including the
grammar. Performance is the actual use of that knowledge in speaking and
understanding, which can be disrupted by things like fatigue, memory limits, or motor
problems. When motor deficits obviously interfere with performance in speaking,
comprehension may provide a clearer window into competence, since understanding a
sentence does not require producing any movements.
Caramazza & Zurif (1976) describe the view that dominated before their work.
Researchers of the time (citing Kellar, 1973, and Lenneberg, 1973) accepted that anterior
damage, meaning damage to the front of the brain where Broca's area lies, spares the
patient's tacit knowledge of his language. Tacit knowledge is knowledge a person has


===== PAGE 8 =====
and uses without being consciously aware of it, such as knowing the rules of grammar
without being able to state them.
One version of this view, from Lenneberg (1973), attributes the telegrammatic
output to a neuromuscular problem.
On this account, the strain of speaking is so great that the patient speaks
asyntactically, leaving out grammatical structure, in order to economize
effort. The patient keeps only the most informative words because every word
costs so much effort to produce.
If this account were correct, Broca's patients should have normal
comprehension of grammar, and finding a grammatical comprehension deficit
would challenge it.
Section 11: Man Bites Dog: Why Special Tests Are Needed
Psycholinguistic tests are needed to observe grammatical difficulties in comprehension,
since in ordinary conversation there are lots of redundant cues to what a speaker
intends. A listener can often arrive at the right meaning without using syntax at all, so
normal-looking comprehension in conversation does not prove that the grammar is
intact.
Consider the sentence "The dog bit the man." There are many ways to get the right
meaning here.
The first is to take the most likely combination of DOG, BITE, and MAN and
form an event representation, either with or without syntax. Dogs biting
people is a familiar event and people biting dogs is not, so world knowledge
alone points to the correct interpretation.
The second is canonical word order. In English, the usual order is Agent – Verb
– Undergoer, which in grammatical terms is Subject – Verb – Object. The agent
is the one who performs the action, and the undergoer is the one the action
happens to. A simple strategy of treating the first noun as the doer and the noun
after the verb as the receiver gives the right answer for this sentence.
In addition, a lot of language comprehension takes place in discourse context, the
surrounding conversation and situation, which helps a lot. Knowing what has already
been discussed narrows down what a new sentence is likely to mean.
Broca's patients' comprehension might depend on these compensatory strategies rather
than on grammar. If that were the case, we would want to test them with sentences more
like "The dog that the man is biting is angry."
In this sentence the man is the one doing the biting, and the dog is the one
being bitten and the one who is angry.
World knowledge pushes toward the wrong answer, since a dog biting a man is
far more likely than a man biting a dog.
The word-order strategy also fails, since "the dog" is the first noun and would be
taken as the doer.
Only correct syntactic analysis of the embedded clause "that the man is biting"
yields the right interpretation, so a patient who relies on likely events or
canonical word order will make systematic errors on sentences of this kind.
This reversal of the expected event, where the man bites the dog, is the source
of the label "Man Bites Dog."

Section 12: Caramazza and Zurif's Sentence Materials
Caramazza and Zurif built a list of exactly this kind of sentence, along with a set of
control sentences, in a study titled "Dissociation of Algorithmic and Heuristic
Processes." An algorithmic process in comprehension is full, rule-based syntactic


===== PAGE 9 =====
analysis that works out the structure of a sentence step by step. A heuristic process is a
shortcut, such as guessing the meaning from which event is most likely or from the usual
word order. The study was designed to separate these two kinds of processing by building
sentences where the shortcuts either help, give no information, or lead to the wrong
answer.
The sentence materials fall into four groups of eight sentences each, for a total of 32
sentences.
A. Control sentences (1–8) are simple sentences with no embedding, such as
"The girl is kicking a green ball," "The cat is chasing a blue bird," and "The man
is reading a yellow book." The doer comes first and the object comes after the
verb, so both syntax and the shortcuts lead to the correct meaning.
B. Center-embedded sentences with semantic constraints (9–16) contain a
clause placed in the middle of the main sentence, such as "The apple that the
boy is eating is red," "The wagon that the horse is pulling is green," and "The
balloon that the clown is holding is round." These sentences are syntactically
complex, but meaning constrains the answer: a boy can eat an apple, while an
apple cannot eat a boy. A listener can therefore get the right interpretation from
word meanings alone, without analyzing the structure.
C. Reversible center-embedded sentences (17–24) have the same structure
but use two nouns that could each perform the action, such as "The cow that the
monkey is scaring is yellow," "The cat that the dog is biting is black," and "The
girl that the boy is tickling is happy." A monkey can scare a cow and a cow can
scare a monkey, so meaning gives no help, and only syntax tells the listener who
did what.
D. Improbable center-embedded sentences (25–32) describe events where the
syntax produces an unlikely meaning, such as "The horse that the girl is kicking
is brown," "The bird that the worm is eating is blue," and "The dog that the man
is biting is black." Here common sense pushes toward the wrong interpretation,
since birds eat worms far more often than worms eat birds, so the listener must
follow the syntax against expectation.
In every center-embedded sentence, the first noun is the one acted upon, while the
second noun is the doer. In "The cow that the monkey is scaring is yellow," the monkey
does the scaring and the cow is scared and yellow. A word-order shortcut that treats the
first noun as the doer therefore fails on all of the center-embedded sentences, and the
three center-embedded groups differ only in whether meaning helps, gives no clue, or
misleads.
Section 13: Results of the Sentence-Picture Matching Task
The data come from a sentence-picture matching task, in which a participant hears a
sentence and picks the picture that matches it. Performance is measured as the mean
number of correct responses for each sentence type, out of a possible 8, since each type
contains eight sentences. A flat reference line sits at 4, which is half of the 8 items. Four
groups were tested: N (Normal), B (Broca), C (Conduction), and W (Wernicke), across
four sentence types: C (control), CE  (semantically constrained center-embedded),
CE  (reversible center-embedded), and CE  (improbable center-embedded).
Healthy controls are more or less equally good at all stimulus types, scoring about 8 out
of 8 on controls, semantically constrained, reversible, and improbable sentences alike. For
a healthy listener, the syntax of a center-embedded sentence is easy to process, so
whether meaning helps or misleads makes little difference.
Wernicke's patients are poor overall, scoring below the other patient groups on the
simple control sentences at about 6, and dropping to about 5 on the improbable
sentences, their lowest point. This overall weakness is probably a result of their general
comprehension deficits, which affect even simple sentences rather than only
syntactically complex ones.

The Broca's patients score close to normal on control sentences, at nearly 8, and stay high
on the semantically constrained sentences, at a little over 7.5. Their scores then fall to
about 6.3 on reversible sentences and about 6.5 on improbable sentences. The conduction

On the semantically constrained sentences, Broca's patients can use word
meanings to reach the right answer, so their syntactic weakness stays hidden.
On reversible and improbable sentences, meaning either gives no help or points
the wrong way, so the listener must rely on syntax, and this is exactly where
Broca's patients fail.
The Broca's patients therefore have a particular difficulty keeping "who did what to
whom" straight when syntax conflicts with common sense, with the probable
semantic roles for the nouns, and with similar cues. A semantic role is the part a noun
plays in an event, such as doer or receiver of the action, and "probable" semantic roles are
the ones a listener would expect from world knowledge.
This specific comprehension deficit was taken as evidence that Broca's aphasia
impacts syntax specifically. The deficit appears only when syntax is the sole route to the
answer, which fits a damaged grammatical system rather than a general comprehension
problem or a purely motor problem with speaking. It also challenges Lenneberg's
neuromuscular account, since a speaking problem should not produce errors in
understanding heard sentences.
Section 14: Grammar Versus Lexicon as a Structure-Function Question
A more specific theory of what the "parts of language" might be opens up new ways of
exploring structure-function relationships, meaning the relationships between
particular brain structures and the particular language functions they support. A theory
that names syntax as its own system gives researchers a precise target to test in patients.
Rather than just dividing aphasic patients into those with good and poor
comprehension, or those who are more or less fluent, researchers can isolate problems
with particular aspects of language. The main division of this kind is the grammar
versus the lexicon, with Broca's aphasia linked to the grammar and Wernicke's aphasia
linked to the lexicon.
Section 15: Problems With the Dichotomy: The Dick et al. Task
Dick et al. tested this dichotomy by having participants listen to four different types of
sentences. In their task (Experiments 1A and 1B), participants faced a computer screen
with two pictures side by side, such as a cat and a dog, and responded with a button box
that had a button on each side. The instructions were to indicate which animal is doing
the action.
The four sentence types all describe the same event, in which the dog bites the cat.
An active sentence: "The dog is biting the cat." The doer comes first, in the
canonical Subject – Verb – Object order.
A subject cleft: "It's the dog that is biting the cat." A cleft sentence splits a
simple sentence into two parts using "It's ... that" to highlight one noun. In a
subject cleft, the highlighted noun is the subject, so the doer still comes first
and the canonical order is kept.
An object cleft: "It's the cat that the dog is biting." The highlighted noun is the
object, so the first noun heard, the cat, is the one being bitten. The order of
agent and undergoer is reversed from the canonical pattern.
A passive: "The cat is bitten by the dog." The undergoer comes first as the
grammatical subject, and the doer appears later after "by."
A listener using the shortcut of treating the first noun as the doer would answer correctly
on actives and subject clefts and incorrectly on object clefts and passives. Every sentence


===== PAGE 11 =====
is also reversible, since a dog can bite a cat and a cat can bite a dog, so world knowledge
gives no help. Only syntax reliably identifies the doer in the object cleft and passive
sentences.
Section 16: Aphasic Patients on the Dick et al. Task
Aphasia patients and healthy aging controls did this task, with patients grouped by the
Western Aphasia Battery, a standard assessment used to sort patients into aphasia
types. Performance is measured as the percentage of correct responses for each
sentence type, and the error bars represent ±1 SEM, the standard error of the mean,
which indicates how precisely each group average is estimated. With two pictures to
choose from, a participant guessing at random would score around 50%.
The results by group follow clear patterns.
The elderly controls score near ceiling throughout, at about 98% on actives,
about 100% on subject clefts, about 97% on object clefts, and about 95% on
passives.
The anomic patients score about 90% on actives and 88% on subject clefts,
then drop to about 69% on object clefts and recover partly to about 73% on
passives.
The conduction patients score about 75% on actives and 73% on subject clefts,
then fall to about 43% on object clefts and about 50% on passives.
The Broca's patients score about 61% on actives and 65% on subject clefts, then
fall to about 42% on object clefts and rise to about 53% on passives.
The Wernicke's patients stay low across every type, at roughly 44% on actives,
46% on subject clefts, 43% on object clefts, and 50% on passives.
A pattern emerges that is much like what Caramazza and Zurif observed. The Broca's
aphasia patients have particular difficulty with the syntactically complex stimuli, the
object clefts and passives, where their scores drop to around or below the guessing level.
However, their performance is overall not so great, since even on the simple actives they
reach only about 61%, far below the elderly controls. Their difficulty is therefore not
limited to complex syntax, which weakens the claim that their deficit is purely
grammatical.
Section 17: Healthy Listeners Under Stress
Dick et al. also tested healthy undergraduates under conditions that stress the language
system in different ways. These "stress" conditions included things like speeding up
the speech and asking people to remember lists of numbers as a secondary task while
they listened.
In the single-stress conditions (Experiment 1B), each participant faced one kind of
stress at a time.
Normal listening produced scores near 99% to 100% on every sentence type,
dipping only to about 98% on object clefts.
Auditory Digits and Visual Digits, in which participants held a list of numbers
in memory that they either heard or saw, lowered object cleft scores only
slightly, to around 97.5%, and passive scores to about 97%.
The Low-Pass Filter condition, which removes the higher frequencies of the
speech and makes it sound muffled, lowered object clefts to about 96% and
passives to about 97%.
The Compressed condition, in which the speech was sped up, kept actives and
subject clefts near 99% but dropped object clefts to about 92% and passives to
about 95%.
The Noise condition, in which background noise was added, produced the
largest single-stress effect, with actives at about 97%, subject clefts at about


===== PAGE 12 =====
94%, object clefts at about 86%, and passives at about 92%.
In the dual-stress conditions, two kinds of stress were combined.
Compressed + Visual Digits produced about 97.5% on actives and 99.5% on
subject clefts, with object clefts falling to about 90.5% and passives to about
96%.
Noise + Visual Digits produced about 98% on actives and 97% on subject clefts,
with object clefts at about 89% and passives at about 95%.
Low Pass + Compression produced by far the largest drop. Actives fell to about
91% and subject clefts to about 89.5%, while object clefts fell to about 65.5%
and passives to about 68%.
Across every manipulation, the object cleft and passive sentences are particularly
vulnerable. Under the hardest condition, low-pass filtering combined with compression,
healthy young adults fall roughly 25 percentage points on object clefts and passives
compared with actives, which is the same shape of deficit seen in the Broca's patients:
relatively good on canonical sentences and much worse on noncanonical ones.
These healthy participants have intact grammars, so their errors cannot come from a loss
of grammatical knowledge. The errors come from limits on processing, such as degraded
input and a divided memory load. This suggests that the comprehension deficits seen in
Broca's aphasia patients may be a "performance" issue after all, in Chomsky's sense,
rather than damage to grammatical competence. Sentences that depart from canonical
word order are simply harder to process, and any strain on the language system, whether
from brain damage or from difficult listening conditions, exposes that difficulty first.
Section 18: Are Broca's Aphasics' Comprehension Deficits Language-Specific?
Saygin et al. (2004) asked whether the comprehension problems of Broca's aphasics are
limited to language. Patients were asked to match an action to an object. In the non-
linguistic version, a picture of a boy pantomiming an action appears alongside two object
pictures, and the correct match is an ice cream cone.
The distractor objects differ in how they relate to the target. A cake is related in
meaning, being another sweet food, which makes it a semantic distractor.
A bouquet of flowers is held up to the face in a similar way, which makes it an
affordance-based distractor. An affordance is the way an object is handled or
used.
A rooster has no meaningful relationship to the action, which makes it an
unrelated distractor.
Accuracy is reported for the non-linguistic version and for a linguistic version of the same
task.
On the non-linguistic version, controls scored 98.5% and aphasic patients
scored 93.4%.
On the linguistic version, controls scored 99.4% and aphasic patients scored
87.5%.
Accuracy also varied by distractor type.
Controls scored 99.8% with unrelated distractors, 99.2% with semantic
distractors, and 97.7% with affordance-based distractors, so they were near
ceiling in every case.
Aphasic patients scored 93.3% with unrelated distractors, 86.3% with semantic
distractors, and 91.6% with affordance-based distractors. Their largest drop
came with semantic distractors, where the wrong object was closest in meaning
to the right one.
Broca's aphasics had difficulty with this task whether the stimuli were linguistic or
not. Their deficit was larger in the linguistic version, but they were still below controls
when the task involved only pictures of a gesture and objects, with no words at all. A


===== PAGE 13 =====
purely grammatical deficit should leave a wordless pantomime task untouched, so this
result suggests that the problems extend beyond language.
Section 19: Lesion Correlates of Pantomime Interpretation Deficits
Saygin et al. also related performance on the non-linguistic (pantomime interpretation)
version to the location of each patient's brain damage. The lesion maps are three
horizontal slices through the brain, numbered 1 (lowest) to 3 (highest), with the left
hemisphere colored. CS marks the central sulcus, the groove separating the frontal lobe
from the regions behind it.
The strongest relationship, marked in red, appears in slice 2, in the frontal region in front
of the central sulcus. Weaker relationships, in green and blue, spread across surrounding
parts of the left hemisphere.
Performance in the non-verbal version of this task was significantly correlated with
damage to Broca's Area. Patients with more damage in Broca's area made more errors in
interpreting pantomimed actions, even though no language was involved. This links
Broca's area to a function that is not specific to language.

Section 22: How Language-Specific Are the Deficits in Aphasia?
How language-specific the deficits in aphasia really are is still a controversy. Evidence
from Caramazza and Zurif supports a specifically grammatical deficit in Broca's aphasia,
while the Dick et al. stress studies and the Saygin et al. non-linguistic tasks suggest that
the deficits reflect processing limits and impairments that reach beyond language.
The terms of the debate have changed greatly from the 19th century. The debate now
rests on three kinds of evidence.
Relatively sophisticated tests designed to tap specific aspects of language,
such as reversible center-embedded sentences and object clefts that isolate
syntax from meaning and word order.
Quantitative analyses of behavior, such as accuracy percentages compared
across groups and sentence types with error bars and significance tests.
Quantitative analyses of structure-function relationships, such as statistical
correlations between task performance and the location of brain damage.
This is a long way from 19th century methods, when Broca's case of Tan rested on a
behavioral description of a single patient and a dissociation between articulate speech
and comprehension.
Section 23: Voxel-Based Lesion-Symptom Mapping
Voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping (Elizabeth Bates, Stephen M. Wilson, Ayse
Pinar Saygin, Frederic Dick, Martin I. Sereno, Robert T. Knight, and Nina F. Dronkers) is
a method for formalizing structure-function relationships in aphasia data using MRI
data. A voxel is a tiny three-dimensional unit of a brain image, like a pixel with depth. At
each voxel, the method asks how strongly damage at that spot is related to a symptom,
and the strength of that relationship is expressed as a t value, with higher values
meaning a stronger association.
The "symptoms" in this analysis are defined very broadly. Rather than testing specific
aspects of grammar, the analysis looks at overall fluency and overall comprehension.
Maps (a–c) mark regions likely to be damaged in individuals with fluency
deficits. The  values range from about 1.7, in blue, to 8.5, in red, and the
strongest values fall in the anterior, or front, part of the left hemisphere.
Maps (d–f) mark regions associated with comprehension deficits. The  values
range from about 1.8, in blue, to 7.6, in red, and the strongest values, clearest in
the lowest slice (d), fall in the posterior, or back, part of the left temporal region.
The slices come from three heights in the brain: (a) and (d) are the lowest, (b)
and (e) are in the middle, and (c) and (f) are the highest.
Data from a large number of patients are consistent with a rough double dissociation.
Posterior regions are associated with perception, since damage there predicts
comprehension deficits, whereas anterior regions are associated with production, since
damage there predicts fluency deficits. The dissociation is called "rough" because the
symptoms are defined so broadly and the colored regions spread and overlap, yet the
overall split between front and back matches the classic division between Broca's and
Wernicke's aphasia.
