Section 1: A Very Old and Very General Model of Speech Perception
The model from Klatt (1979, Journal of Phonetics) describes speech perception as a
series of stages. It begins with the speech waveform, the analog signal that reaches the
ear. It ends with lexical hypotheses, the candidate words the listener considers. Each
stage takes the output of the previous stage and turns it into a more abstract
representation.
The peripheral auditory system, which includes the cochlea, receives the
waveform and performs a spectral analysis. This breaks the incoming sound
into its frequency components.
The spectral analysis feeds the acoustic property detectors. Their outputs are
tracked as detector outputs versus time, so they record how specific acoustic
properties of the signal change from moment to moment.
The detector outputs feed the phonetic feature detectors. Their outputs are
feature probabilities versus time. Each phonetic feature, such as whether a
sound is voiced, receives a probability that changes across time instead of a
fixed yes-or-no value.
The feature probabilities feed segmental analysis, which produces a
segmental feature matrix. This matrix lists the features that make up each
segment, where a segment is a phoneme.
The segmental feature matrix feeds the lexical search, which produces the
lexical hypotheses.
Categorical perception is the idea that somewhere between the analog signal entering
through the cochlea and the activation of phonemes in segmental analysis, information
is lost. Fine acoustic differences within a category are discarded, and what remains is the
category itself.
This loss of information is helpful. Phoneme categories can be matched
against stored word representations, which lets perception make contact with
higher levels of representation.
A natural assumption follows from this model. In order to identify words, a listener first
has to identify their constituent phonemes. Two problems challenge this simple view:
cascaded processing and feedback.
Section 2: A Familiar Voicing Continuum
A voicing continuum is a series of speech sounds that changes in small, equal steps from
one voicing category to another. Here the continuum runs from "peach" to "beach" in nine
steps. The cue that changes is voice onset time (VOT), the delay between the release of
the consonant and the beginning of vocal fold vibration. Moving from "peach" toward
"beach," the voice onset time becomes shorter.
The spectrograms plot frequency from 0 to 5000 Hz across 0.5 seconds. In the
vowel portion, dark horizontal bands mark concentrations of energy at
particular frequencies, and vertical striations mark the individual pulses of
vocal fold vibration.
The "peach" endpoint has a longer interval before voicing begins than the
"beach" endpoint. The steps in between have intermediate delays.
The identification function plots how listeners label each of the nine steps. Steps 1
through 5 receive a value of 1, meaning they are consistently labeled as the "peach" end.
Step 6 receives a value of about 0.33. Steps 7 through 9 receive a value of 0, meaning they
are consistently labeled as "beach." The sharp drop between steps 5 and 7 is a step-like
function rather than a gradual slope, and step 6 (circled in red) is the one stimulus that
produces mixed labeling.

Section 4: How Does Categorical Perception Happen? A Cascaded Model
A cascaded model proposes that activity is passed forward continuously over time from
one level to the next. No threshold has to be crossed before the next level receives the
activity. In this kind of model, categorical perception might happen later on, or might
even be an artifact of the task structure.
In the cascaded version of the same panels, the feature activation curve is identical, with
the same peaks, troughs, and threshold lines. The phoneme activation curve now follows
the same oscillating pattern. It rises to about 1.0, falls to about −0.9, rises again to about
1.1, falls to about −0.85, and finishes near 1.2.
The phoneme level carries the full graded pattern of feature activation,
including the stretches where the evidence is weak or ambiguous.
A categorical response, such as the step-like identification function, would then
be produced later in processing or by the demands of the task, because the
phoneme level itself is not categorical.
Section 5: A Metaphor for Thresholded Activity: Making Furniture
Making furniture is a metaphor for thresholded activity. The process has three layers.
Each layer has its own input and output, and each layer works only after the previous
layer has finished.
The lumber mill takes trees as input and produces lumber as output.
The furniture factory takes lumber as input and produces unassembled
furniture as output.
The IKEA customer takes unassembled furniture as input and produces
assembled furniture as output.
Each layer of the system can only take a specific input that has been transformed
completely by the previous layer. The furniture factory has to wait for the shipment of
lumber before it can start manufacturing, and the customer has to wait for the
unassembled furniture before assembly can begin. Many computer programs function
this way.
A partially processed tree cannot be used by the factory. Activity does not pass
forward until the previous layer has completed its transformation, which is the

Section 6: A Metaphor for Cascaded Processing: A Food Web
A food web is a metaphor for cascaded processing. In a desert food web, desert plants are
eaten by insects, small lizards, and kangaroo rats. Insects are eaten by scorpions and by
large lizards. Small lizards are eaten by large lizards. Kangaroo rats are eaten by snakes,
desert foxes, and hawks. Snakes, large lizards, and scorpions are in turn eaten by desert
foxes and hawks. Many species connect to several others at once.
The "levels" of a food web continuously interact with one another. A drought that reduces
the number of plants immediately has an impact on the health of the kangaroo rats,
which then impacts the snakes, and so on up through the web. No level has to finish
changing before the next level is affected.
A food web also contains clear feedback loops. Adding more hawks changes
the populations of snakes, lizards, and rats, and those changes in turn affect the
hawks' own food supply.
The question for speech perception is whether phoneme identification for word
recognition works more like thresholded processing, as in the lumber mill, or cascaded
processing, as in the food web.

Section 12: Evidence for Cascaded Processing in Speech Perception
The gradient pattern of looks in McMurray et al. (2003) is one piece of evidence for
cascaded processing in speech perception. In a thresholded model, only a stimulus that
crosses the threshold passes any information forward, and activity below the threshold is
blocked from reaching the phoneme level, as the X on the arrow in the feature-to-
phoneme panels indicates.
A thresholded model would predict the same pattern of looks to the competitor
for all stimuli within a category, since none of their sub-threshold differences
would reach the next level.
The number of looks to the other member of the minimal pair instead increases
gradually as the stimulus approaches the category boundary. This gradual
increase is hard to explain in a thresholded model and follows directly from
cascaded activation.

Section 14: Gisses and Kifts for Valentine's Day: The Ganong Effect
The title of this topic is built from two nonwords. "Gisses" and "kifts" are nonwords that
arise when the initial consonants of the real words "kisses" and "gifts" have their voicing
swapped, so a listener who hears an ambiguous sound at the start of these items has a real
word available for only one of the two interpretations.

He constructed word/nonword pairs such as "corpse/gorpse," in which one member of the
pair is a real word and the other differs only in the voicing of the first consonant. He then
produced continua spanning the relevant phoneme boundary in 5 ms steps.

Section 16: The Results of Ganong (1980)
The results are strikingly consistent with the second model. The lexical effect is small at
the short VOT end, at about 0.02, and rises to about 0.12 at −7.5 ms. It peaks at about 0.32
at −2.5 ms, which is next to the boundary. It then falls to about 0.19 at +2.5 ms, about 0.12

The word-nonword curve falls from about 0.95 voiced responses at −7.5 ms to
about 0.67 at −2.5 ms, about 0.28 at +2.5 ms, and about 0.14 at +7.5 ms. It ends

The nonword-word curve falls more steeply, from about 0.83 at −7.5 ms to about

The gap between the curves is therefore largest near the boundary and shrinks
toward the endpoints, which is the pattern predicted when word knowledge
shifts the categorical boundary and not the pattern predicted by a constant bias
to report a word.
Ganong raises a number of possibilities for how this could happen, focusing on different
kinds of guessing strategies. Later models would emphasize the potential role of
feedback in these shifts.

Section 18: TRACE as a Version of the Interactive Activation Model
The Interactive Activation Network Model (after McClelland and Rumelhart, 1981) is a
model of written word recognition with three kinds of units. At the bottom are feature
units, which correspond to simple line segments such as horizontal, vertical, and
diagonal strokes. In the middle are letter units, including units for
,
, and
 in the
final position. At the top are word units, including WEAR, WORK, WORD, and WEAK.
Feature units send activation up to the letter units that contain those features.
Letter units send activation up to the words that contain those letters.
Connections also run back downward, and units at the same level are
interconnected.
A hypothetical set of features extracted on a trial in an experiment on word perception
has parts of the letters covered by black patches. The visible strokes cover the beginning
of the word clearly, and the final letter is partly hidden, so its visible features are
compatible with more than one letter.
With incomplete feature information, several letter units and several word
units become partially active at once. Activation at the word level feeds back to
the letter level, so a letter that completes a plausible word receives support
beyond what its visible features provide.
The TRACE model is a version of the Interactive Activation model for written word
recognition. Many of the principles are the same, including units at multiple levels,
activation passing in both directions, and competition among units. A key issue in speech
perception is the way the stimuli unfold over time. A printed word is available all at once,
while a spoken word arrives one segment after another, which is why TRACE repeats its
units across many starting times.

Section 20: Spoken Word Recognition Is Also Very Fast (Allopenna et al., 1994)
Allopenna et al. (1994, Journal of Memory and Language) recorded eye movements as
listeners heard a spoken word while viewing a grid of pictures. The display is a five-by-
five grid containing a fixation cross in the center and several objects scattered across the
squares. The objects include a flask, a speaker, a beetle, and a baby carriage, along with
plain geometric shapes such as a triangle, a black square, a black circle, and a diamond.
The pictured objects set up four item types. The referent is the named target, a
"beaker," which is the flask. The cohort is a word that begins with the same
sounds as the target, a "beetle." The rhyme is a word that ends with the same
sounds as the target, a "speaker." The unrelated item shares nothing with the
target, a "carriage."
The measure is the probability of fixating each item type over time since target
onset, in the full competitor condition of Experiment 1, averaged over all
stimulus sets.
Fixations to the referent begin to rise at about 200 ms after the onset of the target and
continue climbing steadily until they level off near 0.95 at roughly 800 ms. The average
target offset occurs at about 400 ms, and at that moment the probability of fixating the
referent is only about 0.2. Eye movements are therefore directed toward the target before
the word has finished.
Cohort fixations rise along with the referent fixations and are about equal to
them until roughly 300 to 400 ms, because the beginning of "beaker" and the
beginning of "beetle" are the same. Cohort fixations peak at about 0.18 at
around 500 ms, then fall back to near 0 by about 800 ms once the ending of the
word rules the cohort out.
Rhyme fixations rise somewhat later and stay lower. They peak at about 0.1 at
around 500 to 600 ms and decline slowly, and they are still just above baseline
near 1000 ms. The rhyme competitor receives some activation even though it
differs from the target at the beginning, because the later part of the word
matches.
Unrelated fixations remain near 0 throughout, with only a very small rise at
later times.
