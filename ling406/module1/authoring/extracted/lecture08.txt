The Positional Level
Let’s take the simple-seeming sentence we used as evidence that lemmas are a useful concept:
Where’d I bring it when I put it in?
By the time we’re done at the functional level, we know:
1. that we have two Verbs PUT and BRING
2. that I/ME is the Agent of both verbs
3. that IT is the Undergoer of both verbs
What else?
What is the temporal relationship between the verbs?
Expressing all of those things syntactically is what goes on at the Positional Level.

NOTES

PAGE 4
If we skip the part where we organize the sentence into a question, and work out the
appropriate tenses for the verbs, etc., we get something like:
Where’d I [verb, root form] it when I [verb, past] it in?
(Actually, rather a lot of syntactic work had to happen to get that first clause right, because
we have both Wh- movement and an auxiliary verb to form the question.)
What we are paying attention to here is that that structure of the first clause requires the
base form of the lemma in English.
…and then the next clause requires the past form.
So, what we have at the Positional Level is a syntactic structure with slots for all of the
lemmas we activated at the Functional Level, along with all of the other particles required
by the grammar.
An error like “Where’d I bring it when I put it in,” can be thought of as arising while the slots
generated by the Positional Level are being filled with lemmas activated at the Functional
level.

PAGE 9
This is evidence that derivational morphology is, like, a thing.
Specifically affixes seem to be able to float around at the Positional Level.
An alternative view might be that derived forms are just other words that are related to
their root forms in some rule-like way.
So, you might have a lexicon that looks like this:
needy <> need <> needless
unlearn <> learn <> learner
Etc. That way we wouldn’t need to have a separate representation for affixes, and we
wouldn’t have to remember all of these quirky rules for generating words we use all the
time.
But the fact that errors can produce non-words like “needer” and “enoughly” on the fly,
suggests:
1. Lemmas and affixes are somehow represented separately
2. Affixes have a “slot” in the Positional Level
3. Derived words can be produced by some kind of grammatical process

Phonological Encoding
This is where we go from a specification of lemmas, affixes, syntactic particles, etc. to a
string of phonemes to produce.
So, for “easy enoughly,” the phonological encoding level appears to have worked fine.
The speaker did not say “easy /inəvɪli/,” or make some other error in specifying the
sequence of phonemes to be produced.
We saw a ton of phonological encoding errors last session, e.g.
fish grotto gish frotto
Chomsky and Halle Chomsky and Challe
with this ring I do wed with this wing I do red
In each case, the problem seems to be specifically with the ordering of phonemes.
Menn describes these errors as “neat.” That is, they follow phonotactic patterns of
English, and are (as far as we know) clearly produced.

NOTES

PAGE 12
Speech Gestures
Finally, we go from a sequence of phonemes planned at the phonological encoding level
to a series of gestures actually executed by the motor system.
Unlike phonological encoding errors, these are not necessarily “neat.”
Errors at this level result in unclear or imprecise speech.
Note that many errors are ambiguous:
ad hoc odd hack (/æd hɑk/ -> /ɑd hæk/
This could be metathesis at the Phonological
Encoding level, or a miscuing of tongue
position at the Gestural Level.

Some issues with observational studies
In the next few weeks we’ll look more closely at speech perception and you’ll see
that:
1. People aren’t very good at hearing things that are “halfway” between one
phoneme and another, especially for consonants.
2. People are biased against perceiving phonotactically illegal strings.
3. People are biased in favor of perceiving ambiguous stimuli as words (e.g.
something partway between “giss” and “kiss” will sound more like “kiss.”)
This is what philosophers of science talk about as theory-dependent observation.
We might miss a lot of errors, or whole classes of errors, because of the way our
perceptual systems work.
Experimental methods and careful measurement techniques can remedy some of
these issues (but not all, not by a long shot!)..
