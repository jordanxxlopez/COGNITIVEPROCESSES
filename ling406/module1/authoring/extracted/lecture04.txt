“How Brains Work”
What an amazing thing to call a chapter!
A better title might have been: Some basic metaphors from neuroscience that
are useful in psycholinguistics.
We’ll go through these one by one, because they really are the basis for
understanding most theory (and experimental design, for that matter) in the
field:
- Activation
- Spreading of Activation (over weighted connections)
- Distributed Representations
- Associative (e.g., Hebbian) Learning
- Top-down and Bottom-up Processing

Hubel & Weisel (1968)
Activation
Contemporary with the Cognitive Revolution,
(which we’ll talk more about soon) much important
work was done on relating neural activity to
sensory input.
Here we see simple cells in primary visual cortex
(V1) responding to lines of particular orientations in
a particular receptive field.
Electrochemical activity in the cell tells us about its
representational and computational properties.
This cell activates most strongly to upward
movement of a line at an angle of about 315°, so
we can talk about this cell as representing that
combination of features.

Activation dynamics
Most models of this kind assume that
activation unfolds over time.
One way to explain frequency effects is to
assume that activation dynamics are tuned
up by experience.
Words that are encountered more often get
tuned up to be more sensitive, and are easier
to activate.
.
Different computational models (like
Rumelhart & McClelland’s 1986 Interactive
Activation model shown here) simulate
frequency effects in different ways

NOTES

PAGE 25
Activation dynamics
We can also explain why seeing a word again,
very shortly after just having seen it leads to
fasted identification in these terms.
Activation does not rise and fall
instantaneously.
We might assume (as shown) that it falls off
more slowly than it rises.
If the word is encountered again while
activation is still high from the last exposure,
the “baseline” for the activation is already
higher than usual, so it can be activated more
easily.
This is repetition priming, a basic effect in
psychology.

Spreading Activation
Remember our norms?
One way to think about how those
associations are represented is to
imagine a network in which the
connection strengths between nodes
are determined by their association).
So when you activate “lion,” some of
that activation travels along a
weighted connection to “tiger.”
This is a spreading activation account
of semantic priming.

Learning:
How does all of this stuff get into the brain?
An extremely simple, but powerful idea...
“Neurons that fire together, wire together.”
Of course it is more complicated than this, but an
important property of the neural learning
mechanisms that are relatively well understood is
that they are basically associative.
Historically, these mechanisms were considered
insufficient to learn anything important about language.
More recent arguments are about what role they might
play, and how they might be constrained by innate
knowledge. (Especially given that LLMs can learn all kinds
of structural features of language without much more
than this.)

Distributed Representations:
How associative learning mechanisms can learn relatively sophisticated
patterns.
Remember we talked about how important
generativity is for language?
In order for an associative model to
generalize, it has to be able to represent
similarity.
So, what you know about robins and
canaries can generalize to new birds as you
learn about them.
