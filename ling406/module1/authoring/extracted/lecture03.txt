More commonly, derivational morphology changes the
pronunciation (and spelling) of a word, e.g., by adding
affixes:
certificate /sər.ˈtI.fɨ.kɨt/
certification /ˌsər.tɨ.fɨ.ˈkeI.ʃən/
recertification /ˈri.sər.tɨ.fɨ.ˌkeI.ʃən/
Note how adding an affix can impact the phonology of the rest of the word.
(This is one reason for “The Chaos” of English spelling, and why higher
fidelity to the spoken form doesn’t necessarily improve readability. )

Inflectional morphology changes the meaning of words in
a more predictable way:
PLURALS
knife(n) - knives
one fork - two forks
one mouse - a lot of mice
3RD PERSON SINGULAR PRESENT
knife(v) - knifes
I walk - she walks
you run - he runs
PAST
knife(v) - knifed
Today I walk - yesterday I walked
Today she sings - in the past, she sang

Summary so far:
Both inflectional and derivational morphology:
create new (phonological) forms out of old parts
change the meanings of base forms
Derivational morphology:
creates new words
is productive, but not always, and typically limited
Inflectional morphology:
changes how words interact (syntactically) with other words
is highly productive, but not always regular

Without some way of abstracting away from the details (individual words in this case),
we have no way of talking about these very different surface forms as similar.
Further, it would be impossible to talk about similarities and differences between
languages coherently without this level of abstraction  All languages have NPs, but they
can form them very differently. Here’s one in Chinese that goes PP + N
咖啡店裡面的小貓
coffeeshop inside + cat
(the cat in the coffee shop)
Note the lack of a determiner in the Chinese NP.  This helps explain why learning to use
determiners is so difficult for speakers of Chinese languages to learn determiners in L2.
Why do we need this level of abstraction?

Prepositional Phrases
The video for “More than Words”
We just saw a ton of prepositional phrases.
Breaking them down will reveal a their general structure:
from + { the + early  + ‘nineties }
P     +              { NP }      (NP:  Det + Adj. + N )
with + {only one + hit}
P      + {NP}     (NP: P +  Det + N)
{unrelated to} + {early + ‘nineties + one-hit + wonder + Extreme}

So, all these prepositional phrases are just P + NP!

Clauses
So far, we’ve got this sentence broken down reasonably well:
{The video + for “More than Words”} {came out  + before most of you were
born}
We’ve got a NP that goes Det + N + PP
and a VP that goes V + PP
Now we want to find the clause, which is defined as:
A VP and its subject (defined as the NP that controls the verb).
In our example, the whole first NP is the subject, the head noun is “video,”
and the whole sentence is one clause...

NOTES

PAGE 22
More clauses
A simple, one-clause sentence:
{The plums + that were in the icebox} {were delicious}.
{Det. + N + PP}  {VP}
This is what we mean by “controls:” the verb agrees with “plums,” not “icebox”,
because “plums” is the head of the noun phrase.
The plums in the icebox, which you were probably saving for breakfast, were delicious.
Now we have a sentence that’s a clause within a clause, set off with commas:
“you” is the subject of “were probably saving for breakfast”, and “plums” is the
subject of “were delicious.”
Which verb would change if we changed “you” to “my roommate?”
Forgive me.
(The subject of the first clause is implicit. The speaker wants whoever shares his
icebox to forgive him for eating the plums.)

PAGE 23
Syntactic and Semantic roles
These two sentences mean the same thing, but syntactically they’re
very different:
I have eaten {the plums in the icebox}.
The plums in the icebox were eaten {by me}.
In the first case, the subject (I) appears first, followed by a verb
phrase.
The verb phrase includes the bracketed NP, which is the object. of
the verb.
In the second case, “the plums in the icebox” becomes the subject.
We can tell because the verb “were eaten,” agrees with the plural
noun.
Here, “by me” is just a PP fleshing out the VP.

NOTES

PAGE 24
Syntactic and Semantic roles
The plums in the icebox were eaten {by me}.
If the idea that “me” is not the subject here seems counterintuitive, it’s because the way we
usually speak in English aligns the syntactic subject of a sentence with its agent.
(In grade school, we’re taught that grammar tells us “who did what to whom.” This job is done by
semantic roles, more so than syntactic ones.)
We have:
{The plums} {in the icebox} were eaten {by me}
{Undergoer} + {Location} + Verb + {Agent}
Alternatively:
I + have eaten {the plums} {in the icebox}.
Agent + Verb + Undergoer + Location
So, the semantic roles don’t change.
How different relationships between syntactic and semantic roles influence processing is
something we’ll discuss more when we talk about sentence processing.

Summary
Language is highly generative:
- We can make new words (derivational morphology)
- We can organize words into clauses, clauses into phrases, and phrases
into sentences (syntax)
- We modify words for different syntactic roles (inflectional
morphology)
We do this mostly without thinking explicitly about it.
One of the hardest things to understand about Linguistics is what there
is to explain, because some of the most complex phenomena are
hiding in plain sight.
