An early attempt at relating structure to function: Phrenology
The premise:
1. different parts of the brain are responsible for
different processes.
2. aptitude is related to differences in brain region size.
3. brain region size influences skull shape.
Therefore:

Careful measurements of people's skulls will
reveal relationships between skull shape and
various brain functions.
No one believes in phrenology anymore.
But which of these premises are actually completely and obviously wrong?

Experimental lesions and a critique of Phrenology
Flourens was an early and vociferous critic of Phrenology.
His experiments (mainly with pigeons and rabbits)
established distinct functions of cerebral cortex,
cerebellum, and mid-brain structures.
BUT, he failed to find any correspondence between
specific cortical regions and specific senses.
Instead, he insisted that the size of the lesion was critical,
and that when one sense was lost, all were lost together
(e.g., as when most of cortex is removed and sight,
hearing, etc. disappear together).

In the 1870’s a number of German neurologists —
Lichtheim, Wernicke — began making more systematic
observations of aphasia.
They ascribed specific losses of function to
“disconnections” between “centers.”
So, a loss of the ability to understand spoken words
might be due to a disconnection between “A” and “B.”
A loss of the ability to do verbatim repetition may be
due to a disconnection between “A” and “M,” and so
on.
This approach of describing “centers” for specific
functions is an ancestor of a topic we’ll be dealing with
later on in the course: “modularity”
Eling, P., & Whitaker, H. (2009). History of aphasia:

Sigmund Freud (!) critiqued the “diagram-drawing”
school of aphasia research in his early neurological
research.
He felt it was naïve to expect the brain to be organized
according to psychological categories like “word forms”
or “concepts.”
He proposed instead that language emerged from
“fields” connecting perceptual and motor abilities to
“drives.”
This is a durable tension in cognitive neuroscience:
Can we directly relate brain area functions and
cognitive functions or not?
Is the brain really organized according to our
descriptions of cognitive phenomena?

Back to Leborgne’s brain...
Using MRI, Dronkers and colleagues were able
to assess the extent and location of damage in
Broca's landmark cases very precisely.
The lesion is very deep, and includes the insula.
There is also a lot of white matter damage to
the superior longitudinal fasciculus (which,
basically connects the front and back of the
cerebral cortex).
Indeed, other work by this group suggests that
the insula, not Broca's area, is strongly
associated with speech apraxia (the condition
Broca described in his patients).

Why are we still talking about Broca 150 years later?
His characterization of the behavior was crude.
- But at the time, linguistics was very much focused on historical issues, understanding how
languages were related to each other, and reconstructing ancient languages.
- Further, experimental psychology hadn't really been invented yet. Observations of
behavior were necessarily informal.
His anatomical localization was also crude. And probably wrong.
- Relatively fine-grained anatomical descriptions of the cerebral cortex were still in their
infancy. For example, Brodmann's map wasn't published until 1909.

Metabolic neuroimaging allows us to study brains in action.
Posner et al. describe the results of PET
studies, which used radio-labeled oxygen as a
marker for activity.
Some important things to understand about
how metabolic imaging techniques like PET and
FMRI work:
- they're metabolic. So, you're not looking
at neural activity. You're looking at an index of
oxygen consumption.
- they're slow. Metabolic processes unfold
over seconds. Important neural events can
occur on the order of milliseconds or less.
- they have only fair spatial resolution.
FMRI is better than PET, but resolutions are on
the order of millimeters, whereas important
things are going on at micron scale.

Tying it all together...
Language has turned up at a number of key moments in
debates about localization of function:
Broca's presentation of Tan helped establish the
neuropsychological approach of lesion-behavior analysis.
Some of the very first PET and FMRI studies used
activation of “Broca's Area” in language tasks as evidence
that cognitive functions could be localized.
What about language makes it so well suited to these
investigations?
