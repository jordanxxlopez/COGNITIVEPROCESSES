“The best analogy I have been able to think of for this is a very homely one.  Imagine

again—a manifest physical impossibility—but only to identify.

In the 1870’s a number of German neurologists —
Lichtheim, Wernicke — began making more systematic
observations of aphasia.
They ascribed specific losses of function to
“disconnections” between “centers.”
So, a loss of the ability to understand spoken words
might be due to a disconnection between “A” and “B.”
A loss of the ability to do verbatim repetition may be
due to a disconnection between “A” and “M,” and so
on.
This approach of describing “centers” for specific
functions is an ancestor of a topic we’ll be dealing with
later on in the course: “modularity”

Sigmund Freud (!) critiqued the “diagram-drawing”
school of aphasia research in his early neurological
research.
He felt it was naïve to expect the brain to be organized
according to psychological categories like “word forms”
or “concepts.”
He proposed instead that language emerged from
“fields” connecting perceptual and motor abilities to
“drives.”
This is a durable tension in cognitive neuroscience:
Can we directly relate brain area functions and
cognitive functions or not?
Is the brain really organized according to our
descriptions of cognitive phenomena?
A holist critique

The Functional Level -- Lemmas
No, not Lemmings.
(The term “lemma” is
borrowed from mathematics,
where it refers to a
proposition in an argument.)
And they do not just follow
each other blindly off cliffs,
BTW.
We can’t just say that we select the “words” that
we’re going to produce at the functional level…
Why not?
Consider this error:
Where’d I put it when I brought it in  Where’d I
bring it when I put it in.
So, the verbs switched places, but the actual words
that were produced reflect a change in tense that
must have been applied later.
This couldn’t have happened if the word “brought”
was selected. Rather, we say the lemma BRING was
selected, and was expressed as present tense…

NOTES

PAGE 37
The Functional Level
Why do people so often say the opposite of what they mean?
Antonyms are actually quite similar at a number of levels:

- semantically related

- strongly associated

… it’s like a perfect storm in the spreading activation framework…

further:

- can take on the same semantic roles

- same word class (can take on same syntactic roles)

...so the rest of the speech production system can handle them…
So it’s easy for activation to spread from a lemma to its antonym.

NOTES

PAGE 38
The Functional Level
Functional (Semantic) roles.
Remember:
{The plums} {in the icebox} were eaten {by me}
{Undergoer} + {Location} + Verb + {Agent}
Alternatively:
I + have eaten {the plums} {in the icebox}.
Agent + Verb + Undergoer + Location
So, we have a simple event structure, and we can start slotting
lemmas into semantic roles. We know that the Verb will be TO EAT,
the Agent of the verb will be ME, and that PLUM will be the
Undergoer, etc.
Note there are syntactic consequences for arranging these roles in
different orders, with consequences for how we turn the lemma for
ME into a word.
That will happen at the Positional Level, in our next session…

The Positional Level
Let’s take the simple-seeming sentence we used as evidence that lemmas are a useful concept:

Where’d I bring it when I put it in?
By the time we’re done at the functional level, we know:

1. that we have two Verbs PUT and BRING

2. that I/ME is the Agent of both verbs

3. that IT is the Undergoer of both verbs
What else?
What is the temporal relationship between the verbs?
Expressing all of those things syntactically is what goes on at the Positional Level.

NOTES

PAGE 41
If we skip the part where we organize the sentence into a question, and work out the
appropriate tenses for the verbs, etc., we get something like:

Where’d I [verb, root form] it when I [verb, past] it in?
(Actually, rather a lot of syntactic work had to happen to get that first clause right,
because we have both Wh- movement and an auxiliary verb to form the question.)
What we are paying attention to here is that that structure of the first clause requires the
base form of the lemma in English.
…and then the next clause requires the past form.
So, what we have at the Positional Level is a syntactic structure with slots for all of the
lemmas we activated at the Functional Level, along with all of the other particles
required by the grammar.
An error like “Where’d I bring it when I put it in,” can be thought of as arising while the
slots generated by the Positional Level are being filled with lemmas activated at the
Functional level.

Oldfield and Wingfield eventually addressed this by matching
their high- and low-frequency words for length.
They found a robust frequency effect, so they concluded that
frequency effects are not merely length effects in disguise.
Really rather a lot of psycholinguistics can be characterized as
an attempt to demonstrate that some observed effect is not just
an effect of some other confounding variable…

In speech production, we have to distinguish carefully between activation and selection.
Spreading activation from, e.g., duck to fish means that activating one can help activate
the other. But this can cause problems when our task is to select the correct lemma from a
bunch of active ones…
The WEAVER++ model assumes that selection time is determined by a ratio of the activity
of the most active lemma and the overall activation of all lemmas.
Thus, when there’s feedback from the category level, this slows down lemma selection
because of all the collateral activation for items from the same category.
