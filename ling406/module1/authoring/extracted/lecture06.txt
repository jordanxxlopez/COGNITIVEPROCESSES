Language as a formal/symbolic system
There is a very old tradition of describing natural languages in abstract, formal terms
similar to those used in Artificial Intelligence.
Experimental psychology in the 50’s and 60’s was actively hostile to talking about this
kind of “internal state.”
Skinner’s Verbal Behavior tried to describe how language could be learned by operant
conditioning.
Chomsky’s (1959) review of Verbal Behavior is better remembered than the book itself.
This was an important moment in “The Cognitive Revolution”

Where does this tradition come from?
Interest in the concept of "Artificial
Intelligence" dates back to the beginning of
the digital computer.
Alan Turing, who developed decryption
devices for the British Army during WWII,
thought about this a lot.
Basically, computers were invented as
"universal function approximators." No one
had really made machines that did so many
different kinds of calculations and logical
operations before. This raised the question
of how the computer differed from human
intelligence, if at all.

The “Turing Test”
Original paper is here: http://cogprints.org/499/1/turing.html
The imitation game:
A and B are a man and a woman, C is an “examiner.”
C’s job is to figure out the gender of A and B.
A is trying to fool C.
B is trying to help C guess the correct answer.
The test:
IF you can replace either A or B with a computer, and C
doesn’t notice, the computer has demonstrated
“intelligence.”

Some highlights of Chomsky’s critique
The construction of a grammar which enumerates sentences in such a way that a meaningful structural
description can be determined for each sentence … characterizes abstractly the ability of one who has
mastered the language to distinguish sentences from nonsentences, to understand new sentences (in part),
to note certain ambiguities, etc. These are very remarkable abilities.
It is easy to show that the new events that we accept and understand as sentences are not related to
those with which we are familiar by any simple notion of formal (or semantic or statistical) similarity or
identity of grammatical frame. Talk of generalization in this case is entirely pointless and empty.
It appears that we recognize a new item as a sentence not because it matches some familiar item in any
simple way, but because it is generated by the grammar that each individual has somehow and in some
form internalized. And we understand a new sentence, in part, because we are somehow capable of
determining the process by which this sentence is derived in this grammar.
The fact that all normal children acquire essentially comparable grammars of great complexity with
remarkable rapidity suggests that human beings are somehow specially designed to do this, with data-
handling or "hypothesis-formulating" ability of unknown character and complexity.

description can be determined for each sentence … characterizes abstractly the ability of one who has

Translation:
Linguistic knowledge is characterized as a grammar: a set of abstract rules that produce grammatical
sentences.
There are an infinite number of grammatical sentences, which makes the fact that we can make a
grammaticality judgment for any new sentence remarkable.
People's intuitions about whether sentences are grammatical are the key data for the study of language.

sentences a grammar can generate.

Stimulus control
“We cannot predict verbal behavior in terms of the stimuli in the
speaker's environment, since we do not know what the current stimuli
are until he responds.”
Interpretation:
Skinner doesn't just have a bad theory of language. He is posing the
wrong question.
You can't hope to explain why people say what they do, when they do.
That's determined by a whole matrix of social interactions, cognitive
processes, previous experiences, etc., much of which has nothing to do
with language.
The tractable problem is to study the structure of what people say, and
from there try to derive the set of rules that produces this structure.

“Neither (a) 'colorless green ideas sleep furiously' nor (b) 'furiously sleep

ungrammatical, though the 'stimulus' that likely 'controlled' it is perfectly

Chomsky is trying to describe language at the “computational level
of analysis.”
This term comes from David Marr, a visual neuroscientist who talked
about cognitive systems as having three “levels of analysis:”
1.Computational
2.Algorithmic
3.Implementational
An example will help here…

Cash registers
Computational level of analysis: given prices of items,
determine amount due for a purchase; given payment
amount, compute amount due as change.
Algorithmic level: use operations of addition (price) and
subtraction (change).
Implementational level: Doesn’t matter! As far as these
functions are concerned, a credit card reader or a
computer program are basically “cash registers.”

Type I problems
Marr referred to problems that break down cleanly in these terms as “Type I”
problems.
Viewing language as a Type I problem means you can work at the
computational (Universal Grammar) and algorithmic level (specific
processes and how they’re applied in different languages) without worrying
about the implementational level (errors, memory limitations, associative
learning, etc.)
A note on chronology: Chomsky’s critique pre-dates Marr’s theoretical work by about a decade. Both
were influential in artificial intelligence research and theoretical work in the GOFAI tradition.

In sum:
We've talked about two important traditions in psycholinguistics
1. GOFAI/Modular/Chomskyan:
Assume that linguistic knowledge is essentially abstract and symbolic.
Assume that linguistic representations are different in kind from other kinds of knowledge.
But all of this stuff has to interface with messy perceptual, motor, mnemonic, and other cognitive capa
The psycholinguist's job is to study this interface.
2.Emergentist/functionalist
Assume that symbol- and rule-like behavior are emergent properties of a statistical learning process.
Assume that perceptual, motor, and cognitive processes actually shape linguistic representations.
The psycholinguist's job is thus to study language use, and relate this to more general psychological phe

syntactic rules are open to dialect/individual variation, innovation and
change over time…

A problem with the GOFAI approach
Moravec's paradox : Proving theorems and solving
geometry problems is comparatively easy for computers,
but a supposedly simple task like recognizing a face or
crossing a room without bumping into anything is
extremely difficult.
The opposite is true, of course, for humans.
So maybe characterizing language in formal terms, as you
would for a computer program, is a false trail?
