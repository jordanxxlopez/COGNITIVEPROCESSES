Oldfield and Wingfield eventually addressed this by matching their
high- and low-frequency words for length.
They found a robust frequency effect, so they concluded that
frequency effects are not merely length effects in disguise.
Really rather a lot of psycholinguistics can be characterized as an
attempt to demonstrate that some observed effect is not just an
effect of some other confounding variable…

“Making up materials is a confounded nuisance”*
*Cutler, A. (1981) Making up materials is a confounded nuisance, or: Will we be able to run any psycholinguistic experiments at all in 1990?
Cognition, 10, 65-70.
A pretty beautiful matching job by Cortese & Simpson
(2000)
Careful matching on a wide range of dimensions is
an important part of good stimulus design for an
experiment.
This can get very sophisticated.
One concern with this very fine-grained stimulus
matching is that we may select items that are
somehow “odd,” or for which the statistics are
inappropriate.
Remember that the confound between frequency
and length (for example) is a fact about English (and
most languages). Picking items to avoid this
confound -- even if done well -- creates a somewhat
“artificial” stimulus environment.
Or we might end up with a very small number of
items (the study I took this table from had ten words
per cell, which is indeed small for a word naming
experiment).

Task Design
Most interesting phenomena in
psycholinguistics happen below the level of
conscious awareness.
Before you started this class, you would
probably not be thinking about word
frequency or length if I asked you to name a
bunch of line drawings on a screen.
So, psycholinguists don’t have to be as crafty
about experimental design as people
studying, say, racial stereotyping, or altruism.
Indeed, in norming studies, we routinely just
ask people to rate words on some
dimension, and just hope for the best.
(But remember our example in class did have

Task Design
Many tasks take advantage of our ability to measure
things that participants don’t exercise conscious control
over.
Eye-movements can be very revealing, as we’ll see
when we discuss speech production studies later today.
EEG and FMRI both measure brain activity.
Often this is most interesting not for what it tells us
about the brain per se, but because brain activity is a
dependent variable that participants don’t (usually)
control directly.
So, for example, if we observe different patterns of
brain response to different kinds of stimuli even in the
absence of an overt task, it suggests that those stimuli
are different to the participant.
Of course, reaction time and accuracy are also unlikely
to be under direct control in most experiments...

Belke et al.
In speech production, we have to distinguish carefully between activation and selection.
Spreading activation from, e.g., duck to fish means that activating one can help activate the
other. But this can cause problems when our task is to select the correct lemma from a bunch of
active ones…
The WEAVER++ model assumes that selection time is determined by a ratio of the activity of the
most active lemma and the overall activation of all lemmas.
Thus, when there’s feedback from the category level, this slows down lemma selection because
of all the collateral activation for items from the same category.

Dealing with potential confounds 101
What if the blocking effect arises at the level of
visual object recognition?
Two responses:
1. Stimuli were normed so that objects
in the same category were not more visually
similar to one another than objects in different
categories, on average.
2.  A control experiment was run in
which visual similarity was blocked along, and
different results were found.
Specifically, the blocking effect was
observed in the first block of trials.
Thus, the effects in Experiment 1 aren’t likely to
be due to visual similarity.
