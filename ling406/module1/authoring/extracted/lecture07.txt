Key concepts for understanding how speech errors happen in
terms of “spreading of activation” models:
“Noise” – not just literal, acoustic noise (although this can cause
problems, too), but noise related to the activation in different parts of the
system.
Frequency – remember from our discussion of “how the brain works”
that more common words are easier to “activate” than less common
ones. This seems to impact many different levels of speech production.
Other sources of activation – we have to assume that what you’ve just
said and what you’re planning to say are active to some extent while
you’re talking. There may also be activation from things you’re thinking
about but trying not to say…
Similarity – activation spreads more easily between related items.
Similarity can be semantic, syntactic, phonological, etc…

Malapropisms
These aren’t so much speech
errors, as perceptual errors,
because the speaker intends to
say just what they’ve said.
The speaker seems to have heard
a word like “congenital,” and

The Functional Level -- Lemmas
No, not Lemmings.
(The term “lemma” is
borrowed from
mathematics, where it
refers to a proposition
in an argument.)
And they do not just
follow each other blindly
off cliffs, BTW.
We can’t just say that we select the “words”
that we’re going to produce at the functional
level…
Why not?
Consider this error:
Where’d I put it when I brought it in
 Where’d I
🡪
bring it when I put it in.
So, the verbs switched places, but the actual
words that were produced reflect a change in
tense that must have been applied later.
This couldn’t have happened if the word
“brought” was selected. Rather, we say the
lemma BRING was selected, and was
expressed as present tense…

The Functional Level
Why do people so often say the opposite of what they
mean?
Antonyms are actually quite similar at a number of levels:
- semantically related
- strongly associated
… it’s like a perfect storm in the spreading activation framework…
further:
- can take on the same semantic roles
- same word class (can take on same syntactic roles)
...so the rest of the speech production system can handle them…
So it’s easy for activation to spread from a lemma to its antonym.

The Functional Level
Functional (Semantic) roles.
Remember:
{The plums} {in the icebox} were eaten {by me}
{Undergoer} + {Location} + Verb + {Agent}
Alternatively:
I + have eaten {the plums} {in the icebox}.
Agent + Verb + Undergoer + Location
So, we have a simple event structure, and we can start slotting lemmas into
semantic roles. We know that the Verb will be TO EAT, the Agent of the verb
will be ME, and that PLUM will be the Undergoer, etc.
Note there are syntactic consequences for arranging these roles in
different orders, with consequences for how we turn the lemma for ME into
a word.
That will happen at the Positional Level, in our next session…
