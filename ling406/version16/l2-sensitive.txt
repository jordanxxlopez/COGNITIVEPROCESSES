2|**Second-language, or L2, learning** serves as **a model system for sensitive period research**. A **model system** is a situation that is common and easy to study, used to answer questions about a process that is hard to study directly. || **Lenneberg (1967)** developed an influential theory that **the sensitive period had to do with the development of cerebral dominance**, or **developmental changes in language laterality**. >> **Cerebral dominance** is the specialization of one hemisphere of the brain, usually the left, for language. >> **Language laterality** is the degree to which language depends on one side of the brain rather than both. >> Under this theory, the window for learning language closes as language becomes fixed in the left hemisphere. || Two kinds of evidence motivated the theory. >> **A rough alignment of the literature on brain development with new insights about language milestones**. The graph of neuron growth in cortical layers III, IV and V rises rapidly in the first two years and then levels off, with no measurements between ages 2 and 18 and a shaded band marking the period of language acquisition. Lenneberg linked the end of rapid brain growth to the start of language learning. >> **A review of the literature on resilience after brain injury or surgery**. Children who lost the left hemisphere often developed language, while adults did not recover it. || The theory concerns the brain’s **maturational state**, its stage of physical development. A young, less specialized brain can shift language to other tissue, while a mature, specialized brain has fewer options.
3|**Much of what is known about first-language, or L1, sensitive periods comes from research on idiosyncratic cases or relatively rare phenomena**. **Idiosyncratic** means peculiar to an individual. >> Evidence comes from cases such as children recovering from brain surgery to remove one hemisphere, and Genie, who was isolated from language until age 13. >> Each case involves many unusual circumstances, such as injury, illness or abuse, so it is hard to tell which factor caused the outcome. || **In contrast, many people learn second languages at different times in their lives, often with somewhat limited success**. >> Second-language learners are common, so researchers can study large groups instead of relying on one unusual case. >> They start learning at many different ages, from early childhood to late adulthood, and their eventual success can be measured. || **The limits of L2 success have also been used to reason about the timing of the sensitive or critical period**. >> If people who start L2 learning later consistently reach lower levels, that pattern could reveal when the sensitive period closes. >> The first language complicates the comparison. Every L2 learner already knows a language, and that knowledge shapes how the new one is learned.
4|**Johnson and Newport** studied **critical period effects in second-language learning**, in a paper about **the influence of maturational state on the acquisition of English as a second language**. **Lenneberg (1967) hypothesized that language could be acquired only within a critical period**, from early infancy until puberty. >> In its basic form, the hypothesis only has consequences for first-language acquisition. >> It is important to find out whether it extends to second-language acquisition as well. >> If it does, young children should be better second-language learners than adults and should reach higher final levels of proficiency. || The prediction was tested by comparing the **English proficiency of 46 native Korean or Chinese speakers** who had **arrived in the United States between the ages of 3 and 39**. >> By the time of testing, they had lived in the United States **between 3 and 26 years**. >> They were tested on **a wide variety of structures of English grammar** using a **grammaticality judgment task**. || The results showed **a clear and strong advantage for earlier arrivals over later arrivals**. >> **Test performance was linearly related to age of arrival up to puberty**. Each additional year of age at arrival meant a lower score. >> **After puberty, performance was low but highly variable and unrelated to age of arrival**. || The age effect was **not an accidental result of differences in amount of experience with English, motivation, self-consciousness or American identification**. >> The effect **appeared on every grammatical structure tested**, although the structures varied greatly in how well later learners mastered them. >> The authors concluded that **a critical period for language acquisition extends its effects to second-language acquisition**.
5|**Johnson and Newport** created a **grammaticality judgment test**, or **GJT**. **Participants listened to recorded sentences and simply had to say whether each sentence was grammatical or not**. **The test was untimed**. >> A **grammaticality judgment** is a decision about whether a sentence follows the rules of the language. >> Untimed means participants could take as long as they needed to decide. || Each test item came in a grammatical version and an ungrammatical version, marked with an asterisk. Each pair tests one rule of English, and the test covers many different rules. >> **(1a) “The farmer bought two pigs at the market”** versus **(1b) “The farmer bought two pig at the market”** tests **plural marking** on nouns. >> **(2a) “The little boy is speaking to a policeman”** versus **(2b) “The little boy is speak to a policeman”** tests the **progressive -ing** form of the verb. || **(6a) “Tom is reading a book in the bathtub”** versus **(6b) “Tom is reading book in the bathtub”** tests the use of a **determiner**, here the article “a.” >> **(7a) “The boys are going to the zoo this Saturday”** versus **(7b) “A boys are going to the zoo this Saturday”** tests whether the determiner matches the noun in number. >> **(8a) “Larry went home after the party”** versus **(8b) “Larry went the home after the party”** tests a case where English does **not** allow an article. || **(23a) “When will Sam fix his car?”** versus **(23b) “When Sam will fix his car?”** tests **wh-question** word order, where the helping verb must come before the subject. >> **(24a) “What do they sell at the corner store?”** versus **(24b) “What they sell at the corner store?”** tests the need for the helping verb “do” in a question.
6|**The data** are plotted as **items correct** out of 276 against **age of arrival**, or **AOA**, with one point for each participant. The two graphs split the participants at puberty, the end of the critical period in Lenneberg’s proposal. >> The left graph covers **early arrivals**, aged **3 to 15**, with a correlation of **r = −0.87**. >> The right graph covers **late arrivals**, aged **17 to 39**, with a correlation of **r = −0.16**. >> A **correlation**, written r, measures how closely two variables are related, from −1 to +1. A value near −1 means one variable falls steadily as the other rises. A value near 0 means little relationship. || Among **early arrivals**, scores fall steadily as age of arrival increases. >> Children arriving between about 3 and 7 score near the maximum, around 270. >> Scores fall to around 250 for arrivals near age 10 and to around 210 to 230 for arrivals near age 15. >> The strong negative correlation means age of arrival predicts much of the score in this group. || Among **late arrivals**, scores are lower overall and widely scattered, from about 160 to 250. >> Some late arrivals score around 250, while others of similar age score below 180. >> The weak correlation means age of arrival predicts little about who does well among adults. The pattern combines a strong age effect in childhood with lower, highly variable performance in adulthood.
7|The **Johnson and Newport data** have three main features. >> There is **a strong relationship between AOA and accuracy**. Earlier arrival goes with higher scores. >> **Early learners, up to about age 8, are often near-perfect**, which means **native-like**. Their scores cluster at the top of the scale, close to native English speakers. >> There is a **nearly random distribution of accuracy for late arrivals**. Among adults, age of arrival barely predicts the score. || The first two features describe childhood learners. >> Children arriving very young reach native-like grammar. >> Children arriving between about 8 and 15 reach progressively lower levels, so the decline starts well before puberty. || The phrase **“nearly random distribution”** has a specific meaning. >> It refers to the weak relationship between age of arrival and score among late arrivals. >> Late arrivals scored well above chance, but their scores varied greatly from person to person. Two adults who arrived at the same age could end up with very different scores.
8|**Johnson and Newport argue that these data are consistent with a sensitive period**, which they actually call a **“critical period,”** for L2 learning. Their argument has two parts. || **First, the dependence of ultimate attainment on AOA is consistent with a loss of language-learning ability over development**. >> **Ultimate attainment** is the final level of skill a learner reaches. >> The steady drop in scores with later arrival in childhood suggests that the ability to learn language declines as children mature. >> **The timing is not entirely consistent with Lenneberg’s hypothesis**. Lenneberg placed the end of the critical period at puberty, but the decline in these data starts much earlier, around age 7 or 8. >> Lenneberg **was working with a large gap in the data between early childhood and adolescence**, since there were no brain measurements between ages 2 and 18, so his timing was a rough estimate. || **Second, attainment in late acquisition is all over the place**, with adults who arrived at the same age reaching very different levels. || **After the sensitive period closes, attainment depends on idiosyncratic learning abilities, not on the usual language acquisition processes**. >> **Idiosyncratic learning abilities** are individual differences in skills such as memory, motivation or analytical ability. >> Children all learn through the same natural acquisition process, so they reach similar levels at similar ages. >> Adults no longer have that process available and must rely on their individual abilities, which vary greatly from person to person.
9|There are **two versions of the “critical period” hypothesis**. || **Version one is the exercise hypothesis**. >> **Early in life, humans have a superior capacity for acquiring languages**. >> **If the capacity is not exercised during this time, it will disappear or decline with maturation**. >> **If the capacity is exercised, however, further language-learning abilities will remain intact throughout life**. >> On this view, an adult who learned L1 normally should still be able to learn L2 as well as a child. || **Version two is the maturational state hypothesis**. >> **Early in life, humans have a superior capacity for acquiring languages**. >> **This capacity disappears or declines with maturation**, whether or not it has been used. >> On this view, even someone who learned L1 normally will learn L2 less well as an adult. || Johnson and Newport’s participants all had a normal first language, yet their L2 learning was worse when they started later. >> This fits the **maturational state hypothesis**, matching the title of the paper. || This raises the question of **whether the L1 critical period data should be interpreted differently**. >> Cases like Genie involve a capacity that was never exercised, which both hypotheses predict would lead to poor learning. >> Only second-language data can tell the two versions apart, because only L2 learners have exercised the capacity before learning another language.
10|A **methodological issue** concerns **test design**. **The grammaticality judgment task is very different from fluent language use**. >> In fluent speech, people produce and understand language quickly, without stopping to think about rules. >> In a grammaticality judgment task, people consider each sentence and decide whether it is correct. || **One concern is the use of “metalinguistic” or “explicit” knowledge**. **Metalinguistic knowledge** is conscious knowledge about language, such as knowing the rule that English plurals add “-s.” || **Ellis (2005)** contrasts **implicit** and **explicit knowledge** on several characteristics. >> **Awareness**. Implicit knowledge involves **intuitive awareness** of linguistic norms, while explicit knowledge involves **conscious awareness**. >> **Type of knowledge**. Implicit knowledge is **procedural** knowledge of rules and fragments, meaning knowing how to do something. Explicit knowledge is **declarative** knowledge of grammatical rules and fragments, meaning knowing facts that can be stated. >> **Systematicity**. Implicit knowledge is variable but systematic. Explicit knowledge is often anomalous and inconsistent. >> **Accessibility**. Implicit knowledge is accessed through **automatic processing**. Explicit knowledge is accessed through **controlled processing**, which takes effort. >> **Use of L2 knowledge**. Implicit knowledge is used during **fluent performance**. Explicit knowledge is used when a speaker is **planning** and having difficulty. >> **Self-report**. Implicit knowledge is **nonverbalizable**, so people cannot explain it. Explicit knowledge is **verbalizable**. >> **Learnability**. Implicit knowledge is **potentially only learnable within the critical period**. Explicit knowledge is learnable at **any age**. || If an untimed GJT allows people to use explicit knowledge, it may not measure the implicit knowledge that the critical period is supposed to affect. A learner could reach the right answer by consciously applying a taught rule, and the score would be the same.
11|One suggestion is that **the task demands of the untimed GJT make it a better measure of explicit learning of “metalinguistic” knowledge than of grammar acquisition**. **Ellis (2005)** compared the **design features** of five tests. >> **Imitation** and **oral narrative** rely on feel, are done under time pressure, and focus on meaning. They require no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **timed GJT** relies on feel and is done under time pressure, but focuses on form. It requires no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **untimed GJT** relies on rules, is done without time pressure, and focuses on form. It **does** allow metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **metalanguage** test, which asks people to state rules, also relies on rules, has no time pressure, focuses on form and requires metalinguistic knowledge. || A table gives the **correlations between starting age and years of formal instruction** and each test. >> For **imitation**, the correlations were −0.11 with starting age and 0.10 with formal instruction. >> For **oral narrative**, they were 0.03 and −0.05. >> For the **timed GJT**, starting age had a significant correlation of **−0.23**, while formal instruction had 0.17. >> For the **untimed GJT with grammatical items**, they were −0.05 and 0.03. >> For the **untimed GJT with ungrammatical items**, starting age had −0.02, while formal instruction had a significant **0.27**. >> For **metalinguistic knowledge**, they were 0.05 and 0.18. || **Consistent with this, untimed GJT performance is correlated with years of formal education in L2**. >> Formal classroom instruction teaches explicit rules, which help on the untimed test. >> Starting age was related to the timed GJT, which limits the use of explicit rules. >> An age effect found with an untimed GJT may therefore partly reflect explicit, taught knowledge rather than natural grammar acquisition.
12|Another **methodological and theoretical issue** is **transfer**. **Transfer** is the use of first-language knowledge in learning a second language. >> **Languages that are typologically more similar can benefit more from transfer**. **Typological** similarity is similarity in how languages are structured, such as their word order and word forms. >> Cognates and loan words, such as French **auto**, **rivière** and **bicyclette**, and Japanese **korokke** and **monoreru**, give learners familiar words to build on. || **Johnson and Newport tested people with English as their L2 and Chinese or Korean as their L1**. >> Chinese and Korean are very different from English in grammar and vocabulary. >> These learners got little help from transfer. || The question is **what would happen if the languages were more similar**. >> Speakers of a language close to English could use much of what they already know. >> They might show a different relationship between age and attainment. If so, the age pattern in Johnson and Newport’s data would depend on the particular languages involved as well as on age.
13|**Not all aspects of the grammar are equally difficult**. A graph plots the **mean percentage of errors on 12 types of English rules** for native speakers and for groups that arrived at ages **3–7**, **8–10**, **11–15**, **17–24** and **25–39**. >> The rule types are **determiners** (DET), **plurals** (PLU), **verb subcategorization** (SUB), **past tense** (PST), **pronouns** (PRO), **particles** (PAR), **auxiliaries** (AUX), **third-person singular** (3P), **wh-questions** (WHQ), **yes/no questions** (YNQ), **word order** (ORD) and **progressive -ing** (ING). **Verb subcategorization** is the set of rules for which kinds of words or phrases a verb can take after it. >> Native speakers and the youngest arrivals make very few errors on any rule. >> The latest arrivals, aged 17–24 and 25–39, make the most errors on **determiners**, around 65 to 68%, and on **plurals**, around 45 to 55%. >> Errors on **word order** and **progressive -ing** are low for every group, under about 10%. || **Some of this looks like the order in which children learn language**. **Progressive -ing and word order are easiest**, and these are also among the first structures children master. || **Some could be transfer**. >> **Chinese has no determiners**. Chinese speakers have no L1 category for “a” and “the,” so they find English articles especially hard. >> **Children learn questions very late, but questions are among the easiest structures for late learners**. Adults may benefit from general cognitive skills or knowledge from their L1 when learning question forms. >> Which structures are hard depends on both the structure itself and what the learner already knows from their first language.
14|**Birdsong and Molis (2001)** repeated the Johnson and Newport study with **Spanish speakers learning English**. The graphs compare the two studies. >> For **Chinese and Korean speakers learning English**, from Johnson and Newport, early arrivals aged 3–15 had a correlation of **r = −0.87**, and late arrivals aged 17–39 had **r = −0.16**. >> For **Spanish speakers learning English**, early arrivals aged 3–16 had a correlation of only **r = −0.23**, and late arrivals aged 17–44 had **r = −0.69**. || The Spanish speakers showed almost the reverse pattern. >> **Early arrivals** scored near the maximum regardless of age, with only a slight decline even for those arriving around 16. >> **Late arrivals** showed a strong decline with age, with scores falling from around 260 for those arriving near 18 to around 160 for those arriving after 40. || This raises the question of **what these results mean for the idea that AOA effects on GJT performance reflect maturational constraints**. **Maturational constraints** are limits on learning caused by the brain’s development. >> If a fixed maturational schedule controlled learning, the pattern should look the same for every first language. >> Instead, the pattern changed with the learners’ first language. >> Spanish is much closer to English than Chinese or Korean, so transfer may help Spanish speakers reach native-like grammar even when they arrive late in childhood. The age effect depends on the language pairing as well as on age.
15|It may not even be possible to call these findings **“conclusions.”** **Overall, L2 attainment is lower for older learners**. This much holds across studies. || **How much lower depends on several factors**. >> **What constructions are tested**. Some structures, like determiners, are much harder for late learners than others, like word order. >> **How they are tested**. A timed test and an untimed test can give different results, because the untimed test allows explicit knowledge. >> **Language pairings**. Spanish speakers and Chinese or Korean speakers show different age patterns when learning English. || **It is hard to separate** several factors that tend to go together. >> **Age of acquisition from the amount and quality of experience**. People who arrive younger usually have more years of exposure and more immersive experiences, such as schooling in the language. >> **Transfer effects from L2 acquisition**. Performance in L2 reflects both what is learned and what carries over from L1. >> **Performance from competence**, generally speaking. **Competence** is underlying knowledge of the language, and **performance** is how well that knowledge is used on a particular task. A test score reflects performance, which may not directly reveal competence.
