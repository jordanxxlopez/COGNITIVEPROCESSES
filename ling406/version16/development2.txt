2|Speech is **messy**. **There are no physical boundaries between words** in the speech signal. **Word segmentation** is the process of finding where one word ends and the next begins in continuous speech. >> The spectrogram of the utterance **“I hope it’s in my desk”** runs from 0 to about 1,800 milliseconds, with a pitch track drawn above it and the speech sounds transcribed along the top. >> The dark energy of one word runs straight into the energy of the next, with no blank space separating them. || **Where there are pauses in the speech stream, they come from closures that are as likely to happen within words as between them**. >> A **closure** is a moment when the vocal tract is completely blocked, as during the consonants /p/, /t/, /d/ and /k/. While the tract is closed, almost no sound comes out, so a short silence appears. >> In “hope” and “it’s,” such silences fall inside or at the edges of words in ways that do not line up with word boundaries. >> Treating every silence as a word boundary would chop some words in half and run others together. || **We are nonetheless really good at finding words in this “sea of sounds.”** Listeners hear separate words without effort, even though the signal does not mark them. >> The **harder question** is **how we learn to do this**. >> An adult can use knowledge of words already known, but an infant has to discover the words in the stream before learning what they mean.
3|There are **several cues to word boundaries**, though none works for every word. >> Some words **appear in isolation**, as in **“Hello.” “No.” “Please.” “Go.” “Stop.”** **Not all words** are heard this way. || **Phonotactics** is the set of patterns governing which sequences of sounds occur in a language. **Some sequences of phonemes are very unlikely to occur within words**. >> In **“wordboundary,”** the sequence /db/ rarely occurs inside an English word, so it hints at a boundary between word and boundary. >> In **“speechcode,”** the sequence /tʃk/ marks a boundary in the same way. >> **“Movement”** is a caution. It contains the sequence /vm/, which might look like a boundary, but movement is a single word. || **Stress patterns** offer another cue. **In English, most words are stressed on the first syllable**, from **Jusczyk and colleagues (1999)**. **Stress** makes a syllable louder, longer or higher. >> Jusczyk and colleagues familiarized **7.5-month-old infants** with a word and then played them passages. The **kingdom passage** repeats “kingdom,” as in “Your kingdom is in a faraway place. The prince used to sail to that kingdom when he came home from school.” The **device passage** repeats “device,” as in “Your device can do a lot. Her device only fixes things. My new red device makes ice cream.” >> Infants familiarized with **“kingdom,”** stressed on its first syllable, **showed a preference for the kingdom passage**. >> Infants familiarized with **“device,”** stressed on its second syllable, **did not show the same kind of preference**. || In connected speech, **stress may help signal the beginnings of words**. An infant who treats each stressed syllable as a word beginning will find kingdom easily but will split device in the wrong place. >> **This will not work in every language, or for every word in English**, because languages differ in where they place stress.
4|**Transitional probabilities** provide another cue to word boundaries, through **statistical learning**. **Statistical learning** is learning from the patterns of how often things occur together. >> Consider the phrase **“pretty baby.”** || Across English words, the syllable **/ti/** is **very strongly predicted by the syllable /prɪ/**. >> When a listener hears “pre,” the next syllable is very often “ty,” because pretty is a common word. >> This is **less likely to be true for syllable pairs across words**. After “ty,” many different words can follow, so “ba” is not well predicted. || The comparison can be written as a relationship between frequencies, where **f** means frequency of occurrence. >> The frequency of “pretty” divided by the frequency of “pre” is the probability that “pre” is followed by “ty.” >> The frequency of “tyba” divided by the frequency of “ty” is the probability that “ty” is followed by “ba.” \[\frac{f(\mathrm{pretty})}{f(\mathrm{pre})}\gg\frac{f(\mathrm{tyba})}{f(\mathrm{ty})}\] >> The first value is much greater than the second. The syllables inside pretty predict each other strongly, while the syllables across the boundary between pretty and baby predict each other weakly. || A learner who keeps track of how often each syllable follows each other syllable can therefore find the joints in the speech stream. Pairs inside words recur together again and again, while pairs across word boundaries recur much less reliably.
5|Put more abstractly, **the likelihood of syllable B following syllable A is greater within words than between words**. This likelihood is the **transitional probability** from A to B. >> Inside a word, the same syllables always appear in the same order, so the probability is high. >> At a word boundary, the next word can be almost anything, so the probability is lower. >> A dip in transitional probability is therefore a good guess for where a word boundary falls. || **Of course this is probabilistic**, meaning it works most of the time but not always. >> **“What’s that in the road a/head?”** can be heard as “a head” or “ahead.” The sounds are the same, so the listener settles the boundary using the meaning of the whole sentence. >> **“I scream, you scream, we all scream for ice cream”** plays on the fact that “I scream” and “ice cream” contain nearly the same sounds. The same sequence can be divided into words in two ways. || Transitional probabilities shift the odds toward one segmentation or another, alongside stress, phonotactics and isolated words. >> When cues agree, segmentation is easy. >> When cues conflict or are missing, as in a head versus ahead, the listener relies on context to settle the boundary.
6|**Saffran and colleagues (1996)** isolated **statistical information from other cues** to test whether transitional probabilities alone can be used to identify words. To do this, the stimuli were **tightly controlled**. >> The speech was **monotone**, with **no changes in pitch, duration or amplitude**. Amplitude is loudness. Without these changes, stress could not mark word beginnings. >> The syllables were **simple CV syllables**, each one consonant followed by one vowel. With only CV syllables, there were **no phonotactic cues** to word boundaries, because no unusual consonant clusters appeared. >> The syllables were **presented continuously**, with **no pauses**, so silence could not mark the boundaries. || The researchers created an **artificial language** made of **three-syllable “words,”** such as **tokibu, gikoba** and **gopila**. >> The words were strung together in random order into one long stream. >> The only information about where words began and ended was in the transitional probabilities between syllables. || If infants could still find the words, transitional probabilities must be enough on their own. The design isolates the one cue being tested.
7|The artificial language stream runs together with no breaks, as in **“tokibugikobagopilatipolutokibu,” “gopilatipolutokibugikobagopila,”** and **“gikobatokibugopilatipolugikoba.”** >> This is the kind of input the infant receives, with no spaces and no pauses. >> The four words, tokibu, gikoba, gopila and tipolu, are hidden inside the stream, and each repeats many times in random order. || Even without any marked boundaries, the syllables follow regular patterns. >> Within **tokibu**, “to” is always followed by “ki,” and “ki” is always followed by “bu.” >> After “bu,” however, the stream can continue with “gi,” “go” or “ti,” depending on which word comes next. || The stream contains all the information needed to find the words. >> A learner who tracks which syllables follow which can discover that some pairs are completely predictable while others are not. >> The words emerge from the statistics of the sequence.
8|In the color-coded version of the same stream, each word has its own color, with **tokibu** in red, **gikoba** in green, **gopila** in pink and **tipolu** in purple. >> The colors make the four words visible to the eye. >> The infant hears no colors. It hears only the continuous stream of syllables. || Each word keeps its internal order every time it appears, while its neighbors change. >> “To-ki-bu” always comes in that order, but tokibu can be followed by gikoba, gopila or tipolu. >> The transitional probabilities inside each word are therefore high, while those across word boundaries are lower. || The boundaries the colors mark are exactly the places where transitional probability drops. >> The signal itself has no spaces. >> Tracking that drop in predictability is how the learner recovers where one word ends and another begins.
9|**Testing for an effect of transitional probabilities** requires some care. **After listening to a long stream of syllables, it is trivial to show that infants are more familiar with sequences they have heard than ones they have not**. A preference for heard sequences could come from simple familiarity, without any use of transitional probabilities. || **Two additional details** rule this out. >> **Half of the words were repeated more frequently than the other half**. >> The test compared words against **“part-words”** that were **matched for frequency of occurrence** with real words. A **part-word** is a three-syllable sequence that crosses a word boundary, made of the end of one word and the beginning of the next. || In the example stream **“tokibu gikoba gopila gikoba tokibu gopila tokibu gikoba gikoba tokibu,”** the word **GOPILA** and the part-word **BU-GIKO** both appear **twice**. Their overall frequencies are the same, while their transitional probabilities differ. || The transitional probability of **GI given BU** is **2 out of 4**. >> BU appears four times, at the end of each tokibu, but is followed by GI only twice. \[\frac{2}{4}=0.5\] >> The transitional probabilities inside **GOPILA** are **both 2 out of 2**. GO is followed by PI both times, and PI is followed by LA both times. \[\frac{2}{2}=1\] || If infants treat gopila differently from bu-giko, they are sensitive to transitional probabilities. Matching the frequencies removes the familiarity explanation, so any difference must come from how predictable the syllables are.
10|The test produced **a novelty effect**. **Infants looked longer in the direction of “part-word” stimuli**. >> The bar graph gives mean looking time in seconds. Words received about **6.8 seconds** of looking, and part-words about **7.6 seconds**. >> An asterisk marks the difference as statistically significant. || Longer looking toward part-words is a **novelty preference**. >> Infants often look longer at things that seem new. >> The words had become familiar during exposure, so they held less interest. The part-words seemed less familiar, even though they had been heard just as often. || This suggests that infants **had learned the statistical regularities of the speech stream**, and **were not merely picking up on simple frequency of occurrence**. >> Words and part-words were matched for frequency, so the difference comes from the transitional probabilities that distinguished them. >> In a short exposure, infants extracted units from a continuous stream using only the predictability of syllable sequences.
11|**Identifying word referents** is the next problem. Once children can **“find” the words** in speech, **how do they learn what they mean?** A **referent** is the thing a word refers to. >> A child might hear the word **“cat”** while looking at a cat. >> Recognizing the sound pattern “cat” is one achievement. Knowing that it names the furry animal is another. || Segmentation recovers a recurring unit of sound but says nothing about its meaning. >> Any scene contains many objects, parts, properties and actions. >> The learner needs further evidence to work out which of these the speaker means. || Word learning therefore requires additional evidence about reference. The child has to connect the word form with the speaker’s intended meaning, using evidence from the situation in which the word is used.
12|**The inductive problem of early word learning** is that any single experience is compatible with many possible meanings. **Induction** is reasoning from specific examples to a general rule, and here the rule is the meaning of a word. >> A child hears a new word while looking at a cat. The word could mean **CAT**, **ANIMAL** or **PET**, which are categories at different levels. >> It could mean **FELIX**, the name of this particular cat. >> It could mean a property such as **FURRY** or **ALIVE**, an action such as **WALKING**, or a part such as **WHISKERS** or **LEG**. || The set of possible meanings is called the **hypothesis space**. >> Every one of these candidates is consistent with what the child sees. >> Nothing in the single scene picks out which one the speaker intends. || This is a version of **Quine’s “gavagai” problem**. >> In Quine’s example, a linguist visiting an unknown language hears a speaker say “gavagai” while a rabbit runs past. >> “Gavagai” could mean rabbit, but it could also mean undetached rabbit parts, a stage of a rabbit or something else entirely. >> Children face this problem with every new word and still learn words quickly. Joint attention, intentional cues and default word-learning strategies narrow the possibilities.
13|One way children acquire word meanings is through **joint attention**. Joint attention is **sharing an attentional focus with another person**. >> The drawing has two figures looking and pointing at the same object, with arrows linking each figure to the object and to the other figure. >> Each person knows that the other is attending to the same thing. || **Gaze following emerges around 9 months of age**. >> **Gaze following** is looking where another person is looking. >> When an adult looks at an object, the infant follows the adult’s eyes to that object. || Gaze following **signals an understanding of reference**. >> The infant understands that the adult’s attention is directed at something specific. >> When the adult then says a word, the object of shared attention becomes the most likely referent. Joint attention narrows the hypothesis space by linking the word to what the speaker is attending to.
14|**Joint attention optimizes word learning**, according to **Baldwin and colleagues**. Two groups of infants heard a novel word while playing with an object. >> In the **joint attention group**, an **adult interacting with the baby labeled the object the baby was playing with**. >> In the **no joint attention group**, a **voice from behind a screen labeled the object the baby was playing with**. || The two conditions are matched in important ways. >> In both, the word was spoken while the infant was focused on the same object. >> The only difference was whether a visible person was engaged with the infant and the object. || **Eighteen-month-olds learned the novel word in the joint attention group but not in the no joint attention group**. >> Hearing a word at the same moment as seeing an object was insufficient for learning. >> Infants needed evidence that a speaker was using the word to refer to that object. The voice behind the screen supplied the same word at the same moment, so the difference in learning came from the visible, engaged speaker.
15|Children narrow the hypothesis space for new words partly through **intentional cues from the speaker**, such as **eye gaze direction**. An **intentional cue** is a signal of what a speaker means to refer to. >> The photo of a woman’s face looking toward a small toy cat at one side gives the kind of cue involved. Her eyes indicate which object she is attending to. || **Baldwin** taught new words to **16–17-month-olds** and **18–19-month-olds** in two conditions. >> In **follow-in labeling**, the adult **looked at the toy the infant was focused on** while saying the new word. Both cues point to the same toy. >> In **discrepant labeling**, the adult **looked at a toy in a bucket**, a different toy from the one the infant was focused on, while saying the new word. || The discrepant condition separates the infant’s own focus from the speaker’s focus. >> If infants simply attach a new word to whatever they are looking at, they would map the word onto their own toy. >> If they use the speaker’s gaze, they would map it onto the toy in the bucket. >> The comparison tests whether infants treat the speaker’s gaze as evidence of what the word means.
16|**Gaze direction** helps a child identify which object a speaker is naming when several objects are present. >> A speaker who looks at one object while saying a new word links that word to the object. >> The child can follow the speaker’s gaze to find the intended referent. || The speaker’s focus can differ from the child’s own focus. >> Following it means using information about another person’s attention. >> A new word can then be attached to an object the child was not looking at when the word was said. || Gaze narrows the possible meanings without the word’s sound giving anything away. >> The child combines the new word with evidence about what the speaker intends. >> Following gaze requires understanding that a speaker’s eyes point to what the speaker means, which yields a more specific mapping than linking the word to everything in view.
17|Intentional cues also include **affective and behavioral cues** from the speaker. **Affective** cues are emotional expressions, and **behavioral** cues are the speaker’s actions. >> A speaker’s expression can indicate whether an object is the one they were looking for. >> A speaker’s actions, such as reaching for an object, can reveal what they want. || **Akhtar and colleagues** found that **infants used adults’ facial expressions to identify the intended referent of a novel word**. >> The adult’s emotional reaction to an object indicated whether it was the one the adult meant. >> Infants linked the new word to the object that the adult’s expression marked as intended. || Several objects can be present when a word is spoken, but the speaker’s reaction can single out one of them. >> A satisfied expression signals that the speaker found what they wanted, and a disappointed expression signals that the object is not the one meant. >> Word learning therefore involves understanding the goal of the interaction.
18|A speaker’s **emotional reaction** is an **intentional cue** that can identify the intended object among several candidates. >> A reaction of satisfaction or excitement can link one object to what the speaker was trying to find or do. >> The learner can use that link to interpret a new label. || **Intention is conveyed through more than gaze direction**. >> Facial expressions and actions also reveal what a speaker means. >> A word can be spoken near several objects without all of them being equally good candidates. || The useful information lies in how the adult responds to each object. >> Relating the label to that response narrows the word’s **hypothesis space**, the set of meanings it could have. >> Infants who use emotional reactions are tracking the speaker’s goals. The word becomes linked to the object that satisfies the goal, and objects that disappoint the speaker are ruled out.
19|Children also use **word learning strategies**. These are **default strategies that children bring to the word learning situation**. >> A **default** is a starting assumption that holds unless there is evidence against it. >> These strategies **function to narrow the hypothesis space for new words**, giving children a starting point when other cues are weak. || One example is the **whole object assumption**. >> Under this assumption, a child expects a new word to name **a whole object** rather than one of its parts, its color, its texture or an action it is doing. >> When a child hears a new word while looking at a cat, the assumption favors “cat” over “whiskers,” “furry” or “walking.” || The whole object assumption removes many unlikely meanings at once and matches how adults usually label objects for children. >> It is a starting preference that later evidence can override. >> Further evidence can show that a word names a part or a property, and children do eventually learn such words.
20|Another word learning strategy is **mutual exclusivity**, from **Markman (1989)**. >> **As a default, objects can have only one name**. >> The **implication** is that **new words map to nameless objects**. >> Mutual exclusivity **emerges during the second half of the second year**, that is, between about 18 and 24 months of age. || Pictures of a parrot and a dog stand for a typical situation. >> Suppose a child already knows the word “dog” but has no word for a parrot. >> If the child hears a new word, the child assumes it refers to the parrot, because the dog already has a name. No gaze or emotion cue is needed for this choice. || Mutual exclusivity works together with the whole object assumption. >> The whole object assumption says a new word names an object, and mutual exclusivity says it names an object without a name yet. >> It is a default inference, so later learning can override it when an object turns out to have more than one name.
21|Under **mutual exclusivity**, a child who hears an unfamiliar word assigns it to an object that does not yet have a known name. >> An object the child can already name is unlikely to be the target of a new word. >> The choice depends on comparing the objects that are present. || **Prior vocabulary** decides which objects count as already named. >> Two children could make different guesses in the same scene if they know different words. >> The strategy builds on what the child has already learned. || Vocabulary growth therefore builds on itself. >> A child who knows many names has more objects to rule out. >> The unnamed objects that remain become the most likely referents for new words, so each known word helps narrow the meaning of the next one.
22|**How do children start putting words together?** **In production, it can be hard to tell what is going on when children produce only one, two or three words at a time**. A short utterance can come from several very different processes. || **Menn** distinguishes among four kinds of early utterances. >> **“Just a bunch of relevant words.”** The child strings together words related to the situation without any grammatical structure linking them. >> **Formulas**. A formula is a phrase memorized as a whole unit, without the child knowing it has separate parts. >> **Word combinations**. The child puts separate words together productively, creating new combinations. >> **Stable constructions**, which are consistent patterns the child uses to build utterances. These are **not necessarily adult-like**. || A child might say **“Up!”**, **“Pick you up!”** and **“Mommy up!”** >> “Pick you up!” sounds more advanced, but it could be a formula copied as a whole from what adults say. >> “Mommy up!” is shorter, but it might be a true combination of two separate words. >> Telling which process produced each one requires more than utterance length. || In practice, **a full description of the context in which an utterance is produced is necessary, but often not sufficient, to distinguish among these**. >> Context reveals what the child intends and how the utterance is used across situations. >> Even with full context, the source of a particular utterance can stay uncertain.
23|There is a difference between **comprehension** and **production**. **Even children at the one-word stage in production are able to use basic transitive word order in comprehension**. >> The **one-word stage** is the period when a child speaks only in single words. >> A **transitive** sentence has a verb with a subject and an object. In English, transitive word order puts the actor first and the person acted upon after the verb. || The **structure-mapping account** of **early syntactic bootstrapping**, from **Gertner and Fisher**, describes how this works. **Syntactic bootstrapping** is using sentence structure to help learn about meaning. >> The **input sentence** is **“The boy is tickling the girl!”** The child forms a **partial sentence representation**, **tickle[N1 = boy, N2 = girl]**, noting the verb and the two nouns in order. >> The **observed scene** has a boy tickling a girl. The child forms **conceptual representations** of possible events, such as **Giggle(agent = girl)**, **Tickle(agent = boy, patient = girl)**, **Play(agent = boy)** and **Play(agent = girl)**. An **agent** is the doer of an action, and a **patient** is the one affected by it. >> A **one-to-one mapping between nouns and participant roles** links the two nouns in the sentence to the two participants in the tickling event. The sentence has two nouns, so it fits the event with two participants. || Understanding can therefore run ahead of production. >> A child who says only single words can still use the order of nouns to work out who did what. >> Aligning the nouns with the participants in the scene helps the child work out the meaning of the verb.
24|A **generalization test** checks whether children have really learned the **transitive structure**. **If children have really learned the transitive structure, they will generalize it to new verbs**. >> A child who has only memorized particular sentences will be stuck with an unfamiliar verb. >> A child who knows the structure can use word order to interpret any verb. || To select the picture that could depict **“The duck is gorping the bunny”** from two novel actions, a child needs to know that the first noun is the doer and the second is the one acted upon. The verb **“gorp”** is made up, so only the order of the nouns can help. || **Gertner and colleagues (2006)** showed that **two-year-olds can understand this**. The procedure alternated blank screens with test trials. >> During a **5-second blank screen**, children heard “Hey look! The duck’s gonna gorp the bunny!” >> In the **first 8-second test trial**, two scenes appeared side by side. In one, a person in a duck costume acted on a person in a bunny costume by pushing them in a wagon. In the other, the bunny acted on the duck. Children heard “The duck is gorping the bunny! The duck is gorping the bunny! See?” >> During a **4-second blank screen**, children heard “The duck gorped the bunny! Find gorping!” >> In the **second 8-second test trial**, the scenes appeared again with “The duck is gorping the bunny! Find gorping. Find gorping!” >> Children **looked significantly longer at the duck acting on the rabbit than the reverse**. || At this age, children typically have a **mean length of utterance**, or **MLU**, of **around 2**. MLU is the average number of **morphemes**, meaningful words or word parts, per utterance. >> Their three-morpheme utterances might be like **“Duck gorp bunny.”** >> Their speech is short and incomplete, yet they understand the full transitive structure.
