2|**Babies are overrated** as language learners. When **sensitive periods** for a second language, or **L2**, are taken to mean that infants, toddlers and children are “better” at learning language than adults, it is important not to get carried away. >> A typical child **by 18 months** understands the concepts of “in and out” and “off and on,” points to several body parts when asked, **uses at least 20 words**, responds with words or gestures to simple questions such as “Where’s teddy?” and “What’s that?”, shows some pretend play, such as giving teddy a drink, makes at least four different consonant sounds, such as b, n, d, g, w and h, enjoys being read to, and points to pictures using one finger. >> An adult who **only knew about 20 words after 18 months of language instruction** would be considered a very poor learner. || **Adults initially learn more quickly than children**. This is due largely to two advantages. >> **Transfer from L1**, the first language. Adults can build on what they already know about how language works. >> **Better general cognitive skills**, such as memory, attention and reasoning. || **Where adults do worse is ultimate attainment**, meaning the final level of skill they eventually reach. >> Adults **rarely become native-like** in a second language. >> This may also be **due in part to transfer**. The first language that helps adults start quickly may also hold them back from native-like skill. >> Speed of early learning and final level of success are different measures, and children and adults compare differently on each.
3|Some of the **“language milestones”** in a toddler’s development are actually **general conceptual abilities**. >> Understanding **“in and out”** and **“off and on”** depends on understanding **spatial relations between objects**. >> **Pretend play**, such as giving teddy a drink, depends on **event representations**, which are mental representations of who does what in an event. >> A toddler learning these is building new ideas about the world as well as new words. || **Later learners of a second language already have much of this in place**. >> An adult learning Spanish already understands what “in” and “on” mean as concepts. >> Adults can **focus on learning how these concepts are represented in the target language**, meaning the language being learned. This is a smaller job than building the concepts and the words at the same time. || Later learners **also already know another language**, which affects **how the second language is learned**. >> The first language provides ready-made ways of expressing concepts. >> Some of these fit the second language and help. Others clash with it and cause errors.
4|**Transfer** is **the use of native-language, or L1, processes and representations for a second language, or L2**. >> A learner brings L1 habits for hearing sounds, building sentences and finding words into L2. >> The learner approaches L2 with a fully working language system already in place. || **Transfer can have both positive and negative effects on second-language learning and performance**. >> **Positive transfer** happens when an L1 pattern matches what L2 requires. Shared sounds, words and structures give the learner a head start. >> **Negative transfer** happens when an L1 pattern conflicts with L2. Differences between the languages are where errors appear, because the learner’s habits point the wrong way. || A cartoon of two men at a bar has one asking **“Do you not be happy with me as the translator of the books of you?”** >> The sentence uses English words, but its structure follows the grammar of another language. >> Word-by-word translation carries over the word order and phrasing of the first language, which is negative transfer in grammar.
5|Transfer affects **phonetic and phonological learning**, the learning of speech sounds. **Categorical perception** leads to **acquired similarity** for sounds in the same category. **Acquired similarity** means that sounds a language groups into one category come to sound more alike through experience with that language. || **Minimal pairs in Hindi** reveal the problem. A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in only one sound. >> One pair of words both sound like “tap” to English ears. One means **hot**, and the other means **a kind of beat**. >> One pair both sound like “dab.” One means **pressure**, and the other means **a kind of fruit**. >> One pair both sound like “ari.” One means **saw**, the tool, and the other means **crooked**. >> In each pair, one word has a **dental** consonant, made with the tongue against the teeth, and the other has a **retroflex** consonant, made with the tongue curled back toward the roof of the mouth. || **Native English speakers tend to use the English “alveolar” category to interpret both dental and retroflex consonants**. An **alveolar** consonant, like English /t/ or /d/, is made with the tongue on the ridge just behind the teeth. >> English has only the alveolar category, so both Hindi sounds get mapped onto it and seem the same. || **In this case, transfer makes learning the L2 contrast more difficult**. >> The English category was learned successfully, but it now hides a difference that Hindi uses to distinguish words. >> The learner has to learn to separate sounds that the first language taught them to treat as one.
6|Transfer also affects **grammar learning**. **Classifiers** are a **somewhat unusual grammatical feature across languages**. A **classifier** is a word that must appear between a number or demonstrative and a noun, chosen according to the kind of thing the noun names. >> The structure is **demonstrative pronoun, then nominal classifier, then noun**, as in **yí gè rén**, “one person,” where **gè** is the classifier. || Chinese has many classifiers, each used with a particular kind of noun. >> **Gè** is the **general classifier**. >> **Bǎ** is used with **objects that have a handle**. >> **Běn** is used with **books and similar objects**. >> **Kuài** is used with **objects that are divided into pieces**. >> **Liàng** is used with **wheeled vehicles**. >> **Tái** is used with **devices and gadgets**. >> **Xiē** is used to indicate an **indeterminate amount**, or with the **plural** of pronouns and demonstratives. >> **Zhāng** is used with **flat objects**. >> **Zhī** is used with **long, thin objects**. || **Native English speakers learning Chinese have difficulty learning these**. >> In English, to say **“a chair,” “that dog,” “the car”** or **“one book,”** we do not pay attention to what kind of thing the noun names. >> **In Chinese, different classifiers are required by different nouns**, so the learner must notice properties such as shape and function. || One way to think about this is that **English speakers have learned to ignore the different cues for classifiers** as part of learning English grammar. Those features of objects are irrelevant to English grammar, so learning Chinese requires attending again to cues English taught them to ignore.
7|**Grammatical gender** is another area where **transfer causes problems**. **Grammatical gender is different from social or cultural gender**. It is a system that sorts nouns into classes, often called masculine and feminine. >> **There is nothing “male” about a book or “female” about liberty**. In French, **le livre**, the book, is masculine, and **la liberté**, liberty, is feminine. >> Other French examples include masculine **le cheval** (the horse), **le chien** (the dog) and **le bruit** (the noise), and feminine **la colombe** (the dove), **la chemise** (the shirt) and **la maison** (the house). || **Grammatical gender has implications for agreement**. **Agreement** means that related words must match the noun’s gender. **Pronouns, adjectives, some forms of verbs** and other words **all need to be coordinated with noun gender**. >> English has no grammatical gender on nouns, so English speakers have no habit of tracking it. || French is famous for having **synonyms with different genders**. >> **Auto** and **voiture**, both meaning car, are masculine and feminine. >> **Rivière** and **fleuve**, both meaning river, are feminine and masculine. >> **Vélo** and **bicyclette**, both meaning bicycle, are masculine and feminine. >> Gender therefore has to be learned word by word. || Common **word endings** give statistical clues to gender. >> Endings common with **masculine nouns** include **-age**, as in le fromage (the cheese), **-r**, as in le professeur (the teacher), **-t**, as in le chat (the cat), and **-isme**, as in le capitalisme. >> Endings common with **feminine nouns** include **-ie**, as in la boulangerie (the bakery), **-ion**, as in la nation, **-ité** or **-tié**, as in la fraternité (brotherhood), **-ance**, as in la balance (the scales), and **-nne, -mme** and **-lle**, as in la fille (the girl) and l’indienne.
8|**Transfer is not all bad**. **Vocabulary learning in some L1–L2 situations is aided by the similarity of words across languages**. >> French **auto** resembles English “auto” and refers to a car. >> French **rivière** resembles English “river,” and French **bicyclette** resembles English “bicycle.” >> Japanese **コロッケ, korokke**, refers to a croquette, and Japanese **モノレール, monoreru**, refers to a monorail. || Sometimes the similarity is **due to shared etymology**. **Etymology** is the history of where a word comes from. >> Words with a shared origin are called **cognates**. French rivière and English river both come from the same older word. || Sometimes the similarity is **due to more recent “borrowing.”** >> A **loan word** is a word one language takes from another. >> Japanese korokke and monoreru are borrowed from European languages, with their sounds adapted to Japanese. || Cognates and loan words are **positive transfer** for vocabulary. >> The learner already knows a similar form with a similar meaning, which gives the new word a familiar anchor. >> A learner who knows river can quickly link it to rivière, which makes the new word easier to store and to recall.
9|**Not all similar-sounding words are cognates**. **The bias to interpret L2 words as related to similar-sounding L1 words can be very strong**. **Sometimes the result is embarrassing**. >> A word that looks or sounds like a word in another language but means something different is called a **false friend**. || Spanish **“Arnold está embarazado”** does not mean that Arnold is embarrassed. >> Spanish **embarazado** means **pregnant**. >> An English speaker relying on the similar sound of embarrassed would get the meaning badly wrong. || The link to a familiar word happens before the learner checks the meaning. >> When the meanings match, the link speeds up learning, as with cognates. >> When the meanings differ, the same link leads to errors. The only difference between a helpful cognate and a misleading false friend is whether the meanings actually match.
10|**De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** tested in the lab whether people **use cognates in “second language” learning**. >> They used **paired-associate learning of made-up words**. In **paired-associate learning**, participants study pairs of items and later have to recall one item when given the other. >> The made-up words **varied in their similarity to their Dutch partners**, so some were cognate-like and some were not. >> The words **also varied in “concreteness.”** A **concrete** word names something that can be seen or touched, while an **abstract** word names an idea. || Crossing the two factors gave four kinds of pairs, so the effect of each factor can be measured separately. >> **Gitaar–gettar**, “guitar,” is **concrete and cognate-like**. >> **Tijger–duunze**, “tiger,” is **concrete and non-cognate**. >> **Domein–domaan**, “domain,” is **abstract and cognate-like**. >> **Herstel–groop**, “repair,” is **abstract and non-cognate**. || **Participants were simply presented with pairs of Dutch words and their nonword partners and asked to learn the pairs**. >> They were Dutch speakers, so the Dutch words stood in for their first language, and the made-up words stood in for a new second language. >> **They were then tested a week later on retention**, meaning how well they remembered the pairs after a delay without further study.
11|The results show that **cognates are learned more rapidly and also remembered more easily**. A table gives **recall scores** and **response times** across four sessions, with **session 4** coming **after one week with no training**. || **Recall scores** in percent increased across the three training sessions. >> For **concrete cognates**, recall was 69.7% in session 1, rising to 91.7% and then 93.4%. **Concrete non-cognates** started at only 43.7% and rose to 92.0% by session 3. >> For **abstract cognates**, recall was 42.7% in session 1, rising to 86.0% by session 3. **Abstract non-cognates** started at just 19.0% and reached 72.7%. >> After a week without training, in session 4, concrete cognates dropped to 78.4% and concrete non-cognates to 69.0%. Abstract cognates dropped to 68.4%, while abstract non-cognates fell sharply to **36.0%**. || **Response times**, in milliseconds, were faster for cognates in every session. >> In session 1, concrete cognates took about 1,880 milliseconds, while abstract non-cognates took about 3,641. >> In session 3, concrete cognates took about 1,080 milliseconds and concrete non-cognates about 1,643. >> In session 4, concrete cognates took about 1,833 milliseconds and abstract non-cognates about 3,412. || This is evidence that **cognate status can contribute to the very earliest stages of word learning in a second language**. >> The cognate advantage was largest in the first session and reappeared after a week, when non-cognates were forgotten more. >> Concreteness also had a large effect, and the two factors add together. Abstract non-cognates have neither source of support and were the hardest of all. >> One explanation for concreteness effects is called **context availability**, the idea that concrete words are easier to link with a supporting context.
12|The experiment needs a closer look. **If de Groot and Keijzer had only tested “receptive” vocabulary**, meaning **how easily participants could produce the Dutch word given its made-up partner**, **this would not be very impressive**. >> In receptive testing, the learner sees the new word and has to supply the familiar Dutch word. >> For cognate-like pairs, the new word already looks like the Dutch word, so a participant could answer correctly just by noticing the resemblance. || **Many of the meanings, such as gitaar, tijger and domein, could be guessed without any Dutch training**, because they resemble English guitar, tiger and domain. The **concrete cognates** in the study reveal how close the resemblance is. >> **Broer–breur**, **brood–brodde**, **gitaar–gettar**, **jongen–jange** and **lepel–lippel**. >> **Lichaam–lechem**, **naald–nadel**, **paraplu–parredu**, **rivier–riffer** and **schaar–skare**. >> **Stier–stire**, **trein–trinen**, **varken–vark**, **viool–vialo** and **vrouw–vrauk**. >> In each pair, the made-up word is a slightly changed version of the Dutch word. || The study **also tested “expressive” vocabulary**, which the authors call **“productive” testing**. >> In productive testing, the learner sees the Dutch word and has to produce the new made-up word. >> The learner has to remember the exact new form, gettar, out of many similar possibilities. >> Productive testing removes the shortcut, so a cognate advantage there reflects real learning of new forms.
13|A second table compares **productive testing** and **receptive testing** **immediately after learning, in session 3**, and **at retest a week later, in session 4**. The **effect** row gives the drop in recall over the week. || In **productive testing**, learners had to produce the new word. >> **Concrete** words fell from 92.7% to 73.7%, an effect of 19.0 points. **Abstract** words fell from 79.3% to 52.2%, an effect of 27.1 points. >> **Cognates** fell from 89.7% to 73.3%, an effect of 16.4 points. **Non-cognates** fell from 82.3% to 52.5%, an effect of 29.8 points. || In **receptive testing**, learners had to give the Dutch word. >> **Concrete** words fell from 97.7% to 88.9%, an effect of 8.8 points. **Abstract** words fell from 91.5% to 64.9%, an effect of 26.6 points. >> **Cognates** fell from 98.5% to 90.7%, an effect of 7.8 points. **Non-cognates** fell from 90.7% to 63.0%, an effect of 27.7 points. || Productive scores were lower than receptive scores, because generating a new form is harder than recognizing a familiar one. >> Cognates and concrete words were forgotten less in **both** directions, so their advantage extends from initial learning to long-term retention. >> The cognate advantage held up in productive testing, where the learner had to recall the new form. Similarity to a known word helped learners form a lasting memory of the new word.
14|A key **research question** is whether **similar L1 words are always activated when processing L2**. **There have been many, many studies of this**. >> If a bilingual person hears a word in their second language, words in their first language that sound similar might also become active. >> This activation could happen automatically, even when the person is trying to use only the second language. If so, the two languages are never fully separate in the mind. || **One clear example** comes from a **“visual world” experiment** by **Marian and Spivey (2003)**. >> In a visual world experiment, participants hear spoken instructions while viewing a display of objects, and their eye movements are recorded. >> Looks toward an object whose L1 name sounds like the spoken L2 word would reveal that the L1 word was activated. >> Eye movements capture this activation without asking the person to report it, while the participant goes on to choose the correct object.
15|This kind of experiment parallels **McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)**, a study of **gradient effects of within-category VOT on lexical access**. >> In that study, participants saw a display with a **lamp**, a **ship**, a **bear** and a **pear**, and heard a word from a continuum between “bear” and “pear.” >> The bear and pear differ only in their first consonant, and the lamp and ship are unrelated fillers. || **Eye movements reveal competition between possible responses that participants are considering**. >> A participant could click on the bear while having looked briefly at the pear. >> Those looks reveal that “pear” was active as a competitor. || In the McMurray study, **the amount of competition between BEAR and PEAR depended on distance from the category boundary**. >> Sounds closer to the /b/–/p/ boundary produced more looks to the competitor, and sounds farther from it produced fewer. >> The eye-tracking method therefore measures how strongly an alternative word is active, even when the participant chooses correctly. >> Applying the same method across languages lets researchers ask whether a word in one language competes with a similar word in another.
16|**Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** demonstrated the **“cohort effect.”** A **cohort** is the set of words that share the same beginning sounds. >> Participants viewed a grid containing pictures and shapes and followed spoken instructions. >> The display included a **beaker**, the target, a **beetle**, which shares its beginning with beaker, a **speaker**, which rhymes with beaker, and a **carriage**, which is unrelated. || The graph plots the **probability of fixating** each item over the first 1,000 milliseconds after the target word begins. >> Looks to the **beaker** and the **beetle** rise together early, because both fit the first sounds of the word. >> Looks to the beetle peak near 0.2 at around 500 milliseconds and then fall once the sounds that distinguish the words arrive. >> Looks to the beaker keep rising, approaching 1 by about 1,000 milliseconds. >> Looks to the **speaker** rise later and stay smaller, peaking near 0.1, because its overlap comes at the end of the word. Looks to the carriage stay near zero. || The **cohort effect** is this early competition from words that share a beginning with the target. >> Words compete as soon as they match the incoming sounds. >> They drop out when the input stops matching them, and eye movements track this rise and fall moment by moment.
17|**Marian and Spivey** examined the cohort effect with **“cross-language competitors.”** A cross-language competitor is an object whose name in the **other language** shares its beginning with the spoken target word. || **If L1 words are always activated, native Russian speakers should look at a book of matches when the target is “speaker” in English**. >> English **speaker** begins with the same sounds as Russian **спички, spichki**, meaning **matches**. >> If Russian is active while the participant listens to English, the picture of matches should attract looks. || For **English targets**, each target was paired with an **English competitor** and a **Russian competitor**. >> **Speaker** was paired with **spear** in English and **spichki**, matches, in Russian. >> **Boot** was paired with **book** and **buben**, tambourine. **Shark** was paired with **shovel** and **sharik**, balloon. >> **Chair** was paired with **chess set** and **cherepaha**, turtle. **Marker** was paired with **marbles** and **marka**, stamp. >> **Barbed wire** was paired with **bark** and **barhat**, velvet. **Plug** was paired with **plum** and **platye**, dress. >> **Gun** was paired with **gum** and **gaika**, nut. **Lock** was paired with **lobster** and **lak dlea nogtei**, nail polish. || For **Russian targets**, the pairing was reversed. >> Russian **spichki**, matches, was paired with English **spear** and Russian **spitsi**, knitting needles. >> Russian **marka**, stamp, was paired with English **marker** and Russian **marlya**, cheese cloth. >> Russian **platye**, dress, was paired with English **plum** and Russian **plashch**, raincoat. || The connection between the target and the cross-language competitor is purely in **sound**. >> Speakers and matches have nothing to do with each other in meaning. >> Only the Russian competitor’s overlap depends on knowing Russian, so looks to the matches during an English task directly measure activation of the Russian word spichki.
18|The results for **competition during L2 spoken word recognition**, when Russian–English bilinguals followed instructions in **English**, come from a table giving the **percent of trials** in which participants looked at each competitor. >> When only the **between-language competitor** was present, participants looked at it on **18%** of trials. >> When only the **within-language competitor** was present, participants looked at it on **18%** of trials. >> When **both competitors** were present, participants looked at the between-language competitor on **13%** of trials and the within-language competitor on **19%**. >> When **no competitor** was present, participants looked at a control filler object on only **7%** of trials. || **When only one competitor was present, participants looked at within- and between-language competitors to the same extent**. >> The Russian name of the matches pulled the eyes just as strongly as a similar English word did. || **When both competitors were present, participants looked at the within-language competitor more**, **but still looked at the between-language competitor a lot**, about **twice as often as the control condition**. >> The English competitor won out when the two were side by side. >> The Russian competitor still attracted far more looks than an unrelated object. || The first language therefore stays active during second-language listening. Bilinguals still select the correct object, but L1 words compete along the way.
19|The results for **competition during L1 spoken word recognition**, when the same bilinguals followed instructions in **Russian**, come from a second table. >> When only the **between-language competitor**, an object with a similar **English** name, was present, participants looked at it on **8%** of trials. >> When only the **within-language competitor** was present, participants looked at it on **14%** of trials. >> When **both** were present, they looked at the between-language competitor on **8%** of trials and the within-language competitor on **13%**. >> With **no competitor**, looks to the control filler occurred on **5%** of trials. || **English words do not seem to be activated when native Russian speakers do this experiment in Russian**. >> The **small numerical difference between the between-language and control conditions**, 8% versus 5%, is **not statistically significant**. >> A difference that is not statistically significant is within the range of chance variation. || The within-language competitor still produced a reliable cohort effect in Russian. >> The pattern across the two experiments is **asymmetric**. Russian words became active during English tasks, but English words did not become reliably active during Russian tasks. >> This asymmetry fits a picture in which the first language is more strongly established and more easily activated than the second.
20|**The Revised Hierarchical Model** of **Kroll and Stewart** explains **L1–L2 interactions in terms of spreading activation**. **Spreading activation** is the idea that activating one representation partly activates the representations connected to it. >> The model has three boxes. An **L1** box holds the first-language vocabulary, an **L2** box holds the second-language vocabulary, and a **concepts** box holds meanings. >> **Lexical links** connect the L1 and L2 words directly. **Conceptual links** connect each vocabulary to the concepts. || **The L1 lexicon is assumed to be larger and more strongly connected to relevant concepts**. >> In the model diagram, the L1 box is drawn bigger than the L2 box. >> The conceptual link from L1 to concepts is a thick, solid arrow, while the link from L2 to concepts is a thin, dashed line. || **The L2 lexicon is assumed to have strong forward connections to the L1 lexicon**. >> The lexical link from L2 to L1 is drawn as a solid arrow, while the link from L1 to L2 is dashed. >> **Activation can spread more readily from an L2 word to an L1 word than the other way**. || **This occurs because this direction is emphasized in learning**. >> People usually learn each new L2 word by connecting it to its L1 translation, which builds a strong route from L2 words to L1 words. >> The link from L2 words directly to concepts stays weaker, at least early in learning. As proficiency grows, the L2 conceptual links can strengthen.
21|The **Revised Hierarchical Model of Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** can explain the Marian and Spivey results. Consider what happens in the model during the task in **L2**, with the target “speaker.” >> **The concept for “matches” partially activates the Russian word for matches**, spichki, because L1 words are strongly connected to their concepts. >> Spichki is also partly activated by the sounds of the English word **speaker**, since the two words begin alike. >> **This activity feeds back to the conceptual activation for “matches.”** >> **Conceptual activation for matches leads to eye movements toward the L1 competitor**, the picture of matches. || The link between speaker and spichki is only **sound overlap**. >> Speakers and matches share no meaning. >> The extra activation of spichki strengthens the concept of matches, which draws the eyes to that picture. || **When the task is done in L1**, **the conceptual links with L2 might be too weak to create this kind of interference reliably**. >> In the Russian version, an English competitor would need strong links from the English word to its concept. >> In the model, L2 words have only weak conceptual links, too weak to drive looks to their objects. >> The asymmetry in looks follows directly from the asymmetry in links.
22|The Revised Hierarchical Model makes a **counter-intuitive prediction** even for **“balanced bilinguals.”** A **balanced bilingual** is a person who is about equally skilled in both languages. >> One might expect balanced bilinguals to work like **“two monolinguals in one,”** with each language running separately. >> The model predicts the opposite. || In a balanced bilingual, **the conceptual links are similarly strong for both languages**. >> Every concept strongly activates its word in both languages, so both vocabularies become active whenever either is used. >> **Competitive dynamics and inhibition will be engaged all the time**. **Competitive dynamics** means that the two languages’ words constantly compete for selection. **Inhibition** is the suppression of the word from the language not being used. || This prediction **has led to a lot of work on bilinguals’ advantages in nonlinguistic cognitive control tasks**. >> **Cognitive control** is the ability to manage attention and suppress irrelevant responses. >> If bilinguals constantly practice selecting one language and suppressing the other, they might become better at controlling attention in general, including in tasks that have nothing to do with language.
23|The usual sequence of second-language learning runs along a timeline. >> A person **begins learning L1**. >> Later, the person **learns L2**. >> At the same time, the person **continues learning and using L1**. Both languages are kept in use from then on. || **What happens when someone stops using L1 after L2 is introduced?** >> In the second timeline, the person begins learning L1 and later learns L2, but “continue learning/using L1” is crossed out. >> L1 is no longer heard or used, while L2 takes over. || This situation separates two factors that usually go together, learning a language early and continuing to use it. >> If early learning leaves a permanent foundation, traces of L1 should remain even without use. >> If continued use is needed, L1 might fade or disappear.
24|**Pallier, Dehaene, Poline, LeBihan, Argenti, Dupoux and Mehler** studied **brain imaging of language plasticity in adopted adults**, asking **whether a second language can replace the first**. >> **Adults aged 20 to 32** who **had been adopted into French homes from Korean orphanages between the ages of 3 and 8** were tested. >> These adults had learned Korean as young children but then moved into homes where only French was spoken. >> They stopped using Korean entirely after adoption, so they are a natural test of the second timeline. || The researchers tested whether the adoptees **had any residual knowledge of Korean**. **Residual knowledge** is any remaining trace of the earlier language. >> Monolingual French speakers with no Korean experience served as a comparison group. >> If the adoptees did better than the French speakers on Korean-related tasks, some Korean knowledge would have survived. Any surviving knowledge would reveal what early learning leaves behind when a language is no longer used.
25|Korean has a **three-way stop contrast**. A **stop** is a consonant made by fully blocking the airflow and then releasing it, such as /p/, /t/ or /k/. >> English and French distinguish two kinds of stops at each place, such as /b/ and /p/. >> Korean distinguishes three kinds at each place, which are **aspirated**, **plain** and **tense**. An **aspirated** stop is released with a strong puff of air. A **plain** stop has a lighter release. A **tense** stop is produced with a stiffened, tightened throat. || The contrast appears in three series, recorded in the UCLA Phonetics Archive. >> The **bilabial** series, made with the lips, has **pʰul**, “grass,” **pul**, “fire,” and **p’ul**, “horn.” >> The **dental** series, made with the tongue at the teeth, has **tʰal**, “mask,” **tal**, “moon,” and **t’al**, “daughter.” >> The **velar** series, made with the back of the tongue, has **kʰo**, “nose,” **kok**, “music,” and **k’ok**, “exactly.” || In each series, the three words are made at the same place in the mouth and differ only in the type of stop. >> French speakers have no such contrast, so they tend to hear the three Korean sounds as two, while a Korean speaker hears all three as different. >> Adoptees who kept any Korean sound knowledge should hear the differences better than French speakers.
26|**Ventureyra and colleagues** found that **Korean adoptees in France were no better than monolingual French speakers** at discriminating the Korean contrasts. The graph plots the percentage of **“different” responses** for **adoptees**, **French native speakers** and **Korean native speakers**. >> **DC1** is the **plain–tense** contrast for p and k. Korean speakers responded “different” on about 96% of trials, while adoptees scored about 8% and French speakers about 3%. >> **DC2** is the **plain–aspirated** contrast for p and k. Korean speakers scored about 90%, adoptees about 19% and French speakers about 26%. >> **DC3** is the **tense–aspirated** contrast for p and k. Korean speakers scored about 95%, adoptees about 23% and French speakers about 20%. >> **DS** is the **plain–tense** contrast for s. Korean speakers scored about 79%, adoptees about 26% and French speakers about 23%. || Two control conditions checked general performance. >> **DV** is a **vowel** contrast. All three groups said “different” on about 97 to 99% of trials. >> **P** is the **same** condition, where the two sounds were identical. All groups rarely said “different,” at about 3 to 13%. || The adoptees could do the task, as their near-perfect vowel discrimination and correct “same” responses confirm. >> The difficulty was specific to the Korean stop contrasts. >> On those contrasts, the adoptees heard the differences at the same level as people who had never heard Korean, far below Korean speakers.
27|In **a more extensive study**, **Pallier and colleagues** found that **adoptees were no better than monolingual French speakers** on three tasks, each tapping a different kind of knowledge. >> **(a) Distinguishing Korean from a collection of largely unrelated languages**, which tapped familiarity with the overall sound of Korean. Participants rated on a scale of 1 to 7 how much each sentence sounded like Korean. Both groups rated Korean sentences around 4, but they also rated **Japanese** sentences around 4 to 5. **Polish** sentences got about 2, and **Swedish** and **Wolof** sentences got about 2.5 to 3. >> **(b) Identifying the correct Korean translation of French words**, which tapped memory for vocabulary. Adoptees scored about 55% correct and French speakers about 50%, close to chance for both. >> **(c) Determining whether a short excerpt was repeated from a recorded sentence they had just heard**, which tapped sensitivity to sound sequences. Both groups detected segments in French sentences about 95% of the time. For **Korean**, **Japanese** and **Polish** sentences, both groups scored about 80%, with no adoptee advantage for Korean. || **These are not standard psycholinguistic tasks**. >> Pallier and colleagues **were looking for any remaining trace of Korean**, using tasks designed to catch even weak knowledge. >> They **did not find any**. On every measure, the adoptees looked like people who had never learned Korean.
28|Pallier and colleagues also used brain imaging. **Listening to French activated the classical language network, including superior temporal and inferior frontal regions**, in both groups. >> The **superior temporal** region is the upper part of the temporal lobe, involved in processing speech sounds. >> The **inferior frontal** region is the lower part of the frontal lobe, including Broca’s area. || The comparisons subtracted brain activity for other languages from activity for French. >> **(A) French minus Polish**, **(B) French minus Japanese** and **(C) French minus Korean** were computed for the **French group** and the **Korean group**, the adoptees. >> In every comparison, French produced more activity in the language network than the foreign language did. || **Subtracting Korean from French might have revealed some difference between groups**, if there were **any residual processing ability for Korean left**. >> If adoptees still processed Korean as a language, Korean would activate their language network more than it did for French speakers, and the French-minus-Korean difference would be smaller. >> **It was not**. The adoptees’ brains responded to Korean like a foreign language, much as they responded to Polish and Japanese. The imaging results agree with the behavioral results.
29|**L2–L1 interactions** can also be understood in terms of **competitive dynamics**. >> **L1 can be thought of as a set of connections within and between different brain regions**. >> These connections are built and maintained through use. || The same process that occurs in **monocular deprivation** in vision could apply to language. >> In monocular deprivation in mice, after **2–3 days** the deprived eye’s connections show **increased spine motility**. After **3–4 days**, there is **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration**, the breakdown of cut-off nerve fibers. After **more than a week**, the other eye’s fibers show **axon sprouting and new spine birth**, taking over the lost space. >> The eye that keeps receiving input gains control of connections that the deprived eye loses. || **If the L1 connections are never activated again, they could be “taken over” by L2**. >> L2 receives constant use, so its connections strengthen and spread. >> L1 receives no use, so its connections weaken and can be claimed by L2. >> On this view, L2 replaces L1 through competition for the same neural resources.
30|The answer to whether **a second language can replace the first** appears to be **“Apparently yes!”** The adoptees showed no behavioral or brain evidence of their childhood Korean. >> As far as these tests could measure, French had completely replaced Korean. || A remaining question is whether **traces of L1 are left behind that can be “salvaged.”** >> **Salvaging** a trace would mean recovering or using some leftover effect of the earlier language. >> A trace might not show up on a test of current ability but could still make relearning easier. || Testing current recognition and testing later learning ask different questions. >> One asks what the person can do with the language now. >> The other asks whether new learning benefits from something left over from the past, and relearning is the measure that can reveal such a trace.
31|**Terry Au and colleagues** at UCLA studied this question in **“Overhearing a language during childhood,”** by **Au, Knightly, Jun and Oh**. >> They studied **undergraduate students who overheard Spanish frequently as children but never learned to speak it**. >> **All participants started classroom instruction of Spanish at around age 14** and **were tested at the end of a second-year college Spanish course**. || The table compares **native speakers**, **childhood overhearers** and **typical late L2 learners** on several measures. >> **Childhood slang**, in percent correct. For **production**, native speakers scored 79, overhearers 17 and late learners 3. For **comprehension**, 88, 22 and 3. >> **Voice onset time** in milliseconds. For **word-initial voiceless stops**, native speakers had 19.3, overhearers 19.6 and late learners 36.2. For **word-medial voiceless stops**, 22.4, 23.4 and 31.2. >> **Percentage lenited**. **Lenition** is a softening of a consonant, which Spanish applies to voiced stops between vowels. For **word-initial voiced stops**, native speakers lenited 43%, overhearers 24% and late learners 1%. For **word-medial voiced stops**, 72%, 63% and 23%. >> **Accent rating** on a 5-point scale, where higher means more native-like. For **voiceless stops**, native speakers scored 4.3, overhearers 3.5 and late learners 2.2. For **voiced stops**, 4.4, 3.3 and 2.7. || Other measures showed little or no overhearer advantage. >> **Morphology production**. For **gender agreement**, native speakers scored 93%, overhearers 70% and late learners 68%. For **number agreement**, 92%, 88% and 85%. >> **Grammaticality judgment**. **Accuracy** was 94% for native speakers, 64% for overhearers and 63% for late learners. **Reaction time** was 1,042 milliseconds for native speakers, 2,475 for overhearers and 2,209 for late learners. || Childhood overhearing gave a clear advantage in **pronunciation** but not in grammar. >> Early listening left traces in the sound system that were unmasked when the students later studied Spanish, while grammar seems to need more than overheard input.
32|Taking **the data from Korean adoptees together with the data from overhearers**, a picture emerges. >> **If L1 is removed from the environment entirely, L2 can appear to completely replace it**. The adoptees showed no trace of Korean on any test of current ability. >> **But this replacement is not necessarily complete**. **There may be some residual knowledge that can be “unmasked” with later learning**, as with the Spanish overhearers’ pronunciation. || The two populations also differ in **potentially important ways**. >> The adoptees were tested on their current knowledge without any new Korean instruction. >> The overhearers were tested after two years of Spanish classes, which gave hidden knowledge a chance to show itself. >> These and other differences between the groups make the two sets of results hard to compare directly.
33|Several **potentially important differences between these populations** could explain why one group showed traces of early language and the other did not. >> **Korean is rarely heard or encountered in France**. **Spanish is common in Los Angeles**, so overhearers may have kept hearing it throughout their lives, which could have kept their Spanish sound knowledge active. >> A **“heritage language”** spoken by other family members differs from **a language not spoken by anyone you know**. The overhearers often heard Spanish from relatives, while the adoptees lost all contact with Korean speakers. >> **Cognates** are common between Spanish and English but rare between Korean and French. Shared vocabulary could help overhearers relearn. >> **Shared writing system**. Spanish and English use the same alphabet, while Korean uses a different script from French. || **This is a general issue** with research on **second language acquisition and bilingualism**. >> **Languages and learning situations differ in many, many ways that are hard, perhaps impossible, to control experimentally**. >> Researchers cannot assign people to grow up hearing particular languages in particular settings. >> Untangling these factors would require comparing populations that differ in only one way, which is rarely possible with real language histories.
