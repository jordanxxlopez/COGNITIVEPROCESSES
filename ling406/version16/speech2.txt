2|**Klatt (1979)** describes a very old and very general model of speech perception. It treats recognition as a series of stages, and each stage transforms the output of the stage before it. >> The **speech waveform** enters the **peripheral auditory system**, which performs a **spectral analysis**. The **cochlea** in the inner ear breaks the incoming sound into its frequency components. >> **Acoustic property detectors** respond to particular properties of the sound, and their outputs change over time. >> **Phonetic feature detectors** use those outputs to estimate the probability of features such as voicing at each moment. >> **Segmental analysis** combines the features into a sequence of speech segments, called a **segmental feature matrix**. >> **Lexical search** compares that sequence with stored words and produces **lexical hypotheses**, which are candidate words that might match the input. || Categorical perception fits this picture. Somewhere between the **analog signal** coming through the cochlea and the **activation of phonemes** during segmental analysis, **information is lost**. Many physically different sounds end up as the same phoneme. >> This **loss of information is helpful**, because a shared phoneme sequence can be matched directly against stored words at higher levels of representation. >> One assumption that follows is that **in order to identify words, we need to first identify their constituent phonemes**. || Two problems challenge this simple view. >> The first is **cascaded processing**, in which partial information is passed forward to the next level before the earlier decision is finished. >> The second is **feedback**, in which a higher level, such as the word level, sends influence back down to a lower level, such as the phoneme level.
3|The **beach–peach** continuum is a familiar **voicing continuum**. The words differ in their first consonant, /b/ in beach and /p/ in peach, and these consonants differ mainly in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**. VOT is the delay between the release of the lips and the start of vocal fold vibration. >> The spectrograms run from **“peach”** to **“beach”** along an arrow labeled **shorter voice onset time**. >> As VOT gets shorter, the gap between the release and the voicing bars shrinks. A long gap sounds like peach, and a short gap sounds like beach. || In the **identification** results, a listener labels stimuli 1 through 5 consistently as one word. >> At stimulus 6, responses become mixed, with the proportion near 0.33, and this point is circled. >> From stimulus 7 onward, the listener consistently gives the other word. >> The **category boundary** is the narrow region where the preferred label switches. || In the **discrimination** results, accuracy is near 0 for pairs inside one category. >> Accuracy jumps to 1 for pairs **4–6** and **5–7**, which straddle the identification boundary. >> Pair 6–8 is intermediate, near 0.33, and accuracy falls back to 0 for pair 7–9. >> Equal changes in VOT are much easier to detect across the boundary, where they change the word, than inside one category, where the word stays the same.
4|**How does categorical perception happen?** One answer is a **threshold model**. In a threshold model, **activity is only passed forward from one level to the next when an activity threshold is crossed**. >> A **threshold** is a set level of activity that must be reached before anything is sent onward. >> Activity below the threshold can change without any effect on the next level. || In this kind of model, **categorical perception arises at phoneme identification**. >> The **feature activation** graph plots voicing evidence over time. It swings up and down between a **+voiced** threshold line and a **−voiced** threshold line. >> The **phoneme activation** graph stays flat at zero for /b/ and /p/ while the feature activity is still below threshold. >> Only when +voiced activity finally crosses its threshold does **/b/** activation jump up abruptly at the end. || The circled point in the beach–peach identification graph is the ambiguous stimulus with mixed responses. Under a threshold model, that stimulus either crosses a threshold or does not. >> The word level receives a finished /b/ or a finished /p/. The history of uncertainty stays behind at the feature level. >> Two acoustically different inputs that both end up as /b/ become identical by the time they reach word recognition. >> This all-or-none switch would produce categorical perception directly at the phoneme level.
5|A different answer is a **cascaded model**. In a cascaded model, **activity is passed forward continuously over time from one level to the next**. The next level responds to whatever activity is present at each moment, while the decision is still forming. || In the **feature activation** and **phoneme activation** graphs for this model, both curves swing up and down together. >> As +voiced evidence rises, **/b/** activation rises. >> As the evidence moves toward −voiced, activation shifts toward **/p/**. >> The phoneme level mirrors the feature level continuously. || In this kind of model, **categorical perception might happen later on, or might even be an artifact of the task structure**. An **artifact** is a result produced by the way a measurement is taken rather than by the process being studied. >> In an identification task, a listener must click either beach or peach, even if both words were partly active. >> The answer is categorical because the response options are categorical. >> Two sounds that both end up labeled /b/ can still differ in how much support they gave /p/ along the way. || When the voicing evidence is mixed, both /b/ and /p/ receive some activation in proportion to the evidence for each. >> Graded activation at the phoneme level passes graded activation to words, so a sound near the boundary partly activates both beach and peach. >> The processing before the final choice still carries fine-grained information about how ambiguous the sound was.
6|**Making furniture** is a metaphor for **thresholded activity**. Each operation takes a specific input and turns it into a specific output. >> A **lumber mill** takes **trees** as input and produces **lumber** as output. >> A **furniture factory** takes **lumber** as input and produces **unassembled furniture** as output. >> An **IKEA customer** takes **unassembled furniture** as input and produces finished **furniture** as output. || Each step depends on receiving a completed product in the form it can use. Trees become lumber before any furniture is made, and lumber becomes furniture parts before any assembly begins. >> **Thresholded processing** has the same dependence. A higher level waits until the lower level supplies the required representation. >> Information moves forward only as a completed product.
7|In the furniture metaphor, each **“layer”** of the system **can only take a specific input that has been transformed completely by the previous layer**. We have to wait for the shipment of lumber before manufacturing can start. Many computer programs work this way. >> Each stage performs its own transformation and passes on only its finished output. >> Activity at a lower stage can keep changing while the higher stage sits idle, waiting for a finished product. || In speech recognition, a strict threshold account makes **word processing depend on an identified phoneme**. >> A partly resolved acoustic signal stays at the lower level until it is identified as a phoneme. >> The word level receives only the completed segment. || This arrangement also determines **what information is lost**. >> If different sounds are converted into the same phoneme, their earlier differences never reach word recognition. >> This is useful for treating varied sounds alike. The cost is that later stages never see the uncertainty that earlier stages resolved.
8|A **food web** is a metaphor for **cascaded processing**. In a food web, the **“levels” continuously interact with one another**. The desert food web links **desert plants**, **insects**, **kangaroo rats**, **small lizards**, **large lizards**, **scorpions**, **snakes**, **desert foxes** and **hawks** through arrows marking who eats whom. >> A drought that reduces the number of plants **immediately** affects the health of kangaroo rats. >> The change in kangaroo rats then affects the snakes that eat them, and so on up the web. >> No population waits for the level below it to reach a finished state before being affected. || **Cascaded processing** has the same continuous dependence. >> Changing feature evidence changes phoneme activity as it happens. >> Changing phoneme activity in turn changes support for words. Each level responds to partial information as it arrives. || The food web also contains clear **feedback loops**. Adding more hawks affects the populations of snakes, lizards and rats, and those changes then affect the hawks’ own food supply. >> In a recognition model, **feedback** means influence that travels from a higher level back down to a lower level. >> Cascading and feedback are different ideas. Cascading passes developing evidence forward, while feedback lets activity at a higher level change an earlier representation.
9|The central question is whether **phoneme identification for word recognition** is more like **thresholded processing**, as in the lumber mill, or **cascaded processing**, as in the food web. >> In a **thresholded account**, word recognition begins only after a phoneme decision is finished. >> In a **cascaded account**, a word can gain support while the phoneme is still uncertain. || The difference concerns how much of the developing acoustic evidence reaches candidate words. >> Two sounds can both end up labeled **/b/** while differing in how much they resemble **/p/**. >> A thresholded system reduces both to the same finished /b/, so pear should not compete with bear. >> A cascaded system passes along more /p/ support for the more ambiguous sound, so pear should compete with bear for a while. || Both accounts can predict the same final word. They differ in what happens before the final choice, so the test that separates them measures temporary support for a competing word during recognition.
10|**McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)** tested this question in a study titled **probabilistic constraint satisfaction at the lexical/phonetic interface**, published in the Journal of Psycholinguistic Research. They looked for **gradient effects of within-category VOT on lexical access**. **Lexical access** is the retrieval of a word from memory during recognition. >> **Gradient** means that an effect changes by degrees. >> **Within-category VOT** refers to differences in voice onset time between sounds that are all heard as the same phoneme. || The study used identification of a **/b/–/p/ continuum** in a **“visual world” display** for **eye-tracking**. Listeners hear speech while looking at a display of pictures, and a camera records where their eyes move. >> Each display contained a **target**, such as **pear**, a **competitor**, such as **bear**, and two **filler** items, a **lamp** and a **ship**. >> The target and competitor differ only in their first consonant, and the fillers are unrelated. || Eye movements happen quickly and continuously while a word is being recognized. Listeners look at objects as they think about them, often before they are aware of deciding. >> A listener can eventually choose bear after briefly looking at the pear. >> The number and timing of such looks measure how strongly the competitor was active before the final click.
11|Participants used the **mouse to select the picture** most consistent with what they heard, such as a **“bear”** or a **“pear,”** in the same way as a bear–pear task done at home. >> Each trial presented one sound from the VOT continuum along with the four pictures. >> The click records which word the listener finally settled on. || A **typical, step-like identification function** was observed. The graph plots the proportion of /p/ responses against VOT from 0 to 40 milliseconds. >> For VOTs from 0 to about 15 milliseconds, /p/ responses stay near 0. >> Between about 15 and 20 milliseconds, /p/ responses jump up steeply. >> From about 20 milliseconds onward, /p/ responses stay near 1. >> The **category boundary** falls at **17.5 milliseconds**, plus or minus 0.83 milliseconds. || The final choices group acoustically different sounds together. Several VOTs on the short side all produce bear, and several on the long side all produce pear. >> Taken alone, this sharp curve would seem to support the threshold model. >> A mouse click records only the word chosen at the end of recognition. Eye movements record which other words received support before that choice, so a sharp identification function can sit alongside gradual differences in competition.
12|**Eye movements reveal that ambiguity is gradient**. There are **more looks to the competitor close to the boundary**. Gradient ambiguity means that uncertainty varies by degree. >> The analysis holds the final response constant. It compares only trials where the listener clicked bear, or only trials where the listener clicked pear. >> Every trial in one graph ended with the same click, so differences in competitor looks cannot come from listeners changing their answer. || The left graph covers trials where the **response was bear** and plots the proportion of fixations to the **pear** competitor over time. Separate lines show VOTs of **0, 5, 10 and 15 milliseconds**. >> Competitor looks rise after the word begins, peak around 400 to 600 milliseconds, and then decline as bear wins. >> The lines for VOTs closer to the boundary, such as 15 milliseconds, sit higher and stay elevated longer than the line for 0 milliseconds. || The right graph covers trials where the **response was pear** and plots fixations to the **bear** competitor. Separate lines show VOTs of **20, 25, 30, 35 and 40 milliseconds**. >> The VOTs closest to the boundary, such as 20 milliseconds, produce the most looks to bear. >> The clearest pear sounds, at 40 milliseconds, produce the fewest. || A clear gradient effect of VOT appears in both graphs. The effect is mainly on the **duration of activation**, meaning how long the competitor stays active. >> Two sounds that are both accepted as bear can still produce different amounts of looking at the pear. >> The recognition system keeps track of how ambiguous each sound was, information that a threshold model would throw away.
13|The effect is clearer when the competitor looks are plotted against **VOT** directly. The graph has fixation proportion to the competitor on the vertical axis and VOT from 0 to 40 milliseconds on the horizontal axis, with the **category boundary** marked in the middle. >> To the left of the boundary, the response is bear, and the curves track **looks to pear**. >> To the right of the boundary, the response is pear, and the curves track **looks to bear**. || Two time windows are plotted separately. >> The **early** window, drawn with a dotted line, covers **300–1100 milliseconds** after word onset. >> The **late** window, drawn with a solid line, covers **1100–1900 milliseconds** after word onset. || In both windows, competitor looks **increase as VOT approaches the boundary from either side**. >> On the bear side, late looks to pear rise from about 0.02 at 0 milliseconds to about 0.045 near 15 milliseconds. >> On the pear side, late looks to bear are highest near 20 milliseconds, around 0.04, and fall to about 0.01 by 40 milliseconds. >> Early looks are higher overall, but they follow the same pattern of peaking near the boundary. The rise is gradual on both sides. || Because the effect appears in both windows, uncertainty about the first sound influences looking throughout recognition. A sound can support the eventual winner while still supporting its rival, and the closer the sound is to the boundary, the stronger that rival support is.
14|This finding fits the idea that **activation flows in a cascaded way from features to phonemes**. Changing acoustic features affect phonemes, and changing phoneme activity affects words, all while recognition is still going on. >> **Even stimuli identified as /b/ 100% of the time can vary in how strongly and unambiguously the “voiced” feature is activated**. >> A clear /b/ strongly activates voicing, while a /b/ near the boundary activates voicing only weakly. || **/b/ and /p/** share most of their features. Both are **bilabial**, which means the two lips form the closure, and both are **stops**, which means the airflow is completely blocked and then released. >> If a stimulus is obviously **+bilabial** and **+stop**, those features support /b/ and /p/ equally. >> Only voicing separates them, so weak voicing evidence creates **momentary ambiguity between /b/ and /p/**. >> That ambiguity appears in the **eye movements** while the participant decides where to click. || The **TRACE** model represents this kind of cascade with three layers. >> The **feature layer** receives a **pseudo-spectral input representation**, a simplified stand-in for the sound’s frequency content over time. Its detectors are POW, VOC, DIFF, ACU, GRD, VOI and BUR, which stand for power, vocalic, diffuseness, acuteness, gradualness of onset, voicing and burst. >> The **phoneme layer** has units for phonemes such as /b/, /p/, /r/ and /t/, copied at every possible position in time. >> The **word layer** has units for words, also copied across time positions. >> Arrows pass activity upward from features to phonemes and from phonemes to words. Within each layer, units compete with each other through inhibition.
15|There is evidence that **partial activation can reach all the way to the lexical level**, the level of stored words and their meanings. **Andruski and colleagues (1994)** found that **semantic priming** can occur even with **nonwords**. Semantic priming is the speed-up in responding to a word after hearing a word related in meaning. >> **“Beace”** primes **“war.”** Beace is peace with a voiced first consonant, and peace is related in meaning to war. >> **“Ging”** primes **“queen,”** because ging is king with a voiced first consonant. >> In the **word-to-word** items, altering the voicing of the first consonant of one real word produces another real word, as in **“pet”** becoming **“bet.”** One such item is **“bill”** priming **“drug,”** because bill is an altered form of pill. || Priming was measured with a lexical decision task, in which participants decide as quickly as possible whether a target is a real word. Primes were **unaltered**, altered by reducing VOT by **one third**, altered by **two thirds**, or **unrelated** to the target. >> Black bars give mean reaction times for word-to-word items, and shaded bars give them for word-to-nonword items. >> Faster responses than in the unrelated condition indicate priming. || Mean reaction times in milliseconds reveal the priming. >> Unrelated primes produce the slowest responses, near 950 to 970 milliseconds. >> Unaltered primes produce much faster responses, around 810 to 860 milliseconds. >> With one third of the VOT removed, responses stay fast, around 825 to 855 milliseconds. >> With two thirds removed, word-to-nonword items still stay near 820 milliseconds, while word-to-word items slow to about 890 milliseconds. || **Lexical representations can be activated even by incomplete or imperfect input**. Beace never becomes a real word, but it matches peace closely enough to partly activate it, and that activation spreads to meaning and speeds recognition of war. This fits cascaded processing.
16|The bear–pear eye-tracking result is one piece of evidence for **cascaded processing** in speech perception. It would be hard to explain why **the number of looks to the other member of the minimal pair increases gradually as you approach the category boundary** in a thresholded model. A **minimal pair** is two words that differ by only one sound, such as bear and pear. >> The competitor looks rise steadily as VOT approaches the boundary. >> The effect holds even when the final response is the same, so it comes from graded activation within one category. || In the threshold diagram, the arrow from **feature activation** to **phoneme activation** is crossed out with a red X. >> The feature activation keeps swinging between +voiced and −voiced. >> The phoneme activation stays flat until /b/ suddenly jumps at the end. >> No graded information passes to the phoneme level before the threshold is crossed. || In a threshold-only account, every token assigned to a category sends the **same finished phoneme** to the word level. >> Word-level competition should then be the same for every token of /b/, with no varying input to produce gradually changing competitor looks. >> A cascaded account explains the effect directly. More support for the competing phoneme passes along more support for its word.
17|The **TRACE** model also incorporates **feedback from the lexical layer to the phoneme layer**. In the TRACE diagram, arrows run up from the phoneme layer to the word layer and back down from the word layer to the phoneme layer. >> **Lexical feedback** means that an activated word sends support back to its own phonemes. >> An uncertain sound can partly activate a word, and that word can then strengthen the interpretation of the sound that fits it. || Influence can therefore run in both directions. >> Acoustic evidence changes which words are active. >> Active words change the balance between possible phonemes while the phoneme decision is still forming. >> This helps listeners recover from noisy or ambiguous input. || **Feedback is different from cascading**. >> Cascading passes incomplete evidence forward from features to phonemes and from phonemes to words. >> Feedback sends influence from words back down to phonemes. >> An interactive model can include both. Findings in which knowledge of words changes how sounds are heard, such as the Ganong effect, are what motivate adding feedback.
18|**The Ganong effect** is named after **Ganong (1980)**, who looked at how **phoneme identification is affected by word knowledge**. The heading “Gisses and kifts for Valentine’s day” plays on the effect by swapping the voicing of the first consonants in “kisses and gifts.” >> The question is whether knowing which sound sequences form real words changes how an ambiguous sound is heard. || Ganong created **word/nonword pairs** such as **“corpse/gorpse.”** One end of the continuum is a real word and the other end is a nonword. >> In corpse, the /k/ interpretation produces a familiar word. >> In gorpse, the /g/ interpretation produces something that is not a word. >> He built continua spanning the relevant phoneme boundary in **5-millisecond steps** of VOT. || The design combines **uncertain phonetic evidence** with a **difference in lexical status**. >> The rest of the word, “-orpse,” is the same at every step and tells the listener which beginning would complete a real word. >> If lexical knowledge affects identification, an ambiguous first consonant should more often be heard as the sound that makes a real word. >> The important test is in the ambiguous middle of the continuum, where word knowledge has the most room to act.
19|One possibility is a **late “correction” process**. Under this account, **categorical perception works as usual, but there is a bias to report having heard a word**. >> The listener first categorizes the sound with no help from word knowledge. >> At the response stage, the listener leans toward saying the option that forms a real word. || This account predicts a **general reporting bias**. The prediction graphs plot the **proportion of voiced responses** across the continuum from short VOT to long VOT, with the boundary in the middle at −7.5, −2.5, +2.5 and +7.5 milliseconds. >> Three conditions are compared. In **word–nonword**, the voiced end forms a word. In **neutral**, neither end has a lexical advantage. In **nonword–word**, the voiceless end forms a word. >> The word–nonword curve is shifted up by a constant amount everywhere, including the long VOT end, where it stays near 0.25 instead of falling to 0. >> The nonword–word curve is shifted down at the short VOT end, starting near 0.75 instead of 1. || The top graph plots the **lexical effect**, which is the difference between the two lexical conditions at each step. Under this account, it is a flat line near **0.25** across the whole continuum. >> A reporting bias added after categorization favors the word equally at every point, with the same size at clear and ambiguous steps. >> Perception and knowledge stay separate, and word knowledge only nudges the reported response.
20|A second possibility is that **word knowledge shifts the categorical boundary**. Under this account, word knowledge changes where the listener switches from one phoneme category to the other. >> Near an ambiguous onset, the interpretation that forms a real word gets extra support. >> The effect changes categorization itself. || In the prediction graphs, the word–nonword, neutral and nonword–word curves all start near 1 at short VOT and end near 0 at long VOT. >> The curves separate only in the middle, near the boundary. >> The word–nonword curve crosses the boundary later, and the nonword–word curve crosses earlier. || The **lexical effect** graph forms a peak. It is near 0 at the endpoints, rises to about **0.35** for the items closest to the boundary, and falls again. >> The lexical effect is **strongest for items closest to the category boundary**, because those are the stimuli for which lexical information can help resolve ambiguity about the first phoneme. >> At the endpoints, strong acoustic evidence already settles the consonant, leaving little room for word knowledge to help. || The word ending provides the same lexical advantage at every step, but the onset is not equally uncertain at every step. Where the effect occurs therefore matters. A flat effect points to a reporting bias, while a peaked effect points to a shift in the boundary itself.
21|**Ganong’s results** are strikingly consistent with the **second model**, the boundary-shift account. >> The proportion of voiced responses for **word–nonword** and **nonword–word** conditions starts near 1 at short VOT and falls toward 0 at long VOT. >> The two curves separate most in the middle, near the boundary. >> The **lexical effect** graph rises from about 0.1 at the endpoints to a peak near **0.3** at the boundary and then falls back. || The peaked effect rules out a simple reporting bias. Word knowledge matters most where the acoustic evidence is weakest. >> At the boundary, the word that fits the context tips the balance. >> At the endpoints, the acoustic evidence decides the answer on its own. || Ganong raised a number of possibilities for how this could happen, focusing on different kinds of **guessing strategies**. >> A guessing strategy would let listeners use word knowledge only when they were unsure about the sound. || Other models emphasize the potential role of **feedback** in these shifts. >> In **interactive models**, an activated word sends support back to its own phonemes. >> The whole-word representation can therefore affect how an ambiguous part of the word is interpreted during recognition.
22|A **simulation of the Ganong effect in the TRACE model** tracks **phoneme-level and word-level activations** at several points as a segment unfolds. The input begins with a sound **ambiguous between /b/ and /p/**, followed by **/l/**, the vowel **/ʌ/**, and **/g/**, which together make the word **plug**. >> The figure has four time frames from left to right. Each frame has word activations in the upper panel and phoneme activations in the lower panel. || In the **first frame**, only the ambiguous first sound has arrived. >> **/b/** and **/p/** are both weakly active at about the same level, near 0.15 to 0.2. >> Word activation is still very low. || In the **second and third frames**, /l/ and the vowel arrive. >> Partial /b/ evidence supports words beginning with **bl-**, and partial /p/ evidence supports words beginning with **pl-**. >> Several candidates gain activation together, including **plug**, **blood** and **blush**, which all fit the beginning heard so far. >> Phoneme activity for /p/ starts to pull ahead of /b/. || In the **fourth frame**, the final **/g/** arrives. >> /g/ fits plug but does not fit the endings of blood or blush. >> **Plug** rises to the top of the word panel, at about 0.45, while the alternatives fall away. >> /p/ ends up more active than /b/ at the phoneme level.
23|As the input unfolds, **activity accumulates for “plug,”** especially when the **final /g/** arrives. The /g/ is highly inconsistent with **“blood”** and **“blush,”** which were also activated in the third frame. >> Blood and blush fit the early part of the input, so they were plausible candidates for a while. >> Once /g/ arrives, they lose support because their endings do not match. || TRACE therefore explains **word effects on phoneme category boundaries** in terms of **top-down processing**. Top-down processing means that higher-level knowledge influences lower-level analysis. >> **Activity at the lexical level produces feedback to the phoneme level**. >> The feedback gives **greater activation to the phoneme consistent with a word**. Because plug is the winning word, /p/ receives extra support. || The ambiguous beginning becomes less ambiguous because /p/ belongs to the word supported by the full sequence. >> Information that arrives at the end of a word can reach back and shape how an earlier sound is heard. >> The effect happens through interactions during recognition, so the ambiguous first sound comes to be heard as /p/.
24|The **TRACE** model is a version of the **Interactive Activation model** for **written word recognition**, from McClelland and Rumelhart (1981). Many of the principles are the same in both models. >> In the Interactive Activation network, **feature** units at the bottom connect to **letter** units, and letter units connect to **word** units such as **WEAR, WORK, WORD** and **WEAK**. >> Compatible units at different levels support each other, and competing units at the same level inhibit each other. >> Activated words send support back down to their own letters. || A hypothetical set of features extracted on a trial of a word perception experiment gives the letters **W, O** and **R** clearly, while ink blots cover part of the last letter. >> The visible features of the last letter are consistent with both **R** and **K**. >> W, O and R activate words such as WORK and WORD. Only WORK fits the visible features of the last letter, so WORK becomes the strongest word. >> WORK sends support back to **K**, so the ambiguous letter is perceived as K. This parallels plug supporting /p/ in speech. || A key issue in speech perception is that **the stimuli unfold over time**. >> A written word is all on the page at once, but a spoken word arrives one sound after another. >> TRACE handles this by copying feature, phoneme and word units at every possible position in time. A candidate can fit the beginning, gain activation, and then weaken when the ending conflicts.
25|**Categorical perception** shapes perception through **category boundaries**. **Given the same physical difference, it is harder to distinguish stimuli from the same category than from different categories**. Equal acoustic steps have different perceptual effects depending on whether they cross a boundary. || However, **ambiguity about phoneme identity does have an impact on word recognition**. >> Sounds assigned the same label still differ in how strongly they activate a competing word. >> The word level keeps information about how uncertain each token was, so labels can be categorical even when the processing behind them is graded. || In addition, **word-level information seems to have an impact on phoneme categorization**. >> In the Ganong effect, word knowledge shifts the category boundary. >> In TRACE, this happens through lexical feedback to the phoneme level. || It therefore **cannot be the case that the word recognition system just sits there waiting for the phoneme recognition system to finish its job**, the way a furniture factory waits for a lumber mill. >> **Spoken word recognition is more interactive** than this, more like the food web. >> Partial information passes forward, and activated words send support back down, while uncertainty is still being resolved.

27|**Spoken word recognition is also very fast**. **Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** used a visual world eye-tracking task to track how quickly candidate words become active. >> Participants saw a grid of pictures and geometric shapes and followed spoken instructions to move objects. >> The display included a **beaker**, the **referent** or target, a **beetle**, a **speaker** and a **carriage**. || The pictures were chosen for their sound overlap with the target word **beaker**. >> **Beetle** is a **cohort competitor** because it shares the beginning of the word. >> **Speaker** is a **rhyme competitor** because it shares the ending of the word. >> **Carriage** is **unrelated** and shares no sounds with beaker. || The graph plots the **probability of fixating each item type** over the first 1,000 milliseconds after the target word begins. The average target offset, the moment the spoken word ends, falls a little before 400 milliseconds. >> Looks to both **beaker** and **beetle** rise together early, starting around 200 milliseconds, because both fit the beginning. >> Looks to beetle peak near 0.2 around 500 milliseconds and then fall once the distinguishing sounds arrive. >> Looks to beaker keep climbing, passing 0.8 by about 800 milliseconds and approaching 1 by 1,000 milliseconds. >> Looks to **speaker** rise later and stay smaller, peaking near 0.1. Looks to **carriage** stay near zero. || Fixations on the target start rising before the word has even finished. Recognition begins while the word is still unfolding, and competitors that match the input at a given moment become active and then drop out when they stop matching.
28|The eye-tracking data can be compared with a **TRACE simulation** of the same task. Two graphs plot the **fixation probability** or the **predicted fixation probability** from TRACE over 1,000 milliseconds after target onset. >> The left graph compares referent and **cohort** curves from TRACE and from real fixations. >> The right graph compares referent and **rhyme** curves from TRACE and from real fixations. || The TRACE predictions closely match the eye-movement data. >> The **referent** curves from TRACE and from fixations both rise steadily from about 300 milliseconds and approach 1 by 1,000 milliseconds. >> The **cohort** curves rise early and peak near 0.17 to 0.2 around 500 milliseconds, then fall, in both the model and the data. >> The **rhyme** curves rise later and stay small, peaking near 0.1, in both the model and the data. || The **granularity** of the simulation is fine-grained, meaning it predicts activity moment by moment. >> **Eye movements give an idea of the changes in “activity” for different competitors over time**. >> This makes it possible to **see cognitive processing before a final response is given**. || This parallels the **graded effects of category goodness** in categorical perception. Sounds that are better or worse examples of a category produce different amounts of competitor activity, even when the final label is the same.
