2|**Speech perception** is the process of recovering a linguistic message from the sound of speech. Listeners take an acoustic signal that seems like a mess and pull out a message as clear as “I can see you.” >> In the acoustic signal for “I can see you,” the energy of each sound runs into the next, and nothing in the signal cleanly separates letters or even words. >> Real-time MRI of the **vocal tract**, from USC SPAN, reveals why. The tongue, lips and other articulators move without pause, and the movement for one sound overlaps the movement for the next, so their acoustic effects overlap as well. || We also get **the same message from very different physical stimuli**. Voices and vocal tract sizes differ, so one word has different acoustic properties from each speaker, yet listeners hear the same word. >> Across 76 speakers, each of ten vowels covers a broad range of first and second formant values instead of a single point. >> Many of those ranges **overlap**, so one pair of formant values can be one vowel for one speaker and another vowel for a different speaker. || Recognizing speech therefore means finding a **stable linguistic identity** inside enormous physical variation.
3|A working premise is that we must **turn a highly variable, analog signal into something more abstract** that the language system can use. An **analog signal** varies continuously, so frequency, loudness and timing can each shift by tiny amounts. >> A continuous signal has no built-in markers of where one speech sound stops and the next starts. >> Many small changes leave the speech sound the same, so two physically different productions can still be the same sound. || The abstract form is a **symbolic representation**, which identifies a language unit such as a particular consonant or vowel and sets its fine physical detail aside. The unit is the **phoneme**, a sound category that distinguishes words in a language. >> /b/ in “bat” and /p/ in “pat” are different phonemes, because swapping them changes the word. >> Many slightly different acoustic versions of /b/ all count as one phoneme. || **Categorical perception** is thought to reflect this **“translation” from physical to symbolic**. Listeners grow less sensitive to differences inside a category and more sensitive to equally large differences that cross a category boundary. >> A change stands out when it alters the identity the listener gives the sound. >> A change of the same physical size can pass almost unnoticed when both sounds remain the same phoneme. Categorical perception is one account of the translation, and other accounts exist.
4|The **vocal tract** is the passage sound travels through in speech, from the larynx through the throat and mouth to the lips, and its shape changes as the tongue, jaw and lips move. Following Ladefoged (1996), the shape of the vocal tract is related in systematic ways to **which resonant frequencies get amplified**. >> A **resonant frequency** is one that a space naturally strengthens, so a tube of a given shape boosts some frequencies and dampens others. >> Changing the vocal tract’s shape changes which frequencies get the biggest boost. || **Formants** are the resonant frequencies the vocal tract emphasizes, and every vowel has its own combination of them. >> Across **heed, hid, head and had**, the tongue moves from high and front toward low. Heed has its second formant far above its first, and the two draw closer through hid, head and had. >> Across **hod, hawed, hood and who’d**, the tongue moves back and the lips round more. The amplified frequencies gather at the low end, with the first and second formants close together. >> A vowel is defined by the **relationship among its resonances**, which stays similar across speakers even when the exact frequencies differ. || A **formant transition** is a change in a formant’s frequency over time. As the mouth moves from a consonant into a vowel, the resonances glide from one set of values to another. >> The steady part of a vowel carries information about the vowel. >> The moving part carries information about how the neighboring sound is made.
5|A **spectrogram** maps acoustic energy over time and frequency. Each vowel has several bands of strong energy, and these bands are its **formants**. >> The vowel recordings come from Rob Hagiwara’s English vowel samples. || The words **bead, bid, bade, bed, bad, bod, baud, bode, “bood” and booed** all start with /b/ and end with /d/, so the differences between them come mainly from the vowels. >> **Bead** has a very low first formant and a very high second formant, so the two are far apart. >> Through bid, bade, bed and bad, the first formant rises and the second falls, so they move closer together. >> In the back rounded vowels such as **bode, “bood” and booed**, the second formant drops low and sits near the first. || **F1** is the lowest formant and **F2** is the next one up, and both run through the whole vowel at once. >> The pattern of these two lowest formants carries most of what tells vowels apart. >> Bead pairs a low F1 with a high F2, while booed pairs a low F1 with a low F2.
6|**Source-filter synthesis** builds speech sounds from two parts. The **source** is the sound energy the voice produces, and the **filter** is how the vocal tract shapes that energy. Pulling the two apart makes it possible to change one while the other stays fixed. >> The work is done in **Praat**, free speech analysis software from praat.org that displays recordings, measures formants and resynthesizes sounds. || The recording is a speaker saying **/u/**, the vowel in “booed.” A **waveform** records changes in air pressure over time, and a **spectrogram** breaks the same sound into its frequency components. >> The waveform captures timing and loudness. >> The spectrogram captures the resonance pattern. || This /u/ has its **first formant at about 300 Hz** and a **very low second formant, around 900 Hz**. >> Both resonances run together through the whole vowel. >> Low F1 and low F2 together fit the back rounded quality of /u/. || If changing only the filter changes the vowel, the vowel’s identity must lie in the filter, its pattern of formants. These measured formants are the starting point for new filters.
7|**Praat can find formants automatically**. It follows each resonance through a recording and marks its frequency moment by moment, and in this recording of /u/ it identifies **five formants**. >> Several resonances run at the same time, each one a frequency component of the same sound. >> Automatic tracking measures the resonance pattern without anyone estimating frequencies by eye. || **Only the first three formants are usually important for phonemic processing**. F1, F2 and F3 carry most of the information that separates phonemes, while higher formants depend more on the individual voice. >> The numbers give the order of the resonances from low to high, with F1 the lowest, F2 next and F3 third. || The manipulation targets just **the first two formants**, which in this /u/ sit near 300 Hz and 900 Hz. >> Changing only these two is enough to turn one vowel into another, since they carry most of the vowel information. >> F3 and the higher formants stay fixed, so any change listeners hear comes from F1 or F2 alone.
8|The **source** can be **extracted** from the recorded /u/. Praat models the filter from the measured formants and uses that model to strip the filter’s effect from the recording, leaving a **more-or-less “unfiltered” version of the voice**. >> What remains is the sound energy the vocal folds supplied. >> It is only more or less unfiltered, because the separation is an estimate. || The extracted source **sounds like a buzz**. It keeps its energy and pitch but loses the resonance pattern that made it sound like /u/. >> Its energy is spread across many frequencies, without the strong low formants of /u/. >> Pitch survives because it comes from vocal fold vibration, and vowel quality vanishes because it comes from the filter. || The separation is **a little bit like doing a multiple regression analysis**, where one predictor’s contribution is estimated and removed so the rest can be examined. >> Here the filter’s contribution is estimated and removed, and what is left is treated as the source. >> With the source isolated, new filters can be applied to it. Keeping one source while swapping filters makes the effect of the resonance pattern testable.
9|Three **filters** can be applied to one source. A filter specifies which frequencies get more emphasis and which get less, so every filter is a resonance pattern. >> The **first filter** is simply the first four formants of the original /u/, so applying it should bring back the original vowel. >> The **second filter** raises **the first formant a little** and leaves the others roughly where they were. >> The **third filter** raises **the second formant a lot** and leaves F1 and the higher formants roughly in place. || Each version changes one part of the resonance pattern instead of shifting every frequency together. >> In the original, F1 and F2 are both low, which fits /u/. >> Raising F1 moves only the lowest resonance up, and raising F2 moves only the second resonance up, by a much larger amount. || The **sound source stays the same** for all three filters, so any change in vowel quality comes from the filter. >> No new speaker or recording is needed. >> Comparing the outputs isolates what a change in the lowest resonance does compared with a change in the second.
10|**Multiplying the source by the filters gives new vowels**. In source-filter terms, the output is the source shaped by the filter, and each filter boosts a different set of frequencies, so each combination is a different vowel. >> The source provides the raw sound energy. >> The filter decides how much of that energy stays strong in each frequency region. || One source and three filters give three results. >> With the **original filter**, F1 and F2 stay low and the result keeps the quality of /u/. >> With **F1 raised a little**, the lowest resonance rises and the vowel changes. >> With **F2 raised a lot**, the second resonance rises far and the vowel changes another way, moving the back rounded /u/ toward a more front vowel. || **Source-filter synthesis** pulls apart two contributions that ordinary speech combines. The voice provides the sound, and the vocal tract resonances shape its quality. >> All three outputs share the same buzz, pitch and timing, yet they sound like different vowels. >> A researcher can change vowel quality by swapping only the filter, without asking a speaker to record each version.
11|A speaker’s intended vowel only **partly** determines formant values. They are also shaped by **anatomical features** that differ between speakers of the same language, by **different rates of speech**, and by **context**, such as the phones before and after a vowel. >> **Anatomy** matters because vocal tracts vary in length and shape, and a longer tract gives lower resonances overall. >> **Speaking rate** matters because fast speech gives the articulators less time to reach their targets. >> **Context** matters because the tongue and lips are already heading toward the next sound or leaving the previous one. A **phone** is one particular spoken realization of a speech sound. || Across 76 speakers, each of ten vowels covers a wide range of formant values, and those ranges overlap. >> The same F1 and F2 values can be different vowels for different speakers. >> No single pair of frequencies defines a vowel for everyone. || Formant values **nonetheless** give a pretty good description of **vowel space**, especially for **a specific talker speaking carefully**. Vowel space is the layout of a language’s vowels by their formant frequencies. >> Within one careful talker, the vowels separate far more cleanly than across many talkers. >> All else being equal, **reasonable approximations of vowels** can be made **by manipulating steady-state formants**. A **steady-state formant** is the resonance in the stable middle of a vowel, as opposed to the transitions at its edges.
12|The **pattern playback synthesizer** turns a painted spectrographic pattern back into sound. Researchers paint bands on a transparent sheet wherever they want acoustic energy in time and frequency, and the machine plays the pattern aloud. >> Changing the painting changes the sound. >> Researchers could therefore build controlled, speech-like sounds instead of relying on natural recordings that vary in many uncontrolled ways. || The machine works by shining light through the painted pattern. >> Light passes through a **cylindrical lens** onto a spinning **tone wheel**, whose rings modulate the light at many frequencies at once. >> A lens and **mirror** send that light onto the painted **spectrogram**, and the painted areas pass along the matching frequencies. >> A **light collector** and **photocell** turn the light into an electrical signal, and an **amplifier** and **loudspeaker** turn that signal into sound. || A researcher can alter a single formant band or transition and hear the result right away. >> Every other part of the pattern stays exactly the same. >> Any change in what listeners hear can be traced to the one painted change. Series of patterns differing in tiny steps revealed how formant transitions signal consonants.
13|**Stop consonants are distinguished by formant transitions**. **/b/, /d/ and /g/** are made by briefly closing the vocal tract and then opening it, and the audible part of each happens **while the jaw is opening**. >> During that opening, the vocal tract changes shape quickly. >> The changing shape makes the formants glide from the consonant’s position toward the vowel’s, so the consonant’s identity lies in how the formants move. || **Liberman and colleagues (1957)** used the **pattern playback synthesizer** to build speech-like sounds that copied these transitions. Each pattern had two formants. >> The **lower formant** was nearly the same in every stimulus. >> The **upper formant** started at a different frequency in each stimulus and then settled at the same steady vowel level. >> In some stimuli it started low and **rose** into the vowel, in others it was nearly **flat**, and in others it started high and **fell** into the vowel. || The fourteen patterns kept the vowel the same and changed only where the upper transition began. >> A rising transition was heard as /b/, a roughly flat one as /d/ and a falling one as /g/. >> Since only the transition differed, listeners’ responses could be tied directly to it.
14|A **speech continuum** is a series of sounds that change step by step along one controlled acoustic property. In this one, **F1 is kept constant** and the **F2 onset frequency** is **varied linearly** from stimulus 1 to stimulus 13. The **F2 onset frequency** is where the second formant begins its transition. >> Holding F1 constant removes one possible difference between stimuli. >> Varying the F2 onset **linearly** means it changes by the same step every time, so **adjacent stimuli differ by the same amount across the entire continuum**. || The F2 transition shifts gradually along the series. >> Early on, F2 starts well below the vowel frequency, so it rises. >> As the onset climbs, the rise shrinks until the transition is nearly flat. >> Later, F2 starts above the vowel frequency and falls. || Because every step is physically equal, the continuum fairly tests whether perception follows the acoustics or the categories. >> Some neighboring pairs fall inside one consonant category, and others straddle a boundary. >> A purely acoustic listener would hear every step as the same size. >> If perception is categorical, steps within a category will sound alike and steps across a boundary will sound different, so any advantage for boundary pairs comes from the boundary itself.
15|An **ideal observer model** predicts how a listener would respond if perception worked according to a particular account. This version assumes that perception is **non-categorical**. It tracks the acoustic signal gradually, with no sharp category boundaries. >> The model is a baseline. Real data can be compared against it to see whether perception is more categorical than a gradual account allows. || The first prediction is that **categorization gets “blurry” as you move away from category prototypes**. A **prototype** is the clearest example of a category. The endpoints of the continuum are /b/ and /g/, with /d/ in the middle. >> Between two prototypes, **stimulus ambiguity scales with the distance from the prototype**. The farther a stimulus is from the /b/ prototype, the less /b/-like it sounds and the more /d/-like it sounds. >> **Stimulus 2** would be heard as **75% /b/ and 25% /d/**. It sits a quarter of the way from the /b/ prototype toward the /d/ prototype, so the responses split in that same proportion. || Under this account, **labeling** changes smoothly. Responses for one category fade out in a straight slope while responses for the neighboring category fade in. >> Two neighboring labels are equally likely halfway between their prototypes. >> Every change is gradual, with no sudden step from one label to the next.
16|The same ideal observer can also assume that perception is **linear**, so equal acoustic differences are perceived as equally large. This leads to a second prediction. **Discrimination performance should be independent of labeling**. >> **Labeling** asks a listener to identify a sound as /b/, /d/ or /g/. >> **Discrimination** asks only whether two sounds are the **same or different**. These are separate tasks, even when they use the same continuum. || Labeling **has to have a “peaked” function**. There are three categories, and listeners are choosing between labels, so each category’s responses rise toward its prototype and fall away from it. These peaks come from the structure of the labeling task itself. || Discrimination works differently under the linear account. Pairs are compared two steps apart, from **1 vs. 3** through **9 vs. 11**, and every pair has the same physical separation. >> If listeners perceived differences in F2 onset equally well across the whole continuum, every pair would be equally easy to tell apart. >> Discrimination would then stay level everywhere, even where labeling changes. A flat pattern would mean category boundaries play no role in hearing differences. A peaked pattern would mean the boundaries make some differences easier to hear.
17|**Liberman and colleagues (1957)** measured actual labeling and discrimination along the /b/–/d/–/g/ continuum. Their results departed sharply from the non-categorical, linear predictions. >> Listeners labeled each stimulus and also judged pairs of stimuli as same or different. >> The pairs were **one step**, **two steps** or **three steps** apart. || Labeling showed **sharp changes** in identification. The first few stimuli were heard as /b/ almost every time. Over just a step or two, responses switched almost completely to /d/, and later they switched again, just as quickly, to /g/. >> These switches are far more abrupt than the gradual slopes the ideal observer predicted. >> The narrow region where the label flips is the **category boundary**. || **Listeners were best at discriminating sounds near the category boundaries**. >> Accuracy peaked at the /b/–/d/ boundary and again at the /d/–/g/ boundary. >> Pairs inside one category were discriminated poorly, close to **chance**, the level expected from guessing. >> Larger steps raised accuracy overall, but the peaks stayed at the boundaries. || Liberman and colleagues also predicted discrimination from the labeling data, assuming listeners can tell two sounds apart only when they give them different labels. The predictions captured much of the real pattern. >> Real accuracy was often a little higher than predicted, especially within categories. >> Listeners therefore keep some sensitivity to acoustic differences beyond the category labels alone.

19|Class data from prior years tested categorical perception along a **beach–peach** continuum. The two words differ in their first consonant, /b/ versus /p/. Each listener completed an **identification** task, labeling every stimulus, and a **discrimination** task, judging pairs two steps apart. || For the first listener, identification stays on one word for the early stimuli and then **switches to the other word** around the middle of the continuum. >> Responses are mixed only in a small region near the switch. >> The acoustic change is gradual, but the label changes within a narrow region. || That listener discriminates best for the pairs that straddle the identification boundary. >> Pairs well inside one category are hard to tell apart, even though their physical separation is the same. >> A pair that includes a stimulus with mixed labels falls in between. || The second listener **switches word labels later** along the continuum and discriminates best near that later boundary. >> Different people can place their category boundaries at different points. >> For each listener, the best discrimination falls close to that person’s own identification boundary.
20|The class data support a few general points. **Not everyone has exactly the same category boundary**, so a sound near the beach–peach boundary for one listener can fall squarely inside a category for another. >> A pair that straddles one person’s boundary may stay inside one category for someone else. >> When individual boundaries differ, averaging across the group can flatten the boundary peak. || **In general, discrimination is best for stimuli perceived as being in different categories**. >> Listeners may miss a physical difference between two versions of the same phoneme. >> The same size of difference becomes far easier to hear when the two sounds are heard as different words. || To fit the lab into a short session, the discrimination test used **only “different” trials**, so every pair held two physically different stimuli. >> The correct answer was always **“different,”** provided the listener could tell the sounds apart. >> Answering “same” meant missing a real difference. >> Accuracy is the share of pairs heard as different. Someone who always said different would score perfectly, so low accuracy on within-category pairs means listeners often failed to hear differences that were really there.
21|Further class results compare a **bear–pear** continuum with the **beach–peach** continuum. >> For bear–pear, identification switches fairly clearly from one word to the other. Discrimination, however, jumps up and down from one pair to the next, including a miss on a pair that crosses the boundary. >> Another listener’s beach–peach data fluctuate even more. Identification zigzags instead of switching once, and discrimination bounces between high and low values. || The one iron-clad law of experimental research is that **stuff happens**. Responses to a carefully built continuum can still bounce around. >> Identification records which word a listener chooses, while discrimination records whether a difference is detected. These two behaviors can vary in different ways across trials and people. >> An isolated rise or drop in discrimination reflects noise in a few responses. || The data raise the question of how the experiment could be improved. >> Only “different” trials were used, so there was no measure of how often listeners said “different” when the sounds were identical. >> A short session gave each listener few trials per pair, so a single response could move accuracy a great deal. >> The real test is whether better discrimination lines up with the place where identification changes, across the whole continuum.
22|The central question is **how categorical perception would help**, or **what is the advantage of being worse at hearing some differences between sounds**? Being worse at within-category differences can seem like throwing away useful detail. || The payoff is **stable word recognition**. Every production of a phoneme differs physically from every other, because of speakers, speaking rates and neighboring sounds. >> If each small acoustic change were heard as a new identity, everyday versions of one word would sound like unrelated sounds. >> Reduced sensitivity within a category lets many acoustic versions count as one phoneme. || Differences between categories stay easy to hear, so the distinctions that change words are kept. >> Variation inside a category is tolerated because it leaves the word unchanged. >> Ignoring small within-category differences frees the listener to focus on the differences that change meaning.

24|**Categorical perception in infants** can be tested even though infants cannot name sounds. Researchers watch for a behavior that changes when an infant notices something new, since infants react to novelty. >> In the **conditioned head turn procedure**, from Janet Werker’s infant studies lab, an infant learns to turn toward a sound when it changes, and a correct turn earns an interesting visual reward such as a moving toy. >> After the infant learns this link, a head turn following a change means the infant detected the change. || The results of interest here come from **high-amplitude sucking**. The infant sucks on a pacifier attached to a recorder, and strong sucks make a sound play. >> As the same sound repeats, the infant grows used to it, which is **habituation**, and sucking slows. >> If a new sound makes sucking **increase again**, the infant has noticed the change. || Both methods make perception testable before an infant can talk. >> The experimenter controls exactly which sound difference is presented. >> A head turn or renewed sucking reveals sensitivity to that difference without any labeling, even in infants who are months from saying words.
25|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** tested categorical perception in **4-month-old infants** using **high-amplitude sucking**. The sounds varied in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**, which is the delay between the release of a consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration. >> Adults hear a short VOT as a voiced consonant such as /b/ and a longer VOT as a voiceless consonant such as /p/. >> In a syllable with a long VOT, voicing begins much later after the release. || Infants heard one synthetic syllable over and over until their sucking slowed down. Then the sound either changed or stayed the same. >> In condition **20D**, the VOT changed from **+20 to +40 milliseconds**. This 20-millisecond change **crossed** the adult boundary between two consonant categories. >> In condition **20S**, the VOT also changed by **20 milliseconds**, but both sounds came from the **same** adult category, such as **−20 and 0** or **+60 and +80**. >> In condition **0**, the control group kept hearing the same sound. || In every group, sucking rose at first and then dropped as the sound became familiar. >> After a change across the category boundary, sucking **recovered** sharply. >> After a change within one category, sucking kept falling with little recovery, much like the control group. || The two changed conditions were matched at 20 milliseconds, so the difference comes from crossing the boundary. Infants noticed a change that adults would hear as a new consonant, but not an equally large change inside one category. Even at four months, infants perceive voicing categorically.
26|**Perceptual narrowing** is the process in which experience tunes perception to the sounds of the surrounding language. Sensitivity to some distinctions from other languages declines along the way. **Werker and Tees** tested how age and experience affect infants’ ability to discriminate two **non-English contrasts**. >> The **Hindi contrast** is a distinction between two “ta”-like sounds that English does not use. >> The **Salish contrast** is a distinction between “ki” and “qi” in Nthlakampx, a Salish language, which English also does not use. || The measure was the share of infants who reached a **discrimination criterion** at each age. >> At **6–8 months**, most English-learning infants discriminated both contrasts, nearly all of them for Hindi. >> At **8–10 months**, fewer succeeded, roughly two thirds for Hindi and half for Salish. >> At **10–12 months**, very few succeeded, about one in five for Hindi and one in ten for Salish. >> **Hindi- and Salish-learning infants** discriminated the contrast from their own language nearly every time. || These results are described as **perceptual reorganization during the first year of life**. >> Young infants can detect these nonnative contrasts. >> With experience of English, English-learning infants become much less likely to discriminate them by about their first birthday. >> Infants who hear the contrast in their own language keep it, so the decline depends on language experience. Contrasts the language uses are kept, and contrasts it lacks become hard to hear.
27|These findings feed **a very compelling narrative** about speech perception, built from three linked claims. >> First, speech perception depends on **categorical perception abilities that are a special property of the language system**, so the mechanism that sorts sounds into categories is specialized for language. >> Second, these abilities are **present very early in development**, as the infant sucking and head-turn experiments suggest, long before children talk. || Third, children **start off as “citizens of the world,”** able to hear **all** speech contrasts, and then **rapidly tune their perceptual systems to their specific language**. >> As “citizens of the world,” infants could learn any human language and are ready to hear the distinctions of every one. >> Tuning means the contrasts of the surrounding language stay available while contrasts from other languages fade, as in the Werker and Tees results. || The narrative covers both the **starting state** and **developmental change**. >> It proposes broad, language-specific abilities at birth. >> It treats development mostly as narrowing from that broad start. >> Each early result seems to fit, since infants discriminate categorically before talking, hear contrasts from many languages and lose nonnative contrasts with experience.
28|The compelling narrative meets **some issues**, three of them about **categorical perception**. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to language**, since it also shows up with nonspeech sounds, which undercuts the claim that it is special to language. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to humans**, since nonhuman animals also perceive speech sounds categorically, which undercuts the claim that it is special to humans. >> Categorical perception is **task-dependent**. Results look more or less categorical depending on the task, and choosing between labels makes responses more categorical than a task that measures fine acoustic differences. || Another issue concerns **infant speech perception**, which is **not actually “universal.”** >> Some contrasts are harder for infants to detect than others from early on. >> Development therefore includes initial differences in sensitivity as well as changes driven by experience. || Together, these issues weaken the strong claim that every speech distinction starts out equally available in a mechanism devoted only to language.
29|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** tested categorical perception with **non-speech sounds**. The sounds varied in **rise time**, which is how quickly a sound reaches full loudness after it begins. >> A sound with a **rapid rise time** sounds **plucked**, like a plucked string. >> A sound with a **slow rise time** sounds **bowed**, like a bowed string. || The stimuli included **sawtooth waves**, **sine waves** and **speech syllables**, each made with short and long rise times, such as **10** and **70 milliseconds**. >> With a short rise, the sound reaches full strength almost at once. >> With a long rise, it builds up gradually. >> Pluck and bow are labels for two kinds of sound onset, not speech sounds. || For the sawtooth waves, listeners labeled short rise times as pluck and long rise times as bow. The label switched over a narrow range in the middle of the continuum. >> Discrimination was only a little above chance for most pairs. >> It **peaked** for the pairs near the middle, right where the labels switched. || The link between a label boundary and better discrimination therefore appears for a sound distinction that has nothing to do with language. The physical continuum is gradual, yet the preferred label changes over a narrow region, and discrimination improves there.
30|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** tested whether **infants** show categorical perception of **non-speech sounds**. They used high-amplitude sucking to measure whether a change in sound was detected. >> Infants heard one sound repeatedly until their sucking declined. >> The sound was then shifted, and the change was compared across three conditions. || The three conditions were **no change**, a change **within categories** and a change **between categories**. >> In every condition, sucking declined before the shift as the sound became familiar. >> After **no change**, sucking stayed low. >> After a **within-category** change, sucking also stayed low. >> After a **between-category** change, sucking jumped back up and then declined again as the new sound became familiar. || Infants responded more strongly to a non-speech change that crossed a category boundary than to a change within a category. >> This mirrors the pattern Eimas and colleagues found for speech. >> Infants cannot be drawing on knowledge of words or phonemes when they respond to plucked and bowed sounds. Their categorical response must come from more general auditory processing, which could also underlie their categorical response to speech.
31|Categorical perception of speech sounds has also been found in **chinchillas**, following **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and the earlier work of **Kuhl and Miller (1975)**. The animals were trained with **operant conditioning**, a form of learning in which consequences change how often a behavior occurs. >> Each chinchilla learned that either the **0-millisecond lag** sound or the **80-millisecond lag** sound was an **SD** for food. **SD** stands for **discriminative stimulus**, a signal that a response will be rewarded. >> The lag is **voice onset time**. Humans hear a 0-millisecond lag as /ga/ and an 80-millisecond lag as /ka/. || After training, the chinchillas were tested with sounds in between, all along the **/ga/–/ka/** continuum. Human listeners were tested on the same sounds. >> Short VOTs were labeled /ga/ almost every time, and long VOTs almost never. >> The switch happened over a fairly narrow range of VOTs, and the chinchilla curves had the same steep, categorical shape as the human curves. || The study also varied the **burst** and **aspiration noise**. The **burst** is the brief noise at the release of a stop consonant, and **aspiration** is the breathy noise that follows a voiceless release. >> Making the burst and aspiration louder shifted the boundary toward shorter VOTs, made the switch steeper, or both. >> This happened for one chinchilla and for one human listener, so both species responded to these sound properties in a similar way. >> Animals without human language can sort a speech continuum into categories. This argues against the view that categorical perception is special to human language.
32|Some speech contrasts are **less salient than others** for infants. A **salient** contrast is one that is easy to notice. >> The contrasts tested here differ in **place of articulation**, which is where in the mouth a sound is made. >> In **[ma]**, the nasal is made with the lips. In **[na]**, it is made with the tongue near the ridge behind the teeth. In **[ŋa]**, which begins with the sound at the end of “sing,” it is made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate. || Researchers compared infants’ **looking time** on **same** trials, where the syllable stayed the same, and **change** trials, where it switched. Longer looking on change trials means the infant noticed the change. >> At **4–5 months**, English-hearing infants looked clearly longer on change trials for the native **[ma]–[na]** contrast. >> For the nonnative **[na]–[ŋa]** contrast, looking was about the same on both kinds of trials. These infants did not detect this change, and English never uses [ŋ] at the start of a syllable. || **Filipino-learning infants** hear [na]–[ŋa] as a contrast in their own language. >> At **6–8 months**, they also looked about equally on both kinds of trials, so they did not yet detect the change. >> By **10–12 months**, they looked longer on change trials. || Some contrasts therefore start out hard for infants to hear, even when they belong to the native language. Experience with the language **strengthens** sensitivity to such contrasts over the first year, so development includes gains as well as losses.
