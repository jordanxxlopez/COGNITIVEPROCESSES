2|**Nature and nurture in child language** asks how much of language comes from built-in capacities and how much comes from experience. **“Nativist” positions** arose from **Chomsky’s critique of Skinner**. >> **Skinner**, a behaviorist, explained behavior, language included, with general learning mechanisms such as reward and punishment. >> **Chomsky** argued that those mechanisms could not account for how children acquire grammar. || For Chomsky, **the important feature of language is its generativity**. **Generativity** is the capacity to produce and understand an unlimited number of new sentences. >> **Acquiring a language** therefore means **identifying the abstract grammar that produces the sentences of the language**. || The **general learning mechanisms studied by behaviorists were not up to this task**, for two reasons. >> **Grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are not “similar” in any straightforward way**. One small change can make a sentence ungrammatical, and two grammatical sentences can look nothing alike. >> **The context for language acquisition does not look like operant conditioning**. **Operant conditioning** shapes behavior with rewards and punishments that follow it. Children are not dependably rewarded for grammatical sentences or punished for ungrammatical ones, so the **reward and punishment contingencies do not hold**. || **Nativist approaches** make two claims instead. >> Children have **innate knowledge of what language is like**, known as **Universal Grammar**, a built-in set of principles that limits the grammars a child will consider. >> Children rely on **language-specific acquisition processes**, learning mechanisms built specifically for language. || In Chomsky’s words, **“The fact that all normal children acquire essentially comparable grammars of great complexity with remarkable rapidity suggests that human beings are somehow specially designed to do this, with data-handling or ‘hypothesis-formulating’ ability of unknown character and complexity.”** >> Typical children reach a complex grammar quickly, even though their experiences differ widely.
3|**Categorical perception** is the pattern in which listeners hear differences between speech sounds in terms of categories. **Early theories treated it as a language-specific and species-specific phenomenon**. >> **Language-specific** means it was thought to occur only for language. **Species-specific** means it was thought to occur only in humans. >> At the time, **only humans were known to show categorical perception, and only for speech sounds**, so it looked like a unique feature of human language. || The classic evidence comes from a synthetic consonant continuum. The upper formant transition changes in equal steps from one stimulus to the next, while everything else stays the same. >> In **labeling**, listeners switch sharply from **/b/** to **/d/**, and later from /d/ to **/g/**, each over just a step or two. >> In **discrimination**, accuracy peaks near those boundaries and drops inside each category, whether the pairs are one, two or three steps apart. || Categorical perception is **a compelling example of how similarity between stimuli is defined in linguistic, rather than physical, terms**. >> Two sounds that differ by the same acoustic amount can sound almost identical if they belong to the same category. >> They can sound clearly different if they fall on opposite sides of a category boundary.
4|**Categorical perception in infants** can be measured through behaviors that change when an infant notices something new. Head turns and sucking reveal what an infant hears without the infant saying anything. || In the **conditioned head turn procedure**, used in Janet Werker’s infant studies lab, an infant learns to turn toward a sound when it changes. >> The infant sits on a parent’s lap while an experimenter holds the infant’s attention forward with toys. >> A correct head turn after a change earns an interesting visual reward, such as a lit-up moving toy. >> Once the infant has learned this link, turning after a sound change means the infant heard the change. || **High-amplitude sucking** relies on infants’ natural interest in novelty. >> The infant sucks on a pacifier attached to a pressure recorder, and strong sucks make a sound play. >> As the same sound repeats, the infant grows used to it, which is **habituation**, and sucking slows. >> If a new sound makes sucking **increase again**, the infant has noticed the change. || Neither method asks the infant to name or produce a sound. The experimenter decides exactly which acoustic difference is presented, and a head turn or renewed sucking reveals whether infants hear the same distinctions adults hear.
5|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** tested categorical perception in **4-month-old infants** using **high-amplitude sucking**. The sounds varied in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**, the delay between the release of a stop consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration. >> In a syllable with a long VOT, voicing starts much later after the release than in a syllable with a short VOT. || The experiment had **three conditions**. Infants heard a syllable over and over until their sucking slowed down, and then the sound either changed or stayed the same. >> In **20D**, the two sounds differed by **20 milliseconds** of VOT, **+20 versus +40 milliseconds**, and came from **different adult phonemic categories**. >> In **20S**, the sounds also differed by **20 milliseconds**, such as **−20 versus 0** or **+60 versus +80 milliseconds**, but came from the **same** category. >> In **0**, the **control** condition, the same sound continued throughout. || In every condition, sucking rose at first and then declined as the sound became familiar. >> In **20D**, sucking jumped back up after the change. >> In **20S**, sucking showed little recovery and kept falling. >> In the control condition, sucking kept declining. || Infants responded to a change that crossed an adult phoneme boundary but not to an equally large change within a category. >> The two changed conditions were matched in acoustic size, so the difference comes from crossing the boundary. >> The control condition establishes what happens with repetition alone.
6|Eimas’s result was **a big deal** for several reasons. **Categorical perception was considered to be the first step in turning speech into a symbolic format** that later stages of language processing could use. >> A **symbolic** format stores a sound as a category, such as /b/. >> Finding this ability in infants suggested that the starting point for processing language was already present. || **Four-month-olds are really little**. Typical **gross motor milestones** at this age are **manipulating objects with two hands** and **holding the head and chest up while lying prone**, that is, lying face down. >> Gross motor skills involve large movements of the body. || Their **color vision and general visual acuity are still “in progress.”** **Visual acuity** means sharpness of vision. >> Basic perception in other areas is still developing. || Four-month-olds are also **just starting to produce vowel-like sounds instead of creaky whines**, and they cannot yet make speech sounds well. || That is **very, very early in development for an apparently language-specific ability to emerge**, so it **seemed like evidence for innateness**. >> Infants sort speech sounds into categories before they can produce them and while other skills are immature. >> Because speech categorization ran so far ahead of other abilities, it looked built in instead of learned through long experience.
7|**“Perceptual narrowing”** for speech categories is the decline in sensitivity to sound distinctions that are not used in the language a child hears. **At around six months, English-learning infants show evidence of discriminating speech contrasts they have never heard before**. **Over the course of development, the ability to perceive these contrasts declines**. >> **Werker and Tees** tested English-learning infants on two non-English contrasts. >> The **Hindi contrast** is a distinction between two “ta”-like sounds. The **Salish contrast** is a distinction between “ki” and “qi” from Nthlakampx, a Salish language. || The measure was the share of infants at each age who reached a **discrimination criterion**. >> At **6–8 months**, most English-learning infants discriminated both contrasts. >> At **8–10 months**, fewer did. >> At **10–12 months**, only a small minority did. >> **Hindi- and Salish-learning infants** discriminated their own native contrast nearly every time. || This is **perceptual reorganization during the first year of life**. >> Without experience of a contrast, English-learning infants lose much of their ability to hear it by about their first birthday. >> Infants who hear the contrast in their language keep it, so the decline depends on language experience. || This raises the question of **how perceptual narrowing relates to categorical perception**. >> As infants form the categories of their native language, sounds that their language groups together become harder to tell apart. >> A nonnative distinction that falls within one native category comes to be heard as a within-category difference.
8|These findings support **a very compelling narrative**. >> **Speech perception depends on categorical perception abilities that are a special property of the language system**. >> **These abilities are present very early in development**, according to the infant studies. || **Children start off as “citizens of the world,”** able to hear **all** contrasts, which means **they have innate, language-general knowledge about speech**. >> **Language-general** knowledge applies to the speech sounds of every language. >> Calling infants “citizens of the world” captures the idea that a newborn is ready for any human language. >> Children then **rapidly tune their perceptual systems to the specific language in their environment**. || In this narrative, **the role of learning is to select** among many **innately specified phonetic contrasts** and **narrow down the inventory** to those that are **meaningful in the child’s native language**. >> The **inventory** is the full set of phonetic contrasts the child can perceive. >> Learning works by elimination. It drops the contrasts the native language does not use and keeps the innate categories the language needs.
9|This view faces **some problems**. Three concern **categorical perception**. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to language**, since it also appears for nonspeech sounds. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to humans**, since animals without language also perceive speech sounds categorically. >> Categorical perception is **task-dependent**. Results look more or less categorical depending on the task, such as naming a sound versus judging whether two sounds differ. || Another concerns **infant speech perception**, which is **not actually “universal.”** >> Infants do not begin able to hear every contrast equally well. >> Some contrasts are hard for young infants to detect, even contrasts that occur in their own language. || Taken together, these problems weaken the claim that every speech distinction is innately available in a mechanism used only for language. A full account of development has to explain contrasts that get better with experience as well as contrasts that are lost, so learning must build categories as well as remove them.
10|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** tested categorical perception with **non-speech sounds**. The sounds varied in **rise time**, which is how quickly a sound grows to full loudness after it starts. >> A **short rise time** produces a sound like a **plucked** string. >> A **long rise time** produces a sound like a **bowed** string. || The stimuli included **sawtooth waves**, **speech syllables** and **sine waves**, each made with short and long rise times, such as **10** and **70 milliseconds**. >> With a short rise, the sound reaches full strength almost at once. >> With a long rise, it builds up gradually. || For the sawtooth waves, listeners called short rise times pluck and long rise times bow, with a fairly sharp switch in the middle of the range. >> Discrimination was only a little above chance for most pairs. >> It **peaked** for the pairs right where the labels switched. || The link between a label boundary and better discrimination appears for a sound distinction that has nothing to do with phonemes. >> Pluck and bow are labels for kinds of sound onset, not speech categories. >> Categorical perception can therefore arise in general auditory processing, without a mechanism specific to language.
11|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** tested whether **infants** show categorical perception of **non-speech sounds**, using high-amplitude sucking. >> Infants heard a sound over and over until their sucking slowed down. >> The sound was then changed, and three conditions were compared. || The conditions were **no change**, a change **within categories**, and a change **between categories**. >> In all three, sucking declined before the change as the sound became familiar. >> After no change, sucking stayed low. >> After a change within a category, sucking also stayed low. >> After a change between categories, sucking jumped back up, then declined again as the new sound became familiar. || Infants responded more strongly to a non-speech change that crossed a category boundary than to a change within a category. >> This is the same pattern Eimas and colleagues found for speech. >> Adults show categorical labeling for plucked and bowed sounds, and infants show categorical discrimination for nonspeech changes. Neither group needs language knowledge, so early categorical perception reflects a general auditory ability.
12|**Chinchillas** also show categorical perception of **speech sounds**, following **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and the earlier work of **Kuhl and Miller (1975)**. The animals were trained using **operant conditioning**, a form of learning in which a behavior becomes more likely when it is followed by a reward. >> Each chinchilla learned that either the **0-millisecond lag** sound or the **80-millisecond lag** sound was an **SD** for food. **SD** stands for **discriminative stimulus**, a signal that a response will be rewarded. >> The lag is the **voice onset time**. Humans hear a 0-millisecond lag as /ga/ and an 80-millisecond lag as /ka/. || After training, the animals were tested with sounds in between, all along the **/ga/–/ka/** continuum, and human listeners heard the same sounds. >> Short VOTs were labeled /ga/ almost every time, and long VOTs almost never. >> The switch happened over a narrow range of VOTs, and the chinchilla curves had the same steep, categorical shape as the human curves. || **Increasing the intensity of the burst and aspiration noise** shifted the category boundary to a **shorter VOT**, made the switch steeper, or both, for one chinchilla and one human listener. >> The **burst** is the brief noise at the release of a stop, and **aspiration** is the breathy noise that follows a voiceless release. >> Both species responded to these sound properties in a similar way. >> Animals without language sort a speech continuum into categories, so categorical perception of speech is shared with other species.
13|According to **Narayan and colleagues (2010)**, **some contrasts are less salient than others for infants**. A **salient** contrast is easy to notice. >> The contrasts tested use **nasal consonants** at the start of a syllable, the **onset**. >> They differ in **place of articulation**, the spot in the mouth where the sound is made. **/m/** uses the lips, **/n/** uses the tongue just behind the teeth, and **/ŋ/**, the last sound of “sing,” uses the back of the tongue against the soft palate. || Infants heard either the same syllable repeated or a syllable that switched, and longer looking at a switch meant they noticed it. >> English-hearing infants at **4–5 months** noticed the native **[ma]–[na]** switch. >> The same infants did not notice the nonnative **[na]–[ŋa]** switch. || A **place contrast for nasals in onset**, **/na/–/ŋa/**, is **not readily perceived by four-month-olds, independent of language experience**. >> By comparison, **/ma/–/na/** is **relatively salient**. >> The gap comes from the sounds themselves. Some contrasts start out harder to hear than others, which undercuts the idea that every contrast begins equally available.
14|**Narayan and colleagues (2010)** found that **between 6 and 10 months, exposure to the /na/–/ŋa/ contrast in a Filipino language environment increases its salience**. Filipino treats [na] and [ŋa] as different syllables, so infants learning Filipino hear the contrast often. >> At **6–8 months**, Filipino-learning infants did not yet notice the switch. >> At **10–12 months**, they did. >> English-hearing infants at 4–5 months missed this contrast while still noticing [ma]–[na]. || **It cannot be that all speech contrasts are available innately**. >> Sensitivity to [na]–[ŋa] starts out weak, even in infants whose language uses it. >> Hearing the contrast over the first year makes it easier to detect. || **Exposure to contrasts in the environment is necessary for acquisition in some cases**. >> Learning can strengthen a contrast, not only keep it. >> Development therefore involves building sensitivity to hard contrasts, beyond choosing from a complete innate set, and experience actively shapes speech categories.
15|The **emergentist approach** is the main alternative to nativism. **Emergentist approaches assume that associative learning mechanisms are sensitive to statistical regularities in the environment**. >> **Associative learning** means learning that two things go together because they keep occurring together. >> **Statistical regularities** are patterns in how often things occur and what occurs with what. >> On this view, the structure of language emerges from general learning mechanisms applied to these patterns in rich input. || Classical conditioning is the simplest case of associative learning. It can be described with three neurons, one for a **bell**, one that detects **food** and one that triggers **salivation**. >> The food detector already has a strong link to salivation, so food causes salivation. >> When the bell keeps occurring along with food, the **synapse** joining the bell neuron to the salivation neuron is **strengthened**. A synapse is a connection between neurons. >> After enough pairings, the bell alone triggers salivation. || The question is **how this can extend beyond conditioning to support learning for pattern recognition**. **Pattern recognition** means identifying a recurring structure, such as a speech sound, across many varied examples. >> The same strengthening could link acoustic features that tend to occur together. >> Repeated co-occurrence in speech could then build categories that were never specified in advance. Infants would learn categories by keeping track of which sounds occur together and how often.
16|The **American vowels** give an example of statistical regularities. When **Kuhl and others** talk about **learning the statistics of distributions in the environment**, they mean patterns like these. A **distribution** describes how often each value turns up across many examples. || Each English vowel, measured by its **first** and **second formants** over many productions, occupies a region of **vowel space**. >> The regions **overlap** heavily, so a single pair of formant values can belong to more than one vowel. >> That overlap is what makes speech categorization hard. Listeners must learn where each vowel is centered and how widely it varies. || **We might learn the vowels of our language by extracting the statistics of these distributions**. >> Across many exposures, the listener keeps track of where each vowel’s productions cluster. >> The resulting categories mirror the shape of the input. || A **“prototypical” /a/** would then be **just the mean, or perhaps the mode, of the distribution of all the /a/ sounds we hear**. >> The **mean** is the average value. >> The **mode** is the most frequent value. >> Either way, the prototype grows out of experience, so it can differ between languages and between listeners.
17|**Maye and colleagues** tested whether infants actually learn from distributions. They had infants **listen passively** to sounds from a **[da]–[ta] continuum** of eight steps that differed in **voice onset time**. Passive listening means the infants simply heard the sounds, with no task or reward. || Two groups heard different **distributions** of these sounds. >> In the **bimodal distribution**, sounds near each end of the continuum were played most often and sounds in the middle were rare. Two peaks give **evidence for two different categories**. >> In the **unimodal distribution**, sounds in the middle were played most often and the ends were rare. One peak gives **evidence for a single category**. || **At test, infants heard stimuli 3 and 6** in either **alternating** or **non-alternating** trials. >> In alternating trials, the two sounds switched back and forth. In non-alternating trials, one sound repeated. >> If infants treat 3 and 6 as different sounds, the two kinds of trials should hold their attention differently. || **Only infants pre-exposed to the bimodal distribution were sensitive to the difference between conditions**. >> At both six and eight months, bimodal infants looked longer on non-alternating trials than on alternating ones. >> Unimodal infants looked about equally on both. It is **as if the infants in the unimodal condition had learned to treat stimuli 3 and 6 as members of the same category**. >> A few minutes of exposure to a distribution changed how infants grouped the sounds, which supports statistical learning of speech categories.
18|There is experimental evidence that **social interaction is important for learning**. **Nine-month-old American infants** were exposed to **Mandarin Chinese** in **twelve 25-minute sessions**. >> Some infants had **live exposure**, with a Mandarin speaker playing and reading with them in person. Others heard Mandarin through **auditory or audiovisual** recordings, and a control group heard English. >> Later, infants were tested on a **Mandarin phonetic contrast** with the **head-turn technique**. Only infants with **live** exposure discriminated it better than the English controls. >> Infants who heard the same Mandarin through **TV** or **audio** scored like the controls. **The same exposure to a Mandarin speaker is more effective for learning when it happens in person and is interactive than when it is viewed as a video**. || A second experiment manipulated **social responses**. **Eight-month-old infants** received feedback from their mothers in response to their vocalizations. >> In the **contingent condition**, mothers responded right after the infant vocalized. >> In the **yoked-control condition**, mothers responded on a schedule copied from another infant, so the responses were **noncontingent**. >> Each session moved from a baseline phase to a social response phase and then to an **extinction** phase, in which the responses stopped. || Infants in the contingent group produced far more syllable-like sounds during the social response phase, and they stayed higher even after the responses stopped. The noncontingent group barely changed. >> **Contingent social feedback increased the quantity and complexity of infants’ vocalizations**. || **Social interactions contingent on the production of different sounds seem to work much like a reinforcing stimulus**. >> **One idea is that humans find social interaction with other humans innately reinforcing**. >> **Another is that we are innately good at using social cues to learn from other people**.
19|**Statistical learning with constraints** means learning from patterns in the input in a way that is guided and limited. **Constraints** can mean a lot of things. >> One kind limits **which stimulus properties can be contrastive**. A **contrastive** property tells words apart, such as voicing in /b/ versus /p/. **Speaker** identity and **speech rate** change the sound a lot but do not change which word is spoken, so they are **not** contrastive. >> A learner who tracked every pattern would form categories for different voices and speeds. Learning has to focus on the properties that carry meaning. || A second kind is **based on earlier learning**. >> What has already been learned shapes what is easy to learn next. >> Once native categories exist, new distinctions that cut across them are harder to notice and learn. Constraints like this are central to **sensitive periods**, when earlier experience limits later learning. || A third kind comes from **social and interactive contexts for learning**. >> Sounds are learned better in live, responsive interaction than from a recording. >> Caregivers’ contingent responses shape the sounds infants produce. || Learning depends on the patterns in the input, on what the learner already knows and on the social setting of the input. Together these constraints explain why learning is fast and why it settles on the distinctions that matter.
