2|**Babies are overrated** as language learners. If **sensitive periods** for a second language, or **L2**, are taken to mean that infants, toddlers and children learn language “better” than adults, it is important not to get carried away. >> A typical child **by 18 months** understands “in and out” and “off and on,” points to several body parts when asked, **uses at least 20 words**, answers simple questions such as “Where’s teddy?” and “What’s that?” with words or gestures, shows some pretend play such as giving teddy a drink, makes at least four different consonant sounds such as b, n, d, g, w and h, enjoys being read to, and points to pictures with one finger. >> An adult who **only knew about 20 words after 18 months of language instruction** would count as a very poor learner. || **Adults initially learn more quickly than children**, mostly because of two advantages. >> **Transfer from L1**, the first language, which lets adults build on what they already know about how language works. >> **Better general cognitive skills**, such as memory, attention and reasoning. || **Where adults do worse is ultimate attainment**, the final level of skill they eventually reach. >> Adults **rarely become native-like** in a second language. >> That too may be **due in part to transfer**, since the first language that gives adults a fast start can also keep them from native-like skill. >> Early learning speed and final success are separate measures, and children and adults rank differently on each.
3|Several toddler **“language milestones”** are really **general conceptual abilities**. >> Grasping **“in and out”** and **“off and on”** rests on understanding **spatial relations between objects**. >> **Pretend play**, such as giving teddy a drink, rests on **event representations**, mental models of who does what in an event. >> A toddler meeting these milestones is forming new ideas about the world along with new words. || **Later learners of a second language already have much of this in place**. >> An adult learning Spanish already has the concepts behind “in” and “on.” >> Adults can **focus on learning how these concepts are represented in the target language**, the language being learned, which is a smaller job than building concepts and words together. || Later learners **also already know another language**, and that affects **how the second language is learned**. >> The first language offers ready-made ways to express concepts. >> Some of those ways fit the second language and help, while others clash with it and lead to errors.
4|**Transfer** is **the use of native-language, or L1, processes and representations for a second language, or L2**. >> A learner carries L1 habits for perceiving sounds, building sentences and retrieving words into the L2. >> The learner comes to the L2 with a complete working language system already in place. || **Transfer can have both positive and negative effects on second-language learning and performance**. >> **Positive transfer** occurs when an L1 pattern matches what the L2 needs, so shared sounds, words and structures give a head start. >> **Negative transfer** occurs when an L1 pattern conflicts with the L2. Errors appear wherever the languages differ, because the learner’s habits point the wrong way. || In one cartoon, a man in a pub asks **“Do you not be happy with me as the translator of the books of you?”** >> The words are English, but the structure follows another language’s grammar. >> Translating word by word carries over the first language’s word order and phrasing, which is negative transfer in grammar.
5|Transfer shapes **phonetic and phonological learning**, the learning of speech sounds. **Categorical perception** produces **acquired similarity** for sounds in one category. **Acquired similarity** means that sounds a language groups together come to sound more alike with experience in that language. || **Minimal pairs in Hindi** make the problem clear. A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in just one sound. >> Two words both sound like “tap” to an English speaker. One means **hot**, and the other means **a kind of beat**. >> Two words both sound like “dab.” One means **pressure**, and the other means **a kind of fruit**. >> Two words both sound like “ari.” One means **saw**, the tool, and the other means **crooked**. >> Each pair contrasts a **dental** consonant, made with the tongue against the teeth, with a **retroflex** consonant, made with the tongue curled back toward the roof of the mouth. || **Native English speakers tend to use the English “alveolar” category to interpret both dental and retroflex consonants**. An **alveolar** consonant, such as English /t/ or /d/, is made with the tongue on the ridge behind the teeth. >> English has only the alveolar category, so both Hindi sounds fall into it and seem identical. || **In this case, transfer makes learning the L2 contrast more difficult**. >> The English category was learned perfectly well, but now it hides a difference that Hindi uses to tell words apart. >> The learner must learn to separate sounds that the first language trained them to treat as one.
6|Transfer also shapes **grammar learning**. **Classifiers** are a **somewhat unusual grammatical feature across languages**. A **classifier** is a word that must come between a number or demonstrative and a noun, and which classifier is used depends on the kind of thing the noun names. >> The pattern is **demonstrative pronoun, then nominal classifier, then noun**, as in **yí gè rén**, “one person,” with **gè** as the classifier. || Chinese has many classifiers, each tied to a kind of noun. >> **Gè** is the **general classifier**. >> **Bǎ** goes with **objects that have a handle**. >> **Běn** goes with **books and similar objects**. >> **Kuài** goes with **objects that are divided into pieces**. >> **Liàng** goes with **wheeled vehicles**. >> **Tái** goes with **devices and gadgets**. >> **Xiē** marks an **indeterminate amount**, or goes with the **plural** of pronouns and demonstratives. >> **Zhāng** goes with **flat objects**. >> **Zhī** goes with **long, thin objects**. || **Native English speakers learning Chinese have difficulty learning these**. >> In English, saying **“a chair,” “that dog,” “the car”** or **“one book”** never requires noticing what kind of thing the noun names. >> **In Chinese, different classifiers are required by different nouns**, so the learner has to attend to properties such as shape and function. || In learning English grammar, **English speakers have learned to ignore the different cues for classifiers**. Those object features play no role in English, so learning Chinese means paying attention again to cues English taught them to disregard.
7|**Grammatical gender** is another place where **transfer causes problems**. **Grammatical gender is different from social or cultural gender**. It sorts nouns into classes, often labeled masculine and feminine. >> **There is nothing “male” about a book or “female” about liberty**, yet French **le livre**, the book, is masculine and **la liberté**, liberty, is feminine. >> More French examples are masculine **le cheval** (the horse), **le chien** (the dog) and **le bruit** (the noise), and feminine **la colombe** (the dove), **la chemise** (the shirt) and **la maison** (the house). || **Grammatical gender has implications for agreement**. **Agreement** means related words must match the noun’s gender, so **pronouns, adjectives, some forms of verbs** and other words **all need to be coordinated with noun gender**. >> English nouns have no grammatical gender, so English speakers have no habit of tracking it. || French is known for **synonyms with different genders**. >> **Auto** and **voiture** both mean car, and one is masculine while the other is feminine. >> **Rivière** and **fleuve** both mean river, feminine and masculine. >> **Vélo** and **bicyclette** both mean bicycle, masculine and feminine. >> Gender therefore has to be learned one word at a time. || **Word endings** offer statistical hints about gender. >> **Masculine nouns** often end in **-age**, as in le fromage (the cheese), **-r**, as in le professeur (the teacher), **-t**, as in le chat (the cat), or **-isme**, as in le capitalisme. >> **Feminine nouns** often end in **-ie**, as in la boulangerie (the bakery), **-ion**, as in la nation, **-ité** or **-tié**, as in la fraternité (brotherhood), **-ance**, as in la balance (the scales), or **-nne, -mme** and **-lle**, as in la fille (the girl) and l’indienne.
8|**Transfer is not all bad**, because **vocabulary learning in some L1–L2 situations is aided by the similarity of words across languages**. >> French **auto** looks like English “auto” and means a car. >> French **rivière** looks like English “river,” and French **bicyclette** looks like English “bicycle.” >> Japanese **コロッケ, korokke**, means a croquette, and Japanese **モノレール, monoreru**, means a monorail. || Sometimes the resemblance is **due to shared etymology**, meaning a shared word history. >> Words with a common origin are **cognates**, such as French rivière and English river, which descend from the same older word. || Sometimes the resemblance is **due to more recent “borrowing.”** >> A **loan word** is a word taken from another language. >> Japanese korokke and monoreru were borrowed from European languages and reshaped to fit Japanese sounds. || Cognates and loan words give **positive transfer** for vocabulary. >> The learner already knows a similar form with a similar meaning, which anchors the new word. >> Someone who knows river can link it to rivière right away, so the new word is easier to store and recall.
9|**Not all similar-sounding words are cognates**. **The bias to interpret L2 words as related to similar-sounding L1 words can be very strong**, and **sometimes the result is embarrassing**. >> A **false friend** is a word that looks or sounds like a word in another language but means something else. || Spanish **“Arnold está embarazado”** does not mean that Arnold is embarrassed. >> **Embarazado** means **pregnant**. >> An English speaker going by the sound of embarrassed would badly misread the sentence. || The link to the familiar word forms before the learner checks the meaning. >> If the meanings match, as with cognates, the link speeds learning. >> If they do not, the same link produces errors. A helpful cognate and a misleading false friend differ only in whether the meanings really match.
10|**De Groot and Keijzer (2000)** tested in the lab whether people **use cognates in “second language” learning**. >> The task was **paired-associate learning of made-up words**. In **paired-associate learning**, people study pairs and later recall one member of a pair when given the other. >> The made-up words **varied in their similarity to their Dutch partners**, so some resembled the Dutch word and some did not. >> They **also varied in “concreteness.”** A **concrete** word names something that can be seen or touched, and an **abstract** word names an idea. || Combining the two factors produced four kinds of pairs, which lets each factor be measured on its own. >> **Gitaar–gettar**, “guitar,” is **concrete and cognate-like**. >> **Tijger–duunze**, “tiger,” is **concrete and non-cognate**. >> **Domein–domaan**, “domain,” is **abstract and cognate-like**. >> **Herstel–groop**, “repair,” is **abstract and non-cognate**. || **Participants were simply presented with pairs of Dutch words and their nonword partners and asked to learn the pairs**. >> They were Dutch speakers, so Dutch played the role of the first language and the made-up words played the role of a new second language. >> **They were then tested a week later on retention**, that is, on how well they remembered the pairs after a week without study.
11|The results show that **cognates are learned more rapidly and also remembered more easily**. Recall was tested over three training sessions and again in a fourth session **after one week with no training**. || Recall improved across the three training sessions for every kind of word, but the starting points differed a great deal. >> **Concrete cognates** were easiest from the start, with **69.7%** recalled after the first session. >> **Concrete non-cognates** started much lower, at **43.7%**, and abstract non-cognates started lowest of all. >> By the third session, most kinds of words were recalled well. || The week without training separated the kinds of words again. >> Concrete cognates kept most of what had been learned. >> **Abstract non-cognates** fell sharply, to **36.0%**. >> Responses were also faster for cognates in every session. || This is evidence that **cognate status can contribute to the very earliest stages of word learning in a second language**. >> The cognate advantage was largest in the first session and reappeared after a week, when non-cognates were forgotten more. >> Concreteness also had a large effect, and the two factors add together. Abstract non-cognates have neither source of support and were the hardest of all. >> One explanation for concreteness effects is called **context availability**, the idea that concrete words are easier to link with a supporting context.
12|The experiment deserves a closer look. **If de Groot and Keijzer had only tested “receptive” vocabulary**, meaning **how easily participants could produce the Dutch word given its made-up partner**, **this would not be very impressive**. >> Receptive testing shows the new word and asks for the familiar Dutch word. >> A cognate-like new word already resembles the Dutch word, so a participant could answer just by spotting the resemblance. || **Many of the meanings, such as gitaar, tijger and domein, could be guessed without any Dutch training**, since they resemble English guitar, tiger and domain. The **concrete cognates** in the study are very close to their Dutch partners. >> **Broer–breur**, **brood–brodde**, **gitaar–gettar**, **jongen–jange** and **lepel–lippel**. >> **Lichaam–lechem**, **naald–nadel**, **paraplu–parredu**, **rivier–riffer** and **schaar–skare**. >> **Stier–stire**, **trein–trinen**, **varken–vark**, **viool–vialo** and **vrouw–vrauk**. >> Each made-up word is a slightly altered Dutch word. || The study **also tested “expressive” vocabulary**, which the authors call **“productive” testing**. >> Productive testing shows the Dutch word and asks for the made-up word. >> The learner must recall the exact new form, such as gettar, from many near possibilities. >> That removes the shortcut, so a cognate advantage on productive testing reflects genuine learning of new forms.
13|A second comparison looks at **productive testing** and **receptive testing**, both **immediately after learning** and **at retest a week later**. The key measure is how much recall dropped over the week. || In **productive testing**, learners had to produce the new word. >> Concrete words were forgotten less than abstract words. >> Cognates lost about **16 points** over the week, while non-cognates lost about **30 points**. || In **receptive testing**, learners had to give the Dutch word. >> Scores were higher overall, because recognizing a familiar word is easier than producing a new one. >> Cognates lost only about **8 points**, while non-cognates again lost much more, about **28 points**. || Cognates and concrete words were forgotten less in **both** directions, so their advantage extends from initial learning to long-term retention. >> The cognate advantage held up in productive testing, where the learner had to recall the new form itself. >> Similarity to a known word helped learners form a lasting memory of the new word.
14|A central **research question** is whether **similar L1 words are always activated when processing L2**, and **there have been many, many studies of this**. >> When a bilingual hears a second-language word, first-language words that sound alike might become active too. >> That activation could happen automatically, even while the person tries to use only the second language. If it does, the two languages are never fully separate in the mind. || **Marian and Spivey (2003)** give **one clear example** with a **“visual world” experiment**. >> In this kind of experiment, people hear spoken instructions while looking at a set of objects, and their eye movements are tracked. >> Glances toward an object whose L1 name sounds like the spoken L2 word would reveal that the L1 word became active. >> Eye movements capture that activation without asking for any report, while the person still goes on to pick the correct object.
15|The design follows **McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)**, who studied **gradient effects of within-category VOT on lexical access**. >> Participants saw a **lamp**, a **ship**, a **bear** and a **pear**, and heard a word from a continuum running between “bear” and “pear.” >> Bear and pear differ only in their first consonant, while the lamp and ship are unrelated fillers. || **Eye movements reveal competition between possible responses that participants are considering**. >> Someone can click on the bear after glancing briefly at the pear. >> Those glances mean “pear” was active as a competitor. || **The amount of competition between BEAR and PEAR depended on distance from the category boundary**. >> Sounds near the /b/–/p/ boundary drew more looks to the competitor, and sounds far from it drew fewer. >> Eye tracking therefore measures how active an alternative word is, even when the final choice is correct. >> The same method can ask whether a word in one language competes with a similar word in another.
16|**Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** demonstrated the **“cohort effect.”** A **cohort** is the set of words that share the same beginning sounds. >> Participants viewed a grid containing pictures and shapes and followed spoken instructions. >> The display included a **beaker**, the target, a **beetle**, which shares its beginning with beaker, a **speaker**, which rhymes with beaker, and a **carriage**, which is unrelated. || Looks to the **beaker** and the **beetle** rose together early, because both fit the first sounds of the word. >> Once the sounds that separate the two words arrived, looks to the beetle dropped away, while looks to the beaker kept climbing. >> Looks to the **speaker** rose later and stayed smaller, because its overlap comes at the end of the word. >> Looks to the carriage stayed near zero. || The **cohort effect** is this early competition from words that share a beginning with the target. >> Words compete as soon as they match the incoming sounds. >> They drop out when the input stops matching them, and eye movements track this rise and fall moment by moment.
17|**Marian and Spivey** tested the cohort effect with **“cross-language competitors.”** A cross-language competitor is an object whose name in the **other language** begins like the spoken target word. || **If L1 words are always activated, native Russian speakers should look at a book of matches when the target is “speaker” in English**. >> English **speaker** starts with the same sounds as Russian **спички, spichki**, which means **matches**. >> If Russian is active during English listening, the matches should draw looks. || For **English targets**, each target came with an **English competitor** and a **Russian competitor**. >> **Speaker** came with English **spear** and Russian **spichki**, matches. >> **Boot** came with **book** and **buben**, tambourine, and **shark** came with **shovel** and **sharik**, balloon. >> **Chair** came with **chess set** and **cherepaha**, turtle, and **marker** came with **marbles** and **marka**, stamp. >> **Barbed wire** came with **bark** and **barhat**, velvet, and **plug** came with **plum** and **platye**, dress. >> **Gun** came with **gum** and **gaika**, nut, and **lock** came with **lobster** and **lak dlea nogtei**, nail polish. || For **Russian targets**, the roles were reversed. >> Russian **spichki**, matches, came with English **spear** and Russian **spitsi**, knitting needles. >> Russian **marka**, stamp, came with English **marker** and Russian **marlya**, cheese cloth. >> Russian **platye**, dress, came with English **plum** and Russian **plashch**, raincoat. || The tie between a target and its cross-language competitor is only **sound**. >> Speakers and matches share no meaning. >> The overlap exists only for someone who knows Russian, so looks to the matches during an English task directly measure activation of spichki.
18|Russian–English bilinguals first followed instructions in **English**, which tests **competition during L2 spoken word recognition**. >> When only the **between-language competitor** was present, it drew looks on **18%** of trials. >> An unrelated control object drew looks on only **7%** of trials. || **When only one competitor was present, participants looked at within- and between-language competitors to the same extent**. >> The Russian name of the matches pulled the eyes as strongly as a similar English word did. || **When both competitors were present, participants looked at the within-language competitor more**, **but still looked at the between-language competitor a lot**, about **twice as often as the control condition**. >> With both side by side, the English competitor won out. >> The Russian competitor still drew far more looks than an unrelated object. || The first language therefore stays active during second-language listening. Bilinguals still choose the correct object, but L1 words compete on the way there.
19|The same bilinguals then followed instructions in **Russian**, which tests **competition during L1 spoken word recognition**. >> The within-language competitor, a Russian word, still drew extra looks, so the cohort effect appeared in Russian. >> An object with a similar **English** name drew looks on **8%** of trials, compared with **5%** for the control object. || **English words do not seem to be activated when native Russian speakers do this experiment in Russian**. >> The **small numerical difference between the between-language and control conditions** is **not statistically significant**. >> A difference that is not statistically significant falls within ordinary chance variation. || The two experiments together produce an **asymmetric** pattern. >> Russian words became active during English tasks. >> English words did not become reliably active during Russian tasks. >> The asymmetry fits the idea that the first language is more firmly established and more easily activated than the second.
20|**The Revised Hierarchical Model** of **Kroll and Stewart** explains **L1–L2 interactions in terms of spreading activation**. **Spreading activation** means that activating one representation partly activates the representations linked to it. >> The model has an **L1** vocabulary, an **L2** vocabulary and a store of **concepts**, or meanings. >> **Lexical links** join L1 and L2 words directly, and **conceptual links** join each vocabulary to the concepts. || **The L1 lexicon is assumed to be larger and more strongly connected to relevant concepts**. >> First-language words have strong links to their meanings. >> Second-language words have weaker links to their meanings. || **The L2 lexicon is assumed to have strong forward connections to the L1 lexicon**. >> The lexical link from L2 to L1 is strong, and the link from L1 to L2 is weak. >> **Activation can spread more readily from an L2 word to an L1 word than the other way**. || **This occurs because this direction is emphasized in learning**. >> Learners usually attach each new L2 word to its L1 translation, which builds a strong path from L2 words to L1 words. >> The direct path from L2 words to concepts stays weaker, at least early on, and it can grow stronger as proficiency rises.
21|The **Revised Hierarchical Model of Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** accounts for the Marian and Spivey results. During the **L2** task with the target “speaker,” several things happen in the model. >> **The concept for “matches” partially activates the Russian word for matches**, spichki, because L1 words are tightly linked to their concepts. >> The sounds of English **speaker** also partly activate spichki, since the two words start alike. >> **This activity feeds back to the conceptual activation for “matches.”** >> **Conceptual activation for matches leads to eye movements toward the L1 competitor**, the matches. || Speaker and spichki are linked only by **sound overlap**. >> They share no meaning. >> The extra activation of spichki boosts the concept of matches, which pulls the eyes toward the matches. || **When the task is done in L1**, **the conceptual links with L2 might be too weak to create this kind of interference reliably**. >> In the Russian task, an English competitor would need strong links from the English word to its concept. >> The model gives L2 words only weak conceptual links, too weak to drive looks to their objects. >> The imbalance in looks follows directly from the imbalance in links.
22|The Revised Hierarchical Model makes a **counter-intuitive prediction** even for **“balanced bilinguals,”** people who are about equally skilled in both languages. >> It might seem that a balanced bilingual is **“two monolinguals in one,”** with each language running on its own. >> The model predicts the reverse. || For a balanced bilingual, **the conceptual links are similarly strong for both languages**. >> Each concept strongly activates its word in both languages, so both vocabularies wake up whenever either is used. >> **Competitive dynamics and inhibition will be engaged all the time**. **Competitive dynamics** means that words from the two languages keep competing for selection, and **inhibition** means suppressing the word from the language not in use. || This prediction **has led to a lot of work on bilinguals’ advantages in nonlinguistic cognitive control tasks**. >> **Cognitive control** is the ability to direct attention and hold back irrelevant responses. >> Bilinguals who constantly practice choosing one language and suppressing the other might get better at controlling attention in general, even on tasks unrelated to language.
23|Second-language learning usually follows a familiar sequence. >> A person **begins learning L1**. >> Later, the person **learns L2**. >> Meanwhile, the person **continues learning and using L1**, so both languages stay in use. || **What happens when someone stops using L1 after L2 is introduced?** >> Here L1 is learned first and L2 later, but use of L1 stops. >> L1 is no longer heard or spoken, and L2 takes its place. || This situation pulls apart two things that normally go together, learning a language early and continuing to use it. >> If early learning lays a permanent foundation, traces of L1 should survive without use. >> If ongoing use is required, L1 might fade or vanish.
24|**Pallier, Dehaene, Poline, LeBihan, Argenti, Dupoux and Mehler** used **brain imaging of language plasticity in adopted adults** to ask **whether a second language can replace the first**. >> They tested **adults aged 20 to 32** who **had been adopted into French homes from Korean orphanages between the ages of 3 and 8**. >> These adults learned Korean as young children and then grew up in homes where only French was spoken. >> They stopped using Korean altogether after adoption, which makes them a natural case of L1 use stopping. || The question was whether the adoptees **had any residual knowledge of Korean**, meaning any leftover trace of the earlier language. >> French speakers with no exposure to Korean served as the comparison group. >> If adoptees beat the French speakers on Korean-related tasks, some Korean knowledge must have survived. >> Whatever survived would reveal what early learning leaves behind once a language falls out of use.
25|Korean has a **three-way stop contrast**. A **stop** is a consonant made by fully blocking the airflow and then releasing it, such as /p/, /t/ or /k/. >> English and French have two kinds of stop at each place in the mouth, such as /b/ and /p/. >> Korean has three kinds at each place, **aspirated**, **plain** and **tense**. An **aspirated** stop comes with a strong puff of air, a **plain** stop has a lighter release, and a **tense** stop is made with a stiffened throat. || The contrast appears at three places of articulation, in recordings from the UCLA Phonetics Archive. >> At the lips, the **bilabial** series is **pʰul**, “grass,” **pul**, “fire,” and **p’ul**, “horn.” >> At the teeth, the **dental** series is **tʰal**, “mask,” **tal**, “moon,” and **t’al**, “daughter.” >> At the back of the tongue, the **velar** series is **kʰo**, “nose,” **kok**, “music,” and **k’ok**, “exactly.” || The words in each series share a place of articulation and differ only in stop type. >> French has no such contrast, so French speakers tend to hear the three Korean sounds as two, while Korean speakers hear three distinct sounds. >> Adoptees who kept any Korean sound knowledge should hear these differences better than French speakers do.
26|**Ventureyra and colleagues** found that **Korean adoptees in France were no better than monolingual French speakers** at discriminating the Korean contrasts. Listeners heard pairs of sounds and judged whether they were different. || The test pairs used the Korean stop contrasts, plain versus tense, plain versus aspirated, and tense versus aspirated, along with a plain–tense contrast for s. >> Korean speakers heard these differences nearly every time. >> Adoptees and French speakers rarely heard them, and the adoptees were no better than the French speakers. || Two control conditions checked general performance. >> On a **vowel** contrast, all three groups heard the difference nearly every time. >> On pairs of **identical** sounds, all groups correctly said “same” almost every time. || The adoptees could do the task, as their vowel results and their correct “same” responses confirm. >> The difficulty was specific to the Korean stop contrasts. >> On those contrasts, the adoptees heard the differences at the same level as people who had never heard Korean, far below Korean speakers.
27|In **a more extensive study**, **Pallier and colleagues** found that **adoptees were no better than monolingual French speakers** on three tasks, each tapping a different kind of knowledge. >> **(a) Distinguishing Korean from a collection of largely unrelated languages** tapped familiarity with the overall sound of Korean. Participants rated how much each sentence sounded like Korean. Both groups rated Korean and Japanese sentences as similarly Korean-like, and gave lower ratings to Polish, Swedish and Wolof. Adoptees did not pick out Korean any better. >> **(b) Identifying the correct Korean translation of French words** tapped memory for vocabulary. Both groups scored close to chance. >> **(c) Deciding whether a short excerpt came from a sentence they had just heard** tapped sensitivity to sound sequences. Both groups did best on French, and they did equally well on Korean, Japanese and Polish, with no advantage for Korean among the adoptees. || **These are not standard psycholinguistic tasks**. >> Pallier and colleagues **were looking for any remaining trace of Korean**, using tasks designed to catch even weak knowledge. >> They **did not find any**. On every measure, the adoptees looked like people who had never learned Korean.
28|Pallier and colleagues also scanned the participants’ brains. In both groups, **listening to French activated the classical language network, including superior temporal and inferior frontal regions**. >> The **superior temporal** region, in the upper temporal lobe, handles speech sounds. >> The **inferior frontal** region, in the lower frontal lobe, includes Broca’s area. || For each group, brain activity for French was compared with activity for **Polish**, **Japanese** and **Korean**. >> French always produced more language-network activity than any of the foreign languages. >> This held for the **French group** and for the **Korean group**, the adoptees. || **Subtracting Korean from French might have revealed some difference between groups**, if there were **any residual processing ability for Korean left**. >> Adoptees who still processed Korean as a language would show more language-network activity for Korean than French speakers would, which would shrink the French-minus-Korean difference. >> **It was not** so. The adoptees’ brains treated Korean as a foreign language, much like Polish and Japanese, and the imaging agrees with the behavioral tests.
29|**L2–L1 interactions** can also be framed as **competitive dynamics**. >> **L1 can be thought of as a set of connections within and between different brain regions**. >> Use builds these connections and keeps them in place. || The process seen in **monocular deprivation** in vision could also apply to language. >> When one eye of a mouse is deprived, its connections first become unstable, with **increased spine motility**. >> Next come **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration**, the breakdown of nerve fibers cut off from input. >> After more than a week, the other eye’s fibers show **axon sprouting and new spine birth** and take over the vacated space. >> The eye that keeps getting input wins the connections the deprived eye loses. || **If the L1 connections are never activated again, they could be “taken over” by L2**. >> L2 is used constantly, so its connections grow stronger and spread. >> L1 goes unused, so its connections weaken and become available to L2. >> On this account, L2 replaces L1 by winning a competition for the same neural resources.
30|Asked whether **a second language can replace the first**, the evidence answers **“Apparently yes!”** The adoptees showed no behavioral or brain sign of their childhood Korean. >> As far as these tests could detect, French had fully replaced Korean. || The open question is whether **traces of L1 are left behind that can be “salvaged.”** >> **Salvaging** a trace means recovering or making use of some lingering effect of the earlier language. >> Such a trace might not appear on a test of present ability yet could still make relearning easier. || Testing present recognition and testing later learning ask different questions. >> The first asks what the person can do with the language right now. >> The second asks whether new learning profits from something left over, and relearning is the measure that could expose such a trace.
31|**Terry Au and colleagues** at UCLA studied this question in **“Overhearing a language during childhood,”** by **Au, Knightly, Jun and Oh**. >> They studied **undergraduate students who overheard Spanish frequently as children but never learned to speak it**. >> **All participants started classroom instruction of Spanish at around age 14** and **were tested at the end of a second-year college Spanish course**. || The overhearers were compared with **native speakers** and **typical late L2 learners**. On measures of pronunciation, the overhearers came out far ahead of the late learners. >> Their **voice onset time** for voiceless stops matched native speakers almost exactly, about **19.3** milliseconds for natives and **19.6** for overhearers. Late learners produced much longer, English-like values, about **36.2** milliseconds. >> They also softened voiced stops between vowels more like native speakers. This softening is called **lenition**. >> Their **accent ratings** fell between native speakers and late learners, with native speakers rated about **4.3** on a 5-point scale. >> They knew some childhood slang that late learners did not. || On grammar, the overhearers had little or no advantage. >> In **gender agreement** and **number agreement**, they performed about like late learners. >> Their **grammaticality judgments** were no more accurate than those of late learners. || Childhood overhearing gave a clear advantage in **pronunciation** but not in grammar. Early listening left traces in the sound system that were unmasked when the students later studied Spanish, while grammar seems to need more than overheard input.
32|Combining **the data from Korean adoptees together with the data from overhearers** yields a picture. >> **If L1 is removed from the environment entirely, L2 can appear to completely replace it**, as the adoptees showed no Korean on any test of present ability. >> **But this replacement is not necessarily complete**. **There may be some residual knowledge that can be “unmasked” with later learning**, as with the Spanish overhearers’ pronunciation. || The two groups also differ in **potentially important ways**. >> The adoptees were tested on what they knew, with no new Korean instruction. >> The overhearers were tested after two years of Spanish classes, which gave hidden knowledge a chance to surface. >> These and other differences make the two sets of results hard to compare directly.
33|Several **potentially important differences between these populations** might explain why one group kept traces of an early language and the other did not. >> **Korean is rarely heard or encountered in France**, but **Spanish is common in Los Angeles**, so overhearers may have kept hearing Spanish all their lives, which could keep their sound knowledge alive. >> A **“heritage language”** spoken by family members differs from **a language not spoken by anyone you know**. Overhearers often heard Spanish from relatives, while adoptees lost all contact with Korean speakers. >> **Cognates** are plentiful between Spanish and English but scarce between Korean and French, and shared vocabulary could help overhearers relearn. >> **Shared writing system**. Spanish and English use the same alphabet, while Korean uses a script different from French. || **This is a general issue** in research on **second language acquisition and bilingualism**. >> **Languages and learning situations differ in many, many ways that are hard, perhaps impossible, to control experimentally**. >> Researchers cannot assign people to grow up with particular languages in particular settings. >> Separating these factors would take groups that differ in just one way, which real language histories rarely provide.
