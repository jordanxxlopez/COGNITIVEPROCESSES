2|**Second-language, or L2, learning** works as **a model system for sensitive period research**. A **model system** is a common, easily studied situation used to answer questions about a process that is hard to study directly. || **Lenneberg (1967)** proposed the influential idea that **the sensitive period had to do with the development of cerebral dominance**, also described as **developmental changes in language laterality**. >> **Cerebral dominance** means one hemisphere, usually the left, becomes specialized for language. >> **Language laterality** is how much language relies on one side of the brain instead of both. >> On this idea, the language-learning window shuts once language settles into the left hemisphere. || Two kinds of evidence led to the theory. >> **A rough alignment of the literature on brain development with new insights about language milestones**. Cortical neurons grow quickly for about two years and then level off, and no measurements were available from age 2 to 18. Lenneberg tied the end of fast brain growth to the start of language learning. >> **A review of the literature on resilience after brain injury or surgery**. Children who lost the left hemisphere often went on to develop language, while adults did not regain it. || The theory is about the brain’s **maturational state**, meaning its stage of physical development. A young, less specialized brain can move language to other tissue, and a mature, specialized brain has fewer ways to do so.
3|**Much of what is known about first-language, or L1, sensitive periods comes from research on idiosyncratic cases or relatively rare phenomena**. **Idiosyncratic** means particular to one individual. >> The evidence includes children recovering from surgery that removed a hemisphere, and Genie, who was cut off from language until age 13. >> Every one of these cases carries other unusual circumstances, such as injury, illness or abuse, so the true cause of an outcome is hard to identify. || **In contrast, many people learn second languages at different times in their lives, often with somewhat limited success**. >> Second-language learners are plentiful, so researchers can study large groups instead of single rare cases. >> They begin at every age from early childhood to late adulthood, and their final success can be measured. || **The limits of L2 success have also been used to reason about the timing of the sensitive or critical period**. >> If later starters reliably end up at lower levels, the age where the decline appears could mark when the sensitive period closes. >> The first language complicates matters, since every L2 learner already knows a language and that knowledge shapes how the new one is learned.
4|**Johnson and Newport** examined **critical period effects in second-language learning**, in a paper on **the influence of maturational state on the acquisition of English as a second language**. **Lenneberg (1967) hypothesized that language could be acquired only within a critical period**, running from early infancy to puberty. >> In its basic form, the hypothesis concerns only first-language acquisition. >> Whether it also applies to second-language acquisition is an important open question. >> If it does, young children should learn a second language better than adults and reach higher final proficiency. || The researchers compared the **English proficiency of 46 native Korean or Chinese speakers** who had **arrived in the United States between the ages of 3 and 39**. >> When tested, they had lived in the United States **between 3 and 26 years**. >> A **grammaticality judgment task** probed **a wide variety of structures of English grammar**. || Earlier arrivals held **a clear and strong advantage** over later arrivals. >> **Test performance was linearly related to age of arrival up to puberty**, so each extra year of age at arrival meant a lower score. >> **After puberty, performance was low but highly variable and unrelated to age of arrival**. || The age effect was **not an accidental result of differences in amount of experience with English, motivation, self-consciousness or American identification**. >> It **appeared on every grammatical structure tested**, even though later learners mastered some structures far better than others. >> The authors concluded that **a critical period for language acquisition extends its effects to second-language acquisition**.
5|**Johnson and Newport** built a **grammaticality judgment test**, or **GJT**. **Participants listened to recorded sentences and simply had to say whether each sentence was grammatical or not**, and **the test was untimed**. >> A **grammaticality judgment** is a decision about whether a sentence obeys the rules of the language. >> Being untimed, the test let participants take as long as they liked. || Every item had a grammatical version and an ungrammatical version, marked with an asterisk, and each pair targets one rule of English. >> **(1a) “The farmer bought two pigs at the market”** and **(1b) “The farmer bought two pig at the market”** test **plural marking** on nouns. >> **(2a) “The little boy is speaking to a policeman”** and **(2b) “The little boy is speak to a policeman”** test the **progressive -ing** form of the verb. || Several pairs target articles and other **determiners**. >> **(6a) “Tom is reading a book in the bathtub”** and **(6b) “Tom is reading book in the bathtub”** test whether a needed article is present. >> **(7a) “The boys are going to the zoo this Saturday”** and **(7b) “A boys are going to the zoo this Saturday”** test whether the determiner agrees with the noun in number. >> **(8a) “Larry went home after the party”** and **(8b) “Larry went the home after the party”** test a spot where English forbids an article. || Other pairs target questions. >> **(23a) “When will Sam fix his car?”** and **(23b) “When Sam will fix his car?”** test **wh-question** word order, where the helping verb comes before the subject. >> **(24a) “What do they sell at the corner store?”** and **(24b) “What they sell at the corner store?”** test the need for the helping verb “do” in a question.
6|**The data** relate each participant’s score on the grammar test to their **age of arrival**, or **AOA**. The participants are split at puberty, which marks the end of the critical period in Lenneberg’s proposal. >> A **correlation**, written r, measures how closely two variables are related, from −1 to +1. A value near −1 means one variable falls steadily as the other rises. A value near 0 means little relationship. || Among **early arrivals**, aged **3 to 15**, the correlation was **r = −0.87**. >> Children who arrived youngest scored near the maximum. >> Scores fell steadily with each year of later arrival. >> Age of arrival predicts most of the score in this group. || Among **late arrivals**, aged **17 to 39**, the correlation was only **r = −0.16**. >> Scores were lower overall and widely scattered. >> Some adults scored nearly as well as older child arrivals, while others who arrived at the same age scored far lower. >> Age of arrival predicts little about who does well among adults. || The pattern combines a strong age effect in childhood with lower, highly variable performance in adulthood.
7|The **Johnson and Newport data** have three main features. >> **AOA**, age of arrival, is strongly related to accuracy, with earlier arrival going with higher scores. >> **Early learners, up to about age 8, are often near-perfect**, meaning **native-like**, scoring close to native English speakers. >> Late arrivals show a **nearly random distribution of accuracy**, since among adults age of arrival barely predicts the score. || The first two features concern learners who arrived as children. >> The youngest arrivals reach native-like grammar. >> Arrivals between about 8 and 15 reach steadily lower levels, so the decline begins well before puberty. || A **“nearly random distribution”** has a specific meaning here. >> It means that among late arrivals, age of arrival and score are only weakly related. >> Late arrivals scored well above chance, but individual scores varied enormously. Two adults who arrived at the same age could end up far apart.
8|**Johnson and Newport argue that these data are consistent with a sensitive period** for L2 learning, though they call it a **“critical period.”** The argument has two parts. || **First, the dependence of ultimate attainment on AOA is consistent with a loss of language-learning ability over development**. >> **Ultimate attainment** is the final level a learner reaches. >> Scores fall steadily as childhood arrival age rises, which suggests that language-learning ability declines as children mature. >> **The timing is not entirely consistent with Lenneberg’s hypothesis**, which put the end of the critical period at puberty, while the decline here starts around age 7 or 8. >> Lenneberg **was working with a large gap in the data between early childhood and adolescence**, with no brain measurements between ages 2 and 18, so his timing was only a rough estimate. || **Second, attainment in late acquisition is all over the place**, and adults who arrived at the same age reach very different levels. || **After the sensitive period closes, attainment depends on idiosyncratic learning abilities, not on the usual language acquisition processes**. >> **Idiosyncratic learning abilities** are individual differences in skills such as memory, motivation or analytical skill. >> Children share one natural acquisition process, so they reach similar levels at similar ages. >> Adults have lost that process and must lean on their own abilities, which differ a great deal between people.
9|The **“critical period” hypothesis** comes in **two versions**. || **Version one is the exercise hypothesis**. >> **Early in life, humans have a superior capacity for acquiring languages**. >> **If the capacity is not exercised during this time, it will disappear or decline with maturation**. >> **If the capacity is exercised, however, further language-learning abilities will remain intact throughout life**. >> An adult who learned an L1 normally should then learn an L2 as well as a child does. || **Version two is the maturational state hypothesis**. >> **Early in life, humans have a superior capacity for acquiring languages**. >> **This capacity disappears or declines with maturation**, whether or not it has been used. >> Even a person with a normal L1 should then learn an L2 less well as an adult. || Every Johnson and Newport participant had a normal first language, yet later starters learned the L2 less well. >> That result supports the **maturational state hypothesis**, which is the idea named in the paper’s title. || This prompts the question of **whether the L1 critical period data should be interpreted differently**. >> In cases like Genie, the capacity was never exercised, and both hypotheses predict poor learning for such cases. >> Only second-language data can separate the two versions, because only L2 learners have already exercised the capacity before learning another language.
10|**Test design** raises a **methodological issue**, because **the grammaticality judgment task is very different from fluent language use**. >> In fluent use, people speak and understand quickly without stopping to consider rules. >> In a grammaticality judgment task, people weigh each sentence and decide whether it is correct. || **One concern is the use of “metalinguistic” or “explicit” knowledge**. **Metalinguistic knowledge** is conscious knowledge about language, such as knowing that English plurals add “-s.” || **Ellis (2005)** compares **implicit** and **explicit knowledge** on several dimensions. >> **Awareness**. Implicit knowledge brings **intuitive awareness** of linguistic norms, and explicit knowledge brings **conscious awareness**. >> **Type of knowledge**. Implicit knowledge is **procedural**, a knowing-how of rules and fragments. Explicit knowledge is **declarative**, a knowing-that about grammatical rules and fragments. >> **Systematicity**. Implicit knowledge is variable but systematic, and explicit knowledge is often anomalous and inconsistent. >> **Accessibility**. Implicit knowledge is reached through **automatic processing**, and explicit knowledge through effortful **controlled processing**. >> **Use of L2 knowledge**. Implicit knowledge serves **fluent performance**, and explicit knowledge serves a speaker who is **planning** or struggling. >> **Self-report**. Implicit knowledge is **nonverbalizable**, so people cannot explain it, and explicit knowledge is **verbalizable**. >> **Learnability**. Implicit knowledge is **potentially only learnable within the critical period**, and explicit knowledge is learnable at **any age**. || An untimed GJT lets people draw on explicit knowledge, so it may miss the implicit knowledge the critical period is thought to affect. A learner who consciously applies a classroom rule can earn the same score as one who knows the rule implicitly.
11|One suggestion is that **the task demands of the untimed GJT make it a better measure of explicit learning of “metalinguistic” knowledge than of grammar acquisition**. **Ellis (2005)** compared the **design features** of five tests. >> **Imitation** and **oral narrative** rely on feel, are done under time pressure, and focus on meaning. They require no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **timed GJT** relies on feel and is done under time pressure, but focuses on form. It requires no metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **untimed GJT** relies on rules, is done without time pressure, and focuses on form. It **does** allow metalinguistic knowledge. >> The **metalanguage** test, which asks people to state rules, also relies on rules, has no time pressure, focuses on form and requires metalinguistic knowledge. || Ellis then related each test to two background measures, the age when people started learning and their years of formal instruction. Only two relationships were significant. >> Starting age was related to the **timed GJT**, with a correlation of **−0.23**. >> Years of formal instruction were related to the **untimed GJT with ungrammatical items**, with a correlation of **0.27**. >> The other tests showed only weak relationships with either measure. || **Consistent with this, untimed GJT performance is correlated with years of formal education in L2**. >> Formal classroom instruction teaches explicit rules, which help on the untimed test. >> Starting age matters for the timed test, which limits the use of explicit rules. >> An age effect found with an untimed GJT may therefore partly reflect explicit, taught knowledge rather than natural grammar acquisition.
12|**Transfer** raises another **methodological and theoretical issue**. **Transfer** means using first-language knowledge while learning a second language. >> **Languages that are typologically more similar can benefit more from transfer**. **Typological** similarity is similarity in structure, such as word order and word forms. >> Cognates and loan words, such as French **auto**, **rivière** and **bicyclette**, and Japanese **korokke** and **monoreru**, hand learners familiar words to build on. || **Johnson and Newport tested people with English as their L2 and Chinese or Korean as their L1**. >> Chinese and Korean differ greatly from English in grammar and vocabulary. >> Those learners could get little help from transfer. || The open question is **what would happen if the languages were more similar**. >> Speakers of a language close to English could reuse much of what they know. >> Their scores might relate to age differently. If so, the age pattern in Johnson and Newport’s data would depend on the specific languages involved as well as on age.
13|**Not all aspects of the grammar are equally difficult**. Johnson and Newport compared errors on **12 types of English rules** across native speakers and groups who arrived at different ages. >> The rule types are **determiners**, **plurals**, **verb subcategorization**, **past tense**, **pronouns**, **particles**, **auxiliaries**, **third-person singular**, **wh-questions**, **yes/no questions**, **word order** and **progressive -ing**. **Verb subcategorization** covers which kinds of words or phrases may follow a given verb. >> Native speakers and the youngest arrivals make very few errors of any type. >> The latest arrivals struggle most with **determiners** and **plurals**. >> **Word order** and **progressive -ing** cause few errors for anyone. || **Some of this looks like the order in which children learn language**, because **progressive -ing and word order are easiest** for late learners and are among the first structures children master. || **Some could be transfer**. >> **Chinese has no determiners**, so Chinese speakers have no L1 category for “a” and “the,” which makes English articles especially hard. >> **Children learn questions very late, but questions are among the easiest structures for late learners**. Adults may bring general cognitive skills or L1 knowledge to question forms. >> How hard a structure is depends on the structure itself and on what the learner’s first language provides.
14|**Birdsong and Molis (2001)** repeated the Johnson and Newport study with **Spanish speakers learning English**. >> For **Chinese and Korean speakers**, in Johnson and Newport’s data, early arrivals had a strong correlation with age, **r = −0.87**, and late arrivals had a weak one, **r = −0.16**. >> For **Spanish speakers**, early arrivals had only a weak correlation, **r = −0.23**, and late arrivals had a strong one, **r = −0.69**. || The Spanish speakers showed almost the reverse pattern. >> **Early arrivals** scored near the top regardless of age, with only a slight decline even for those arriving in their mid-teens. >> **Late arrivals** declined steadily with age, so adults who arrived later reached lower levels. || This raises the question of **what these results mean for the idea that AOA effects on GJT performance reflect maturational constraints**. **Maturational constraints** are limits on learning caused by the brain’s development. >> If a fixed maturational schedule controlled learning, the pattern should look the same for every first language. >> Instead, the pattern changed with the learners’ first language. >> Spanish is much closer to English than Chinese or Korean, so transfer may help Spanish speakers reach native-like grammar even when they arrive late in childhood. The age effect depends on the language pairing as well as on age.
15|Calling these findings **“conclusions”** may go too far. **Overall, L2 attainment is lower for older learners**, and that much holds across studies. || **How much lower depends on several factors**. >> **What constructions are tested**, since some structures, such as determiners, are far harder for late learners than others, such as word order. >> **How they are tested**, since timed and untimed tests can disagree, with untimed tests leaving room for explicit knowledge. >> **Language pairings**, since Spanish speakers and Chinese or Korean speakers show different age patterns in learning English. || **It is hard to separate** several factors that usually travel together. >> **Age of acquisition from the amount and quality of experience**. Younger arrivals usually get more years of exposure and more immersion, such as schooling in the language. >> **Transfer effects from L2 acquisition**. L2 performance reflects what was learned and also what carries over from the L1. >> **Performance from competence**, generally speaking. **Competence** is underlying knowledge of a language, and **performance** is how well that knowledge is used on a given task. A score measures performance, which may not reveal competence directly.
