2|Speech is **messy**, because **there are no physical boundaries between words** in the speech signal. **Word segmentation** is the job of working out where one word stops and the next one starts in continuous speech. >> In an utterance such as **“I hope it’s in my desk,”** the sound energy of each word flows straight into the next word. >> No silent gap separates the words from one another. || **Where there are pauses in the speech stream, they come from closures that are as likely to happen within words as between them**. >> A **closure** is a moment when the vocal tract is fully blocked, as for the consonants /p/, /t/, /d/ and /k/. Very little sound escapes during a closure, so a brief silence appears. >> In “hope” and “it’s,” these silences fall inside words or at their edges, and they do not match the word boundaries. >> A listener who treated every silence as a boundary would split some words and merge others. || **We are nonetheless really good at finding words in this “sea of sounds.”** Adults hear separate words with no effort, although the signal does not mark them. >> The **harder question** is **how we learn to do this**. >> Adults can lean on the words they already know. Infants have to discover the words in the stream before they can learn what those words mean.
3|Listeners have **several cues to word boundaries**, but no single cue works for every word. >> Some words **appear in isolation**, as in **“Hello.” “No.” “Please.” “Go.” “Stop.”** >> **Not all words** are ever heard alone, so this cue covers only part of the vocabulary. || **Phonotactics** describes which sound sequences a language allows. **Some sequences of phonemes are very unlikely to occur within words**. >> In **“wordboundary,”** the sequence /db/ almost never occurs inside an English word, so it suggests a break between word and boundary. >> In **“speechcode,”** the sequence /tʃk/ suggests a break in the same way. >> **“Movement”** calls for caution, since its /vm/ sequence might suggest a break even though movement is one word. || **Stress patterns** give another cue. **In English, most words are stressed on the first syllable**, according to **Jusczyk and colleagues (1999)**. A **stressed** syllable is louder, longer or higher in pitch. >> **7.5-month-old infants** first heard a single word repeated and then heard passages. One passage kept using “kingdom,” as in “The prince used to sail to that kingdom when he came home from school.” Another kept using “device,” as in “My new red device makes ice cream.” >> Infants who had heard **“kingdom,”** with first-syllable stress, **showed a preference for the kingdom passage**. >> Infants who had heard **“device,”** with second-syllable stress, **did not show the same kind of preference**. || In connected speech, **stress may help signal the beginnings of words**. An infant who starts a new word at every stressed syllable finds kingdom easily but cuts device in the wrong place. >> **This will not work in every language, or for every word in English**, since languages put stress in different places.
4|**Transitional probabilities** offer one more cue to word boundaries, through **statistical learning**. **Statistical learning** means picking up patterns in how often things occur together. >> The phrase **“pretty baby”** makes the idea concrete. || In English, the syllable **/ti/** is **very strongly predicted by the syllable /prɪ/**. >> After “pre,” the next syllable is very often “ty,” since pretty is a common word. >> This is **less likely to be true for syllable pairs across words**. Many different words can follow “ty,” so “ba” is poorly predicted. || The comparison can be written with frequencies, where **f** stands for frequency of occurrence. >> Dividing the frequency of “pretty” by the frequency of “pre” gives the chance that “pre” is followed by “ty.” >> Dividing the frequency of “tyba” by the frequency of “ty” gives the chance that “ty” is followed by “ba.” \[\frac{f(\mathrm{pretty})}{f(\mathrm{pre})}\gg\frac{f(\mathrm{tyba})}{f(\mathrm{ty})}\] >> The first value is far larger than the second. Syllables inside pretty predict each other strongly, and syllables that straddle pretty and baby predict each other weakly. || A learner who counts how often each syllable follows each other syllable can find the joints in speech. Syllable pairs inside a word recur together again and again, while pairs that cross a word boundary recur much less reliably.
5|In general terms, **the likelihood of syllable B following syllable A is greater within words than between words**. That likelihood is the **transitional probability** from A to B. >> Inside a word, the same syllables always come in the same order, so the probability is high. >> At a word boundary, almost any word can come next, so the probability is lower. >> A drop in transitional probability is therefore a good guess for a word boundary. || **Of course this is probabilistic**, so it works most of the time but not every time. >> **“What’s that in the road a/head?”** can be heard as “a head” or as “ahead.” The sounds are identical, and the meaning of the whole sentence decides the boundary. >> **“I scream, you scream, we all scream for ice cream”** plays on “I scream” and “ice cream” having almost the same sounds, so one sequence can be split into words two ways. || Transitional probabilities tip the odds toward one way of splitting the stream. They work alongside stress, phonotactics and words heard alone. >> When the cues agree, segmentation is easy. >> When cues clash or are missing, as with a head and ahead, listeners fall back on context.
6|**Saffran and colleagues (1996)** separated **statistical information from other cues** to find out whether transitional probabilities by themselves are enough to identify words. The stimuli were **tightly controlled** for this purpose. >> The speech was **monotone**, with **no changes in pitch, duration or amplitude**. Amplitude means loudness. With none of these changes, stress could not mark where words begin. >> Every syllable was a **simple CV syllable**, one consonant followed by one vowel. That left **no phonotactic cues**, since no unusual consonant clusters ever occurred. >> The syllables were **presented continuously**, with **no pauses**, so silence could not signal a boundary. || The researchers built an **artificial language** of **three-syllable “words,”** such as **tokibu, gikoba** and **gopila**. >> These words were joined in random order into one long stream. >> The only clue to where each word began and ended was the transitional probability between syllables. || Infants who found the words under these conditions must have used transitional probabilities alone, because every other cue had been removed.
7|The stream in the artificial language has no breaks at all, as in **“tokibugikobagopilatipolutokibu,” “gopilatipolutokibugikobagopila,”** and **“gikobatokibugopilatipolugikoba.”** >> This is exactly what the infant hears, with no spaces and no pauses. >> Four words, tokibu, gikoba, gopila and tipolu, are buried in the stream, and each recurs many times in random order. || Even with no marked boundaries, the syllables follow regular patterns. >> Inside **tokibu**, “to” is always followed by “ki,” and “ki” is always followed by “bu.” >> After “bu,” though, the stream may go on with “gi,” “go” or “ti,” depending on the next word. || Everything needed to find the words is in the stream. >> A learner who keeps track of which syllables follow which can discover that some pairs are fully predictable and others are not. >> The words come out of the statistics of the sequence.
8|Marking each of the four words separately makes their positions in the stream visible to a reader. >> **Tokibu**, **gikoba**, **gopila** and **tipolu** each appear many times. >> The infant gets no such marking and hears only one unbroken run of syllables. || Each word keeps the same inner order every time, while its neighbors keep changing. >> “To-ki-bu” always comes in that order, but tokibu may be followed by gikoba, gopila or tipolu. >> Transitional probabilities are therefore high inside each word and lower across word edges. || The word edges are exactly where transitional probability falls. >> The signal itself contains no spaces. >> By noticing where predictability drops, the learner recovers where one word ends and the next begins.
9|**Testing for an effect of transitional probabilities** takes some care. **After listening to a long stream of syllables, it is trivial to show that infants are more familiar with sequences they have heard than ones they have not**. Infants could prefer heard sequences just because they are familiar, without using transitional probabilities at all. || **Two additional details** remove that possibility. >> **Half of the words were repeated more frequently than the other half**. >> Test items compared words with **“part-words”** that were **matched for frequency of occurrence** with real words. A **part-word** is a three-syllable sequence that spans a word boundary, joining the end of one word to the start of the next. || In the stream **“tokibu gikoba gopila gikoba tokibu gopila tokibu gikoba gikoba tokibu,”** the word **GOPILA** and the part-word **BU-GIKO** each occur **twice**. They are equally frequent, but their transitional probabilities differ. || The transitional probability of **GI given BU** is **2 out of 4**. >> BU occurs four times, once at the end of every tokibu, but only two of those are followed by GI. \[\frac{2}{4}=0.5\] >> Inside **GOPILA**, both transitional probabilities are **2 out of 2**, because GO is always followed by PI and PI is always followed by LA. \[\frac{2}{2}=1\] || Since frequency is equal, familiarity cannot explain a difference between gopila and bu-giko. Any difference must come from how predictable the syllables are.
10|Testing revealed **a novelty effect**, since **infants looked longer in the direction of “part-word” stimuli**. >> Part-words kept infants’ attention longer than words did. >> The difference was statistically significant. || This longer looking is a **novelty preference**. >> Infants tend to look longer at whatever seems new. >> The words had grown familiar during listening, so they were less interesting. The part-words felt less familiar, even though infants had heard them just as often. || The pattern suggests that infants **had learned the statistical regularities of the speech stream**, and **were not merely picking up on simple frequency of occurrence**. >> Frequency was equal for words and part-words, so only their transitional probabilities could separate them. >> After a brief exposure, infants pulled units out of a continuous stream using nothing but how predictable the syllables were.
11|**Identifying word referents** comes after segmentation. Once children can **“find” the words** in speech, **how do they learn what they mean?** A **referent** is whatever a word refers to. >> A child may hear **“cat”** while looking at a cat. >> Recognizing the sound pattern “cat” is one achievement, and knowing that it names the furry animal is a separate one. || Segmentation delivers a recurring chunk of sound, but it carries no information about meaning. >> Any scene holds many objects, parts, properties and actions. >> The learner needs more evidence to tell which of these the speaker has in mind. || Learning a word therefore means linking its form to the speaker’s intended meaning. The child gathers that evidence from the situation in which the word is used.
12|**The inductive problem of early word learning** is that a single experience fits many possible meanings. **Induction** means reasoning from particular examples to a general rule, and the rule here is a word’s meaning. >> A child hears a new word while watching a cat. It might mean **CAT**, **ANIMAL** or **PET**, which are categories at different levels. >> It might be **FELIX**, this particular cat’s name. >> It might name a property such as **FURRY** or **ALIVE**, an action such as **WALKING**, or a part such as **WHISKERS** or **LEG**. || All the candidate meanings together make up the **hypothesis space**. >> Each candidate fits what the child sees. >> Nothing in one scene singles out the meaning the speaker intends. || This is a form of **Quine’s “gavagai” problem**. >> In Quine’s example, a linguist studying an unknown language hears a speaker say “gavagai” as a rabbit runs by. >> “Gavagai” might mean rabbit, but it might also mean undetached rabbit parts, a stage of a rabbit or something else. >> Children meet this problem with every new word and still learn words fast. Joint attention, cues to the speaker’s intention and default strategies cut down the possibilities.
13|Children learn word meanings partly through **joint attention**, which is **sharing an attentional focus with another person**. >> Two people look at the same object. >> Each knows that the other is attending to it too. || **Gaze following emerges around 9 months of age**. >> **Gaze following** means looking where someone else is looking. >> When an adult looks over at an object, the infant follows the adult’s eyes to it. || Gaze following **signals an understanding of reference**. >> The infant grasps that the adult’s attention is aimed at something specific. >> If the adult then says a word, the shared object becomes the most likely referent. >> Joint attention therefore shrinks the hypothesis space by tying the word to what the speaker is attending to.
14|According to **Baldwin and colleagues**, **joint attention optimizes word learning**. Two groups of infants heard a new word while they played with an object. >> In the **joint attention group**, an **adult interacting with the baby labeled the object the baby was playing with**. >> In the **no joint attention group**, a **voice from behind a screen labeled the object the baby was playing with**. || The two conditions matched closely. >> In each, the word came while the infant was focused on the same object. >> They differed only in whether a visible person was engaged with the infant and the object. || **Eighteen-month-olds learned the novel word in the joint attention group but not in the no joint attention group**. >> Hearing a word while seeing an object was not enough. >> Infants needed evidence that a speaker meant the word to refer to that object. >> The hidden voice gave the same word at the same moment, so the learning difference came from the visible, engaged speaker.
15|Children also narrow the meanings of new words with **intentional cues from the speaker**, such as **eye gaze direction**. An **intentional cue** is a signal of what the speaker means to refer to. >> When a speaker looks toward one toy, the direction of the eyes marks the object of the speaker’s attention. || **Baldwin** taught new words to **16–17-month-olds** and **18–19-month-olds** in two conditions. >> In **follow-in labeling**, the adult **looked at the toy the infant was focused on** while saying the new word, so both cues pointed to one toy. >> In **discrepant labeling**, the adult **looked at a toy in a bucket**, a different toy from the infant’s, while saying the new word. || The discrepant condition pulls the infant’s focus apart from the speaker’s focus. >> Infants who simply attach a new word to whatever they are looking at would link it to their own toy. >> Infants who follow the speaker’s gaze would link it to the toy in the bucket. >> Comparing the two conditions reveals whether infants treat the speaker’s gaze as evidence of meaning.
16|**Gaze direction** helps a child work out which object a speaker is naming when several objects are present. >> A speaker who looks at one object while saying a new word ties the word to that object. >> The child can follow the speaker’s eyes to the intended referent. || A speaker’s focus can be different from the child’s own. >> Following it means using information about someone else’s attention. >> The child can then attach a new word to an object it was not looking at when the word was spoken. || Gaze narrows the possible meanings even though the word’s sound gives no clue. >> The child combines the new word with evidence of what the speaker intends. >> Following gaze rests on understanding that a speaker’s eyes point to what the speaker means, which produces a far more specific mapping than linking the word to everything in view.
17|Intentional cues include **affective and behavioral cues** from the speaker. **Affective** cues are emotional expressions, and **behavioral** cues are the speaker’s actions. >> An expression can reveal whether an object is the one the speaker was searching for. >> An action, such as reaching for something, can reveal what the speaker wants. || **Akhtar and colleagues** found that **infants used adults’ facial expressions to identify the intended referent of a novel word**. >> The adult’s emotional reaction to each object signaled whether it was the one meant. >> Infants linked the new word to the object that the adult’s expression picked out. || Several objects may be present when a word is spoken, and the speaker’s reaction can single out one. >> Satisfaction signals that the speaker found the desired object, and disappointment signals that an object is not the one meant. >> Word learning therefore involves grasping the goal behind the interaction.
18|A speaker’s **emotional reaction** works as an **intentional cue** that can pick out the intended object from several candidates. >> Satisfaction or excitement can tie one object to what the speaker was trying to find or do. >> The learner can use that tie to interpret a new label. || **Intention is conveyed through more than gaze direction**. >> Expressions and actions also reveal what a speaker means. >> A word may be said near several objects that are not all equally likely referents. || The helpful information lies in how the adult responds to each object. >> Connecting the label to that response shrinks the word’s **hypothesis space**, the set of meanings it might have. >> Infants who use emotional reactions are following the speaker’s goals. The word attaches to the object that meets the goal, and objects that disappoint the speaker drop out.
19|Children also bring **word learning strategies** to the task. These are **default strategies that children bring to the word learning situation**. >> A **default** is a starting assumption that holds until evidence says otherwise. >> These strategies **function to narrow the hypothesis space for new words**, which gives children a place to start when other cues are weak. || The **whole object assumption** is one example. >> It leads a child to expect a new word to name **a whole object**, not a part of it, its color, its texture or something it is doing. >> A child who hears a new word while looking at a cat will favor “cat” over “whiskers,” “furry” or “walking.” || This assumption rules out many unlikely meanings at once, and it fits the way adults usually name objects for children. >> It is only a starting preference, and later evidence can override it. >> Children do eventually learn words for parts and properties once the evidence points that way.
20|**Mutual exclusivity**, from **Markman (1989)**, is another word learning strategy. >> **As a default, objects can have only one name**. >> The **implication** is that **new words map to nameless objects**. >> Mutual exclusivity **emerges during the second half of the second year**, roughly between 18 and 24 months. || Suppose a child knows the word “dog” but has no word for a parrot, and both animals are in view. >> When the child hears a new word, the child takes it to mean the parrot, since the dog already has a name. >> No gaze or emotional cue is needed for this choice. || Mutual exclusivity combines with the whole object assumption. >> The whole object assumption says a new word names an object, and mutual exclusivity says it names an object that has no name yet. >> Because it is a default, later learning can override it when an object turns out to have two names.
21|With **mutual exclusivity**, a child who hears an unfamiliar word gives it to an object that has no known name yet. >> An object the child can already name is an unlikely target for a new word. >> The choice comes from comparing the objects in view. || **Prior vocabulary** determines which objects count as already named. >> Two children in the same scene can guess differently if they know different words. >> The strategy builds directly on earlier learning. || Vocabulary growth therefore feeds itself. >> A child who knows many names can rule out many objects. >> The unnamed objects left over become the likeliest referents for new words, so every word the child knows helps narrow the meaning of the next.
22|**How do children start putting words together?** **In production, it can be hard to tell what is going on when children produce only one, two or three words at a time**, since very different processes can produce the same short utterance. || **Menn** separates early utterances into four kinds. >> **“Just a bunch of relevant words.”** The child strings together words that fit the situation, with no grammar linking them. >> **Formulas**, phrases memorized as whole units without the child knowing they have separate parts. >> **Word combinations**, in which the child joins separate words productively to make new combinations. >> **Stable constructions**, consistent patterns the child uses to build utterances. They are **not necessarily adult-like**. || A child might say **“Up!”**, **“Pick you up!”** and **“Mommy up!”** >> “Pick you up!” sounds more advanced, but it may be a formula copied whole from adult speech. >> “Mommy up!” is shorter, but it may be a real combination of two separate words. >> Utterance length alone cannot tell which process produced each one. || **A full description of the context in which an utterance is produced is necessary, but often not sufficient, to distinguish among these**. >> Context reveals what the child intends and how the utterance is used across situations. >> Even with complete context, the source of a given utterance can remain uncertain.
23|**Comprehension** and **production** develop at different rates. **Even children at the one-word stage in production are able to use basic transitive word order in comprehension**. >> In the **one-word stage**, a child speaks only in single words. >> A **transitive** sentence has a verb with a subject and an object. English transitive order puts the actor first and the person acted on after the verb. || The **structure-mapping account** of **early syntactic bootstrapping**, from **Gertner and Fisher**, describes how children manage this. **Syntactic bootstrapping** means using sentence structure to help learn meaning. >> From the **input sentence** **“The boy is tickling the girl!”** the child builds a **partial sentence representation**, **tickle[N1 = boy, N2 = girl]**, which records the verb and the two nouns in order. >> From the **observed scene** of a boy tickling a girl, the child builds **conceptual representations** of possible events, such as **Giggle(agent = girl)**, **Tickle(agent = boy, patient = girl)**, **Play(agent = boy)** and **Play(agent = girl)**. An **agent** performs an action, and a **patient** is affected by it. >> A **one-to-one mapping between nouns and participant roles** lines up the two nouns with the two participants in the tickling event. Two nouns fit an event with two participants. || Comprehension can therefore get ahead of production. >> A child who says only single words can still use noun order to tell who did what. >> Matching nouns to the participants in a scene helps the child figure out what the verb means.
24|A **generalization test** checks whether children have truly learned the **transitive structure**. **If children have really learned the transitive structure, they will generalize it to new verbs**. >> A child who has only memorized particular sentences will be lost with an unfamiliar verb. >> A child who knows the structure can use word order to interpret any verb. || Picking out the scene that fits **“The duck is gorping the bunny”** requires knowing that the first noun is the doer and the second is the one acted on. **“Gorp”** is invented, so only noun order can help. || **Gertner and colleagues (2006)** found that **two-year-olds can understand this**. >> Children first heard “Hey look! The duck’s gonna gorp the bunny!” >> Two scenes then appeared side by side. In one, a person dressed as a duck pushed a person dressed as a bunny in a wagon. In the other, the bunny acted on the duck. Children heard “The duck is gorping the bunny! See?” >> After a short break they heard “The duck gorped the bunny! Find gorping!” and the scenes appeared again. >> Children **looked significantly longer at the duck acting on the rabbit than the reverse**. || Children this age usually have a **mean length of utterance**, or **MLU**, of **around 2**. MLU is the average number of **morphemes**, meaningful words or word parts, in each utterance. >> Their three-morpheme utterances might sound like **“Duck gorp bunny.”** >> Their own speech is short and incomplete, yet they understand the full transitive structure.
