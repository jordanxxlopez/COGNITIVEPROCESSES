2|**“The horse raced past the barn fell”** is grammatical English, even though it usually feels broken the first time through. The trouble comes from the way the opening words push the reader toward the wrong structure. >> Most readers take **raced** as the main verb, so “the horse raced past the barn” seems to be a complete event in which the horse did the racing. >> When **fell** shows up, that structure has no room for it, and the sentence seems to collapse. || The sentence actually describes **two events**. Someone raced the horse past the barn, and afterward the horse fell. >> The intended meaning is **“The horse that was raced past the barn fell.”** >> In this reading, “raced past the barn” picks out which horse is meant, and **fell** is the main verb telling what happened to it. || Restoring the missing words **disambiguates** the sentence, as in “the horse that was raced past the barn fell.” >> “That was” makes the modifying relationship explicit, so raced can no longer be read as the main verb. >> Understanding a sentence depends on assigning relationships among its words, not only on knowing what each word means. || It could fairly be called **the sentence that launched a thousand experiments**, since researchers have used it over and over to study how readers build structure as words come in.
3|**“The horse raced past the barn fell”** is nearly as famous as **“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously.”** >> The colorless green ideas sentence is grammatical but makes no sense. >> The horse sentence is grammatical and makes sense, but it is very hard to process. || It is the classic case of the **“garden path” effect**. A **garden path sentence** steers the reader into one reading early on, and later words reveal that reading to be wrong. >> The name comes from the saying “to lead someone down the garden path,” meaning to mislead. >> Raced looks like the main verb, and fell then clashes with the structure already built, so the reader must go back and treat “raced past the barn” as “that was raced past the barn.” >> Garden paths arise because readers build structure word by word, committing to one analysis before the sentence is over. || One comic strip jokes that nobody would ever say such a sentence, and the reply is that farmers might, if their horses kept falling over. >> The joke works only because the sentence really is grammatical, even though it sounds wrong. || **Grammaticality and processing difficulty are different things**. >> A sentence can have a perfectly acceptable structure and still be very hard to follow as it unfolds. >> The garden path is about which analysis the reader picks first and how hard that analysis is to replace.
4|**Syntactic explanations** of the garden path effect begin with a **parser**, a mental process that builds a **syntactic tree** for each sentence we hear or read. A syntactic tree records how words group into phrases and how the phrases relate to each other. || In the correct tree, the subject is a large noun phrase, or **NP**, that contains “the horse” plus a reduced clause meaning “that was raced past the barn.” >> “That” and “was” are silent in the actual sentence, and the clause also has an empty subject position. >> Inside the clause, raced takes the prepositional phrase, or **PP**, “past the barn.” >> The main verb phrase, or **VP**, contains only **fell**. || In the failed parse, “the horse” is an ordinary NP and “raced past the barn” is the main VP, with raced as the main verb. >> **Fell** is then left with nowhere to attach, and the parse breaks down. || Any parser has to work inside a mind with **limited memory** and **time constraints**. >> Words come quickly, so the parser cannot wait for the end of the sentence before building structure. >> It can keep only a limited number of structures in play at once.
5|The **grammatical version** of the horse sentence is **much more complex syntactically** than the failed parse, because it has **many more nodes**. A **node** is a point in a syntactic tree that stands for a grammatical grouping, such as an NP, a VP or a sentence. >> The correct tree needs extra nodes for the embedded clause and for the silent “that” and “was.” >> The failed parse simply makes “raced past the barn” the main VP, which takes far fewer nodes. || One space-saving strategy is to **initially prefer smaller trees**, a strategy called **“minimal attachment.”** >> Under minimal attachment, each new word is fitted into the existing structure with the fewest possible new nodes. >> Fewer nodes means less structure to build and keep in memory at that moment. || In the horse sentence, minimal attachment favors **raced as the main verb**, because that reading needs the fewest nodes. >> The reading where “raced past the barn” modifies horse needs an embedded relative clause, so the parser passes it over at first. >> The smaller structure lightens the immediate load but leads down the garden path. When fell arrives, it demands the larger structure that was passed over, and the reader must rebuild the tree.
6|**“Late closure”** is another parsing strategy in this framework. It is a bias to **keep the current clause “open” as long as possible**, so new words get attached to the phrase under construction. >> Keeping a clause open means adding incoming words to it instead of closing it and starting something new. || In **“The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite,”** the phrase **“with the dynamite”** wants to join the big **verb phrase** that begins with **“blew open.”** Frazier (1987) contrasts two structures for the sentence. >> In the **preferred** structure, the PP attaches to the **VP**. This **verb-phrase attachment** makes the dynamite the **instrument** used to blow open the safe. >> In the **unpreferred** structure, the PP attaches to the **NP** “the safe.” This **noun-phrase attachment** makes the phrase describe which safe was blown open, the one that had the dynamite. || Both attachments are grammatical, but they say different things. >> Late closure keeps the verb phrase open, so the new phrase joins it. >> Here the preferred VP attachment is also the minimal attachment, since it needs fewer nodes than building an extra NP around “the safe with the dynamite.” >> For this sentence, the preferred parse happens to be the sensible one, so readers reach the right meaning easily.
7|Late closure shows its effect in a sentence that **requires the “unpreferred” parse to be sensible**, such as **“The burglar blew open the safe with the jewels.”** >> Jewels make a poor tool for blowing open a safe. >> The sensible meaning is that the burglar blew open **the safe that contained the jewels**, which requires attaching “with the jewels” to the noun phrase “the safe,” the unpreferred NP attachment. || **Careful tests of reading time and eye movements** reveal that people are **slower to read sentences where the sensible reading requires the unpreferred parse**. >> Reading time measures how long readers spend on each part of the sentence. >> Eye movement records track where readers look and when they jump back to reread. >> The extra time shows up right where the parse has to change. || Readers **momentarily entertain the idea that the jewels were used to blow open the safe**, and then must **retrace their steps**. >> Following its usual preference, the parser first attaches the phrase to the verb phrase. >> The meaning of jewels clashes with the instrument reading, so the parser moves the attachment to the noun phrase. >> The final meaning is easy, but getting there carries a measurable processing cost.
8|This syntactic explanation depends on a **parser that deals exclusively with the abstract syntactic structure** of the sentence. It parallels the **“lumber mill”** model of thresholded processing in speech perception. >> In a lumber mill, each stage works on its own material and hands a finished product to the next stage. >> In the same way, the parser builds structure from grammatical categories alone, without checking meaning or context, and meaning is computed afterward from the finished structure. || The parser has **its own operating characteristics**. >> It aims for **the simplest tree possible**, which is **minimal attachment**. >> It does this partly by **leaving clauses open as long as possible**, which is **late closure**. >> Both preferences limit how much structure has to be built and held at once. || This handles ambiguity **very efficiently**, by choosing to **ignore ambiguity until it becomes a problem**. >> The parser commits to one likely structure and builds only that one, and most of the time the first choice is right. >> When a later word fails to fit, the structure must be revised. That revision causes garden path difficulty, which is the price of an efficient strategy.
9|A **purely syntactic explanation** runs into **problems**, and one is that **syntactically “difficult” sentences are not always difficult to parse**. >> Many published sentences contain **reduced relative clauses**, relative clauses with “that was” or “that were” left out, as in “the horse raced past the barn fell.” || Examples come straight from research articles, with the verb that opens each reduced relative written in capitals. >> “Thus, a noun phrase **FOLLOWED** by a **MORPHOLOGICALLY AMBIGUOUS VERB** … will be temporarily ambiguous,” from Trueswell, Tanenhaus and Garnsey (1994). >> “Referential information **PROVIDED** by the discourse is of no help,” from Britt, Perfetti, Garrod and Rayner (1992). >> “Recent research **REPORTED** by **FERREIRA AND CLIFTON** (1986) has demonstrated that syntactic processing is quite independent and that the initial syntactic analysis **ASSIGNED** to a sentence is little influenced by the semantic information **ALREADY ANALYZED**,” from Frazier and Rayner (1987). >> “The pattern of results **OBTAINED** with global reading times is reflected by differences **LOCATED** on the critical disambiguating phrase,” from Altmann and Steedman (1988). || Every capitalized verb begins a reduced relative with the same structure as the horse sentence. **But these are not impossibly difficult sentences**, and readers follow them easily without any garden path. >> In “recent research reported by Ferreira and Clifton,” reported describes research that someone reported. || **MacDonald, Perlmutter and Seidenberg (1994)** argue instead that **semantic factors and statistical regularities interact with parsing from very early on**. >> Semantic factors are about meaning, and statistical regularities are patterns in how often words occur in particular structures. >> Particular words and their familiar uses can favor a reduced relative reading right from the start.
10|The collection of reduced relatives is **wonderfully passive-aggressive**, because **many of its examples are drawn from papers that use the difficulty of parsing reduced relatives as evidence for an autonomous parser**. An **autonomous parser** builds syntactic structure with no help from meaning or context. >> Those authors argued that reduced relatives are hard to process. >> Yet they wrote reduced relatives in their own articles, and readers understood them without trouble. || More examples make the same point. >> In “a noun phrase **FOLLOWED** by a morphologically ambiguous verb,” followed modifies noun phrase. >> In “the initial syntactic analysis **ASSIGNED** to a sentence,” assigned modifies analysis. >> In “differences **LOCATED** on the critical disambiguating phrase,” located modifies differences. >> “A person **PRESENTED** with the evidence **EXAMINED** by the lawyer may interpret the evidence as a theme, not an agent, because examined requires an animate agent,” from Ferreira and Clifton (1986). Presented modifies person, and examined modifies evidence. || These were **not the only reduced relatives** in those articles. >> They turned up at about **one per journal page** across the sample. >> Reduced relatives are therefore routine in scientific writing, and in a supportive context a formally complex structure is easy to grasp.
11|When we read **“Recent research reported by…,”** several factors beyond syntax are at work. >> **Semantics**. Research is **inanimate**, not a living thing, so it is an unlikely **subject** for a verb like **report**. Treating research as something that was reported fits the word meanings. >> **Lexical statistics**. **“Reported” occurs frequently in reduced relatives**, and lexical statistics are learned patterns of how often particular words appear in particular structures. >> **Lexical statistics and “world knowledge.”** “Reported” is especially common in reduced relatives **in scientific papers, especially when following the word “research,”** and world knowledge here means knowing how people actually write in that setting. || These and **many other factors interact with the syntax of the sentence very early on**. They act from the first moments of reading and help decide which structure gets chosen at all. || A sentence pair makes the role of meaning clear. >> **“The burglar arrested in the parking lot was charged today.”** Burglars tend to be arrested, so arrested is easily read as starting a reduced relative about the burglar. >> **“The policeman arrested in the parking lot was charged today.”** Policemen tend to do the arresting, so arrested is first read as the main verb, and “was charged” sets off a garden path. >> The two sentences share the same grammar. The difference in difficulty comes from what we know about burglars and policemen.
12|**“World knowledge” can be very local**, coming from the visual scene right in front of us as well as from general knowledge. **Spivey and colleagues (1995)** tested this with a visual world task. >> Participants saw real objects, **a pencil, a box, an empty towel, and an apple sitting on a towel**. >> They heard **“Put the apple on the towel in the box”** and carried it out while their eyes were tracked. || With **minimal attachment**, “the towel” would briefly seem to end the clause **“put the apple on the towel.”** >> In that reading, “on the towel” names the **destination** for the apple. >> In the intended reading, “on the towel” says **which apple** to move, the one already on a towel, and **“in the box”** is the destination. || People **do seem to look at the empty towel while planning their movements**, as if treating it as a target, before they plan a movement to the box. >> With one apple in view and the ambiguous instruction, a typical sequence runs from the apple to the empty towel and then to the box. >> With the unambiguous instruction **“Put the apple that’s on the towel in the box,”** the eyes go from the apple straight to the box. || The listener recognizes every word correctly. The error lies in taking the location phrase as the destination, which briefly changes the planned action.
13|In a **two-referent context**, the display holds **two apples**, one on a towel and one elsewhere, along with the empty towel and the box. >> “The apple” by itself no longer says which apple to move. >> **“On the towel”** now does useful work, since it picks out one of the two apples. || Here participants usually look at the apple on the towel and then at the box. >> Looks to the empty towel become much less common. >> **The sequence and the timing of eye movements**, measured against the nouns in the speech, **did not differ** between the ambiguous and the unambiguous instructions. || In this setting, the ambiguous instruction is handled exactly like the explicit one with “that’s on the towel.” >> With two apples in view, the listener needs to know which apple, so a phrase that singles one out is expected. >> Listeners take “on the towel” as describing the apple from the start, and the destination reading never takes hold. The same sentence receives a different grammatical analysis because the visual context differs.
14|Once **two apples** are in view, it suddenly becomes **felicitous**, meaning natural and appropriate, to add information about **which apple** the speaker means. >> With one apple, there is no reason to say which apple, so “on the towel” looks like extra information and is easily heard as a destination. >> With two apples, “on the towel” has a clear purpose, picking out the right apple. || As a result, **people do not look at the empty towel as much**. >> With one apple, the ambiguous instruction drew looks to the empty towel on about **55%** of trials. >> With two apples, the same instruction drew such looks on only about **17%** of trials, close to the level for the unambiguous instruction. || The instruction’s words were the same in both displays, so the visual context alone changed how often listeners were misled. >> This is an effect of **referential context**, the set of objects a phrase could refer to. >> Referential context affects which grammatical relationship listeners assign while they are still hearing the sentence.
15|For **purely syntactic accounts**, the Spivey results are **a big problem**. **Different parses of the same sentence are favored under different semantic and pragmatic conditions, and this effect happens very early**. >> **Semantic** conditions have to do with meaning. >> **Pragmatic** conditions have to do with how language is used in a situation, such as whether a speaker must specify which object is meant. || One ambiguous sentence misleads listeners often when one apple is present and rarely when two are present. >> Its words never change. >> Only the scene changes, and the scene alters the parse the listener adopts first. || The difference appears in **eye movements during the sentence**, before the action begins, so it captures the parse while it is being built. >> If context only repaired the parse afterward, listeners in both displays should first look at the empty towel. >> Because those looks drop with two apples, context must have shaped the first parse, which a parser that always starts with the same structure cannot predict.
16|**Mouse movements** have more recently been used to study these questions. **Farmer and colleagues (2007)** tracked the path of the computer mouse while participants carried out spoken instructions in a version of the apple and towel display. >> It is not just eye movements but **the actual execution of arm movements** that can be influenced by **syntactic ambiguity** and its **interaction with the visually defined context**. >> Mouse tracking records the whole path of the hand, so a competing interpretation can pull the movement toward another location before the hand reaches its target. || With one apple, the hand moved from the apple toward the box, but on ambiguous instructions its path **curved toward the empty towel**. >> The bend was largest in the middle of the movement. >> The hand still ended at the correct place. || With two apples, the paths for ambiguous and unambiguous instructions were nearly identical. The second apple makes the location phrase useful for picking out an apple, so it is no longer taken as a destination. || Researchers also measured the **Euclidean distance between the ambiguous and unambiguous paths** at each moment. Euclidean distance is the straight-line distance between two points. >> With one apple, the two paths grew far apart before coming back together. >> With two apples, they separated less and came together sooner. >> Comprehension and action planning interact while both are underway. A competing interpretation bends the path of the hand even when the movement ends correctly.

18|**The** is a **determiner**, a word that opens a **noun phrase**. Once the parser meets “the,” it expects a noun soon, perhaps with an adjective or another modifier first. >> “The” names nothing on its own. >> It restricts which kinds of words can come next, so the parser can anticipate the shape of the phrase. || The expectation concerns a **grammatical category**, not one particular word. >> Many nouns could follow, such as pizza, dog or book. >> An adjective could also come first, as in “the hot pizza.” >> The parser predicts the form of the phrase while its meaning is still open. Words that fit the prediction go through smoothly, and words that break it cause trouble.
19|**Pizza** is a **noun** that completes the noun phrase opened by “the.” It is the **head of the noun phrase**, the central word that the rest of the phrase is built around. >> “The” and “pizza” together form “the pizza,” which can now serve as the subject of the sentence. >> With a full noun phrase in place, the parser expects a verb or verb phrase saying what the pizza did or what happened to it. || Recognizing the word delivers two kinds of information. >> Its **category**, noun, tells the parser how it can combine with other words. >> Its **meaning**, a kind of food, raises expectations about likely events, such as being eaten or baked. >> Grammatical expectations and meaning-based expectations both shape how the next words are handled.
20|**Was** is a past-tense form of **be**. It can serve as an **auxiliary**, a helping verb that joins another verb to mark grammatical information such as tense or voice. >> In “the pizza was eaten,” was helps build a **passive** with the participle eaten. >> In “the pizza was in the oven,” was links the subject to a phrase giving a location. || Once “was” arrives, the sentence could go several ways, describing where the pizza was, what it was like or what was done to it. >> A location would come next with a preposition, and a passive would come next with a participle such as eaten. >> The parser must keep the growing structure available while more words arrive, because the role of was depends partly on what follows.
21|**In** is a **preposition**, a word that expresses a relationship such as location or containment, and it opens a **prepositional phrase**. >> “In” does not name the location itself. >> It creates the expectation of a noun phrase that supplies the location, as in “in the oven” or “in the box.” || Coming after “the pizza was,” the word “in” narrows the possible structures, and the sentence now heads toward saying where the pizza was. >> The parser expects a noun phrase to finish the prepositional phrase. >> That expectation is grammatical, so the next word must be able to begin a noun phrase, such as “the,” “an” or a noun.
22|**The** opens a new **noun phrase** inside “in the …,” the phrase that will name the location. >> “The” does not supply the location by itself. >> A noun is still required, maybe with modifiers in front, as in “in the hot oven.” || The parser now strongly expects a **noun**, and the expectation concerns a **word category**, not merely a sensible topic. >> Whatever comes next has to work grammatically inside a noun phrase. >> Knowing the sentence is about pizza makes words such as oven or box likely, but the grammatical requirement holds whatever the meaning. A word that cannot belong to a noun phrase will clash with it.
23|**Eaten** is the **past participle** of **eat**. It is a verb form, not a noun, so it cannot finish the noun phrase that “in the” has started. >> **“In the eaten”** puts a verb form exactly where a noun is required. >> That is a **word-category violation**, a word of the wrong grammatical category in a position that demands a different one. || A category error differs from a strange but grammatical meaning. >> “The pizza was in the ocean” is odd in meaning but grammatically fine, since ocean is a noun. >> No unusual situation can rescue “the pizza was in the eaten,” because the problem lies in the grammatical type of the word, which breaks the structure itself. || The contrast between category errors and meaning errors makes such sentences valuable for brain research. Comparing brain responses to the two kinds of error can reveal whether grammar and meaning are handled differently.
24|The **early left anterior negativity**, or **ELAN**, is an **ERP** response triggered by **word class violations**. An **ERP**, or **event-related potential**, is electrical brain activity recorded at the scalp and averaged over many trials, timed to an event such as the start of a word. >> A **word class** is a grammatical category such as noun, verb or preposition. || Classic ELAN sentences contain word-category violations. >> **“The pizza was in the eaten,”** with a participle where a noun belongs. >> **“The scientist criticized Max’s of proof the theorem,”** with the preposition **of** where a noun should follow “Max’s.” || The ELAN has three main features. >> It is **early**, arising about **100–200 milliseconds** after the critical word. >> It is **left and anterior**, strongest over the left front of the scalp, which has raised the question of whether it is specific to language. >> It appears **specifically for word category violations**, such as a noun where a verb is expected or a preposition in place of a noun. || Incorrect sentences produce an early negative response over left frontal sites shortly after the critical word, while correct sentences do not. >> The response comes so fast that it was taken as evidence for a very quick syntactic check of word categories that runs before meaning is processed.
25|With visually presented words, **the ELAN came from visual cortex**, the region at the back of the brain that processes sight. The result comes from **Dikker, Rabagliati, Farmer and Pylkkänen (2010)**, “Early occipital sensitivity to syntactic category is based on form typicality.” Visual cortex sits in the **occipital** lobe. >> For **bimorphemic nouns**, which end in a recognizable noun suffix, **“The beautifully princess …”** drew a larger visual response than **“The beautiful princess …”** about 120 to 130 milliseconds after the noun appeared. >> Beautiful is an adjective, which correctly predicts a noun. Beautifully is an adverb, which predicts a verb or adjective, so princess comes as a surprise. || When a verb is expected, for example, **that prediction can feed back down to the earliest parts of the visual system** and anticipate visual patterns that fit **the way verbs are written**. >> A predicted grammatical category implies likely visual forms, such as typical word endings. >> The very early response to the error might reflect **a mismatch between that predictive process and what finally appears on the screen**. || That raises a question. **Does this only work for items that are morphologically marked as nouns or verbs?** Or does it work for **open-class versus closed-class** words? >> A word is **morphologically marked** when part of it, such as a suffix, signals its category. >> **Open-class** words are content words like nouns and verbs, which keep gaining new members. **Closed-class** words are function words like prepositions and determiners, a small fixed set.
26|Many English words can be either a **noun** or a **verb**, and the rest of the sentence decides which. >> **(3a)** “Chris and Ben are glad that the bird **perches seem** easy to install.” Perches is a **noun** here, meaning the rods birds sit on, and seem is the verb. >> **(3b)** “Chris and Ben are glad that the bird **perches comfortably** in the cage.” Perches is a **verb** here, saying what the bird does. >> **(4a)** “The teacher told the principal that the student **needs were** not being met.” Needs is a **noun**, the student’s requirements. >> **(4b)** “The teacher told the principal that the student **needs to be** more focused.” Needs is a **verb**. || The word form alone does not fix its grammatical role, and the **continuation** after it settles the question. || Words also vary in **phonological form typicality**, how closely a word’s sound pattern matches typical nouns or typical verbs. An analysis of **3,158 words** measured how much each word sounds like nouns and like verbs. >> Nouns generally sound more like other nouns, and verbs more like other verbs. >> Some words run against the trend. Besides **noun-like nouns** and **verb-like verbs**, there are **verb-like nouns** and **noun-like verbs**, words that sound like one category while belonging to the other.
27|In **form typicality**, **“perches” is noun-like** and **“needs” is verb-like**. >> These are learned statistical tendencies in how words sound, and both words can still fill either role. >> Among thousands of English words, perches sounds like a typical noun and needs sounds like a typical verb. || For **perches**, its noun-like form fits one sentence and clashes with another. >> In **“the bird perches seem easy to install,”** perches is a noun, matching its form. >> In **“the bird perches comfortably in the cage,”** perches must be a verb, against its form. || For **needs**, the pattern flips. >> In **“the student needs to be more focused,”** needs is a verb, matching its form. >> In **“the student needs were not being met,”** needs must be a noun, against its form. || Each word appears once as a noun and once as a verb, so the same form can be compared in a matching use and a mismatching use. >> If form typicality guides parsing, the mismatching uses should be harder to process, even though every one of these sentences is grammatical.
28|Reading is **slower when a word’s required category conflicts with its form typicality**. The measure is the extra reading time at **disambiguation**, compared with reading time at the **point of ambiguity**. >> The **point of ambiguity** is the ambiguous word itself, such as perches or needs. >> **Disambiguation** is the point where the continuation makes the intended category clear, such as “seem” or “comfortably.” >> The times are adjusted for word length, and a larger increase signals more difficulty. || For **noun-like homonyms** such as perches, a noun reading added only a small cost, about **50 milliseconds**. A verb reading added a much larger cost, about **120 milliseconds**. >> A noun-like form is harder to resolve as a verb. || For **verb-like homonyms** such as needs, the pattern reversed. A noun reading added about **110 milliseconds**, while a verb reading added almost nothing. >> A verb-like form is harder to resolve as a noun. || The ambiguous word stays the same in each pair, and only the continuation changes. >> The extra cost appears specifically when the continuation demands the category that does not match the form. >> Parsing therefore uses learned statistics about how word forms sound, along with the structure the sentence finally requires.
29|The **ELAN effect for nouns where a verb is expected** is limited to cases where there is either **syntactic information**, morphemes that indicate noun-ness, or **statistical form-based information**, “nouniness,” that is **physically available** in the word. **No effect was observed for neutral nouns like “infant” that lack strong cues**. >> A **morpheme** is the smallest meaningful unit of a word, such as a suffix that marks a noun. >> “Nouniness” is how closely a word’s form resembles typical nouns, even with no noun suffix. || The early brain response studied here is the **M100**, which peaks about 100 milliseconds after a word appears. Three kinds of nouns were compared. >> **Bimorphemic nouns**, as in “The beautiful princess …” versus “The beautifully princess …” Princess carries a noun-marking suffix, and the unexpected version produced a reliably larger response. >> **Typical nouns**, as in “The tasteless soda …” versus “The tastelessly soda …” Soda has a statistically noun-like form, and the unexpected version again produced a reliably larger response. >> **Neutral nouns**, as in “The cute infant …” versus “The cutely infant …” Infant has neither a suffix nor a typical noun form, and the two versions did not differ. || An abstract category mismatch is therefore not enough to trigger this earliest visual response. >> An expected category produces predictions about what the next word will look like. >> The early response appears only when the arriving word carries physical evidence that contradicts that prediction.
30|**Sentence processing can be summarized in four sentences**, each carrying one key idea. >> **“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously.”** **Syntax is independent of semantics to some extent**. At the extreme, a sentence can be grammatical but meaningless, because words can fill a grammatical structure without describing a sensible event. || **“The horse raced past the barn fell.”** A sentence’s syntactic structure is often highly **ambiguous**, or more precisely **vague**, but we notice only where processing fails. >> **Reading times** for preferred and unpreferred structures suggest that syntactic processes **take shortcuts to save memory** instead of activating every possible parse. >> When a shortcut picks the wrong structure, as in the horse sentence, the reader goes down the garden path. || **“Put the apple on the towel in the box.”** **Context, pragmatics and other factors interact with syntactic processing from very early on**. >> With two apples in view, listeners read “on the towel” as describing the apple instead of naming a destination. >> The visual scene shapes the parse while the sentence is still unfolding. || **“The bird perches were comfortable.”** **The very earliest syntactic effects observable in the brain are driven in part by form typicality**, not necessarily by morphosyntactic structures. >> **Morphosyntactic** structure is grammatical structure signaled by word parts and word order. >> These early effects seem tied to **predictions about the physical forms of words**. Perches has a noun-like form, so its use here as a noun matches the prediction its form creates.
