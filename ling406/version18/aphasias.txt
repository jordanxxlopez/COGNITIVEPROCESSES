2|**Aphasia** is a loss or impairment of language caused by brain damage. In the **1870s**, several German neurologists, among them **Lichtheim** and **Wernicke**, started making **more systematic observations of aphasia**. >> They traced particular losses of function to **“disconnections” between “centers.”** >> A **center** is a brain region holding one kind of language representation, and a disconnection is damage to the pathway between two centers. || **Lichtheim’s “House” model** arranges these centers and pathways in the shape of a house. >> **A** is the center for **sound images**, the stored sound patterns of words. **M** is the center for **movement images**, the stored motor plans for saying words. **B** is the center for **concepts**. >> **a** is the **acoustic pathway** carrying heard speech into A, and **m** is the **motor pathway** carrying plans from M to the speech muscles. >> Seven numbered sites mark different forms of aphasia, each tied to damage at a particular center or pathway. || Each damage site predicts its own pattern of loss. >> Losing the ability to **understand spoken words** could come from a break between **A and B**, since the word’s sound arrives but cannot reach its concept. >> Losing **verbatim repetition** could come from a break between **A and M**, since a heard word cannot pass straight to the plan for saying it. || **“Box and arrow” models** of this kind are foundational in **aphasiology**, the study of aphasia, and in modeling normal language. >> The **Interactive Activation model** of word recognition shares the same basic form, with levels of representation joined by pathways. >> This history comes from Eling and Whitaker (2009), “History of aphasia, from brain to language.”
3|The **“Geschwind–Wernicke model”** ties language functions to specific brain regions and the pathways that join them. **Norman Geschwind revived** the box-and-arrow approach of Wernicke and Lichtheim **in the 1970s**. >> **Broca’s area** lies toward the front of the left hemisphere, in the frontal lobe. >> **Wernicke’s area** lies farther back, in the temporal lobe. >> The **arcuate fasciculus** is a bundle of nerve fibers linking Wernicke’s area to Broca’s area. || Two major grooves serve as landmarks. >> The **central sulcus**, or **fissure of Rolando**, divides the frontal lobe from the parietal lobe behind it. >> The **lateral sulcus**, or **fissure of Sylvius**, divides the temporal lobe from the frontal and parietal lobes above it. || In the hundred years between Wernicke and Geschwind, **the field of linguistics evolved dramatically**. >> Linguists built a theory in which **syntax was independent of other aspects of language** and ran on **its own computational principles**. >> That opened the way to more sophisticated thinking about **dissociations** and **double dissociations** of language functions. || In a **dissociation**, one ability is impaired while another is spared. In a **double dissociation**, one patient loses the first ability and keeps the second, while another patient shows the opposite. >> A double dissociation is strong evidence that the two abilities rely on separate systems. >> Once syntax, word knowledge and meaning are treated as distinct parts of language, a patient can be described as impaired in one part while the others work.
4|In “Some thoughts about the brain/mind/language interface,” **Fromkin (1997)** describes the classic profiles of the two main aphasias. >> **Broca’s aphasia** patients speak with **word-finding pauses**, **loss of grammatical morphemes**, and quite often **disturbed word order**. A **grammatical morpheme** is a small word or word part that marks a grammatical relationship, such as “the,” “is” or past-tense “-ed.” >> Their understanding of everyday conversation **gives the impression of being generally good**, though **controlled testing reveals considerable impairments**. || **Agrammatism**, difficulty producing or understanding grammatical structure, is almost a synonym for Broca’s aphasia. >> Still, **some patients with lesions in Broca’s area are not agrammatic**. >> And **some agrammatic patients would not be classified neurologically as Broca’s aphasics**. >> The behavior and the damaged region overlap a great deal but not perfectly. Broca’s aphasia names a pattern of behavior, and that pattern does not always follow damage to Broca’s area. || **Wernicke’s aphasia** patients produce **fluent speech** with **good intonation and pronunciation**. >> Their speech has **many word substitutions**, some related in meaning to the intended word and some not. >> They produce **neologisms**, invented words, and **phonological errors**, mistakes in the sounds of words. >> They also have **comprehension difficulties**. Fluency describes how smoothly speech flows, which is separate from whether it carries a coherent message.
5|An appealing **dichotomy**, a two-way split, sets the two aphasias against each other. **Wernicke’s aphasia** is called **“word salad”** or **“Jabberwocky,”** often full of **neologisms**, while **syntax is roughly intact**. “Jabberwocky” is Lewis Carroll’s grammatical nonsense poem. >> Asked about his health, one Wernicke’s patient said **“I felt worse because I can no longer keep in mind from the mind of the minds to keep me from mind and up to the ear which can be to find among ourselves,”** from Kriendler and colleagues (1971). >> Asked about his poor vision, another said **“My wires don’t hire right.”** >> Asked whether he was a doctor, an aphasic physician answered **“Me? Yes, sir, I’m a male demaploze on my own. I still know my tubaboys what for I have that’s gone hell and some of them go.”** **Demaploze** and **tubaboys** are neologisms. >> One patient called a **fork** **“a need for a schedule”** and a **spoon** **“How many schemes on your throat.”** || **Broca’s aphasia** is called **“telegraphic” speech**, with **difficulty with word order and grammatical particles** and **relative sparing of important content words**. >> Asked what brought him to the hospital, one Broca’s patient said **“Yes … ah … Monday … ah … Dad and P.H. (the patient’s name) and Dad … hospital. Two … ah doctors … and ah … thirty minutes … and yes … ah … hospital. And, er Wednesday … nine o’clock and eh Thursday, ten o’clock … doctors. Two doctors … and ah … teeth. Yeah … fine,”** from Badecker and Caramazza (1985). >> Words such as Dad, hospital and doctors carry the story, and the small grammatical words that would join them are missing. || The two patterns look like mirror images, with fluent grammar and wrong words on one side and meaningful words with little grammar on the other.
6|The dichotomy can also be put in terms of what is damaged. **Wernicke’s aphasia** reflects **problems with activation, or possibly inhibition, in the lexicon**, and **Broca’s aphasia** reflects **problems with grammar**. >> The **lexicon** is the mental store of words. >> **Activation** makes candidate words available, and **inhibition** holds back competitors that should not be chosen, so weak inhibition could let wrong words into speech. || That description is **fairly distant from how Broca described his original cases**. Paul Broca’s account of **Leborgne**, known as **“Tan,”** reads differently. >> Tan **could only produce one jargon syllable**, “tan.” >> He **seemed otherwise to be in possession of his senses** and in fact **seemed to understand what was being said**. || Broca’s account raises several questions. >> Why does it matter that Leborgne’s other senses were intact? Intact senses mean the loss was specific to speech, which makes it a dissociation. >> Why does a “capacity for articulate speech” seem a more believable job for a brain region than “criminality” or “philoprogenitiveness,” a phrenology term for love of one’s children? >> If Tan could be tested today, which measurements would pin down his deficits at the **levels of description used in modern linguistics**, such as phonetic, phonological, lexical and syntactic? || The result is a confusion. **Broca’s actual patients would not necessarily be classified as Broca’s aphasics** today. >> They were arguably **unable to produce speech at all**, seemingly because of **motor control problems**. >> Today they would be diagnosed with **apraxia of speech**, a disorder of planning and coordinating speech movements, which can sharply limit output without any loss of grammatical knowledge.
7|The split between Wernicke’s aphasia as a lexicon problem and Broca’s aphasia as a grammar problem is appealing partly because it **aligns well with a sharp distinction drawn in late 20th-century formal linguistics between the grammar and the lexicon**. >> The **grammar** is the rule system for combining words into sentences. >> The **lexicon** is the store of individual words, which have to be memorized. || The distinction begins with **Chomsky’s formulation of the computational problem of language learning**. >> We recognize a new string as a sentence **not because it matches some familiar item in any simple way**, but because **it is generated by the grammar** each speaker has internalized. >> A sentence never heard before can be understood because the listener can work out how the grammar builds it. || Chomsky also offered **compelling demonstrations of the independence of syntax and semantics**. In Chomsky (1955), neither **(a) “colorless green ideas sleep furiously”** nor **(b) “furiously sleep ideas green colorless”** has ever occurred in an English speaker’s past experience, and yet **(a) is grammatical while (b) is not**. >> Sentence (a) makes no sense, but its word order obeys English grammar. >> Sentence (b) puts the same words in an order the grammar forbids. >> Both are equally unfamiliar and equally meaningless, so the contrast must come from grammar as a separate system. || The aphasia split lines up with this division, with Wernicke’s aphasia as a disorder of the lexicon and Broca’s aphasia as a disorder of the grammar.
8|The dichotomy also matches levels of the **speech production model**, especially the contrast between the **functional level** and the **positional level**. >> At the conceptual **message level**, the speaker is **choosing a story angle and arousing concepts of referents and event structure**. >> At the **functional level**, the first linguistic level, the speaker is **arousing word meanings and semantic roles**. A **semantic role** is a participant’s part in an event, such as the doer or the receiver. >> At the **positional level**, the second linguistic level, the speaker is **getting word forms in grammatically appropriate order**. >> The **phonological encoding level** then supplies the word sounds, and the **speech gesture level** handles **articulation**, the physical movements of speech. || At the functional level, **lemmas have an arbitrary relationship to their referents**. A **lemma** is a word’s lexical entry, linking its meaning to its grammatical properties. >> Nothing about a concept fixes its word, so there is no reason a dog has to be called “dog.” || At the positional level, **syntactic trees, affixes and so on are the product of abstract syntactic processes**. An **affix** is a word part attached to a stem, such as “-ed” or “-ly,” and this level is the rule-built, grammatical side of language. || One objection is that **HOUSEBOAT** and **DOGHOUSE** have meanings that are clearly not arbitrary, since they are built from parts. >> The answer comes from **speech error evidence** that **derivational morphology** happens at the positional level. **Derivational morphology** forms new words from existing parts, such as adding “-ly” to make an adverb. >> In the slip **“easy enoughly,”** the speaker meant “easily enough,” but “-ly” attached to the wrong word. The suffix moved as a separate piece, so a grammatical operation at the positional level must have added it.
9|Popular theories of **sentence processing** hold that **the syntactic structure of a sentence is computed independently of meaning** while we listen or read. An autonomous parser builds the structure first, and meaning comes after. || **Isolating these “parsing” mechanisms requires carefully constructing stimulus materials**. >> In **“The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite,”** the **preferred** structure attaches “with the dynamite” to the **verb phrase**, making the dynamite the instrument. >> The **unpreferred** structure attaches it to the **noun phrase** “the safe,” so the phrase tells which safe. >> The words are identical in both readings and only the attachment differs, so sentences like this can probe parsing without changing word meanings. || **One possibility** is that **Broca’s patients generally understand what is being said even though they have a core grammatical deficit**. >> In everyday conversation, word meanings, familiar events and context all point to one message. >> A listener can follow along using these cues without fully computing the grammar. >> A real test of a parsing deficit needs sentences whose grammatical relations cannot be guessed from the likeliest event, such as reversible sentences where either person could plausibly do the action.
10|**None of this theory was available to Broca back in the 1860s**, and for him it was remarkable enough to **dissociate “articulate speech” from comprehension**. >> Leborgne could say only one syllable but seemed to understand what was said to him. >> Proving that speaking and understanding can separate was the central result of its time. || The modern question is different. Suppose the goal is to show that **some patients have a difficulty that is more generally with syntax**. >> A **syntactic impairment** affects how grammatical relationships are organized. >> Such an impairment should disrupt both producing and understanding sentences. || The way to find out is to **test comprehension**. >> A comprehension test lets the patient answer without speaking, which removes motor problems from the picture. >> If the patient still struggles to understand grammatical relationships, the deficit goes beyond articulation.
11|Finding out whether **Broca’s patients have comprehension deficits** mattered for several reasons. >> **Broca’s aphasia is almost always accompanied by some level of motor dysfunction**. >> Agrammatic speech might **merely reflect an attempt to communicate despite motor limitations**. When speaking takes great effort, a patient might drop the small grammatical words and keep only the most informative content words. || **Hughlings Jackson** made a related point, that **we are observing what the individual can do despite the impairment**. >> A brain injury that produces agrammatic speech does not literally mean that **the grammar itself is injured**. >> Caramazza and Zurif (1976) cite a version of this idea from Lenneberg (1973). If anterior damage leaves the patient’s tacit knowledge of language intact, the telegraphic output can be blamed on a neuromuscular problem, because **the strain of speaking is so great that the patient speaks asyntactically in order to economize effort**. || **Chomsky’s distinction between competence and performance** applies here too. >> **Competence** is a speaker’s underlying knowledge of language. >> **Performance** is the use of that knowledge in speaking or understanding, which memory limits, distraction or motor problems can disrupt. >> **When motor deficits obviously interfere with performance, comprehension may provide a clearer window into competence**. || **Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** therefore tested comprehension in Broca’s patients, since speech alone cannot separate the two explanations. >> If these patients understand grammar normally, their agrammatic speech is a motor performance problem. >> If they also fail to understand grammatical relationships, the deficit reaches the grammar itself.
12|**“Man bites dog”** is the classic newspaper example of an unexpected event. **Psycholinguistic tests** are needed to reveal grammatical problems in comprehension, because **in conversation, there are lots of redundant cues to what a speaker intends**. **Redundant** cues are separate sources of information that all point to the same meaning. || **“The dog bit the man”** offers **many ways to get the right meaning**. >> A listener can build the **most likely combination of DOG, BITE and MAN into an event representation**, **with or without syntax**, since dogs bite people far more often than the reverse. >> The sentence also uses **canonical word order**, **agent, then verb, then undergoer**, which is **subject, then verb, then object**. The **agent** performs the action and the **undergoer** receives it, and in English the first noun is usually the agent. >> Much comprehension also takes place in a **discourse context**, the surrounding conversation, which clears up a great deal. || Broca’s patients might understand by leaning on these **compensatory heuristics**. A **heuristic** is a mental shortcut, and a compensatory one covers for a weakness somewhere else. >> A patient could just assume the most plausible event, or assume the first noun is the doer. >> Either shortcut handles “The dog bit the man” with no grammatical analysis. || Testing grammar directly takes sentences that defeat the shortcuts, such as **“The dog that the man is biting is angry.”** The **man** is the biter, although the dog comes first and dogs are likelier biters, so only grammatical analysis gives the right answer.
13|**Caramazza and Zurif** wrote **a list of exactly this kind of sentence, along with a set of control sentences**, organized in four groups of eight. >> **A. Control sentences** are simple and in canonical order, such as **“The girl is kicking a green ball,” “The cat is chasing a blue bird”** and **“The boy is riding a new bicycle.”** || **B. Center-embedded sentences with semantic constraints** put one clause inside another, but meaning makes the roles obvious. A **center-embedded** sentence has a relative clause in the middle of the main clause. >> Examples are **“The apple that the boy is eating is red,” “The wagon that the horse is pulling is green”** and **“The balloon that the clown is holding is round.”** Apples cannot eat boys, so meaning alone reveals who does what. || **C. Reversible center-embedded sentences** have two participants who could each sensibly do the action. >> Examples are **“The cow that the monkey is scaring is yellow,” “The horse that the bear is kicking is brown”** and **“The girl that the boy is tickling is happy.”** Meaning offers no help, so only grammar reveals the roles. || **D. Improbable center-embedded sentences** describe events that run against expectations. >> Examples are **“The bird that the worm is eating is blue,” “The man that the horse is riding is fat”** and **“The policeman that the robber is chasing is skinny.”** Common sense points to the wrong answer. || In every embedded sentence, the first noun is acted on, and the doer sits inside the relative clause. >> A listener who relies on meaning should pass groups A and B and fail C and D. >> A listener who parses normally should pass all four.
14|Caramazza and Zurif used a **sentence-picture matching task**, in which a listener hears a sentence and picks the picture that fits it. >> Accuracy reveals whether the listener grasped who did what to whom, with no spoken answer required. >> Healthy listeners and patients with Broca’s, conduction and Wernicke’s aphasia each heard all four sentence types. || **Healthy controls are more or less equally good at all stimulus types**, scoring near the top on each one. || **Wernicke’s patients are poor overall**. >> They do badly even on the simple control sentences. >> That likely reflects their **general comprehension deficits**, which reach word meanings as well as grammar. || **Broca’s patients** have a **particular difficulty keeping “who did what to whom” straight** when **syntax conflicts with common sense** or with the likely semantic roles of the nouns. >> They do well on control sentences and on semantically constrained sentences, where meaning gives the answer away. >> They do poorly on reversible and improbable sentences, where only grammar can settle the roles. || This **specific comprehension deficit** was read as evidence that **Broca’s aphasia affects syntax specifically**. >> Because the trouble shows up in understanding as well as speaking, it lies in the grammar itself. >> The paper’s title, “Dissociation of algorithmic and heuristic processes,” sums it up. Broca’s patients lose the **algorithmic** route of grammatical analysis and keep the **heuristic** shortcuts that run on meaning.
15|A more **specific theory of the “parts of language”** creates **new ways of exploring structure-function relationships**, meaning links between a brain structure and the function it supports. || **Rather than just dividing aphasics into those with good and poor comprehension, or those who are more or less fluent**, researchers can **isolate problems with particular aspects of language**. >> One patient might know word meanings well but assign the wrong roles in complex sentences. >> Another might produce a fluent grammatical frame filled with words that miss the intended message. >> A single fluency score would hide the difference between them. || The central division is **the grammar versus the lexicon**. >> **Grammar** covers the rules that relate words to one another within a sentence. >> The **lexicon** covers stored words and the ability to retrieve the right one. || This division yields specific predictions, and controlled tasks can connect an impaired operation to a particular brain region. >> A grammar deficit predicts trouble with reversible sentences but not with word meanings. >> A lexical deficit predicts trouble with word meanings and naming but not with grammatical structure.
16|The dichotomy runs into **problems**. **Dick and colleagues** had participants hear **four types of sentences** and **indicate which animal was doing the action**. >> A dog and a cat were pictured on a screen, and participants pressed a button to pick the animal. >> The event was always a dog biting a cat, while the sentence structure varied. || Two sentence types put the doer first. >> **Active**, as in **“The dog is biting the cat.”** >> **Subject cleft**, as in **“It’s the dog that is biting the cat.”** A **cleft** sentence breaks a simple sentence apart with “It’s … that” to focus on one part, and in a subject cleft the focused noun is the doer. >> For both types, picking the first animal mentioned gives the right answer. || Two types put the receiver of the action first. >> **Object cleft**, as in **“It’s the cat that the dog is biting,”** where the focused noun is the one bitten. >> **Passive**, as in **“The cat is bitten by the dog,”** where the receiver becomes the grammatical subject. >> For both, the first-animal shortcut fails. Only a listener who parses the grammar succeeds on all four types.
17|When **aphasia patients and healthy aging controls** do this task, **a pattern emerges much like what Caramazza and Zurif observed**. Patients were grouped by type of aphasia using the Western Aphasia Battery. >> **Elderly controls** were nearly perfect on all four sentence types. >> **Anomic** patients, whose main problem is finding words, were fairly accurate on actives and subject clefts but dropped on object clefts and passives. >> **Conduction** aphasia patients dropped sharply on object clefts. >> **Wernicke’s** patients scored low on every sentence type, near chance. || **Broca’s aphasia patients have particular difficulty with the syntactically complex stimuli**, the object clefts and passives. >> On object clefts, they were barely above chance. >> These are **noncanonical** structures, meaning they depart from the usual doer-first order. || **Their performance is overall not so great**, however. >> Even on simple active sentences, Broca’s patients were well below the elderly controls. >> The pattern combines a general drop in performance with an extra cost for complex structures. The groups differ in both their overall accuracy and the size of that extra cost.
18|The same task was given to **healthy undergraduates** under **conditions that stress the language system** in different ways. **Stress conditions** included **speeding up the speech**, adding noise, and **asking people to remember lists of numbers as a secondary task**. >> **Compressed** speech is speech played faster than normal. >> A **low-pass filter** removes the higher frequencies from speech, making it sound muffled. >> **Auditory digits** and **visual digits** are number lists that participants had to remember while listening, which uses up memory resources. || With a single stressor, accuracy stayed high on actives and subject clefts. >> Object clefts suffered most, especially under noise. >> Passives also dropped somewhat. || When two stressors were combined, the effects grew larger. >> With muffled speech that was also sped up, students stayed accurate on actives and subject clefts. >> On object clefts and passives, their accuracy fell to about two thirds. || **Object cleft and passive sentences are particularly vulnerable to all the manipulations**, the same pattern found in Broca’s aphasia. These students have no brain damage, so stressing their processing system is enough to produce an aphasia-like profile. >> This suggests that **the comprehension deficits in Broca’s aphasia may be a “performance” issue after all**. >> Complex structures may need more processing resources, so any reduction in resources hurts them first. The patients may keep their grammatical knowledge but lack the resources to use it well.
19|A further question is whether **Broca’s aphasics’ comprehension deficits are language-specific**. **Saygin and colleagues (2004)** asked patients to **match an action to an object**. >> In the **non-linguistic** version, a drawing has a boy pantomiming an action, such as licking. Participants chose the matching object from pictures such as an ice cream cone, a cake, broccoli and a rooster. >> In the **linguistic** version, the action was given in words rather than by pantomime. || Controls were nearly perfect in both versions. Aphasic patients made more errors in both. >> In the non-linguistic version, controls scored **98.5%** and patients **93.4%**. >> In the linguistic version, controls scored **99.4%** and patients **87.5%**. >> **Broca’s aphasics had difficulty with this task whether the stimuli were linguistic or not**. || Accuracy also depended on the kind of wrong choice offered, called the **distractor**. >> Patients made the most errors when the distractor was **semantic**, meaning related in meaning to the correct object. >> They made fewer errors with **unrelated** distractors and with **affordance-based** distractors. An **affordance** is an action an object allows, such as a different object that can also be licked. || The nonverbal version removes language from the input but keeps meaning in the task. A pantomimed action has to be understood and linked to the right object, much as a verb is linked to its object. >> A purely grammatical deficit predicts good performance on the nonverbal version. >> Difficulty on that version points to a broader problem with action meanings, shared with language.
20|**Performance in the non-verbal version of this task was significantly correlated with damage to Broca’s area**. Damage linked to deficits in **non-linguistic pantomime interpretation** clustered in the left frontal region in front of the **central sulcus**, which includes Broca’s area. || The task asked participants only to interpret a pantomimed action and pick an object. >> No sentence had to be produced. >> No spoken instruction had to be parsed. || Damage to Broca’s area therefore affects more than syntax. >> Patients with damage there can have trouble linking an action to its meaning even when no language is involved. >> Any account of Broca’s area must explain the grammatical comprehension findings and this nonlinguistic difficulty with actions together.
21|A parallel question is whether **Wernicke’s aphasics’ comprehension deficits are language-specific**. **Saygin and colleagues** asked patients to **match environmental sounds to pictures**. >> In the **nonverbal** version, a speaker plays an environmental sound, such as a cow mooing, and participants choose between pictures, such as a cow and a sheep. >> In the **verbal** version, the speaker plays a spoken phrase, such as **“cow mooing,”** and participants choose between pictures, such as a cow and a violin. || The groups differed in overall accuracy. >> Controls, patients with **right hemisphere damage** and anomic patients were all nearly perfect. >> Broca’s patients were a little less accurate. >> **Wernicke’s patients were particularly bad at this task**, scoring **90.0% verbal** and **91.3% nonverbal**. || **No significant difference was observed between the verbal and nonverbal versions**. >> Wernicke’s patients struggled just as much with an animal call as with a spoken phrase. >> The deficit therefore goes beyond understanding spoken words. Their difficulty concerns linking sounds of any kind to their meanings, whether the meaning arrives through language or through ordinary sounds.
22|**Deficits in both verbal and nonverbal sound-picture matching are associated with Wernicke’s area**. >> Eight patients were **impaired in the nonverbal version**, and ten were **impaired in the verbal version**. >> In both groups, the damage most often overlapped in the posterior temporal area of the left hemisphere, around Wernicke’s area. || The verbal and nonverbal impairments therefore come from damage to the same general region. >> A spoken phrase carries meaning through words. >> An animal call carries meaning without language. || Both tasks demand the same thing, connecting meaningful sounds to what they stand for. Wernicke’s area seems to support meaningful sound processing in general, and language is one important case of it.
23|**How language-specific are the deficits in aphasia?** The question is **still a controversy**. >> Some patients are especially vulnerable to complex grammatical structures, which suggests that some parts of language are handled in specialized ways. >> The same patients can also struggle with action meanings or environmental sounds in tasks with no language, which suggests that language shares brain systems with other kinds of meaning. >> A full explanation has to cover both kinds of result. || What is clear is **how the terms of the debate have evolved**. The field now relies on three kinds of tools. >> **Relatively sophisticated tests that tap specific aspects of language**, such as reversible and improbable sentences, cleft sentences and nonverbal matching tasks. >> **Quantitative analyses of behavior**, measuring accuracy across sentence types, patient groups and stress conditions. >> **Quantitative analyses of structure-function relationships**, relating patterns of brain damage to performance across many patients. || These methods have come **a long way from the 19th century**. >> Wernicke and Lichtheim built boxes and arrows from a handful of individual cases. >> Modern research can test which specific operations are impaired and map those impairments onto damaged tissue across large patient groups.

25|**Structure-function relationships** in aphasia can be **formalized** with MRI data. **Voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping**, from Bates, Wilson, Saygin, Dick, Sereno, Knight and Dronkers, relates where the brain is damaged to how severe the symptoms are across many patients. >> A **voxel** is a small three-dimensional unit of an MRI image, a kind of 3D pixel. >> At each voxel, patients with damage there are compared with patients without it. >> If the damaged patients score much worse, that voxel is linked to the symptom. || The **“symptoms”** are **defined very broadly**. >> **Fluency deficits** are linked most strongly to damage toward the front of the left hemisphere. >> **Comprehension deficits** are linked most strongly to damage farther back, in the posterior temporal region. || **Data from a large number of patients is consistent with a rough double dissociation**. >> **Posterior regions** are associated with **perception**, or comprehension. >> **Anterior regions** are associated with **production**, or fluency. >> Every patient supplies a damage pattern and a continuous score, so the method turns many single cases into one statistical map that ties brain location to symptoms.
