54|The **Revised Hierarchical Model of Kroll and Stewart (1990, 1994)** accounts for interactions between a first language, **L1**, and a second language, **L2**, through **spreading activation**, where activating one representation partly activates the representations linked to it. >> The model joins an L1 vocabulary, an L2 vocabulary and a shared store of concepts. >> **Lexical links** connect words across the two languages, and **conceptual links** connect each vocabulary to meanings. >> L1 words are strongly linked to concepts, and L2 words are linked more weakly. || In the **Marian and Spivey** task in **L2**, Russian–English bilinguals hear an English instruction to pick up the **speaker**. >> **The concept for “speaker” partially activates the Russian word for “matches,”** which is **spichki**. >> Speaker and spichki are connected only by **sound overlap**. They begin with the same sounds, so activity spreads from one to the other, although speakers and matches share no meaning. || **The activity of spichki feeds back to the conceptual activation for “matches.”** >> L1 words are tightly linked to their concepts, so the partly active Russian word strengthens the concept it names. >> **Conceptual activation for “matches” leads to eye movements toward the L1 competitor**, the matches, even though the listener still picks up the speaker. || **When the task is done in L1**, the **conceptual links with L2 might be too weak to create this kind of interference reliably**. >> An English competitor would need a strong link from its English name to its concept to draw the eyes during a Russian task. >> The model gives L2 words only weak conceptual links, so interference runs from the first language into the second but not the other way.
