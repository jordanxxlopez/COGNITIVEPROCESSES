2|**Klatt (1979)** describes a very old and very general model of speech perception, in which recognition runs through a series of stages and each stage transforms the output of the one before. >> The **speech waveform** enters the **peripheral auditory system**, which carries out a **spectral analysis**, as the **cochlea** in the inner ear splits the sound into its frequency components. >> **Acoustic property detectors** respond to particular properties of the sound, and their outputs change over time. >> **Phonetic feature detectors** use those outputs to estimate the probability of features such as voicing at each moment. >> **Segmental analysis** combines features into a sequence of speech segments, a **segmental feature matrix**. >> **Lexical search** compares that sequence with stored words and yields **lexical hypotheses**, candidate words that might match. || Categorical perception fits this model. Somewhere between the **analog signal** leaving the cochlea and the **activation of phonemes** in segmental analysis, **information is lost**, and many physically different sounds become the same phoneme. >> This **loss of information is helpful**, since a shared phoneme sequence can be matched directly against stored words at higher levels. >> It follows that **in order to identify words, we need to first identify their constituent phonemes**. || Two problems challenge this simple picture. >> **Cascaded processing**, in which partial information moves on to the next level before the earlier decision is complete. >> **Feedback**, in which a higher level, such as words, sends influence back down to a lower level, such as phonemes.
3|The **beach–peach** continuum is a familiar **voicing continuum**. The words differ in their first consonant, /b/ in beach and /p/ in peach. These consonants differ mainly in **voice onset time**, or **VOT**, which is the delay between the release of the lips and the start of vocal fold vibration. >> Along the continuum, VOT gets shorter in equal steps. >> A long delay before voicing sounds like peach, and a short delay sounds like beach. || One listener’s **identification** results have a sharp, step-like shape. >> The early stimuli are labeled as one word every time, and the late stimuli as the other word every time. >> Only one stimulus near the middle gets mixed labels. >> The **category boundary** is the narrow region where the preferred label switches. || The same listener’s **discrimination** results follow the boundary. >> Pairs inside one category are almost never heard as different. >> Pairs that straddle the boundary are heard as different every time. >> Equal changes in VOT are much easier to detect across the boundary, where they change the word, than inside one category, where the word stays the same.
4|**How does categorical perception happen?** One answer is a **threshold model**. In a threshold model, **activity is only passed forward from one level to the next when an activity threshold is crossed**. >> A **threshold** is a set level of activity that must be reached before anything is sent onward. >> Activity below the threshold can rise and fall without any effect on the next level. || In this kind of model, **categorical perception arises at phoneme identification**. Consider the voicing evidence for a sound between /b/ and /p/. >> At the feature level, the voicing evidence swings up and down over time, between a **+voiced** threshold and a **−voiced** threshold. >> At the phoneme level, nothing happens while the evidence wavers below threshold. Both /b/ and /p/ stay silent. >> Only when the evidence finally crosses the +voiced threshold does **/b/** switch on, all at once. || An ambiguous sound with mixed responses either crosses a threshold or does not. >> The word level receives a finished /b/ or a finished /p/, and the history of uncertainty stays behind at the feature level. >> Two acoustically different sounds that both end up as /b/ become identical by the time they reach word recognition. >> This all-or-none switch would produce categorical perception directly at the phoneme level.
5|A **cascaded model** gives a different answer. In it, **activity is passed forward continuously over time from one level to the next**, and each level responds to whatever activity exists at the moment, while decisions are still forming. || Feature activity and phoneme activity rise and fall together. >> As evidence for +voiced grows, **/b/** activation grows. >> As the evidence shifts toward −voiced, activation moves toward **/p/**. >> The phoneme level tracks the feature level continuously. || In such a model, **categorical perception might happen later on, or might even be an artifact of the task structure**. An **artifact** is a result created by the way something is measured instead of by the process under study. >> An identification task forces a listener to click beach or peach, even if both words were partly active. >> The answer looks categorical because the choices are categorical. >> Two sounds that both end up labeled /b/ can still differ in how much they supported /p/ along the way. || When voicing evidence is mixed, /b/ and /p/ each get activation in proportion to their evidence. >> Graded phoneme activation passes graded activation to words, so a sound near the boundary partly activates both beach and peach. >> Processing before the final choice keeps fine-grained information about how ambiguous the sound was.
6|**Making furniture** works as a metaphor for **thresholded activity**, where each operation turns one specific input into one specific output. >> A **lumber mill** turns **trees** into **lumber**. >> A **furniture factory** turns **lumber** into **unassembled furniture**. >> An **IKEA customer** turns **unassembled furniture** into finished **furniture**. || Each step needs a finished product in a form it can use. Trees must become lumber before furniture is made, and lumber must become parts before assembly starts. >> **Thresholded processing** depends on its inputs the same way, with a higher level waiting until the lower level delivers the required representation. >> Information moves forward only as a completed product.
7|In the furniture metaphor, each **“layer”** **can only take a specific input that has been transformed completely by the previous layer**, so manufacturing waits until the lumber shipment arrives. Many computer programs run this way. >> Each stage does its own transformation and hands on only its finished output. >> A lower stage can keep working while the higher stage sits idle, waiting for a finished product. || In speech recognition, a strict threshold account makes **word processing depend on an identified phoneme**. >> A partly resolved acoustic signal stays at the lower level until it is identified as a phoneme. >> The word level receives only the finished segment. || This design also decides **what information is lost**. >> When different sounds become the same phoneme, their earlier differences never reach word recognition. >> That helps treat varied sounds alike, but later stages never learn how uncertain the earlier stages were.
8|A **food web** works as a metaphor for **cascaded processing**, because its **“levels” continuously interact with one another**. A desert food web links **desert plants**, **insects**, **kangaroo rats**, **small lizards**, **large lizards**, **scorpions**, **snakes**, **desert foxes** and **hawks** by who eats whom. >> A drought that cuts back the plants **immediately** affects the health of kangaroo rats. >> The change in kangaroo rats then affects the snakes that eat them, and so on up the web. >> No population waits for the level below to reach a finished state. || **Cascaded processing** has the same continuous dependence. >> Shifting feature evidence changes phoneme activity as it happens. >> Shifting phoneme activity changes support for words, so each level reacts to partial information as it arrives. || The food web also has clear **feedback loops**. More hawks change the numbers of snakes, lizards and rats, and those changes in turn affect the hawks’ food supply. >> In a recognition model, **feedback** is influence traveling from a higher level back down to a lower one. >> Cascading and feedback are different ideas. Cascading sends developing evidence forward, and feedback lets higher-level activity change an earlier representation.
9|The central question is whether **phoneme identification for word recognition** works like **thresholded processing**, the lumber mill, or like **cascaded processing**, the food web. >> In a **thresholded account**, word recognition starts only once a phoneme decision is complete. >> In a **cascaded account**, a word can gather support while the phoneme is still uncertain. || The two accounts differ in how much developing acoustic evidence reaches candidate words. >> Two sounds can both be labeled **/b/** and still differ in how much they resemble **/p/**. >> A thresholded system turns both into the same finished /b/, so pear should not compete with bear. >> A cascaded system passes along more /p/ support for the more ambiguous sound, so pear should compete with bear for a while. || Both accounts can predict the same final word. They part ways before the final choice, so the deciding test measures temporary support for a competing word during recognition.
10|**McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin and Spivey (2003)** tested the question in **probabilistic constraint satisfaction at the lexical/phonetic interface**, in the Journal of Psycholinguistic Research. They searched for **gradient effects of within-category VOT on lexical access**. **Lexical access** is retrieving a word from memory during recognition. >> **Gradient** means changing by degrees. >> **Within-category VOT** means differences in voice onset time among sounds all heard as the same phoneme. || Listeners identified sounds from a **/b/–/p/ continuum** in a **“visual world” display** while **eye-tracking** recorded where they looked among pictures. >> Each display had a **target** such as **pear**, a **competitor** such as **bear**, and two unrelated **fillers**, a **lamp** and a **ship**. >> Target and competitor differ only in their first consonant. || Eye movements are fast and continuous during word recognition, and listeners look at objects as they think about them, often before they know they have decided. >> A listener may end up choosing bear after a quick look at the pear. >> How often and when such looks happen measures how active the competitor was before the final click.
11|Participants used the **mouse to select the picture** that best matched what they heard, such as a **“bear”** or a **“pear,”** in the same way as a bear–pear task done at home. >> Each trial presented one sound from the VOT continuum along with the four pictures. >> The click records which word the listener finally settled on. || The clicks produced a **typical, step-like identification function**. >> Short VOTs were almost never heard as pear, and long VOTs almost always were. >> The switch happened abruptly at a **category boundary** of about **17.5 milliseconds**, with very little variation. || Judged by the clicks alone, perception looks fully categorical. A sound well inside the /b/ category and a sound just short of the boundary both produce bear nearly every time. >> On its own, this sharp shift in labels would seem to support the threshold model. >> A click records only the word chosen at the end of recognition. Eye movements record which other words received support before that choice, so a sharp identification function can sit alongside gradual differences in competition.
12|**Eye movements reveal that ambiguity is gradient**. There are **more looks to the competitor close to the boundary**. Gradient means that uncertainty varies by degree rather than all at once. >> The analysis holds the final response constant. It compares only trials where the listener clicked bear, or only trials where the listener clicked pear. >> Since every trial in a comparison ended with the same click, differences in competitor looks cannot come from listeners changing their answer. || On trials where listeners clicked **bear**, they still glanced at the **pear** for a while. >> Those looks rose after the word began and then faded as bear won. >> Sounds closer to the boundary drew more looks to the pear, and those looks lasted longer, than the clearest /b/ sounds. || On trials where listeners clicked **pear**, the pattern was the mirror image. >> Sounds just past the boundary drew the most looks to the bear. >> The clearest /p/ sounds drew the fewest. || The effect of VOT is mainly on the **duration of activation**, meaning how long the competitor stays active. Two sounds that both end up as bear can still produce different amounts of looking at the pear. The recognition system keeps track of how ambiguous each sound was, which is information a threshold model would throw away.
13|The gradient effect is easiest to see when looks to the competitor are compared across the whole VOT range. On one side of the **category boundary**, listeners clicked bear, and the measure is how often they looked at the pear. On the other side, they clicked pear, and the measure is how often they looked at the bear. || Looks were measured in two time windows. >> The **early** window covers **300–1100 milliseconds** after the word begins. >> The **late** window covers **1100–1900 milliseconds** after the word begins. || In both windows, competitor looks **increase as VOT approaches the boundary from either side**. >> On the bear side, looks to the pear grow steadily as the sound moves closer to the boundary. >> On the pear side, looks to the bear are highest just past the boundary and fall steadily as the sound becomes a clearer /p/. >> Early looks are higher overall, since listeners are still uncertain early in the word, but they follow the same pattern. || The effect appears in both windows, so uncertainty about the first sound shapes looking throughout recognition. A sound can support the eventual winner while still supporting its rival. The closer the sound is to the boundary, the stronger that rival support is.
14|The result fits the idea that **activation flows in a cascaded way from features to phonemes**, with changing features affecting phonemes and changing phonemes affecting words while recognition is under way. >> **Even stimuli identified as /b/ 100% of the time can vary in how strongly and unambiguously the “voiced” feature is activated**. >> A clear /b/ activates voicing strongly, while a /b/ near the boundary activates it only weakly. || **/b/ and /p/** share most features. Both are **bilabial**, with the closure made by the two lips, and both are **stops**, with airflow fully blocked and then released. >> A stimulus that is clearly **+bilabial** and **+stop** supports /b/ and /p/ equally. >> Only voicing tells them apart, so weak voicing evidence creates **momentary ambiguity between /b/ and /p/**. >> That ambiguity surfaces in the **eye movements** while the listener decides where to click. || The **TRACE** model captures this cascade with three layers. >> The **feature layer** takes a **pseudo-spectral input representation**, a simplified version of the sound’s frequency content over time. Its detectors are POW, VOC, DIFF, ACU, GRD, VOI and BUR, for power, vocalic, diffuseness, acuteness, gradualness of onset, voicing and burst. >> The **phoneme layer** has units for phonemes such as /b/, /p/, /r/ and /t/, repeated at every point in time. >> The **word layer** has word units, also repeated across time. >> Activity passes up from features to phonemes and from phonemes to words, and units within a layer compete through inhibition.
15|There is evidence that **partial activation can reach all the way to the lexical level**, the level of stored words and their meanings. **Andruski and colleagues (1994)** found that **semantic priming** can occur even with **nonwords**. Semantic priming is the speed-up in recognizing a word after hearing a word related in meaning. >> **“Beace”** primes **“war.”** Beace is peace with a voiced first consonant, and peace is related in meaning to war. >> **“Ging”** primes **“queen,”** because ging is king with a voiced first consonant. >> In the **word-to-word** items, changing the voicing of the first consonant of one real word produces another real word, as in **“pet”** becoming **“bet.”** One such item is **“bill”** priming **“drug,”** because bill is an altered form of pill. || Priming was measured with a **lexical decision task**, in which participants decide as quickly as possible whether a target is a real word. Faster responses than after an unrelated prime indicate priming. >> Primes were **unaltered**, had their VOT reduced by **one third**, had it reduced by **two thirds**, or were **unrelated** to the target. || Every related prime sped up responses compared with unrelated primes. >> For word-to-nonword items, priming stayed strong at every level of alteration. A prime drifting toward a nonword still activated the meaning of the real word it came from. >> For word-to-word items, priming weakened at two thirds. There the altered prime also partly activates a competing real word, such as bill competing with pill, which reduces the benefit. || **Lexical representations can be activated even by incomplete or imperfect input**. Beace never becomes a real word, but it matches peace closely enough to partly activate it. That activation spreads to meaning and speeds recognition of war, which fits cascaded processing.
16|The bear–pear eye-tracking result is evidence for **cascaded processing** in speech perception. A thresholded model would struggle to explain why **the number of looks to the other member of the minimal pair increases gradually as you approach the category boundary**. A **minimal pair** is two words that differ in a single sound, such as bear and pear. >> Looks to the competitor climb steadily as VOT nears the boundary. >> This happens even when the final answer is the same, so it reflects graded activation within one category. || In a thresholded model, graded feature activity never reaches the phoneme level. >> Feature evidence can swing between +voiced and −voiced. >> The phoneme level stays silent until /b/ is chosen all at once. || Under a threshold-only account, every token placed in a category sends the **same finished phoneme** to the word level. >> Word-level competition would then be identical for every /b/, leaving nothing to produce gradually changing competitor looks. >> A cascaded account explains the effect directly, because more support for the competing phoneme passes on more support for its word.
17|**TRACE** also includes **feedback from the lexical layer to the phoneme layer**, so activity runs up from phonemes to words and back down from words to phonemes. >> **Lexical feedback** means that an active word sends support back to its own phonemes. >> An uncertain sound can partly activate a word, and that word can then boost the reading of the sound that fits it. || Influence therefore travels both ways. >> Acoustic evidence changes which words are active. >> Active words shift the balance between possible phonemes while the phoneme decision is still forming. >> This helps listeners cope with noisy or ambiguous input. || **Feedback is different from cascading**. >> Cascading sends incomplete evidence forward, from features to phonemes and from phonemes to words. >> Feedback sends influence from words back down to phonemes. >> An interactive model can have both, and findings where word knowledge changes how sounds are heard, such as the Ganong effect, are the reason to add feedback.
18|**The Ganong effect** comes from **Ganong (1980)**, who examined how **phoneme identification is affected by word knowledge**. The heading “Gisses and kifts for Valentine’s day” plays on it by swapping the voicing of the first consonants in “kisses and gifts.” >> The question is whether knowing which sound sequences are real words changes how an ambiguous sound is heard. || Ganong built **word/nonword pairs** such as **“corpse/gorpse,”** with a real word at one end of the continuum and a nonword at the other. >> Hearing /k/ yields the familiar word corpse. >> Hearing /g/ yields gorpse, which is not a word. >> The continua crossed the relevant phoneme boundary in **5-millisecond steps** of VOT. || The design pairs **uncertain phonetic evidence** with a **difference in lexical status**. >> The rest of the word, “-orpse,” never changes, and it tells the listener which beginning would complete a real word. >> If word knowledge affects identification, an ambiguous first consonant should more often be heard as the sound that makes a word. >> The crucial test lies in the ambiguous middle of the continuum, where word knowledge has the most room to act.
19|One possibility is a **late “correction” process**. Under this account, **categorical perception works as usual, but there is a bias to report having heard a word**. >> The listener first categorizes the sound with no help from word knowledge. >> At the response stage, the listener leans toward the option that forms a real word. || This account predicts a **general reporting bias**. Three kinds of continua are compared. >> In **word–nonword**, the voiced end forms a word. In **nonword–word**, the voiceless end forms a word. In **neutral**, neither end has a lexical advantage. >> If the bias is added after perception, it should push responses toward the word by the same amount everywhere. >> Even a clearly voiceless sound would sometimes be reported as voiced when the voiced version makes a word, and the reverse would hold at the other end. || The **lexical effect** is the difference in voiced responses between the two lexical conditions. Under this account, the lexical effect is the same size at every step of the continuum. >> The bias favors the word equally at clear and ambiguous steps. >> Perception and knowledge stay separate, and word knowledge only nudges the reported answer.
20|A second possibility is that **word knowledge shifts the categorical boundary**. Under this account, word knowledge changes where the listener switches from one phoneme category to the other. >> Near an ambiguous onset, the interpretation that forms a real word gets extra support. >> The effect changes categorization itself, not just the reported answer. || This account predicts that the lexical conditions differ only in the middle of the continuum. >> At both ends, the sound is clear, so every condition gives the same answer. >> Near the boundary, the word–nonword condition crosses later and the nonword–word condition crosses earlier. || The **lexical effect** should therefore form a peak. It is near zero at the endpoints and largest for the items closest to the boundary. >> The effect is **strongest for items closest to the category boundary**, because those are the stimuli where lexical information can help resolve ambiguity about the first phoneme. >> At the endpoints, strong acoustic evidence already settles the consonant, leaving little room for word knowledge to help. || The word ending gives the same lexical advantage at every step, but the onset is not equally uncertain at every step. Where the effect occurs therefore matters. A flat effect points to a reporting bias, while a peaked effect points to a shift in the boundary itself.
21|**Ganong’s results** are strikingly consistent with the **second model**, the boundary-shift account. >> At the short and long ends of the VOT continuum, the word and nonword conditions give nearly the same answers. >> The **lexical effect** is small at the endpoints and largest near the boundary, where it reaches about **0.3**. || A peak at the boundary rules out a simple reporting bias, because word knowledge matters most where the acoustic evidence is weakest. >> At the boundary, the word that fits the context tips the balance. >> At the endpoints, the acoustics settle the answer alone. || Ganong considered several ways this could happen, centered on different **guessing strategies**. >> A guessing strategy would let listeners use word knowledge only when unsure of the sound. || Other models stress a possible role for **feedback** in these shifts. >> In **interactive models**, an active word sends support back to its own phonemes. >> The whole-word representation can then shape how an ambiguous part of the word is interpreted during recognition.
22|A **simulation of the Ganong effect in the TRACE model** tracks **phoneme-level and word-level activations** at several points as a segment unfolds. The input begins with a sound **ambiguous between /b/ and /p/**, followed by **/l/**, the vowel **/ʌ/**, and **/g/**, which together make the word **plug**. >> Activations are recorded at four moments, as each new sound arrives. || In the **first frame**, only the ambiguous first sound has arrived. >> **/b/** and **/p/** are both weakly active, at about the same level. >> Word activation is still very low. || In the **second and third frames**, /l/ and the vowel arrive. >> Partial /b/ evidence supports words beginning with **bl-**, and partial /p/ evidence supports words beginning with **pl-**. >> Several candidates gain activation together, including **plug**, **blood** and **blush**, which all fit the beginning heard so far. >> Phoneme activity for /p/ starts to pull ahead of /b/. || In the **fourth frame**, the final **/g/** arrives. >> /g/ fits plug but does not fit the endings of blood or blush. >> **Plug** becomes the most active word by a wide margin, while the alternatives fall away. >> /p/ ends up more active than /b/ at the phoneme level.
23|As the input continues, **activity accumulates for “plug,”** above all when the **final /g/** arrives, since /g/ clashes with **“blood”** and **“blush,”** which had also become active. >> Blood and blush matched the early part of the input, so they were plausible for a while. >> When /g/ arrives, they lose support because their endings do not fit. || TRACE therefore explains **word effects on phoneme category boundaries** through **top-down processing**, in which higher-level knowledge influences lower-level analysis. >> **Activity at the lexical level produces feedback to the phoneme level**. >> That feedback gives **greater activation to the phoneme consistent with a word**, so /p/ gains extra support from plug, the winning word. || The ambiguous beginning becomes clearer because /p/ belongs to the word the whole sequence supports. >> Information arriving at the end of a word can reach back and shape how an earlier sound is heard. >> This happens through interactions during recognition, and the ambiguous first sound comes to be heard as /p/.
24|**TRACE** is a version of the **Interactive Activation model** of **written word recognition**, from McClelland and Rumelhart (1981), and the two share many principles. >> In the Interactive Activation network, **feature** units connect to **letter** units, and letter units connect to **word** units such as **WEAR, WORK, WORD** and **WEAK**. >> Compatible units at different levels support each other, and competing units within a level inhibit each other. >> Active words send support back down to their own letters. || Suppose a reader sees **W, O** and **R** clearly, while ink blots hide part of the final letter. >> The visible parts of that letter fit both **R** and **K**. >> W, O and R activate words such as WORK and WORD, but only WORK fits the visible features of the final letter, so WORK becomes the strongest word. >> WORK sends support back to **K**, and the ambiguous letter is perceived as K, just as plug supports /p/ in speech. || A key issue in speech perception is that **the stimuli unfold over time**. >> A written word is on the page all at once, while a spoken word arrives one sound at a time. >> TRACE handles this by repeating feature, phoneme and word units at every point in time, so a candidate can match the beginning, gain activation and then fade when the ending conflicts.
25|**Categorical perception** shapes perception through **category boundaries**. **Given the same physical difference, it is harder to distinguish stimuli from the same category than from different categories**, so equal acoustic steps have unequal perceptual effects depending on whether they cross a boundary. || Even so, **ambiguity about phoneme identity does have an impact on word recognition**. >> Sounds with the same label differ in how strongly they activate a competing word. >> The word level keeps information about how uncertain each token was, so labels can be categorical while the processing behind them is graded. || **Word-level information** also **seems to have an impact on phoneme categorization**. >> In the Ganong effect, word knowledge moves the category boundary. >> In TRACE, that happens through lexical feedback to the phoneme level. || It therefore **cannot be the case that the word recognition system just sits there waiting for the phoneme recognition system to finish its job**, the way a furniture factory waits on a lumber mill. >> **Spoken word recognition is more interactive** than that, more like the food web. >> Partial information moves forward and active words send support back down, all while uncertainty is still being resolved.

27|**Spoken word recognition is also very fast**. **Allopenna and colleagues (1994)** used a visual world eye-tracking task to measure how quickly candidate words become active. >> Participants saw a grid of pictures and geometric shapes and followed spoken instructions to move objects. >> The display included a **beaker**, which was the **referent** or target, along with a **beetle**, a **speaker** and a **carriage**. || The pictures were chosen for their sound overlap with the word **beaker**. >> **Beetle** is a **cohort competitor** because it shares the beginning of the word. >> **Speaker** is a **rhyme competitor** because it shares the ending of the word. >> **Carriage** is **unrelated** and shares no sounds with beaker. || Looks to the beaker began rising before the word had even finished. >> Early on, listeners looked at the beaker and the beetle about equally, since both fit the first sounds. >> Once the sounds that separate the two words arrived, looks to the beetle dropped away, and looks to the beaker kept climbing. >> Looks to the speaker rose later and stayed smaller, since it matches only the end of the word. Looks to the carriage stayed near zero. || Recognition begins while the word is still unfolding. Words that match the input at a given moment become active, and they drop out as soon as they stop matching.
28|The eye-tracking data can be compared with a **TRACE simulation** of the same task. The model’s word activations over time were converted into predicted looks and compared with real fixations. || The TRACE predictions closely match the eye movements. >> For the **referent**, the predicted and real curves rise together and level off at nearly the same point. >> For the **cohort** competitor, the model predicts an early rise followed by a decline, matching the real looks in timing and size. >> For the **rhyme** competitor, the model predicts a smaller and later rise, again matching the real looks. || The **granularity** of the simulation is fine-grained, meaning it predicts activity moment by moment rather than only the final choice. >> **Eye movements give an idea of the changes in “activity” for different competitors over time**. >> This makes it possible to **see cognitive processing before a final response is given**. || This parallels the **graded effects of category goodness** in categorical perception. Sounds that are better or worse examples of a category produce different amounts of competitor activity, even when the final label is the same.
