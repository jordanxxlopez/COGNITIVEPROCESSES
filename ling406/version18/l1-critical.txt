2|**Critical periods** and **sensitive or “optimal” periods** are two ways to describe how the timing of experience shapes learning. They differ in how strict their time limits are. || A **critical period** has two defining features. >> It has a **discrete, “programmed” onset and offset**, so the window opens and shuts at fixed points in development, as if on a biological timetable. >> **Change in response to the environment, which is learning, is strictly limited to this period**. There is **no sensitivity to the environment before or after** the window, so experience outside it has no effect. || A **sensitive or “optimal” period** describes a looser link between timing and learning. >> **Learning is possible throughout the life span**. >> **Learning is optimal during particular windows**, when it is easiest or works best. >> **These windows can have a gradual onset and offset**, and their timing **can itself be affected by experience**. || The two ideas differ as impossibility differs from reduced effectiveness. >> A critical period predicts no learning at all after the window, so any late improvement counts against it. >> A sensitive period predicts that late learning is harder but still possible, with a lasting advantage for early learners. >> Even the classic critical period in vision fits the label sensitive period better, and the same question arises for language.
3|**Amblyopia** is **poor vision in one eye despite normal peripheral function**. **Peripheral function** means the working of the eye itself, so the eye can be healthy while vision through it is poor. The trouble lies in how the brain handles that eye’s input. || Three conditions can lead to amblyopia. >> **Strabismus** is a misalignment of the eyes, so they point in different directions and send the brain two conflicting images. >> A **cataract** clouds the lens of the eye and blurs or blocks the light that reaches the back of the eye. >> **Anisometropia** is a large difference in focusing power between the eyes, which makes one eye’s image much blurrier than the other’s. || **Periods of deprivation from normal visual input** due to these conditions **can result in poor vision long after the conditions themselves have been remediated**, meaning corrected. >> Straightening the eyes, removing the cataract or correcting the focus restores the eye’s input. >> The brain may still fail to use that input well. >> A short-lived problem early in life can leave a permanent effect, so when the problem occurs matters as much as what it is.
4|**Amblyopia occurs because of competitive dynamics between cortical inputs**. Both eyes send input to **visual cortex**, and the two inputs **compete** to control the neurons there. >> With both eyes working normally, they share cortical neurons evenly. >> **Abnormal vision in one eye can lead to “colonization” of the cortical representation of that eye by the other eye**, so the stronger eye takes over neurons that would have served the weaker one. || **Monocular deprivation**, blocking or degrading input to one eye, reveals how the takeover unfolds in mice. >> Within **2–3 days**, **dendritic spines**, the small projections where neurons receive connections, become unstable and move around, which is **increased spine motility**. >> Within **3–4 days**, there is **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration**. Connections from the deprived eye disappear, and **Wallerian degeneration** is the breakdown of nerve fibers cut off from their source. >> After **more than a week**, there is **axon sprouting and new spine birth**, as the open eye’s fibers branch out and form connections in the space the deprived eye lost. || **When normal function is restored to the deprived eye, it remains at a competitive disadvantage**. >> The other eye now holds the connections. >> **Normal cortical responses cannot be restored by normal experience**, since ordinary viewing with both eyes open keeps favoring the stronger eye.
5|**Treatments for amblyopia are based on competition**, and **one of the most effective treatments involves intermittently patching the “good” eye**. >> A patch on the stronger eye shuts off its input for part of the day. >> The brain then has to rely on the weaker eye. || Patching **restores the competitive balance between the eyes** and **permits the brain’s own plasticity to restore normal function**. **Plasticity** is the brain’s capacity to rewire its connections in response to experience. >> With the strong eye covered, the weak eye’s input can win back cortical connections. >> The patch leaves the eye itself unchanged. It alters the brain’s experience, and plasticity does the rest. || **This treatment is most effective early in development**, and it **may be ineffective by late childhood, around 10 years**. >> A young brain is highly plastic, so evening out the input leads to fast reorganization. >> As the visual system matures, the same treatment helps less and less, so when treatment happens matters as much as the treatment itself.
6|**Human adult plasticity after the critical period** is not entirely gone. **Levi and Polat (1996)** found that **plasticity is not abolished entirely, even well into adulthood**. **Some of these patients were in their 50s**, an age normally considered past the point where treatment works. || **Long-term, intensive training on specific psychophysical tasks** in the affected eye can lead to **improvements in both task performance and general visual function**. A **psychophysical task** measures the relationship between a physical stimulus and what a person perceives, such as the faintest contrast a person can detect. >> Participants practiced visual judgments with the amblyopic eye over many thousands of trials. >> The benefits spread beyond the trained task to vision more broadly. || Improvement was measured as a falling **threshold**, the smallest stimulus a person can reliably detect. A lower threshold means better vision. >> Early in training, the threshold dropped steadily, and this change was highly significant. >> It then leveled off for a long stretch of training. >> With continued practice, it dropped again to a new, lower level and stayed there. || The adult visual system therefore keeps some **experience-dependent plasticity**. The right kind and amount of training can still produce change after ordinary late treatment stops working.
7|**Li, Ngo, Nguyen, Lam and Levi (2008)** found that **playing video games enhances visual acuity and visual attention in adult amblyopia**. Adults played an action video game, a first-person shooter set on a battlefield, with their amblyopic eye. >> **Visual acuity** is sharpness of vision, the ability to see fine detail. Better acuity means clearer vision through the weak eye. >> **Visual attention** is the ability to pick out and focus on the important parts of a scene, such as targets in a busy battle. || Action games demand quick visual decisions and constant attention, which force the visual system to use the amblyopic eye’s input. >> These adults were long past childhood visual development, so their gains came from the training itself. >> Intensive visual experience improved vision well beyond the age when patching normally works best. || Video game training adds to the evidence for **plasticity** in adult amblyopia. >> A demanding, engaging visual task can drive change in a mature visual system. >> The closing of an early optimal window does not remove every chance of improvement.
8|An **interim summary** pulls the vision evidence together. **Amblyopia is a classic example of a sensitive period**. >> A **peripheral disturbance in early experience can lead to life-long disruption in visual function**, so an early problem with the eye itself permanently affects vision. >> **The long-term loss of function is the sequela of a temporary deficit during experience-dependent organization of the brain**. A **sequela** is a lasting result of an earlier condition. The brain wired itself around abnormal input, and that wiring remains. >> **Treatment is most effective during a limited developmental window**, since patching works best early and loses its power by late childhood. || **New evidence suggests that the right kind of experience can reverse deficits late into adulthood**. >> Intensive perceptual training and video game play improved adults’ vision. >> Late learning is harder and needs special conditions, but it does happen. || This pattern matches a **sensitive period** better than a strict critical period. >> A strict critical period would forbid any late improvement. >> Amblyopia pairs a strong early window with some ability to change later, which is what defines a sensitive period.
9|The vision evidence connects to language through two lines of work. >> **Lenneberg’s early attempts to combine developmental neurology with the notion of a “language acquisition device.”** **Developmental neurology** studies how the nervous system develops, and a **language acquisition device** is a proposed innate capacity for learning language. >> **Studies of early deprivation**, including **data from neglected children** and from **Deaf children with limited input to sign language**. || Lenneberg asked whether changes in the maturing brain could open and close a window for language. >> He tied language milestones to stages of brain development. >> If such a window exists, learning language depends on the state of the brain as well as on the input. || Deprivation studies examine people who met an accessible language unusually late. >> Neglected children were cut off from ordinary language input. >> Some Deaf children have no access to sign language in early childhood. >> Comparing them with typical learners tests whether the age of first exposure affects the final outcome.
10|In **Biological Foundations of Language (1967)**, **Lenneberg was attempting to combine contemporary linguistics with what was known about brain development**. **Studies of language development were starting to show a fairly robust and stage-like pattern**. || Data from Austrian, British and American children follow the same order. >> **Single words** appear first, mostly between about 10 and 18 months. >> **Two-word phrases** come next. >> **Sentences of five words or more** come last, toward the end of the third year. || Lenneberg paired these language milestones with motor milestones at each age. >> Around **8 months**, infants stand while holding on, and **reduplicated** babbling, such as repeated syllables, becomes frequent. >> Around **12 months**, infants walk with one hand held, and words such as **mama** or **dada** emerge. They understand some words and simple commands. >> By **18 months**, children have a small vocabulary of more than three but fewer than fifty words, and comprehension grows rapidly. || **The idea that language might have maturational milestones was seen as consistent with the unfolding of a biological program**. >> Language milestones line up with motor milestones, which suggests a shared biological timetable. >> Lenneberg proposed that brain maturation sets the pace for both, so the timing of language learning depends on the developing brain.
11|**Data on typical brain development were sparse** in Lenneberg’s time, **because they were almost entirely based on post-mortem analyses of tissue**. **Post-mortem** means after death. >> Researchers could not follow the same children’s brains as they grew. >> They had to piece together development from the brains of children who had died at different ages. || Measurements of **neuron size** in the cerebral cortex show rapid growth in the first two years, followed by leveling off. >> The data have a large gap between ages **2 and 18**, because **no measurements were available** for that period. >> That gap overlaps much of the period of **language acquisition**. || Lenneberg observed that **abrupt changes in neuron size and packing density before age two might be permissive for the beginning of language acquisition**. >> **Packing density** is how closely neurons are crowded together in the tissue. >> **Permissive** means these changes make language acquisition possible without directly causing it. || On this view, **the critical period seems to open after this initial period of rapid development**. >> The brain first goes through a burst of physical growth, and once that growth settles, the window for learning language opens. >> Lenneberg filled the gap in the data with a reasonable guess about how the brain matures, which later research would need to test directly.
12|**More extensive data were available from studies of recovery from stroke**, and they reveal that **aphasia is surprisingly rare in children with brain lesions**. A **lesion** is an area of damaged brain tissue. >> Damage that leaves an adult with lasting aphasia often has much milder effects in a child. || The most extreme cases involve **hemispherectomy**, surgery that removes or disconnects a whole **cerebral hemisphere**, usually **to relieve seizures in people with epilepsy**. >> **Children in whom the left hemisphere is removed nonetheless seem to develop typical language abilities**. >> **Adults do not recover language after this procedure**. || Lenneberg’s summary of hemispherectomy outcomes, drawn from Basser (1962), sorts cases by age at injury. >> Among children injured **before the teens** whose **left** hemisphere was removed, **49** had speech that was **not affected or improved**, and only **3** had permanent aphasia, all of whom had it before surgery. >> Children who lost the right hemisphere did similarly well. >> Among **adults** whose **left** hemisphere was removed, **none** had speech unaffected or improved, and **6** had permanent aphasia. >> Adults who lost the right hemisphere kept their speech. || These outcomes led Lenneberg to propose that **a key factor in closing the critical period was the development of adult-like cerebral dominance**. >> **Cerebral dominance** is the specialization of one hemisphere, usually the left, for language. >> A young child’s language is not yet locked into the left hemisphere, so the right hemisphere can take over. Once dominance is set in adulthood, losing the left hemisphere leaves permanent aphasia.
13|**As always, things are more complicated**. **Systematic studies of children with perinatal strokes** add important detail. A **perinatal stroke** happens between the **prenatal period and 28 days after birth**. >> **Outcomes are clearly much better than for adults with similar injuries**. >> The children still show some effects of the injury. || **Early language deficits appear in both right- and left-hemisphere patients**. >> Adults mainly lose language after damage to the left side. >> Young children show early language delays after damage to either side. >> **Some deficits are worse for children with right-hemisphere damage**, the reverse of the adult pattern. || **Language outcomes are generally in the low-normal range**. >> That points to **a persistent delay rather than catching up**. >> Children recover far better than adults but often fall short of children without injury. || Early plasticity is therefore real but limited. A young brain can reorganize to support language, yet the reorganized system may work less well than the original would have.
14|**Curtiss and colleagues (1974)** studied the **effects of early deprivation** through the case of **Genie**. >> Genie was born in **April 1957**. >> When researchers first met her, she was **13 years and 7 months old** and so thin that she looked six or seven. || Her early life involved extreme isolation and abuse. >> **From the age of 20 months**, she was shut in a small room under ever tighter physical restraint. >> She spent most of her time harnessed to an infant’s potty chair, and otherwise lay in a homemade sleeping bag inside a crib covered with wire mesh. >> Her care came from a mother who was quickly losing her sight, and she ate only infant food. >> She was **physically punished by her father if she made any sounds**. Her father and older brother never spoke to her, though they sometimes barked at her like dogs. >> The home had no television or radio, and she had almost no contact with other people. || When she was hospitalized for malnutrition, Genie **could not stand up straight or chew food**, was **not toilet trained**, and **did not speak, cry or produce any vocal sounds**. >> Her case reveals what happens when a first language is learned starting in adolescence. >> Her lack of language came with severe physical, social and emotional deprivation, so the effects of missing language input cannot be pulled apart from her other hardships.
15|**During the four years that her language was observed, Genie learned quite a bit**. >> She came to **comprehend hundreds of words**. >> She came to **comprehend some aspects of morphology**, and she was **particularly good with plurals**. **Morphology** is the system of meaningful word parts, such as the plural ending “-s.” >> She came to **comprehend some prepositions**, words for spatial relationships between objects. || A test of prepositions used **buttons and plastic boxes** of different colors and sizes. Genie moved the buttons and boxes according to spoken instructions. >> A sample item was **“Put the red box behind the blue box.”** >> The test covered **under, over, in, on, behind** and **in front of**, along with **in back of** and **next to**. || Across the year of testing, Genie gave more correct than incorrect responses for every preposition. >> She was strongest on **in** and **on**, with **37 correct out of 41** for in and **36 out of 45** for on. >> She also did well on spatial phrases such as in front of and next to. || Some language can therefore be acquired late. This learning took place after puberty, long after the window Lenneberg proposed, but it was uneven across different parts of language.
16|Genie also **did not learn** several things. **Genie’s production lagged severely behind her comprehension**. >> **Some lag is typical of development**, since every child understands more than they can say. >> Genie had been **punished for making sounds**, which could also hold back her speech, so what she said understated what she knew. || **Her grammatical abilities developed much more slowly than her vocabulary**. In a test of **conjunctions**, **five familiar objects in a row** were set in front of her, and she was asked to point to one or more of them. >> Items included **“Show me the fork and pencil,” “Show me the crayon or the knife”** and **“Show me either the spoon or the crayon.”** >> **And** calls for pointing to every named object. **Or** calls for pointing to just one, which depends on grasping a logical relationship. || At first she treated **or** exactly like **and** and pointed to both objects. **Later responses** told and from or apart, but she never understood **either/or**. >> Over about two years of testing, she answered almost every **and** sentence correctly. >> She answered only a handful of **or** sentences correctly and got most of them wrong. >> She mastered and while failing or, despite knowing hundreds of words. That combination of strong vocabulary and weak grammar fits the idea that grammar depends more on early exposure than vocabulary does.
17|Genie’s case leaves **lots of questions**. >> **Was Genie learning disabled?** She may have had difficulties from birth that would have affected her language even without deprivation. >> **What were the residual effects of neglect and abuse on her social development?** Severe trauma could disrupt learning in many ways besides the missing language input. || Another limit is that **after four years, funding to study her was pulled**. >> **We cannot know what she might have learned after a longer time**. >> The evidence reaches only as far as those four years. || **Nonetheless, the disparity between her vocabulary development and her grammatical development is widely interpreted as suggesting that grammar learning in a first language is specifically impaired after a sensitive period**. >> She picked up hundreds of words but made little headway with grammar. >> On this reading, words can be learned at any age, while grammar relies on early exposure. >> Disability, abuse and late exposure could each have affected her grammar, and one case cannot separate them. Her pattern still became an influential piece of evidence for a sensitive period in first-language learning.
18|**Mayberry, Lock and Kazmi (2002)** studied deaf people who acquired a first language late. **Many deaf children do not get sign language input but have an otherwise typical upbringing**. >> Unlike Genie, these children grow up in ordinary, caring homes. >> Their only unusual experience is a lack of accessible language early in life, which makes them a cleaner test of first-language timing. || **Children aged 9 to 15 have difficulty learning American Sign Language, or ASL, if it is their first time learning a language**. >> A **comparison group** of children who **went deaf after initially learning a spoken language** **do much better** at learning ASL. >> Both groups learned ASL late, but only one group had an earlier language. >> **These differences persist into adulthood**. || Two tasks measured the outcome in adulthood. >> In **recall of complex ASL sentences**, adults with **no early language** scored about **36%**, while adults with an **early spoken language** scored about twice as well. >> In **judging whether complex English sentences were grammatical**, where chance is 50%, deaf adults with **no early language** were close to chance. Deaf adults who had ASL in infancy did about as well as hearing adults with an early spoken language. || The advantage comes from **having any language early**, whatever its type. >> Early ASL helped later English learning as much as an early spoken language did. >> People with no early language struggled with both ASL and English. Early accessible language, signed or spoken, provides the foundation for learning languages later.
19|The evidence speaks to whether a first language, or **L1**, has a **“critical period”** or a **“sensitive period.”** >> **Recovery from stroke, and certainly hemispherectomy, looks very, very different in childhood and adulthood**. Children can build language after damage that would leave an adult permanently aphasic. >> Age therefore strongly affects the brain’s ability to reorganize for language. || **Deprivation of input does not appear to extend the critical period**. >> It might seem that a child with no language input would keep the window open until input arrives. >> Instead, Genie and deaf adults who learned sign late both show lasting trouble, especially with grammar. >> The window appears to follow a maturational schedule whether or not language has been experienced. || **All of these data come from highly unusual cases with many other things going on**, so it is **difficult to reach firm conclusions**. >> Stroke, brain surgery, abuse and neglect all add complications beyond the timing of input. >> There is also a **lack of animal models**, since animals do not learn human language. >> Experiments such as **gene knockouts**, which switch off specific genes to test what they do, **cannot be done with people**.
