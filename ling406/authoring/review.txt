Syntactic explanations of the “garden path” effect
The explanation rests on the idea of a parser that deals exclusively with the abstract 
syntactic structure of the sentence (remember the “lumber mill” example from speech 
perception).
The parser has its own operating characteristics. It wants to make the simplest tree possible 
(minimal attachment) and it accomplishes this, in part, by leaving clauses open as long as 
possible (late closure).
This is one way of dealing with ambiguity very efficiently – ignore it until it becomes a 
problem!

===== PDF PAGE 36 =====
A very compelling narrative:
Speech perception depends on categorical perception abilities that are a special 
property of the language system.
These are present very early in development.
In fact, children start off as “citizens of the world,” able to hear all contrasts (i.e., 
they have innate, language-general knowledge about speech) but then rapidly 
tune their perceptual systems to the specific language in their environment. 
The role of learning, then, is to select among the many possible innately specified 
phonetic contrasts, and narrow down the inventory to just those that are 
meaningful in the child’s native language.

A model explaining L1-L2 interactions in terms of spreading activation: 
The revised hierarchical model of Kroll & Stewart
The L1 lexicon is assumed to be:
- larger
- more strongly connected to 
relevant concepts
The L2 lexicon is assumed to have strong 
forward connections to the L1 lexicon.
That is, activation can spread more readily 
from an L2 word to an L1 word than the other 
way.
(This occurs because this direction is 
emphasized in learning.)


===== PDF PAGE 54 =====
A model explaining L1-L2 interactions in terms of spreading activation: 
The Revised Hierarchical Model of Kroll & Stewart (1990; 1994)
Consider what happens in this model when 
performing the Marian & Spivey task in L2:
- the concept for “speaker” partially 
activates the Russian word for “matches”
- this activity feeds back to the 
conceptual activation for “matches”
- conceptual activation for “matches” 
leads to eye-movements toward the L1 
competitor.
When the task is done in L1, we might imagine 
that the conceptual links with L2 are too weak 
to create this kind of interference reliably.
