===== PDF PAGE 2 =====
In the 1870’s a number of German neurologists —
Lichtheim, Wernicke — began making more 
systematic observations of aphasia.
They ascribed specific losses of function to 
“disconnections” between “centers.”
So, a loss of the ability to understand spoken words 
might be due to a disconnection between “A” and 
“B.”
A loss of the ability to do verbatim repetition may 
be due to a disconnection between “A” and “M,” 
and so on.
This kind of ”box and arrow” model is foundational, 
not just in aphasiology, but in modeling of typical 
language function (consider the interactive 
activation model we discussed in the last lecture).
Eling, P., & Whitaker, H. (2009). History of aphasia: 
from brain to language. In Handbook of clinical 
neurology (Vol. 95, pp. 571-582).
Reminder: Reasoning about aphasia Before Chomsky


===== PDF PAGE 3 =====
The “Geschwind-Wernicke model” of brain and language
In the 1970’s Norman Geschwind revived 
this approach.
In the century between Wernicke and 
Geschwind, the field of linguistics evolved 
dramatically.
Specifically, the development of a 
theoretical model in which syntax was 
independent of other aspects of language, 
had its own computational principles, etc., 
opened the door for theorizing about the 
(double) dissociations of language functions 
in more sophisticated ways.

===== PDF PAGE 11 =====
Why was it interesting to show that Broca’s patients have 
comprehension deficits?
Broca’s aphasia is almost always accompanied with some level of motor dysfunction.
If agrammatic speech merely reflects an attempt to communicate despite motor limitations.
Remember Hughlings Jackson: We are observing what the individual can do despite the 
impairment. Just because a brain injury leads to agrammatic speech doesn’t literally mean 
that the grammar itself is injured!
Consider also Chomsky’s distinction between competence and performance. When motor 
deficits obviously interfere with performance, comprehension may provide a clearer 
window into competence…
Caramazza & Zurif (1976)


===== PDF PAGE 12 =====
Man Bites Dog
Why do we need psycholinguistic tests to observe grammatical difficulties in 
comprehension?
In conversation, there are lots of redundant cues to what a speaker intends:
The dog bit the man.
There are so many ways to get the right meaning here:
- Most likely combination of DOG, BITE and MAN into an event representation with 
or without syntax.
- Canonical word order: Agent – Verb – Undergoer; Subject – Verb – Object
Further, a lot of language comprehension takes place in discourse context that clarifies a 
lot...
Maybe Broca’s patients’ comprehension depends on these compensatory heuristics. If 
that were the case you might want to test them with sentences more like:
The dog that the man is biting is angry.

Data from a sentence-picture

Healthy controls are more or less

Wernicke’s patients are poor

The Broca’s patients seem to have a

This specific comprehension deficit 
was taken as evidence that Broca’s

Problems with this dichotomy
Dick et al. had participants listen to four 
different types of sentences:
Active:
The dog is biting the cat.
Subject cleft:
It’s the dog that is biting the cat.
Object cleft: 
It’s the cat that the dog is biting.
Passive:
The cat is bitten by the dog. 


===== PDF PAGE 17 =====
When aphasia patients and healthy aging controls do this task, a pattern emerges much 
like what Caramazza and Zurif observed. The Broca’s aphasia patients have particular 
difficulty with the syntactically complex stimuli (although note that their performance is 
overall not so great).

Are Broca’s aphasics comprehension deficits language-specific?
Saygin et al. (2004) asked patients to 
match the action to the object.
Broca’s aphasics had difficulty with this 
task, whether the stimuli were linguistic 
or not.

===== PDF PAGE 23 =====
So, how language-specific are the deficits in aphasia?
This is still a controversy, actually.
What should be clear from this discussion is how the terms of the debate have evolved.
- relatively sophisticated tests to tap specific aspects of language
- quantitative analyses of behavior
- quantitative analyses of structure-function relationships
We actually have come quite a long way from the 19th Century!
