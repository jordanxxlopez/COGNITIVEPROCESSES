Transfer and grammar learning
Classifiers are a somewhat unusual grammatical 
feature cross-linguistically.
Native English speakers learning Chinese (for 
example) have difficulty learning these.
In English, when we want to say “a chair,” “that 
dog,” “the car,” or “one book,” we don’t worry 
too much about noun semantics when picking a 
determiner or pronoun.
But in Chinese, different classifiers are required 
by different nouns.
One way to think about this is that English 
speakers have learned to ignore the different 
cues for classifiers as part of learning English 
grammar.  


===== PDF PAGE 7 =====
Another example where transfer causes 
problems is with grammatical gender.
Grammatical gender is different from 
social/cultural gender.
There’s nothing “male” about a book or 
“female” about liberty (although: hold that 
thought for when we discuss “language and 
thought” later in the semester).
But grammatical gender has implications for 
agreement. Pronouns, adjectives, some forms of 
verb, etc. all need to be coordinated with noun 
gender.
French is famous for having synonyms with different gender:
auto/voiture
rivière/fleuve
vélo/bicyclette

Transfer in the lab: 
Do people use cognates in “second language” 
learning?
DeGroot & Keijzer (2000, Language learning) 
- paired associate learning of made-up words
- varying in their similarity to their Dutch associates
- also varying in “concreteness.” 
For example:
gitaar – gettar (“guitar,” concrete, cognate)
tijger - duunze (“tiger,” concrete, non-cognate)
domein – domaan (“domain,” abstract, cognate)
herstel – groop (“repair,” abstract, non-cognate)
Participants were simply presented with pairs of Dutch words and their non-word paired 
associates, and asked to learn these pairs.
They were then tested a week later on retention.

When only one competitor was present, participants made eye movements to both within- and 
between-language competitors to the same extent.
When both within- and between-language competitors were present, participants looked at the 
within-language competitor more, but still looked at the between-language competitor a lot 
(i.e., twice as often as a control condition).

===== PDF PAGE 20 =====
A model explaining L1-L2 interactions in terms of spreading activation: 
The revised hierarchical model of Kroll & Stewart
The L1 lexicon is assumed to be:
- larger
- more strongly connected to relevant 
concepts
The L2 lexicon is assumed to have strong 
forward connections to the L1 lexicon.
That is, activation can spread more readily 
from an L2 word to an L1 word than the other 
way.
(This occurs because this direction is 
emphasized in learning.)


===== PDF PAGE 21 =====
A model explaining L1-L2 interactions in terms of spreading activation: 
The Revised Hierarchical Model of Kroll & Stewart (1990; 1994)
Consider what happens in this model when 
performing the Marian & Spivey task in L2:
- the concept for “matches” partially 
activates the Russian word for “matches”
- this activity feeds back to the conceptual 
activation for “matches”
- conceptual activation for “matches” 
leads to eye-movements toward the L1 
competitor.
When the task is done in L1, we might imagine 
that the conceptual links with L2 are too weak 
to create this kind of interference reliably. 


===== PDF PAGE 22 =====
Note that this model makes a counter-intuitive prediction even for “balanced bilinguals,” 
i.e., that they do not look like “two monolinguals in one,” but rather that competitive 
dynamics and inhibition will be engaged all the time b/c the conceptual links are similarly 
strong for both languages.
This has lead to a lot of work on bilinguals’ advantages in non-linguistic cognitive control 
tasks…

===== PDF PAGE 28 =====
Pallier et al. also found that listening to French activated the (now familiar?) classical 
language network, including superior temporal and inferior frontal regions.
Subtracting Korean from French might have revealed some difference between groups, if 
there were any residual processing ability for Korean left. But it didn’t.
