===== PDF PAGE 3 =====
One premise:
Somehow we have to turn a highly variable, analog signal, into 
something more abstract, so that the language system can 
operate on it.
Categorical perception is thought to reflect this kind of 
“translation” from physical to symbolic.
Note, next time, we’ll talk about some alternative ideas 
about this.

===== PDF PAGE 11 =====
Remember that formant values are 
partly determined by anatomical 
features that differ between 
speakers of the same language, by 
different rates of speech, and by 
context (e.g., the phones 
produced before and after a 
vowel).  
Nonetheless, formant values can 
provide a pretty good description 
of “vowel space,” especially for a 
specific talker, speaking carefully.
All else being equal, we can produce 
reasonable approximations of 
vowels by manipulating steady-
state formants.

A simple “ideal observer” model assuming perception 
is linear.
But the other important thing we’d predict is that Discrimination performance should 
be independent of Labeling. 
Obviously labeling has to have a sort of “peaked” function, because you’ve got three 
categories, and you’re asking people to make binary responses. But for the 
discrimination task, you’re just asking people to say “same” or “different.” If 
perception of differences in F2 onset were constant over the whole continuum, 
discrimination accuracy would also be constant.

“Perceptual narrowing” for speech categories


===== PDF PAGE 27 =====
A very compelling narrative:
Speech perception depends on categorical 
perception abilities that are a special property of 
the language system.
These are present very early in development.
In fact, children start off as “citizens of the world,” 
able to hear all contrasts, but then rapidly tune 
their perceptual systems to their specific 
language.
