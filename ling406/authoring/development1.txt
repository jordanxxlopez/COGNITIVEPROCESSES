Nature and Nurture in Child Language
“Nativist” positions: Remember Chomsky’s critique of Skinner
The important feature of language is its generativity. 
Acquiring a language = identifying the abstract grammar that produces sentences in the 
language.
General learning mechanisms studied by behaviorists were not up to this task:
- grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are not “similar” in any straightforward 
way.
- The context for language acquisition does not look like operant conditioning –
reward/punishment contingencies don’t hold
Instead, nativist approaches assert:
- innate knowledge of what language is like (Universal Grammar)
- language-specific acquisition processes 
“The fact that all normal children acquire essentially comparable grammars of great complexity 
with remarkable rapidity suggests that human beings are somehow specially designed to do this, 
with data-handling or ‘hypothesis-formulating’ ability of unknown character and complexity.”

Remember Categorical Perception
Early theories of categorical perception treated it as a language-specific and species-
specific phenomenon. Only humans were known to show categorical perception, and 
only for speech sounds.
It’s a compelling example of how similarity between stimuli is defined in linguistic, 
rather than physical terms.

===== PDF PAGE 6 =====
Why was this a big deal?
Categorical perception was considered to be the first 
step in turning speech into a symbolic format that 
could be used by later linguistic processes.
Four month olds are really little. Some gross motor 
milestones for that age include:
- manipulating objects with two hands
- holding their head and chest up while prone
Their color vision and general visual acuity are still “in 
progress.”
They are just starting to produce vowel-like sounds 
instead of creaky whines.
This is very, very early in development for an 
apparently language-specific ability to emerge, so it 
seemed like evidence for innateness.

“Perceptual narrowing” for speech categories
At around six months, English-

of discriminating speech

before.
Over the course of 
development, the ability to

How is this related to 
categorical perception?


===== PDF PAGE 8 =====
A very compelling narrative:
Speech perception depends on categorical perception abilities that are 
a special property of the language system.
These are present very early in development.
In fact, children start off as “citizens of the world,” able to hear all 
contrasts (i.e., they have innate, language-general knowledge about 
speech) but then rapidly tune their perceptual systems to the 
specific language in their environment. 
The role of learning, then, is to select among the many possible 
innately specified phonetic contrasts, and narrow down the 
inventory to just those that are meaningful in the child’s native 
language.


===== PDF PAGE 9 =====
Some problems for this view
Categorical perception:
- not limited to language
- not limited to humans
- task-dependent
Infant speech perception:
- not actually “universal”

===== PDF PAGE 16 =====
Here are the American vowels 
again!
When Kuhl and others talk about 
learning the statistics of 
distributions in the environment, 
they mean things like this.
We can think of a “vowel space” 
determined by the first and second 
formants. 
Maybe we learn the vowels of our 
language by extracting the 
statistics of these distributions.
So, a “prototypical” /a/ is just the 
mean (or mode?) of the 
distribution of all the /a/ sounds 
we hear.  


===== PDF PAGE 17 =====
Does anything like that actually happen?
Maye et al. had infants listen passively to 
two distributions of voice onset time.
The dashed lines indicate a bimodal 
distribution, where the input provided 
evidence for two different categories.
The solid line indicates a unimodal
distribution, providing evidence for a 
single category.
At test, infants listened to stimuli 3 and 6 
on the continuum in either alternating or 
non-alternating trials.
Only infants pre-exposed to the bimodal 
distribution were sensitive to the 
difference between conditions.
…as if the infants in the unimodal
condition had learned to treat stimuli 3 
and 6 as members of the same category.


===== PDF PAGE 18 =====
Social/interactive 
context for learning.
Some experimental evidence that social 
interaction is important for learning:
The same exposure to a Mandarin 
speaker is more effective for learning 
when it happens in person (and is 
interactive) than when it is viewed as a 
video.
Social interactions contingent on 
production of different sounds seem to 
function much like a reinforcing 
stimulus.
One idea is that humans find social 
interaction with other humans innately 
reinforcing.
Another is that we are innately good at 
using social cues to learn from other 
people.
