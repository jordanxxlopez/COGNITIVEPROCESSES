Some cues to word boundaries
Words sometimes appear in isolation: “Hello.” “No.” “Please.” 
“Go.” “Stop.” etc., but not all words…
Phonotactics: Some sequences of phonemes are very unlikely to 
occur within words: “wordboundary,” “speechcode,” 
“movement.” 
Stress patterns: In English, most words are stressed on the first 
syllable. (Jusczyk et al., 1999)
Kingdom passage
Your kingdom is in a faraway place. The 
prince used to sail to that kingdom when 
he came home from school. One day he 
saw a ghost in this old kingdom…
Device passage
Your device can do a lot. Her device only 
fixes things. My new red device makes ice 
cream. The pink device sews clothes….
7.5 month old Infants familiarized with 
“kingdom” showed a preference for the 
“kingdom” passage.
Infants familiarized with “device” did not show 
the same kind of preference.
In connected speech, stress may help signal 
the beginnings of words. (Of course this won’t 
work in every language, or for every word in 
English!)

Isolating statistical information from 
other cues (Saffran et al., 1996)
In order to show that transitional probabilities can 
be used to identify words, stimuli were tightly 
controlled:
monotone (no changes in pitch, duration, amplitude, etc.)
simple CV syllables (no phonotactic cues to word boundaries)
presented continuously (no pauses)
A set of three-syllable “words” was created, (e.g., 
tokibu, gikoba, gopila)

Testing for an effect of transitional 
probabilities
After listening to a long stream of syllables, it’s trivial to show that infants are 
more familiar with sequences they have heard than ones they haven’t.
Two additional details are important:
1. Half of the words were repeated more frequently than the other half.
2. Test for familiarity was against “part words” that were matched for 
frequency of occurrence with real words.
Example:
tokibu gikoba gopila gikoba tokibu gopila tokibu gikoba gikoba tokibu
Here GOPILA and BU-GIKO both appear twice, but the probability of GI given BU is 
2/4, whereas the TPs for GOPILA are both 2/2.

How do children acquire word meanings?
Joint attention:
Optimizes word learning
Baldwin et al. 
Joint attention group:  adult interacting with baby 
labeled the object the baby was playing with
No joint attention group: voice from behind a screen 
labeled the object the baby was playing with
Results:
18-month-olds learned the novel word in the Joint 
attention group but not in the No joint attention group.

How do children start putting words together?
In production, it can be difficult to distinguish what’s going on when children produce only 
one, two or three words at a time.
Menn distinguishes among:
- “just a bunch of relevant words”
- formulas
- word combinations
- stable constructions (not necessarily adult-like)
Up!
Pick you up!
Mommy up!
In practice, a full description of the context in 
which an utterance is produced is necessary 
(but often not sufficient!) to distinguish among 
these.

A generalization test:
If children have really learned the transitive 
structure, they will generalize it to new verbs.
Think about what you need to know in order 
to select the picture that could depict “The 
duck is gorping the bunny” from these two 
novel actions.
Gertner et al (2006) showed that two-year 
olds can understand this: They look 
significantly longer at the duck acting on the 
rabbit than vice-versa.)
At this age, kids typically have a mean length 
of utterance (MLU) of around 2. 
Their three morpheme utterances might be 
like “Duck gorp bunny.”
