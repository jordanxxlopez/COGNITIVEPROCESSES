Critical periods vs. “sensitive” or “optimal periods”:
Characteristics of critical periods
- discrete, "programmed" onset and offset
- change in response to the environment (learning) is strictly limited to this 
period; no sensitivity to the environment before or after.
The notion of sensitive or “optimal” periods
- learning is possible throughout the life-span
- but learning is optimal during particular windows
- these windows might have a gradual onset/offset, which can itself be affected 
by experience.
First, we’ll look at a classic “critical period” in vision, and see that even there the term 
“sensitive period” might be more apt.
Then we’ll look at some of the evidence for critical periods in language learning.


===== PDF PAGE 3 =====
Amblyopia: poor vision in one eye despite normal 
peripheral function
strabismus
cataract
anisometropia
Periods of deprivation from normal visual input caused by these conditions 
can result in poor vision long after the conditions themselves have been 
remediated.
Images courtesy Dennis Levi


===== PDF PAGE 4 =====
Amblyopia occurs because of competitive dynamics
between cortical inputs
Abnormal vision in one eye can lead 
to “colonization” of cortical 
representation of that eye by the 
other.
When normal function is restored to 
the eye, it remains at a competitive 
disadvantage, and normal cortical 
responses cannot be restored by 
normal experience.


===== PDF PAGE 5 =====
Treatments for amblyopia are based on competition
Image courtesy Terri Lewis
One of the most effective treatments for 
amblyopia involves intermittently patching the 
“good” eye.
This restores the competitive balance between the 
eyes and permits the brain’s own plasticity to 
restore normal function.
This treatment is most effective early in 
development, however, and may be ineffective by 
late childhood (10y).

Levi & Polat (1996, PNAS)

Long-term, intensive training on

===== PDF PAGE 8 =====
Interim summary
Amblyopia is a classic example of a sensitive period:
- peripheral disturbance in early experience can lead to life-long 
disruption in visual function
- the long-term loss of function is the sequela of a temporary deficit 
during experience-dependent organization of the brain
- treatment is most effective during a limited developmental window
However, new evidence suggests that the right kind of experience can 
reverse defecits late into adulthood.

===== PDF PAGE 10 =====
Lenneberg’s (1967) Biological Foundations of Language
Lenneberg was attempting to synthesize contemporary 
linguistics with what was known about brain development.
Studies of language development were starting to show a 
fairly robust and stage-like pattern of language 
development.
The idea that language 
might have maturational 
milestones was seen as 
consistent with the 
unfolding of a biological 
program.


===== PDF PAGE 11 =====
The data on typical brain development were 
sparse, because they were almost entirely 
based on post-mortem analyses of tissue. 
(See, e.g., the figure depicting neuron size, for 
which no data were available between ages 2 
and 18.)
Nonetheless, Lenneberg observed that abrupt 
changes in things like neuron size and packing 
density before age two might be permissive for 
the beginning of language acquisition.
That is, the critical period seems to open after 
this initial period of rapid development.
Data on typical brain development


===== PDF PAGE 12 =====
More extensive data were available from studies 
of recovery from stroke.
Aphasia is surprisingly rare in children 
with brain lesions. 
In extreme cases, a hemispherectomy
was performed (typically to relieve 
seizures of people with epilepsy).
Children in whom the left 
hemisphere is removed nonetheless 
seem to develop typical language 
abilities.
Adults do not recover language after 
this procedure.
This (among other things) led 
Lenneberg to hypothesize that a key 
factor to closing the critical period 
was the development of adult-like 
cerebral dominance.


===== PDF PAGE 13 =====
As always, things are more complicated.
Systematic studies of children with perinatal (prenatal to 28d) strokes show that outcomes 
are clearly much better than for adults with similar injuries, BUT:
1. Early language deficits in both right and left hemisphere patients. (Some deficits 
worse for children with RH damage.)
2. Language outcomes generally in low-normal range, suggesting a persistent delay
rather than a catching up.

===== PDF PAGE 15 =====
What did Genie learn?
During the four years that her language was observed, Genie learned quite a bit:
- comprehension of hundreds of words
- comprehension of some aspects of morphology (particularly good with plurals)
- comprehension of some prepositions


===== PDF PAGE 16 =====
What didn’t Genie learn?
Genie’s production lagged severely behind her comprehension:
- some lag is typical of development
- remember that she was punished for making sounds as a child
Her grammatical abilities developed much more slowly than her vocabulary.


===== PDF PAGE 17 =====
Lots of questions:
Was Genie learning disabled?
What were the residual impacts of neglect and abuse on her social development?
…etc.
BUT:
After four years, funding to study her was pulled, so we can’t know what she might 
have learned after a longer time. 
Nonetheless, the disparity between her vocabulary development and grammatical 
development is widely interpreted as suggestive that grammar learning in a first 
language is specifically impaired after a sensitive period.


===== PDF PAGE 18 =====
Mayberry, Lock, & Kazmi (2002, Nature)
Many deaf children do not get sign language input, 
but have an otherwise typical upbringing.
Children (ages 9-15) have difficulty learning 
American Sign Language (ASL) if this is their first time 
learning a language.
A comparison group of children who went deaf after 
initially learning a spoken language do much better.
These differences persist into adulthood.


===== PDF PAGE 19 =====
So, is there a “critical period” or a “sensitive 
period” for L1?
- Recovery from stroke, and certainly hemispherectomy looks very, very different in 
childhood vs. adulthood. 
- Deprivation of input does not appear to extend the critical period. 
- All of these data are from highly unusual cases with lots of other things going on. Difficult 
to reach firm conclusions. (Also note lack of animal models; can’t do gene knockout 
experiments with people…)
