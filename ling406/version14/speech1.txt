2|**Speech perception** is the process of recovering a linguistic message from the sound of speech. We take an acoustic signal that looks like a mess and retrieve a message as clear as “I can see you.” >> A **spectrogram** of “I can see you” has time along the bottom and frequency up the side, with darker regions marking stronger energy. The dark bands run into one another, and no clean gaps line up with separate letters or even separate words. >> The **vocal tract** image from the USC SPAN real-time MRI traces the tongue, lips and other articulators during speech. These articulators move continuously, and the movement for one sound overlaps with the movement for the next. Because the movements overlap, their acoustic effects overlap too. || We also extract **the same message from very different physical stimuli**. People differ in voice and vocal tract size, so the same word comes out with different acoustic properties from each speaker. Listeners still hear it as the same word. >> The vowel plot graphs the **frequency of the second formant against the frequency of the first formant** for ten vowels produced by 76 speakers. Each vowel fills a large oval region rather than a single point. >> Many of those ovals **overlap**. One pair of frequency values can belong to one vowel for one speaker and to a different vowel for another. || Recognizing speech therefore means finding a **stable linguistic identity** inside a great deal of physical variation.
3|One working premise is that we have to **turn a highly variable, analog signal into something more abstract**, so the language system can operate on it. An **analog signal** changes continuously. Frequency, loudness and timing can each shift by tiny amounts. >> A continuous signal has no built-in boundaries marking where one speech sound ends and the next begins. >> Many small changes in the signal leave the speech sound the same. Two productions can differ physically and still count as the same sound. || The abstract form is a **symbolic representation**. A symbol identifies a language unit, such as a particular consonant or vowel, and sets aside its fine physical detail. The relevant unit is the **phoneme**, a sound category that distinguishes words in a language. >> The /b/ in “bat” and the /p/ in “pat” are different phonemes because swapping them changes the word. >> Many slightly different acoustic versions of /b/ all count as the same phoneme. || **Categorical perception** is thought to reflect this **“translation” from physical to symbolic**. Listeners become less sensitive to differences inside a category and more sensitive to similar-sized differences that cross a category boundary. >> A change is especially noticeable when it changes the identity a listener assigns to the sound. >> A change of the same physical size can go almost unnoticed when both sounds still count as the same phoneme. Categorical perception is one account of this translation, and there are alternative ideas about how it happens.
4|The **vocal tract** is the passage sound travels through during speech, from the larynx through the throat and mouth to the lips. Its shape changes as the tongue, jaw and lips move. The shape of the vocal tract is related in systematic ways to **which resonant frequencies get amplified**, following Ladefoged (1996). >> A **resonant frequency** is a frequency that a space naturally strengthens. A tube of a particular shape boosts some frequencies and weakens others. >> Changing the shape of the vocal tract changes which frequencies get the strongest boost. || **Formants** are the resonant frequencies the vocal tract emphasizes. Each vowel has its own combination of formants, and the drawings pair a vocal tract shape with a graph of amplified frequencies for each vowel. >> For **heed, hid, head and had**, the tongue moves from high and front toward lower in the mouth. In heed, the second peak sits far above the first, and the two peaks move closer together across hid, head and had. >> For **hod, hawed, hood and who’d**, the tongue is farther back and the lips become more rounded. The amplified frequencies cluster at the low end, so the first and second peaks sit close together. >> A vowel is characterized by the **relationship among its resonances**, which stays similar across speakers even when the exact frequencies shift. || A **formant transition** is a change in a formant’s frequency over time. When the mouth moves from a consonant into a vowel, the resonances glide from one set of values to another. >> The steady part of a vowel carries information about the vowel itself. >> The moving part carries information about how the neighboring sound is produced.
5|A **spectrogram** displays acoustic energy across time and frequency. Time runs from left to right, frequency runs from bottom to top, and darker shading marks stronger energy. Each vowel has several dark horizontal bands, and these bands are its **formants**. >> The vowel samples come from Rob Hagiwara’s English vowel recordings. >> Yellow lines mark the centers of the formant bands so their frequencies can be compared across words. || The words **bead, bid, bade, bed, bad, bod, baud, bode, “bood” and booed** all begin with /b/ and end with /d/. Because the consonants match, the differences in the spectrograms come mainly from the vowels. >> In **bead**, the first formant is very low, and the second formant is very high, above 2,000 Hz. The two bands are far apart. >> Moving through bid, bade, bed and bad, the first formant rises and the second formant comes down, so the bands get closer together. >> In the back rounded vowels such as **bode, “bood” and booed**, the second formant drops close to 1,000 Hz or below and sits near the first formant. || **F1** is the lowest formant and **F2** is the second-lowest. Both resonances run through the whole vowel at the same time. >> The pattern of these two lowest formants carries most of what distinguishes one vowel from another. >> Bead combines a low F1 with a high F2, while booed combines a low F1 with a low F2.
6|**Source-filter synthesis** builds speech sounds by treating them as two parts. The **source** is the sound energy produced by the voice, and the **filter** is the shaping of that energy by the vocal tract. Separating the two lets us change one while holding the other constant. >> The analysis uses **Praat**, free software for speech analysis available at praat.org. Praat can display recordings, measure formants and resynthesize sounds. || The recording is a speaker saying the vowel **/u/**, the vowel in “booed.” The top panel is a **waveform**, which tracks changes in air pressure over time. The bottom panel is a **spectrogram** of the same sound, which separates it into its frequency components. >> The waveform makes timing and loudness easy to see. >> The spectrogram makes the resonance pattern easy to see. || In this /u/, the **first formant is at about 300 Hz** and the **second formant is very low, around 900 Hz**. The cursor sits on the first formant, and the display reads close to 296 Hz at that point. >> Both resonances are present together throughout the vowel. >> Low values for both F1 and F2 fit the back rounded quality of /u/. || If only the filter changes and the vowel changes with it, the vowel’s identity must come from the filter, which is the pattern of formants. The measured values of about 300 Hz and 900 Hz become the starting point for building new filters.
7|**Praat can find formants automatically**. It tracks each resonance through the recording and marks its frequency at each moment. In this /u/ recording, Praat identifies **five formants**, and each track follows one resonance across time. >> Several tracks running at once describe several frequency components of the same spoken sound. >> Automatic tracking measures the resonance pattern without estimating each frequency by eye. || **Only the first three formants are usually important for phonemic processing**. F1, F2 and F3 carry most of the information that tells one phoneme from another, while the higher formants depend more on individual voice properties. >> The numbers refer to the order of the resonances from low frequency to high frequency. F1 is the lowest, F2 is the next one up, and F3 is the third. || The manipulation focuses on just **the first two formants**. In this /u/, F1 is near 300 Hz and F2 is near 900 Hz. >> Changing just these two resonances is enough to turn one vowel into another, because they carry most of the information that distinguishes vowels. >> F3 and the higher formants stay fixed, so any change listeners hear can be traced to F1 or F2 alone.
8|The **source** can be **extracted** from the recorded /u/. Praat builds a model of the filter from the measured formant frequencies and uses that model to remove the filter’s effect from the recording. What remains is a **more-or-less “unfiltered” version of the voice**. >> Removing the estimated filter leaves the sound energy that the vocal folds supplied. >> The result is called more-or-less unfiltered because the separation is an estimate. || The extracted source **sounds like a buzz**. It still has energy and a pitch, but it has lost the resonance pattern that made it sound like /u/. >> The spectrogram of the source has energy spread across many frequencies, without the strong low bands that marked the formants of /u/. >> The pitch stays because it comes from the vibration of the vocal folds. The vowel quality disappears because it comes from the filter. || The separation is **a little bit like doing a multiple regression analysis**. In multiple regression, the contribution of one predictor is estimated and removed so the remaining contribution can be examined. >> Here the contribution of the filter is estimated and removed, and the remaining signal is treated as the source. >> Once the source is isolated, new filters can be applied to it. Keeping the same source while swapping the filter makes the effect of the resonance pattern testable.
9|Three different **filters** can be applied to the same source. A filter specifies which frequencies get more emphasis and which get less, so each filter is a resonance pattern. The three spectrograms each run from 0 to 2.5 seconds in time and from 0 to 5,000 Hz in frequency. >> The **first filter** is just the first four formants from the original /u/ recording. Applying it to the source should restore the original vowel quality. >> The **second filter** raises **the first formant a little**, and the other formants stay roughly where they were. >> The **third filter** raises **the second formant a lot**, while F1 and the higher formants stay roughly where they were. || Each manipulation changes one part of the resonance pattern rather than shifting every frequency together. >> In the original filter, F1 and F2 are both low, which matches /u/. >> Raising F1 moves only the lowest band upward. Raising F2 moves only the second band upward, and by a much larger amount. || The **sound source stays the same** across all three filters. Any difference in vowel quality therefore comes from the filter. >> No new speaker and no new recording are needed. >> Comparing the outputs isolates what a change in the lowest resonance does compared with a change in the second resonance.
10|**Multiplying the source by the filters gives new vowels**. In source-filter terms, the output sound is the source shaped by the filter. Each filter boosts a different set of frequencies, so each combination comes out as a different vowel. >> The source supplies the raw sound energy. >> The filter decides how much of that energy stays prominent in each frequency region. || The three resulting spectrograms come from one source and three filters. >> With the **original filter**, F1 and F2 stay low and the result keeps the quality of /u/. >> With **F1 raised a little**, the lowest band moves upward and the vowel quality changes. >> With **F2 raised a lot**, the second band moves far upward and the vowel quality changes in a different way. Raising F2 for a back rounded vowel moves it toward a more front vowel quality. || **Source-filter synthesis** therefore separates two contributions that are combined in ordinary speech. The voice supplies the sound, and the vocal tract resonances shape its quality. >> All three outputs share the same buzz, pitch and timing, yet they sound like different vowels. >> A researcher can change vowel quality by replacing only the filter, without asking a speaker to produce a new utterance for every version.
11|Formant values are only **partly** determined by which vowel a speaker intends. They are also shaped by **anatomical features** that differ between speakers of the same language, by **different rates of speech**, and by **context**, such as the phones produced before and after a vowel. >> **Anatomy** matters because vocal tracts differ in length and shape. A longer vocal tract produces lower resonances overall. >> **Speaking rate** matters because fast speech gives the articulators less time to reach their targets. >> **Context** matters because the tongue and lips are already moving toward the next sound or away from the previous one. A **phone** is a specific spoken realization of a speech sound. || The F2-by-F1 plot for ten vowels from 76 speakers displays this variability. Each vowel forms a cloud of points enclosed by an oval, and the ovals overlap. >> The same F1 and F2 values can belong to different vowels for different speakers. >> No single pair of frequencies defines a vowel for everyone. || Formant values can **nonetheless** provide a pretty good description of **vowel space**, especially for **a specific talker speaking carefully**. Vowel space is the arrangement of a language’s vowels by their formant frequencies. >> Within one careful talker, the vowels separate much more cleanly than they do across many talkers. >> All else being equal, we can produce **reasonable approximations of vowels by manipulating steady-state formants**. A **steady-state formant** is the resonance during the stable middle part of a vowel, as opposed to the transitions at its edges.
12|The **pattern playback synthesizer** turns a painted spectrographic pattern back into sound. Researchers paint dark bands on a transparent sheet where they want acoustic energy to occur across time and frequency, and the machine plays that pattern aloud. >> Changing the painted pattern changes the sound. >> Researchers could create controlled, speech-like sounds instead of relying on natural recordings that differ in many uncontrolled ways. || The machine works by passing light through the painted pattern. >> A **light source** shines through a **cylindrical lens** onto a rotating **tone wheel**. The tone wheel has rings that modulate the light at different frequencies, so the beam carries many frequencies at once. >> A lens and a **mirror** direct the modulated light onto the **spectrogram** painted on the sheet. The painted areas reflect light at particular positions and let the matching frequencies through. >> A **light collector** and a **photocell** convert the reflected light into an electrical signal. An **amplifier** strengthens the signal, and a **loudspeaker** turns it into audible sound. || With this device, a researcher can alter one formant band or one transition and hear the result immediately. >> Every other part of the pattern stays exactly the same. >> Differences in what listeners hear can then be traced to the single acoustic change that was painted. Painting a series of patterns that differ in tiny steps made it possible to discover how formant transitions signal consonants.
13|**Stop consonants are distinguished by formant transitions**. The stop consonants **/b/, /d/ and /g/** are made by briefly closing the vocal tract and then releasing it. The audible part of a /b/, /d/ or /g/ happens **while the jaw is opening**. >> During the opening movement, the vocal tract shape is changing rapidly. >> The changing shape makes the formants glide from the consonant’s position toward the vowel’s position. The consonant’s identity is carried by how the formant frequencies move. || **Liberman and colleagues (1957)** used the **pattern playback synthesizer** to create speech-like sounds that replicated these transitions. Each painted pattern has two formants plotted against time in milliseconds, with frequency in cycles per second from 0 up to about 2,400. >> The **lower formant** looks nearly the same in every stimulus. It rises briefly and then stays steady near 600 cycles per second or below. >> The **upper formant** starts at a different frequency in each stimulus before settling at the same steady level near 1,800 cycles per second. >> In the early stimuli, the upper formant starts low and **rises** into the vowel. In the middle stimuli, it is nearly **flat**. In the later stimuli, it starts high and **falls** into the vowel. || The fourteen numbered patterns keep the following vowel alike and change only the onset of the upper transition. >> A rising transition is heard as /b/, a roughly flat one as /d/ and a falling one as /g/. >> Because only the transition differs, listeners’ responses can be tied directly to it.
14|A **speech continuum** is a set of sounds that change gradually along one controlled acoustic property. These stimuli are built by **keeping F1 constant** and **varying the F2 onset frequency linearly** from stimulus 1 to stimulus 13. The **F2 onset frequency** is the frequency at which the second formant begins its transition. >> Keeping F1 constant removes one possible source of difference between stimuli. >> Varying the F2 onset **linearly** means the onset frequency changes by the same step every time. **Adjacent stimuli differ by the same amount across the entire continuum**. || The F2 transitions move gradually along the continuum. >> At the start, F2 begins well below its steady vowel frequency, so the transition rises. >> As the onset frequency increases, the rise shrinks until the transition is nearly flat. >> Further along, F2 starts above the vowel frequency, and the transition falls. || Because each step is physically equal, the continuum is a fair test of whether perception follows the acoustics or the categories. >> Some neighboring pairs fall inside one consonant category, and some pairs cross a category boundary. >> A purely acoustic listener would hear every step as the same size of change. >> If perception is categorical, steps within a category will sound alike while steps across a boundary will sound different. Any advantage for boundary-crossing pairs comes from the boundary itself.
15|An **ideal observer model** predicts how a listener would respond if perception worked according to a particular account. This version assumes that perception is **non-categorical**, so it tracks the acoustic signal gradually without sharp category boundaries. >> The model is a baseline. Real data can be compared against it to see whether perception is more categorical than a gradual account allows. || The first prediction is that **categorization gets “blurry” as you move away from category prototypes**. A **prototype** is the clearest example of a category. The endpoints of the continuum are /b/ and /g/, with /d/ in the middle. >> Between two prototypes, **stimulus ambiguity scales with the distance from the prototype**. The farther a stimulus is from the /b/ prototype, the less /b/-like and the more /d/-like it sounds. >> **Stimulus 2** would be heard as **75% /b/ and 25% /d/**. It is a quarter of the way from the /b/ prototype toward the /d/ prototype, so the responses split in the same proportion. || The predicted **labeling** graph plots the proportion of /b/, /d/ and /g/ responses from 0 to 1 across stimuli 1 to 11. >> The /b/ line stays at 1 through stimulus 2 and then falls in a straight line to 0 at stimulus 6. >> The /d/ line rises in a straight line from stimulus 2 to a peak of 1 at stimulus 6 and then falls back to 0 at stimulus 10. >> The /g/ line starts rising after stimulus 6 and reaches 1 at stimulus 10. Neighboring lines cross at 0.5 halfway between their prototypes. Every change is a gradual straight slope rather than a sharp step.
16|The same ideal observer can also assume that perception is **linear**, so equal acoustic differences are perceived as equally large. This leads to a second prediction, which is that **discrimination performance should be independent of labeling**. >> **Labeling** asks a listener to identify a sound as /b/, /d/ or /g/. >> **Discrimination** asks only whether two sounds are the **same or different**. These are separate tasks, even when they use the same continuum. || Labeling **has to have a “peaked” function**. There are three categories, and listeners are choosing between labels, so each category’s responses rise toward its prototype and fall away from it. >> In the predicted labeling graph, the /d/ line forms a peak at stimulus 6 while /b/ and /g/ rise toward opposite ends. >> These peaks come from the structure of the labeling task. || The predicted **discrimination** graph plots accuracy for pairs two steps apart, from **1 vs. 3** through **9 vs. 11**. Accuracy is a flat line at about 0.8 for every pair. >> If the perception of differences in F2 onset were constant across the continuum, discrimination accuracy would also be constant. >> Each pair has the same physical separation, so a linear account gives each pair the same chance of being heard as different. >> Under this account, discrimination stays level even where labeling changes. A flat line would mean category boundaries play no role in hearing differences, while a peaked line would mean the boundaries make some differences easier to hear.
17|**Liberman and colleagues (1957)** measured actual labeling and discrimination along the /b/–/d/–/g/ continuum. Their results departed sharply from the non-categorical, linear predictions. >> Listeners labeled each stimulus and also judged pairs of stimuli in discrimination tests. >> The discrimination tests compared stimuli **one step**, **two steps** and **three steps** apart. || The **labeling data** show **sharp changes** in identification. >> Stimuli 1 through about 3 are labeled /b/ close to 100% of the time. >> Around stimuli 4 and 5, /b/ responses drop steeply and /d/ responses climb to nearly 100%. /d/ stays dominant through about stimulus 8. >> Around stimuli 9 and 10, /d/ responses drop steeply and /g/ responses rise to near 100%. These switches are far more abrupt than the straight-line slopes the ideal observer predicted. || **Listeners were best at discriminating sounds near the category boundaries**. >> In each discrimination graph, the solid line is the obtained accuracy and the dashed line is the accuracy predicted from the labeling data. >> Accuracy peaks around the /b/–/d/ boundary and again around the /d/–/g/ boundary. Between those peaks, the predicted accuracy falls to about 50%, which is chance. >> The peaks appear in one-step, two-step and three-step comparisons. Larger steps raise accuracy overall, but the peaks stay at the boundaries. || The predictions derived from labeling capture much of the shape of the obtained discrimination. >> Obtained accuracy is often somewhat higher than the prediction, especially within categories. >> Listeners therefore keep some sensitivity to acoustic differences beyond pure category labels.

19|Class data from prior years tested categorical perception along a **beach–peach** continuum. The words differ in their first consonant, /b/ versus /p/. Each panel pair gives one listener’s **identification** results and that listener’s **discrimination** results. >> The identification graphs plot the proportion of one word label across stimuli 1 to 9. >> The discrimination graphs plot accuracy for pairs two steps apart, from **1–3** through **7–9**. || For the first listener, identification stays at one word for the early stimuli and then **switches to the other word** around the middle of the continuum. >> Responses are mixed for a small region near the switch, and past that region the other word becomes the consistent answer. >> The acoustic change is gradual, but the label changes within a narrow region. || That listener’s discrimination is highest for **pairs 4–6 and 5–7**, which straddle the identification boundary. >> Pairs well inside one category are discriminated less accurately even though their physical separation is the same. >> Pair 6–8 falls between these two patterns because stimulus 6 itself received mixed labels. || The second listener **switches word labels later** along the continuum and has stronger discrimination near that later boundary. >> Different people can place their category boundaries at different points. >> For both listeners, the best discrimination falls close to their own identification boundary.
20|The class data lead to some general observations. **Not everyone has exactly the same category boundary**. A sound near the beach–peach boundary for one listener can sit inside a category for another. >> A pair that crosses one person’s boundary can stay within one category for someone else. >> Group averages can therefore blur the boundary peak when individual boundaries differ. || **In general, discrimination is best for stimuli perceived as being in different categories**. >> A listener may miss a physical difference between two versions of the same phoneme. >> A difference of the same physical size becomes much more noticeable when the two sounds are heard as different words. || To make the lab doable in a short time, the discrimination experiment included **only “different” trials**. Every pair contained two physically different stimuli. >> The correct answer on every trial was therefore **“different,”** if the listener could discriminate the two sounds. >> A “same” response meant the listener missed a difference that was really there. >> Accuracy is the proportion of pairs a listener heard as different. A listener who always answered different would score perfectly, so low accuracy for within-category pairs means listeners often failed to hear real differences.
21|Further class results compare a **bear–pear** continuum with the **beach–peach** continuum. >> The **bear–pear identification** graph has a fairly clear switch from one word label to the other across stimuli 1 to 9. >> The **bear–pear discrimination** graph, for pairs 1–3 through 6–8, varies irregularly. Accuracy is 0 for pairs 1–3 and 2–4, jumps to 1 for 3–5, drops back to 0 for 4–6, returns to 1 for 5–7 and falls to 0 for 6–8. >> The **beach–peach** graphs from another listener fluctuate even more. Identification zigzags across the continuum instead of switching once, and discrimination bounces between high and low values. || The one iron-clad law of experimental research is that **stuff happens**. Responses to a carefully built continuum can still bounce up and down. >> Identification records which word a listener chooses, while discrimination records whether a difference is detected. These two behaviors can vary differently across trials and people. >> An isolated rise in discrimination reflects noise in a few responses. || The data raise the question of how the experiment could be improved. >> Only “different” trials were used, so there was no measure of how often listeners said “different” when the sounds were identical. >> A short session gave each listener few trials per pair, so single responses could move accuracy a great deal. >> The scientific test is whether better discrimination lines up with the place where identification changes, compared across the whole continuum.
22|The key question is **how categorical perception would help**. Put another way, **what is the advantage of being worse at hearing some differences between sounds**? Categorical perception makes listeners worse at hearing differences within a category, which at first sounds like throwing away useful detail. || The advantage is **stable word recognition**. Different productions of the same phoneme are always physically different, because of speakers, speaking rates and surrounding sounds. >> If every small acoustic change were heard as a new identity, ordinary versions of one word would seem like unrelated sounds. >> Being less sensitive to within-category differences lets many acoustic versions count as the same phoneme. || Differences that separate categories stay easy to hear, so the distinctions that change words are preserved. >> Variation within a category is tolerated because it does not change the word. >> Ignoring small differences within a category lets the listener focus on the differences that change meaning.

24|**Categorical perception in infants** can be tested even though infants cannot name sounds. Researchers measure a behavior that changes when an infant notices something new, since infants respond to novelty. >> The **conditioned head turn procedure**, from Janet Werker’s infant studies lab, trains an infant to turn toward a sound when the sound changes. A correct head turn is rewarded with an interesting visual display, such as a moving toy. >> Once the infant learns this association, a head turn after a change is evidence that the infant detected the change. || The results of interest here come from **high-amplitude sucking**. The infant sucks on a pacifier connected to a recording device, and strong sucks trigger the presentation of a sound. >> When the same sound repeats, the infant gets used to it, a process called **habituation**, and the sucking rate drops. >> If a new sound makes sucking **increase again**, the infant has noticed the change. || Both methods make perception testable before an infant can speak. >> The experimenter controls exactly which sound difference is presented. >> The infant’s head turn or renewed sucking then reveals sensitivity to that difference without any labeling task. These methods allow testing of infants who are months away from producing words.
25|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** tested categorical perception in **4-month-old infants** using **high-amplitude sucking**. The acoustic dimension was **voice onset time**, or **VOT**, which is the delay between the release of a consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration. >> Adults hear a short VOT as a voiced consonant such as /b/ and a longer VOT as a voiceless consonant such as /p/. >> The spectrograms compare a syllable with a VOT of **+10 milliseconds** and one with **+100 milliseconds**. With the longer VOT, the voicing bars start much later after the release. || Infants heard one synthetic syllable repeatedly until their sucking rate declined, and then the sound changed or stayed the same. >> In condition **20D**, the VOT changed from **+20 to +40 milliseconds**. This 20-millisecond difference **crossed** the adult boundary between two phonemic categories. >> In condition **20S**, the VOT also changed by **20 milliseconds**, either from **−20 to 0** or from **+60 to +80 milliseconds**. Both stimuli came from the **same** adult category. >> In condition **0**, the control group heard the same stimulus throughout. || The graphs plot mean sucking responses per minute, with the shift marked by a dashed line. >> Sucking rose early and then declined to around 30 to 40 responses per minute before the shift. >> In **20D**, sucking **recovered** sharply after the shift, back up near 50. >> In **20S**, sucking kept dropping, with little recovery. In **0**, it continued to decline toward about 15. || Infants discriminated the change that crossed an adult category boundary but not the equally large change within a category. >> The two changed conditions were matched at 20 milliseconds, so the difference comes from crossing the boundary. >> The control condition establishes what continued repetition alone does to sucking.
26|**Perceptual narrowing** is the process in which experience tunes perception to the sounds of the surrounding language, while sensitivity to some distinctions from other languages declines. **Werker and Tees** tested how age and experience affect infants’ ability to discriminate two **non-English contrasts**. >> The **Hindi contrast** is a distinction between two “ta”-like sounds that English does not use. >> The **Salish contrast** is a distinction between “ki” and “qi” in Nthlakampx, a Salish language, which English also does not use. || The bar graph gives the **percentage of infants reaching the discrimination criterion** at each age. >> At **6–8 months**, English-learning infants did well, with about 95% meeting criterion for Hindi and about 80% for Salish. >> At **8–10 months**, performance dropped to about 67% for Hindi and about 52% for Salish. >> At **10–12 months**, performance was much lower, near 20% for Hindi and near 10% for Salish. >> **Hindi- and Salish-learning infants** met criterion close to 100% for the contrast in their own language. || These results are described as **perceptual reorganization during the first year of life**. >> Young infants can detect these nonnative contrasts. >> With experience of English, English-learning infants become much less likely to discriminate them by around their first birthday. >> Infants who hear the contrast in their own language keep strong discrimination, so the decline depends on language experience. Contrasts used in the native language are kept, and contrasts absent from it become hard to hear.
27|There is **a very compelling narrative** about speech perception built from these findings. It has three connected claims. >> First, speech perception depends on **categorical perception abilities that are a special property of the language system**. The mechanism that sorts sounds into categories is claimed to be specialized for language. >> Second, these abilities are **present very early in development**. The infant experiments with sucking and head turns are taken as evidence that the mechanism is in place long before children talk. || Third, children **start off as “citizens of the world,”** able to hear **all** speech contrasts. They then **rapidly tune their perceptual systems to their specific language**. >> “Citizens of the world” means an infant could learn any human language and is ready to hear the distinctions of every one. >> Tuning means contrasts used in the surrounding language stay available while contrasts from other languages fade, as in the Werker and Tees results. || The narrative is an account of both the **starting state** and **developmental change**. >> It claims broad, language-specific abilities at birth. >> It describes development mainly as narrowing from that broad starting point. >> Each early finding seems to support it, since infants discriminate categorically before they talk, hear contrasts from many languages and lose nonnative contrasts with experience.
28|The compelling narrative runs into **some issues**. For **categorical perception**, three problems stand out. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to language**. It also occurs with nonspeech sounds, which challenges the claim that it is special to language. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to humans**. Nonhuman animals also show categorical patterns for speech sounds, which challenges the claim that it is special to humans. >> Categorical perception is **task-dependent**. How categorical the results look depends on what the listener is asked to do. Choosing between labels tends to make responses more categorical than a task that measures fine acoustic differences. || For **infant speech perception**, the problem is that it is **not actually “universal.”** >> Some contrasts are already harder for infants to detect than others early in life. >> Development therefore includes initial differences in sensitivity, along with changes driven by experience. || Together these issues challenge the strong claim that every speech distinction begins equally available within a mechanism devoted only to language.
29|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** tested categorical perception with **non-speech sounds**. The acoustic dimension was **rise time**, which is how quickly a sound reaches its full loudness after it begins. >> A sound with a **rapid rise time** sounds **plucked**, like a plucked string. >> A sound with a **slow rise time** sounds **bowed**, like a bowed string. || The stimuli included **sawtooth waves**, **sine waves** and **speech syllables**, displayed as oscillograms with **10-millisecond** and **70-millisecond** rise times. An **oscillogram** tracks the shape of the sound wave over time. >> With a 10-millisecond rise, the waveform reaches full size almost immediately. >> With a 70-millisecond rise, the waveform grows gradually. >> Pluck and bow are perceptual labels for two kinds of onset, not phonemes. || The results for **sawtooth waves** plot identification and discrimination against rise time from 0 to 80 milliseconds. >> **Pluck responses** start near 100% for short rise times and fall steeply around 30 to 50 milliseconds. **Bow responses** rise in the same region to near 100% at long rise times. >> **Discrimination** of pairs differing by 10 milliseconds stays around 55 to 60% for most pairs. It **peaks near 75%** for the pairs around 30 to 50 milliseconds, right where the labels switch. || The link between a label boundary and improved discrimination therefore appears for a non-linguistic sound distinction. The physical continuum is gradual, but the preferred label changes over a narrow region and discrimination improves there.
30|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** tested whether **infants** show categorical perception of **non-speech sounds**. They used high-amplitude sucking to measure whether a change in sound was detected. >> Infants heard one sound repeatedly until their sucking declined. >> The sound was then shifted, and the change was compared across three conditions. || The three conditions were **no change**, a change **within categories** and a change **between categories**. Each graph plots mean sucking responses per minute, with time measured from the shift and a baseline rate marked “B.” >> In every condition, sucking started near 20 per minute and declined to about 12 before the shift as the sound became familiar. >> After **no change**, sucking stayed low, near 12. >> After a **within-category** change, sucking also stayed low. >> After a **between-category** change, sucking jumped back up to about 18 and then declined again as the new sound became familiar. || Infants responded more strongly to a non-speech change that crossed a category boundary than to a change within a category. >> This mirrors the pattern Eimas and colleagues found for speech. >> Infants cannot be drawing on knowledge of words or phonemes when they respond to plucked and bowed sounds. Their categorical response must come from more general auditory processing, which could also underlie their categorical response to speech.
31|Categorical perception of speech sounds has also been found in **chinchillas**, following **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and the earlier work of **Kuhl and Miller (1975)**. The animals were trained with **operant conditioning**, a form of learning in which consequences change how often a behavior occurs. >> The chinchillas learned that either the **0-millisecond lag** stimulus or the **80-millisecond lag** stimulus was an **SD** for food. **SD** stands for **discriminative stimulus**, a signal that a response will be rewarded. >> The lag is **voice onset time**. Humans hear a 0-millisecond lag as /ga/ and an 80-millisecond lag as /ka/. || After training, the chinchillas were tested with **intermediate VOTs** along the **/ga/–/ka/** continuum. >> The graphs plot the proportion labeled /ga/ against VOT from 0 to 120 milliseconds for two chinchillas and two human listeners. Separate curves compare a 0–80 millisecond continuum, a 0–120 millisecond continuum and stimuli with an enhanced burst. >> In every panel, /ga/ labeling stays near 1 for short VOTs and then drops steeply to near 0, mostly between about 30 and 70 milliseconds. >> The chinchilla curves have the same steep, categorical shape as the human curves. || The comparison also varied the **burst** and **aspiration noise**. The **burst** is the brief noise at the release of a stop consonant, and **aspiration** is the breathy noise that follows a voiceless release. >> Increasing the intensity of the burst and aspiration noise shifted the boundary to a shorter VOT, made the curve steeper, or both. >> This happened for one chinchilla and for one human listener, so the two species were affected in a similar way. >> Animals without human language can therefore sort a speech continuum into categories, which argues against the view that categorical perception is special to human language.
32|Some speech contrasts are **less salient than others** for infants. A **salient** contrast is one that is easy to notice. >> The contrasts tested here differ in **place of articulation**, which is where in the mouth a sound is made. >> In **[ma]**, the nasal is made with the lips. In **[na]**, it is made with the tongue near the ridge behind the teeth. In **[ŋa]**, the sound at the end of “sing,” it is made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate. || The **setup** compared **looking time** on **same** trials, where the syllable did not change, with **change** trials, where it switched. Longer looking on change trials is evidence that the infant noticed the change. Error bars mark the standard error of each mean. >> At **4–5 months**, English-hearing infants looked about 3.3 seconds on same trials and about 5.4 seconds on change trials for the native **[ma]–[na]** contrast. >> For the nonnative **[na]–[ŋa]** contrast, the same infants looked about 4.5 seconds on same trials and about 4.2 seconds on change trials. There was no increase for the change, and English does not use [ŋ] at the start of syllables. || **Filipino-learning infants** hear [na]–[ŋa] as a contrast in their own language. >> At **6–8 months**, their looking time was about 3.8 seconds on both same and change trials, so the change produced no detectable response. >> By **10–12 months**, looking rose from about 3.3 seconds on same trials to about 4.8 seconds on change trials. || Some contrasts therefore start out hard for infants to hear, even when they are native. Experience with the language **strengthens** sensitivity to such contrasts over the first year, so development includes gains as well as losses.
