2|**Nature and nurture in child language** concerns how much of language comes from built-in capacities and how much comes from experience. **“Nativist” positions** grew out of **Chomsky’s critique of Skinner**. >> **Skinner** was a behaviorist who explained behavior, including language, through general learning mechanisms such as reward and punishment. >> **Chomsky** argued that these mechanisms could not explain how children acquire grammar. || For Chomsky, **the important feature of language is its generativity**. **Generativity** is the ability to produce and understand an unlimited number of new sentences. >> **Acquiring a language** means **identifying the abstract grammar that produces the sentences of the language**. Most sentences a person will ever hear or say are new. || The **general learning mechanisms studied by behaviorists were not up to this task**, for two reasons. >> **Grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are not “similar” in any straightforward way**. A tiny change can make a sentence ungrammatical, while two grammatical sentences can look completely different. >> **The context for language acquisition does not look like operant conditioning**. **Operant conditioning** shapes behavior through rewards and punishments that follow it. Children are not reliably rewarded for grammatical sentences or punished for ungrammatical ones, so the **reward and punishment contingencies do not hold**. || Instead, **nativist approaches** assert two things. >> Children have **innate knowledge of what language is like**, called **Universal Grammar**, a built-in set of principles that limits which grammars a child will consider. >> Children use **language-specific acquisition processes**, meaning learning mechanisms designed specifically for language. || Chomsky put it this way. **“The fact that all normal children acquire essentially comparable grammars of great complexity with remarkable rapidity suggests that human beings are somehow specially designed to do this, with data-handling or ‘hypothesis-formulating’ ability of unknown character and complexity.”** >> The argument rests on speed and uniformity. Every typical child arrives at a complex grammar quickly, despite very different experiences.
3|**Categorical perception** is the pattern in which listeners hear differences between speech sounds in terms of categories. **Early theories treated it as a language-specific and species-specific phenomenon**. >> **Language-specific** means it was thought to occur only for language. **Species-specific** means it was thought to occur only in humans. >> At the time, **only humans were known to show categorical perception, and only for speech sounds**, so it looked like a unique feature of human language. || The classic evidence comes from a synthetic consonant continuum. >> The upper formant transitions change in equal steps across fourteen stimuli, while the lower formant stays the same. >> In the **labeling data**, listeners switch sharply from **/b/** to **/d/** around stimuli 4 and 5, and from /d/ to **/g/** around stimuli 9 and 10. >> In the **one-step, two-step and three-step discrimination** results, accuracy peaks near those boundaries and falls lower inside each category. The solid line marks obtained accuracy and the dashed line marks accuracy predicted from labeling. || Categorical perception is **a compelling example of how similarity between stimuli is defined in linguistic, rather than physical, terms**. >> Two sounds that differ by the same acoustic amount can sound almost identical if they belong to the same category. >> They can sound clearly different if they fall on opposite sides of a category boundary.
4|**Categorical perception in infants** can be tested using behaviors that change when an infant notices something new. Head turns and sucking reveal what an infant hears without any need for words. || The **conditioned head turn procedure**, pictured in a photo from Janet Werker’s infant studies lab, trains an infant to turn toward a sound when it changes. >> The infant sits on a parent’s lap while an experimenter keeps the infant’s attention forward with toys. >> When the sound changes, a correct head turn is rewarded by an interesting visual display, such as a lighted, moving toy. >> Once the infant learns this link, a head turn after a sound change is evidence that the infant heard the change. || **High-amplitude sucking** is a second method, which relies on the infant’s natural response to something new. >> The infant sucks on a pacifier connected to a pressure recorder, and strong sucks trigger the presentation of a sound. >> As one sound is repeated, the infant becomes used to it, which is called **habituation**, and sucking slows down. >> If a new sound makes sucking **increase again**, the infant has noticed the change. || Neither method requires the infant to name or produce sounds. The experimenter controls exactly which acoustic difference is presented, and the infant’s head turn or renewed sucking reveals whether infants hear the same distinctions that adults hear.
5|**Eimas and colleagues (1971)** tested categorical perception in **4-month-old infants** using **high-amplitude sucking**. The acoustic dimension was **voice onset time**, or **VOT**, the delay between the release of a stop consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration. >> Spectrograms compare syllables with VOTs of **+10 milliseconds** and **+100 milliseconds**. With the longer VOT, voicing starts much later after the release, with the first formant cut back at the start. || In the main experiment, there were **three conditions**. Infants heard a syllable repeatedly until sucking declined, and then the sound changed or stayed the same. >> In **20D**, the two stimuli differed by **20 milliseconds** of VOT, **+20 versus +40 milliseconds**, and came from **different adult phonemic categories**. >> In **20S**, the stimuli also differed by **20 milliseconds**, either **−20 versus 0** or **+60 versus +80 milliseconds**, but came from the **same** phonemic category. >> In **0**, the **control** condition, the same stimulus continued throughout. || The graph plots the **mean number of sucking responses per minute**, with time measured from the stimulus shift. The letter **B** marks the baseline rate, and a dashed line marks the shift. >> In every condition, sucking rose above baseline and then declined before the shift. >> In **20D**, sucking jumped back up after the shift, to around 50 responses per minute. >> In **20S**, sucking showed little recovery and continued downward. >> In **0**, sucking kept declining, down to about 15 responses per minute. || Infants responded to the change that crossed an adult phoneme boundary but not to the equally large change within a category. >> The two changed conditions were matched in acoustic size, so the difference comes from crossing the boundary. >> The control condition establishes what happens with continued repetition alone.
6|Eimas’s finding was **a big deal** for several reasons. **Categorical perception was considered to be the first step in turning speech into a symbolic format** that later linguistic processes could use. >> A **symbolic** format represents a sound as a category, such as /b/. >> Finding this ability in infants suggested that the starting point for language processing was already in place. || **Four-month-olds are really little**. Some **gross motor milestones** at that age are **manipulating objects with two hands** and **holding the head and chest up while lying prone**, meaning face down. >> Gross motor skills involve large body movements. || Their **color vision and general visual acuity are still “in progress.”** **Visual acuity** is the sharpness of vision. >> Basic perceptual abilities in other areas are still maturing. || Four-month-olds are also **just starting to produce vowel-like sounds instead of creaky whines**. They cannot yet produce speech sounds well at all. || This is **very, very early in development for an apparently language-specific ability to emerge**, so it **seemed like evidence for innateness**. >> Infants categorize speech sounds before they can produce them, while other abilities are still immature. >> Speech categorization seemed far ahead of other developing skills, so it appeared to be built in rather than learned through long experience.
7|**“Perceptual narrowing”** for speech categories is the decline in sensitivity to sound distinctions that are not used in the language a child hears. **At around six months, English-learning infants show evidence of discriminating speech contrasts they have never heard before**. **Over the course of development, the ability to perceive these contrasts declines**. >> **Werker and Tees** tested English-learning infants on two non-English contrasts. >> The **Hindi contrast** is a distinction between two “ta”-like sounds. The **Salish contrast** is a distinction between “ki” and “qi” from Nthlakampx, a Salish language. || The bar graph gives the **percentage of infants reaching the discrimination criterion** at each age. >> At **6–8 months**, about **95%** of English-learning infants discriminated the Hindi contrast and about **80%** discriminated the Salish contrast. >> At **8–10 months**, these fell to about **67%** and **52%**. >> At **10–12 months**, they fell to about **20%** and **10%**. >> **Hindi- and Salish-learning infants** discriminated their native contrast close to **100%** of the time. || The figure describes **perceptual reorganization during the first year of life**. >> Without experience of a contrast, English-learning infants lose much of their ability to discriminate it by about their first birthday. >> Infants who hear the contrast in their language keep it, so the decline depends on language experience. || This raises the question of **how perceptual narrowing relates to categorical perception**. >> As infants form the categories of their native language, sounds that their language groups together become harder to tell apart. >> A nonnative distinction that falls within one native category comes to be heard as a within-category difference.
8|There is **a very compelling narrative** built from these findings. >> **Speech perception depends on categorical perception abilities that are a special property of the language system**. >> **These abilities are present very early in development**, as the infant studies suggest. || **Children start off as “citizens of the world,”** able to hear **all** contrasts. This means **they have innate, language-general knowledge about speech**. >> **Language-general** means the knowledge applies to the speech sounds of every language. >> “Citizens of the world” captures the idea that a newborn is ready to learn any human language. >> Children then **rapidly tune their perceptual systems to the specific language in their environment**. || Under this narrative, **the role of learning is to select** among the many possible **innately specified phonetic contrasts** and **narrow down the inventory** to just those that are **meaningful in the child’s native language**. >> An **inventory** here is the full set of phonetic contrasts the child can perceive. >> Learning is a process of elimination. It removes the contrasts the native language does not use and keeps the innate categories that the language needs.
9|There are **some problems for this view**. For **categorical perception**, three issues stand out. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to language**. It also occurs for nonspeech sounds, so the mechanism extends beyond language. >> Categorical perception is **not limited to humans**. Animals without language also show categorical patterns for speech sounds. >> Categorical perception is **task-dependent**. How categorical the results look depends on what the listener is asked to do, such as naming a sound versus judging whether two sounds differ. || For **infant speech perception**, the issue is that it is **not actually “universal.”** >> Infants do not start out able to hear every contrast equally well. >> Some contrasts are hard for infants to detect early on, even when those contrasts occur in their own language. || Together, these issues challenge the claim that every speech distinction is innately available in a mechanism devoted only to language. An account of development must explain contrasts that improve with experience as well as contrasts that are lost, so learning has to build categories as well as remove them.
10|**Cutting and Rosner (1974)** tested categorical perception with **non-speech sounds**. The dimension they varied was **rise time**, which is how quickly a sound grows to full loudness after it starts. >> A **short rise time** produces a sound like a **plucked** string. >> A **long rise time** produces a sound like a **bowed** string. || The stimuli included **sawtooth waves**, **speech syllables** and **sine waves**, displayed as **oscillograms**, which are plots of the sound wave over time. >> Each kind of sound was made with a **10-millisecond** rise time and a **70-millisecond** rise time. >> With a 10-millisecond rise, the wave reaches full size almost immediately. With a 70-millisecond rise, it grows gradually. || The results for **sawtooth waves** plot identification and discrimination against rise time from 0 to 80 milliseconds. >> **Pluck responses** start near 100% at short rise times and drop steeply between about 30 and 50 milliseconds. **Bow responses** rise in the same region to near 100% at long rise times. >> **Discrimination** of pairs 10 milliseconds apart stays near 55 to 60% for most pairs. It **peaks near 75%** for pairs around 30 to 50 milliseconds, where the labels switch. || The link between a label boundary and better discrimination appears for a sound distinction that has nothing to do with phonemes. >> Pluck and bow are perceptual labels for kinds of sound onsets, not speech categories. >> Categorical perception can therefore arise in general auditory processing, without a mechanism specific to language.
11|**Jusczyk and colleagues (1977)** tested whether **infants** show categorical perception of **non-speech sounds**, using high-amplitude sucking. >> Infants heard a sound repeatedly until sucking declined. >> The sound was then shifted, and three conditions were compared. || The conditions were **no change**, a change **within categories**, and a change **between categories**. Each graph plots **mean sucking rates** per minute, with time measured from the shift, marked by a vertical dashed line, and the baseline rate marked by the letter **B**. >> In all three conditions, sucking started near 20 per minute and declined to about 12 before the shift. >> After **no change**, sucking stayed near 12. >> After a **within-category** change, sucking also stayed near 12. >> After a **between-category** change, sucking jumped back up to about 18 and then declined again. || Infants responded more strongly to a non-speech change that crossed a category boundary than to a change within a category. >> This is the same pattern Eimas and colleagues found for speech. >> Adults show categorical labeling for plucked and bowed sounds, and infants show categorical discrimination for nonspeech changes. Neither group needs language knowledge, so early categorical perception reflects a general auditory ability.
12|**Chinchillas** also show categorical perception of **speech sounds**, following **Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999)** and the earlier work of **Kuhl and Miller (1975)**. The animals were trained using **operant conditioning**, a form of learning in which a behavior becomes more likely when it is followed by a reward. >> The chinchillas learned that either the **0-millisecond lag** stimulus or the **80-millisecond lag** stimulus was an **SD** for food. **SD** stands for **discriminative stimulus**, a signal that a response will be rewarded. >> The lag is the **voice onset time**. Humans hear a 0-millisecond lag as /ga/ and an 80-millisecond lag as /ka/. || After training, the animals were tested with **intermediate VOTs** along the **/ga/–/ka/** continuum. The graphs plot the **proportion of stimuli labeled /ga/** against VOT from 0 to 120 milliseconds for two chinchillas, C1 and C3, and two human listeners, S3 and S4. >> Separate curves cover a 0–80 millisecond continuum, a 0–120 millisecond continuum and stimuli with an **enhanced burst**. >> In every panel, /ga/ responses stay near 1 at short VOTs and then fall steeply to near 0, mostly between about 30 and 70 milliseconds. >> The chinchilla curves have the same steep categorical shape as the human curves. || **Increasing the intensity of the burst and aspiration noise** shifted the category boundary to a **shorter VOT**, made the curve steeper, or both, for one chinchilla and one human listener. >> The **burst** is the brief noise at the release of a stop, and **aspiration** is the breathy noise that follows a voiceless release. >> Both species responded to these acoustic properties in a similar way. >> Animals without language sort a speech continuum into categories, so categorical perception of speech is shared with other species.
13|**Some contrasts are less salient than others for infants**, according to **Narayan and colleagues (2010)**. A **salient** contrast is one that is easy to notice. >> The contrasts involve **nasal consonants** at the start of a syllable, which is the **onset**. >> They differ in **place of articulation**, which is where in the mouth the sound is made. **/m/** is made with the lips, **/n/** with the tongue behind the teeth, and **/ŋ/**, the final sound in “sing,” with the back of the tongue against the soft palate. || The **setup** compared **looking time** on **same** trials, where the syllable did not change, with **change** trials, where it switched. Longer looking on change trials is evidence that the infant noticed the change. Error bars mark the standard error. >> For the native **[ma]–[na]** contrast, English-hearing infants at **4–5 months** looked about 3.3 seconds on same trials and about 5.4 seconds on change trials. >> For the nonnative **[na]–[ŋa]** contrast, the same infants looked about 4.5 seconds on same trials and about 4.2 seconds on change trials, with no increase for the change. || A **place contrast for nasals in onset**, **/na/–/ŋa/**, is **not readily perceived by four-month-olds, independent of language experience**. >> In contrast, **/ma/–/na/** is **relatively salient**. >> The difference lies in the sounds themselves. Some contrasts are acoustically harder to detect than others from the start, which challenges the idea that all contrasts begin equally available.
14|**Between 6 and 10 months, exposure to the /na/–/ŋa/ contrast in a Filipino language environment increases its salience**, according to **Narayan and colleagues (2010)**. In Filipino, [na] and [ŋa] are different syllables, so Filipino-learning infants hear this contrast regularly. >> At **6–8 months**, Filipino-learning infants looked about 3.8 seconds on both same and change trials, so the change had no measurable effect. >> At **10–12 months**, looking rose from about 3.3 seconds on same trials to about 4.8 seconds on change trials. >> English-hearing infants at 4–5 months showed no response to this contrast, while they did respond to [ma]–[na]. || **It cannot be that all speech contrasts are available innately**. >> The [na]–[ŋa] contrast is weak at the start, even for infants whose language uses it. >> Experience with the contrast improves sensitivity over the first year. || **Exposure to contrasts in the environment is necessary for acquisition in some cases**. >> Learning can strengthen a contrast as well as preserve it. >> Development includes building up sensitivity to hard contrasts, beyond selecting from a complete innate inventory, so experience plays an active role in shaping speech categories.
15|The alternative to the nativist approach is the **emergentist approach**. **Emergentist approaches assume that associative learning mechanisms are sensitive to statistical regularities in the environment**. >> **Associative learning** is learning that two things go together because they repeatedly occur together. >> **Statistical regularities** are patterns in how often things occur and which things occur together. >> In an emergentist view, language structure emerges from general learning mechanisms working on these patterns in rich input. || Classical conditioning is the simplest example of associative learning. The neural diagram has three neurons, a **bell** neuron, a **food detector** neuron and a **salivation** neuron. >> The food detector already connects strongly to salivation, so food produces salivation. >> When the bell repeatedly occurs together with food, the **synapse** between the bell neuron and the salivation neuron is **strengthened**. A synapse is a connection between neurons. >> After enough pairings, the bell alone can produce salivation. || The question is **how this can extend beyond conditioning to support learning for pattern recognition**. **Pattern recognition** is the ability to identify a recurring structure, such as a speech sound, across many varied examples. >> The same strengthening of connections could link acoustic features that tend to occur together. >> Repeated co-occurrence in speech could then build up categories without every category being specified beforehand. Infants would learn categories by tracking which sounds occur together and how often.
16|The **American vowels** offer an example of statistical regularities. When **Kuhl and others** talk about **learning the statistics of distributions in the environment**, they mean things like this. A **distribution** describes how often each value occurs across many examples. || The plot, titled “Why is speech categorization difficult?”, places many vowel tokens in a **vowel space** defined by the **first formant** on the horizontal axis, from 300 to 1,200 Hz, and the **second formant** on the vertical axis, from 600 to 3,400 Hz. >> Each colored dot is one production of a vowel. >> Each vowel forms a cloud of dots, enclosed by an oval. >> The ovals **overlap** heavily, so a single pair of formant values can belong to more than one vowel. Listeners must learn where each vowel’s cloud is centered and how far it spreads. || **We might learn the vowels of our language by extracting the statistics of these distributions**. >> Over many exposures, the listener tracks where tokens of each vowel tend to cluster. >> The categories reflect the shape of the input. || A **“prototypical” /a/** would then be **just the mean, or perhaps the mode, of the distribution of all the /a/ sounds we hear**. >> The **mean** is the average of all the values. >> The **mode** is the most frequent value. >> Either way, the prototype comes from experience, so it can differ across languages and listeners.
17|**Maye and colleagues** tested whether infants actually learn from distributions. They had infants **listen passively** to stimuli from a **[da]–[ta] continuum** of eight steps that differed in **voice onset time**. Passive listening means the infants simply heard the sounds, with no task or reward. || The two groups heard different **distributions** during familiarization. The graph plots how often each stimulus was presented. >> The **dashed line** marks a **bimodal distribution**. Stimuli 2 and 7 were presented most often, 16 times each, while the middle stimuli were rare. Two peaks give **evidence for two different categories**. >> The **solid line** marks a **unimodal distribution**. Stimuli 4 and 5 in the middle were presented most often, while the ends were rare. One peak gives **evidence for a single category**. || **At test, infants heard stimuli 3 and 6** in either **alternating** or **non-alternating** trials. >> In alternating trials, the two stimuli switched back and forth. In non-alternating trials, one stimulus repeated. >> If infants treat 3 and 6 as different, the two kinds of trials should hold their attention differently. || The table gives **mean looking times** in seconds. >> **Six-month-olds after unimodal exposure** looked 4.85 seconds on alternating trials and 4.53 seconds on non-alternating trials. >> **Eight-month-olds after unimodal exposure** looked 4.98 and 5.20 seconds. >> **Six-month-olds after bimodal exposure** looked 5.66 and 6.41 seconds. >> **Eight-month-olds after bimodal exposure** looked 5.45 and 6.15 seconds. || **Only infants pre-exposed to the bimodal distribution were sensitive to the difference between conditions**. >> It is **as if the infants in the unimodal condition had learned to treat stimuli 3 and 6 as members of the same category**. >> A few minutes of exposure to a distribution changed how infants grouped the sounds, which supports statistical learning of speech categories.
18|There is experimental evidence that **social interaction is important for learning**. **Nine-month-old American infants** were exposed to **Mandarin Chinese** in **twelve 25-minute sessions**. >> Some infants had **live exposure**, with a Mandarin speaker playing and reading with them in person. Others had **auditory or audiovisual exposure** on audio or television, and a control group had English exposure. >> On a **Mandarin phonetic contrast** tested with the **head-turn technique**, infants with **live Mandarin exposure** scored about **65% correct**, above the English control group at about **56%**. >> Infants with **TV** or **audio** Mandarin exposure scored about the same as controls, close to chance. >> **The same exposure to a Mandarin speaker is more effective for learning when it happens in person and is interactive than when it is viewed as a video**. || A second experiment manipulated **social responses**. **Eight-month-old infants** received feedback from their mothers in response to their vocalizations. >> In the **contingent condition**, mothers responded right after the infant vocalized. **Contingent** means the response depends on and follows the infant’s behavior. >> In the **yoked-control condition**, mothers responded on a schedule copied from another infant, so the responses were **noncontingent**. >> Each session had a familiarization phase of 30 minutes, then a 10-minute baseline, a 10-minute social response phase and a 10-minute **extinction** phase, in which responses stopped. || The graph plots the **proportion of syllables** among the infants’ vocalizations. >> At baseline, both groups produced about 0.05 to 0.08. >> During social response, the **contingent** group rose to about **0.32**, while the noncontingent group stayed near **0.05**. >> During extinction, the contingent group stayed higher at about 0.2, compared with about 0.1. >> **Contingent social feedback increased the quantity and complexity of infants’ vocalizations**. || **Social interactions contingent on the production of different sounds seem to work much like a reinforcing stimulus**, something that makes a behavior more likely when it follows that behavior. >> **One idea is that humans find social interaction with other humans innately reinforcing**. >> **Another is that we are innately good at using social cues to learn from other people**.
19|**Statistical learning with constraints** describes learning from patterns in the input that is guided and limited in several ways. **Constraints** can mean a lot of things. >> First, constraints on **which stimulus properties can be contrastive**. A **contrastive** property is one that distinguishes words, such as voicing in /b/ versus /p/. Properties such as **speaker** identity and **speech rate** change the sound a great deal but do not change which word is said, so they are **not** contrastive. >> A learner tracking every statistical pattern would create categories for different voices and speeds. Learning has to focus on the properties that matter for meaning. || Second, constraints **based on earlier learning**. >> What a learner has already acquired shapes what is easy to learn next. >> Once native categories are in place, new distinctions that cut across them become harder to notice and learn. Constraints of this kind are central to **sensitive periods**, in which earlier experience limits later learning. || Third, **social and interactive contexts for learning**. >> The same sounds are learned more effectively in live, responsive interaction than from a recording. >> Contingent responses from caregivers shape infants’ own vocal behavior. || Learning therefore depends on the patterns in the input, on what the learner already knows, and on the social situation in which the input arrives. These constraints help explain why learning is fast and why it focuses on the distinctions that matter.
