2|**Aphasia** is a loss or impairment of language ability caused by brain damage. In the **1870s**, a number of German neurologists, including **Lichtheim** and **Wernicke**, began making **more systematic observations of aphasia**. This reasoning about aphasia came before modern linguistics and before Chomsky. >> They ascribed specific losses of function to **“disconnections” between “centers.”** >> A **center** is a brain region that stores one kind of language representation. A disconnection is damage to the pathway linking two centers, so information can no longer travel between them. || **Lichtheim’s “House” model** draws these centers and pathways in the shape of a house. >> **A** is the center for **sound images**, the stored sound patterns of words. **M** is the center for **movement images**, the stored motor plans for saying words. **B** is the center for **concepts**, at the peak of the roof. >> **a** is the **acoustic pathway** bringing heard speech into A, and **m** is the **motor pathway** carrying plans from M out to the speech muscles. >> The numbers **1 through 7** mark different forms of aphasia. Sites 1 and 2 are the centers M and A. Site 3 is the link between M and A. Sites 4 and 6 are the links from M and A up to B. Sites 5 and 7 are the motor output and acoustic input pathways. || Different patterns of loss follow from different damage sites. >> A loss of the ability to **understand spoken words** might be due to a disconnection between **A and B**. The sound of the word arrives, but it cannot reach the concept. >> A loss of the ability to do **verbatim repetition** might be due to a disconnection between **A and M**. The heard word cannot be passed directly to the plans for saying it. || This kind of **“box and arrow” model** is foundational in **aphasiology**, the study of aphasia, and in modeling typical language function. >> The **Interactive Activation model** of word recognition has the same basic form, with levels of representation connected by pathways. >> The historical account comes from Eling and Whitaker (2009), “History of aphasia, from brain to language.”
3|The **“Geschwind–Wernicke model”** of brain and language links language functions to particular brain regions and the pathways between them. In the **1970s, Norman Geschwind revived** the box-and-arrow approach of Wernicke and Lichtheim. >> **Broca’s area** sits toward the front of the left hemisphere, in the frontal lobe. >> **Wernicke’s area** sits farther back, in the temporal lobe. >> The **arcuate fasciculus** is a bundle of nerve fibers that connects Wernicke’s area with Broca’s area. || The brain drawing marks two major grooves used as landmarks. >> The **central sulcus**, also called the **fissure of Rolando**, runs down the side of the brain and separates the frontal lobe from the parietal lobe behind it. >> The **lateral sulcus**, also called the **fissure of Sylvius**, runs along the side and separates the temporal lobe below it from the frontal and parietal lobes above. || In the century between Wernicke and Geschwind, **the field of linguistics evolved dramatically**. >> Linguists developed a theoretical model in which **syntax was independent of other aspects of language** and had **its own computational principles**. >> This opened the door to theorizing about **dissociations** and **double dissociations** of language functions in more sophisticated ways. || A **dissociation** occurs when one ability is impaired while another is spared. A **double dissociation** occurs when one patient has the first ability impaired and the second spared, while another patient has the reverse pattern. >> A double dissociation is strong evidence that two abilities depend on separate systems. >> Once syntax, word knowledge and meaning are treated as separate parts of language, a patient can be described as impaired in one specific part while the others work well.
4|**Fromkin (1997)**, in “Some thoughts about the brain/mind/language interface,” describes the classic profiles of the two main aphasias. >> The speech of **Broca’s aphasia** patients has **word-finding pauses**, **loss of grammatical morphemes**, and quite often **disturbed word order**. A **grammatical morpheme** is a small word or word part that expresses a grammatical relationship, such as “the,” “is” or the past-tense ending “-ed.” >> Auditory comprehension of everyday conversation **gives the impression of being generally good**, although **controlled testing reveals considerable impairments**. || The term **agrammatism**, meaning difficulty producing or understanding grammatical structure, is almost a synonym for Broca’s aphasia. >> Even so, **some patients with lesions in Broca’s area are not agrammatic**. >> And **some agrammatic patients would not be classified neurologically as Broca’s aphasics**. >> The behavioral symptom and the damaged brain region overlap heavily but imperfectly. A label such as Broca’s aphasia describes a pattern of behavior, and that pattern does not always follow damage to the area with the same name. || Patients with **Wernicke’s aphasia** produce **fluent speech** with **good intonation and pronunciation**. >> Their speech contains **many word substitutions**, some related in meaning to the intended word and some unrelated. >> They also produce **neologisms**, which are invented words, as well as **phonological errors**, which are errors in the sounds of words. >> They also have **comprehension difficulties**. Fluency describes how smoothly speech is delivered, which is separate from whether the words carry a coherent message.
5|An attractive **dichotomy**, or two-way division, contrasts the two aphasias. **Wernicke’s aphasia** is described as **“word salad”** or **“Jabberwocky,”** often with **neologisms**, while **syntax is roughly intact**. “Jabberwocky” is Lewis Carroll’s poem made of grammatical sentences filled with nonsense words. >> One Wernicke’s patient, asked about his health, replied **“I felt worse because I can no longer keep in mind from the mind of the minds to keep me from mind and up to the ear which can be to find among ourselves,”** from Kriendler and colleagues (1971). >> Another patient, asked about his poor vision, said **“My wires don’t hire right.”** The frame is normal, but the words miss the meaning. >> An aphasic physician, asked if he was a doctor, replied **“Me? Yes, sir, I’m a male demaploze on my own. I still know my tubaboys what for I have that’s gone hell and some of them go.”** **Demaploze** and **tubaboys** are neologisms. >> One patient described a **fork** as **“a need for a schedule”** and a **spoon** as **“How many schemes on your throat.”** || **Broca’s aphasia** is described as **“telegraphic” speech**, with **difficulty with word order and grammatical particles** and **relative sparing of important content words**. Old telegrams left out small words to save cost. >> One Broca’s patient, asked what brought him to the hospital, said **“Yes … ah … Monday … ah … Dad and P.H. (the patient’s name) and Dad … hospital. Two … ah doctors … and ah … thirty minutes … and yes … ah … hospital. And, er Wednesday … nine o’clock and eh Thursday, ten o’clock … doctors. Two doctors … and ah … teeth. Yeah … fine,”** from Badecker and Caramazza (1985). >> Content words such as Dad, hospital, doctors and teeth carry pieces of the story. The grammatical words that would connect them are largely missing, and the speech is full of pauses. || The two patterns look like mirror images. Wernicke’s patients have fluent grammar with empty or wrong words, while Broca’s patients have meaningful words with little grammar.
6|The dichotomy can also be stated in terms of what is damaged. **Wernicke’s aphasia** reflects **problems with activation, or possibly inhibition, in the lexicon**, and **Broca’s aphasia** reflects **problems with grammar**. >> The **lexicon** is the mental store of words. >> **Activation** makes a word candidate available. **Inhibition** suppresses competing words that should not be chosen, so weak inhibition could let wrong words slip into speech. || This description is **fairly distant from how Broca described his original cases**. Paul Broca’s presentation of **Leborgne**, known as **“Tan,”** gives this behavioral description. >> Tan **could only produce one jargon syllable**, “tan.” >> He **seemed otherwise to be in possession of his senses**, and he in fact **seemed to understand what was being said**. || Broca’s presentation raises several questions. >> Why is it critical that Leborgne had his other senses intact? Intact senses show that the loss is specific to speech, which makes it a dissociation. >> Why does a “capacity for articulate speech” sound like a more plausible function for a brain region than “criminality” or “philoprogenitiveness,” a phrenology term for love of one’s children? >> If Tan could be examined today, what measurements would specify his deficits in terms of the **levels of description used in modern linguistics**, such as phonetic, phonological, lexical and syntactic? || This creates a confusion. **Broca’s actual patients would not necessarily be classified as Broca’s aphasics** today. >> They were arguably **unable to produce speech at all**, apparently because of **motor control problems**. >> They would be classified as having **apraxia of speech**, a disorder in planning and coordinating the movements needed to speak. Apraxia of speech can limit output severely without any loss of grammatical knowledge.
7|The Wernicke-as-lexicon, Broca-as-grammar dichotomy is interesting partly because it **aligns well with a sharp distinction drawn in late 20th-century formal linguistics between the grammar and the lexicon**. >> The **grammar** is the system of rules for combining words into sentences. >> The **lexicon** is the store of individual words, which must be memorized. || This distinction starts with **Chomsky’s formulation of the computational problem of language learning**. >> We recognize a new item as a sentence **not because it matches some familiar item in any simple way**, but because **it is generated by the grammar** that each person has internalized. >> A sentence never heard before can still be understood because we can work out how the grammar builds its structure. || Chomsky also gave **compelling demonstrations of the independence of syntax and semantics**. In Chomsky (1955), neither **(a) “colorless green ideas sleep furiously”** nor **(b) “furiously sleep ideas green colorless”** has ever occurred in an English speaker’s past experience, and yet **(a) is grammatical while (b) is not**. >> Sentence (a) has no sensible meaning, but its word order follows English grammar. >> Sentence (b) uses the same words in an order the grammar does not allow. >> The two are equally unfamiliar and equally meaningless, so the difference comes from grammar as its own system. || The aphasia dichotomy maps onto this split. Wernicke’s aphasia would be a disorder of the lexicon, and Broca’s aphasia would be a disorder of the grammar.
8|The dichotomy also fits the levels of the **speech production model**, particularly the difference between the **functional level** and the **positional level**. >> The **message level**, which is conceptual, involves **choosing a story angle and arousing concepts of referents and event structure**. A **referent** is the person, object or event being talked about. >> The **functional level**, the first linguistic level, involves **arousing word meanings and semantic roles**. A **semantic role** is the part a participant plays in an event, such as the doer or the receiver. >> The **positional level**, the second linguistic level, involves **getting word forms in grammatically appropriate order**. >> The **phonological encoding level** supplies the sounds of the words, and the **speech gesture level** handles **articulation**, the physical movements of speaking. || At the functional level, **lemmas have an arbitrary relationship to their referents**. A **lemma** is a word’s entry in the lexicon, linking its meaning to its grammatical properties. >> Arbitrary means that nothing about the concept determines the word. There is no reason a dog must be called “dog.” This is the lexical side of language. || At the positional level, **syntactic trees, affixes and so on are the product of abstract syntactic processes**. An **affix** is a word part added to a stem, such as “-ed” or “-ly.” This is the grammatical side of language, built by rules. || An objection is that words like **HOUSEBOAT** and **DOGHOUSE** have meanings that are clearly not arbitrary, since they are built from parts. >> The reply comes from **speech error evidence** that **derivational morphology** is done at the positional level. **Derivational morphology** builds new words from existing parts, such as adding “-ly” to make an adverb. >> In the error **“easy enoughly,”** the speaker meant “easily enough,” but the “-ly” suffix attached to the wrong word. The suffix moved as a separate piece, so a grammatical operation at the positional level added it.
9|Popular theories of **sentence processing** hold that **the syntactic structure of a sentence is computed independently of meaning** as we listen or read. An autonomous parser builds the structure first, and meaning is worked out afterward. || **Isolating these “parsing” mechanisms requires carefully constructing stimulus materials**. >> In **“The burglar blew open the safe with the dynamite,”** the **preferred** structure attaches “with the dynamite” to the **verb phrase**, so the dynamite is the instrument. >> The **unpreferred** structure attaches it to the **noun phrase** “the safe,” so the phrase describes which safe. >> The words are the same in both structures. Only the attachment changes, so a test built on such sentences can target parsing without changing word meanings. || **One possibility** is that **Broca’s patients generally understand what is being said even though they have a core grammatical deficit**. >> In ordinary conversation, word meanings, familiar events and context all point toward the same message. >> A listener can use these cues to understand without fully computing the grammar. >> Testing a parsing deficit therefore requires sentences where the grammatical relationships cannot be recovered by guessing the most likely event, such as reversible sentences in which either person could plausibly do the action.
10|**None of this theory was available to Broca back in the 1860s**. For him, it was interesting enough to **dissociate “articulate speech” from comprehension**. >> Leborgne could produce only one syllable, yet he appeared to understand what was said to him. >> Showing that speaking and understanding can come apart was the key finding at the time. || The modern question is different. Suppose we want to show that **some patients have a difficulty that is more generally with syntax**. >> A **syntactic impairment** would concern the organization of grammatical relationships. >> Such an impairment should affect both producing and understanding sentences. || The answer is to **test comprehension**. >> A comprehension test lets the patient respond without speaking, which takes motor problems out of the picture. >> If a patient struggles to understand grammatical relationships, the deficit goes beyond articulation. >> Leborgne’s presentation already points this way, with its question of what measurements would specify his deficits at phonetic, phonological, lexical and syntactic levels.
11|Showing that **Broca’s patients have comprehension deficits** was interesting for several reasons. >> **Broca’s aphasia is almost always accompanied by some level of motor dysfunction**. >> Agrammatic speech might **merely reflect an attempt to communicate despite motor limitations**. If speaking is very effortful, a patient might drop the small grammatical words and keep only the most informative content words. || **Hughlings Jackson** made a related point. **We are observing what the individual can do despite the impairment**. >> Just because a brain injury leads to agrammatic speech does not literally mean that **the grammar itself is injured**. >> Caramazza and Zurif (1976) cite a version of this idea from Lenneberg (1973). If anterior damage spares the patient’s tacit knowledge of language, the telegraphic output can be blamed on a neuromuscular problem. **The strain of speaking is so great that the patient speaks asyntactically in order to economize effort**. || **Chomsky’s distinction between competence and performance** also applies. >> **Competence** is a person’s underlying knowledge of language. >> **Performance** is the actual use of that knowledge in speaking or understanding, which can be disrupted by memory limits, distraction or motor problems. >> **When motor deficits obviously interfere with performance, comprehension may provide a clearer window into competence**. || **Caramazza and Zurif (1976)** therefore tested comprehension in Broca’s patients. Speech production cannot separate the two explanations, because both predict telegraphic speech. >> If these patients understand grammar normally, their agrammatic speech is a motor performance problem. >> If they also fail to understand grammatical relationships, the deficit reaches the grammar itself.
12|**“Man bites dog”** is the newspaper example of an unexpected event. We need **psycholinguistic tests** to observe grammatical difficulties in comprehension because **in conversation, there are lots of redundant cues to what a speaker intends**. **Redundant** cues are several different sources of information that all point to the same meaning. || In **“The dog bit the man,”** there are **many ways to get the right meaning**. >> The listener can form the **most likely combination of DOG, BITE and MAN into an event representation**, **with or without syntax**. Dogs bite people much more often than people bite dogs. >> The sentence also follows **canonical word order**, which is **agent, then verb, then undergoer**, or **subject, then verb, then object**. The **agent** is the doer of the action, and the **undergoer** is the one the action happens to. In English, the first noun is usually the agent. >> In addition, a lot of comprehension happens in a **discourse context**, the surrounding conversation, which clarifies a great deal. || Broca’s patients’ comprehension might depend on these **compensatory heuristics**. A **heuristic** is a mental shortcut, and a compensatory one makes up for a weakness elsewhere. >> A patient might simply assume the most plausible event, or assume the first noun is the doer. >> These shortcuts give the right answer for “The dog bit the man” without any grammatical analysis. || To test grammar directly, the sentences need to defeat the shortcuts, as in **“The dog that the man is biting is angry.”** Here the **man** is doing the biting, even though dog comes first and dogs are more likely biters, so only grammatical analysis gets it right.
13|**Caramazza and Zurif** built **a list of exactly this kind of sentence, along with a set of control sentences**. Their table of sentence materials has four groups of eight sentences each, arranged to vary how much meaning helps. >> **A. Control sentences** are simple sentences in canonical order, such as **“The girl is kicking a green ball,” “The cat is chasing a blue bird”** and **“The boy is riding a new bicycle.”** The doer comes first and the event is ordinary. || **B. Center-embedded sentences with semantic constraints** contain a clause inside another clause, but meaning makes the roles obvious. A **center-embedded** sentence has a relative clause placed in the middle of the main clause. >> Examples include **“The apple that the boy is eating is red,” “The wagon that the horse is pulling is green”** and **“The balloon that the clown is holding is round.”** Apples cannot eat boys, so meaning alone reveals who did what. || **C. Reversible center-embedded sentences** use two participants who could each plausibly do the action. >> Examples include **“The cow that the monkey is scaring is yellow,” “The horse that the bear is kicking is brown”** and **“The girl that the boy is tickling is happy.”** Meaning gives no help, so only grammar reveals the roles. || **D. Improbable center-embedded sentences** describe events that go against expectations. >> Examples include **“The bird that the worm is eating is blue,” “The man that the horse is riding is fat”** and **“The policeman that the robber is chasing is skinny.”** Common sense points to the wrong answer here. || In all the embedded sentences, the first noun is the one acted upon, and the doer is inside the relative clause. >> The grammatical structure stays the same across groups B, C and D, while the help or interference from meaning changes. >> A listener who relies on meaning should succeed on groups A and B and fail on C and D. A listener who parses normally should succeed on all four.
14|The data come from a **sentence-picture matching task**. Participants heard a sentence and chose which picture matched its meaning. >> Accuracy reveals whether the listener understood who did what to whom, without requiring a spoken answer. >> The graph plots **mean correct responses** for each patient group across four sentence types. **C** is control sentences, **CE-S** is semantically constrained, **CE-R** is reversible, and **CE-I** is improbable. The groups are **N** for normal, **B** for Broca’s, **C** for conduction and **W** for Wernicke’s aphasia. || **Healthy controls are more or less equally good at all stimulus types**. Their line stays flat near the maximum score. || **Wernicke’s patients are poor overall**. >> Their scores are low even on the simple control sentences. >> This is probably a result of their **general comprehension deficits**, which affect understanding of words as well as grammar. || **Broca’s patients** have a **particular difficulty keeping “who did what to whom” straight** when **syntax conflicts with common sense** or with the probable semantic roles for nouns. >> Their scores are high on control sentences and on semantically constrained sentences, where meaning supplies the answer. >> Their scores drop on reversible and improbable sentences, where only grammar can reveal the roles. || This **specific comprehension deficit** was taken as evidence that **Broca’s aphasia affects syntax specifically**. >> The deficit appeared in understanding as well as speaking, which places it in the grammar itself. >> The paper’s title, “Dissociation of algorithmic and heuristic processes,” captures the idea. Broca’s patients lose the **algorithmic** process of grammatical analysis but keep the **heuristic** shortcuts based on meaning.
15|A more **specific theory of the “parts of language”** opens up **new ways of exploring structure-function relationships**. A **structure-function relationship** is a link between a brain structure and the function it supports. || **Rather than just dividing aphasics into those with good and poor comprehension, or those who are more or less fluent**, researchers can **isolate problems with particular aspects of language**. >> One patient might know word meanings well but assign the wrong roles in complex sentences. >> Another might produce a fluent grammatical frame filled with words that fail to carry the intended message. >> A single fluency rating would hide the difference between these two patients. || The key division is **the grammar versus the lexicon**. >> **Grammar** concerns the rules that relate words to one another in a sentence. >> The **lexicon** concerns stored words and the ability to retrieve the right ones. || This division gives specific hypotheses to test, and controlled tasks can relate an impaired operation to a particular brain region. >> A grammar deficit predicts trouble with reversible sentences but not with word meanings. >> A lexical deficit predicts trouble with word meanings and naming but not with grammatical structure.
16|There are **problems with this dichotomy**. **Dick and colleagues** had participants listen to **four types of sentences** and **indicate which animal was doing the action**. >> Two pictures appeared on a computer screen, one of a dog and one of a cat, and participants pressed a button on a response box to choose the animal. >> The event is always a dog biting a cat, but the sentence structure changes. || Two sentence types put the doer first. >> **Active**, as in **“The dog is biting the cat.”** >> **Subject cleft**, as in **“It’s the dog that is biting the cat.”** A **cleft** sentence splits a simple sentence to put focus on one part, using “It’s … that.” In a subject cleft, the focused noun is the subject, the doer. >> For these two types, a shortcut of choosing the first animal mentioned gives the right answer. || Two sentence types put the receiver of the action first. >> **Object cleft**, as in **“It’s the cat that the dog is biting.”** The focused noun is the object, the one being bitten. >> **Passive**, as in **“The cat is bitten by the dog.”** The passive makes the receiver the grammatical subject. >> For these two types, the first-animal shortcut gives the wrong answer. A listener who parses the grammar succeeds on all four types.
17|When **aphasia patients and healthy aging controls** do this task, **a pattern emerges much like what Caramazza and Zurif observed**. The graph plots percent correct for each group across active, subject cleft, object cleft and passive sentences, with patients grouped by the Western Aphasia Battery. >> **Elderly controls** score close to 100% on all four sentence types. >> **Anomic** patients, whose main problem is word finding, score about 89% on actives and subject clefts, dropping to about 69% on object clefts and about 73% on passives. >> **Conduction** aphasia patients score about 75% on actives and subject clefts and fall to about 42% on object clefts. >> **Broca’s** patients score about 61% on actives and 65% on subject clefts, then drop to about 42% on object clefts and about 54% on passives. >> **Wernicke’s** patients score low across the board, around 43% to 50% for every sentence type. || **Broca’s aphasia patients have particular difficulty with the syntactically complex stimuli**, the object clefts and passives. >> These are **noncanonical** structures, meaning they depart from the usual doer-first order. || **Their performance is overall not so great**, however. >> Even on simple active sentences, Broca’s patients are far below the elderly controls. >> The pattern combines a general drop in performance with an extra cost for complex structures. The groups differ in both overall accuracy and the size of that extra cost.
18|The same task was given to **healthy undergraduates** under **conditions that stress the language system** in different ways. **Stress conditions** included **speeding up the speech**, adding noise, and **asking people to remember lists of numbers as a secondary task**. >> **Compressed** speech is speech played faster than normal. >> A **low-pass filter** removes the higher frequencies from speech, making it sound muffled. >> **Auditory digits** and **visual digits** are number lists that participants had to remember while listening, which uses up memory resources. || Under **single-stress conditions**, accuracy stays near 99% for the normal condition and stays high for actives and subject clefts in every condition. >> Object clefts suffer most. With **noise**, accuracy falls from about 97% on actives to about 86% on object clefts. >> Passives also drop under noise, to about 91%. || Under **dual-stress conditions**, which combine two stressors, the effects grow larger. >> With **low-pass filtering plus compression**, accuracy is about 91% on actives and 90% on subject clefts, but drops to about 66% on object clefts and 68% on passives. >> Other combinations, such as compression plus visual digits, lower object clefts to about 90% while leaving actives near 98%. || **Object cleft and passive sentences are particularly vulnerable to all the manipulations**, the same pattern seen in Broca’s aphasia. These students have no brain damage, so stressing their processing system is enough to produce an aphasia-like profile. >> This suggests that **the comprehension deficits in Broca’s aphasia may be a “performance” issue after all**. >> Complex structures may demand more processing resources, so any reduction in resources hurts them first. The patients may keep their grammatical competence but lack the resources to use it efficiently.
19|A further question is whether **Broca’s aphasics’ comprehension deficits are language-specific**. **Saygin and colleagues (2004)** asked patients to **match an action to an object**. >> In the **non-linguistic** version, a drawing has a boy pantomiming an action, such as licking. Participants chose the matching object from pictures such as an ice cream cone, a cake, broccoli and a rooster. >> In the **linguistic** version, the action was given in words rather than by pantomime. || The bar graph gives accuracy for each version. >> In the non-linguistic version, controls scored **98.5%** and aphasic patients **93.4%**. >> In the linguistic version, controls scored **99.4%** and aphasic patients **87.5%**. >> **Broca’s aphasics had difficulty with this task whether the stimuli were linguistic or not**. || A second graph breaks down accuracy by the type of wrong choice offered, called the **distractor**. >> With **unrelated** distractors, controls scored 99.8% and aphasic patients 93.3%. >> With **semantic** distractors, which are related in meaning to the correct object, controls scored 99.2% and aphasic patients dropped to **86.3%**. >> With **affordance-based** distractors, controls scored 97.7% and aphasic patients 91.6%. An **affordance** is an action an object allows, such as a different object that can also be licked. || The nonverbal version removes language from the input while keeping meaning in the task. A pantomimed action has to be understood and linked to the right object, just as a verb would be linked to its object. >> A purely grammatical deficit predicts good performance on the nonverbal version. >> Difficulty on that version points to a broader problem with action meanings, shared with language.
20|**Performance in the non-verbal version of this task was significantly correlated with damage to Broca’s area**. The lesion maps give the brain areas linked to deficits in **non-linguistic pantomime interpretation**. >> Three horizontal slices of the brain appear, at levels marked on a small side view of the brain. >> The colored regions mark where damage was associated with poorer performance, with red and yellow marking the strongest relationships. >> The strongest associations fall in the left frontal region in front of the **central sulcus**, marked **CS**, which includes Broca’s area. || Participants only had to interpret a pantomimed action and choose an object. >> They did not have to produce a sentence. >> They did not have to work out the grammar of a spoken instruction. || Damage to Broca’s area therefore affects more than syntax. >> Patients with damage there can struggle to connect an action with its meaning even when no language is involved. >> Any account of Broca’s area has to explain both the grammatical comprehension findings and this nonlinguistic difficulty with actions.
21|A parallel question is whether **Wernicke’s aphasics’ comprehension deficits are language-specific**. **Saygin and colleagues** asked patients to **match environmental sounds to pictures**. >> In the **nonverbal** version, a speaker plays an environmental sound, such as a cow mooing, and participants choose between pictures, such as a cow and a sheep. >> In the **verbal** version, the speaker plays a spoken phrase, such as **“cow mooing,”** and participants choose between pictures, such as a cow and a violin. || The bar graph gives accuracy for each group in each version. >> **Controls** scored 99.2% verbal and 98.1% nonverbal. >> Patients with **right hemisphere damage**, labeled **RHD**, scored 99.1% and 98.2%. >> **Anomic** patients scored 98.6% and 96.7%. >> **Broca’s** patients scored 96.3% and 95.2%. >> **Wernicke’s** patients scored **90.0% verbal** and **91.3% nonverbal**. || The groups differed in overall accuracy. Controls, right hemisphere patients and anomic patients performed equally well, Broca’s patients performed worse, and Wernicke’s patients performed worst. >> **Wernicke’s patients were particularly bad at this task**. >> **No significant difference was observed between the verbal and nonverbal versions**. || The deficit therefore goes beyond understanding spoken words. >> Wernicke’s patients also struggle to connect a meaningful nonverbal sound with the object that makes it. >> Their difficulty concerns linking sounds of any kind to their meanings, whether the meaning arrives through language or through ordinary sounds.
22|**Deficits in both verbal and nonverbal sound-picture matching are associated with Wernicke’s area**. The lesion overlays mark where damage occurred in patients who were impaired on each version. >> The top row covers patients **impaired in the nonverbal version**, with **n = 8**. >> The bottom row covers patients **impaired in the verbal version**, with **n = 10**. >> Each row has three horizontal brain slices, and red and yellow mark areas damaged in the largest number of patients. || The two rows look very similar. >> In both, the region of greatest overlap falls in the posterior temporal area of the left hemisphere, around Wernicke’s area. >> The verbal and nonverbal impairments therefore come from damage to the same general region. || The two versions differ in their input, but they share a common demand. >> A spoken phrase conveys the meaning through words, while an animal call conveys it without language. >> Both tasks require connecting meaningful sounds to what they represent. Wernicke’s area appears to support meaningful sound processing in general, with language as one important case.
23|**How language-specific are the deficits in aphasia?** This is **still a controversy**. >> Some patients show particular vulnerability to complex grammatical structures, which suggests some aspects of language are handled in specialized ways. >> The same patients can also struggle with action meanings or environmental sounds in tasks that involve no language, which suggests language shares brain systems with other kinds of meaning. >> An explanation has to account for both kinds of finding. || What should be clear is **how the terms of the debate have evolved**. The field now relies on three kinds of tools. >> **Relatively sophisticated tests that tap specific aspects of language**, such as reversible and improbable sentences, cleft sentences and nonverbal matching tasks. >> **Quantitative analyses of behavior**, which measure accuracy across sentence types, patient groups and stress conditions. >> **Quantitative analyses of structure-function relationships**, which relate patterns of brain damage to performance across many patients. || These methods have come **a long way from the 19th century**. >> Wernicke and Lichtheim drew boxes and arrows from a small number of individual cases. >> Modern research can test which specific operations are impaired and map those impairments onto damaged tissue across large groups of patients.

25|**Structure-function relationships** in aphasia data can be **formalized** using MRI data. **Voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping**, from Bates, Wilson, Saygin, Dick, Sereno, Knight and Dronkers, relates the location of brain damage to the severity of symptoms across many patients. >> A **voxel** is a small three-dimensional unit of an MRI brain image, like a 3D pixel. >> For each voxel, patients with damage there are compared with patients without damage there. >> If damaged patients score much worse, that voxel is associated with the symptom. The colors in the maps, from blue for weaker to red for stronger, mark how strong that association is. || The **“symptoms”** here are **defined very broadly**. >> Maps **(a) through (c)** mark regions likely to be damaged in individuals with **fluency deficits**. The strongest associations fall in the front part of the left hemisphere. >> Maps **(d) through (f)** mark regions associated with **comprehension deficits**. The strongest associations fall farther back, in the posterior temporal region. || **Data from a large number of patients is consistent with a rough double dissociation**. >> **Posterior regions** are associated with **perception**, or comprehension. >> **Anterior regions** are associated with **production**, or fluency. >> Because each patient contributes both a damage pattern and a continuous score, the method turns many individual cases into one statistical map linking brain location to symptoms.
