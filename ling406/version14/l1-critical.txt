2|**Critical periods** and **sensitive or “optimal” periods** are two ways of describing how the timing of experience affects learning. They differ in how strict the time limits are. || **Critical periods** have two defining characteristics. >> They have a **discrete, “programmed” onset and offset**. The window opens and closes at set points in development, as if following a biological schedule. >> **Change in response to the environment, which is learning, is strictly limited to this period**. There is **no sensitivity to the environment before or after** the window. Outside the window, the relevant experience has no effect at all. || **Sensitive or “optimal” periods** describe a weaker relationship between timing and learning. >> **Learning is possible throughout the life span**. >> **Learning is optimal during particular windows**, meaning it is easiest or most effective then. >> **These windows can have a gradual onset and offset**, and their timing **can itself be affected by experience**. || The difference is between impossibility and reduced effectiveness. >> A critical period predicts that late exposure produces no learning of the relevant ability, so any late improvement argues against it. >> A sensitive period predicts that late learning is harder but still possible, alongside a lasting early advantage. >> Even the classic critical period in vision fits the term “sensitive period” better, and the same question applies to language learning.
3|**Amblyopia** is **poor vision in one eye despite normal peripheral function**. **Peripheral function** is the working of the eye itself, so the eye can be healthy while vision through it stays poor. The problem lies in how the brain processes input from that eye. || Three conditions can cause amblyopia. >> **Strabismus** is a misalignment of the eyes, so the two eyes point in different directions and the brain receives two conflicting images. >> A **cataract** is a clouding of the lens of the eye, which blocks or blurs the light reaching the back of the eye. >> **Anisometropia** is a large difference in focusing power between the two eyes, so one eye’s image is much blurrier than the other’s. || **Periods of deprivation from normal visual input** caused by these conditions **can result in poor vision long after the conditions themselves have been remediated**, or corrected. >> Correcting the misalignment, removing the cataract or fixing the focus restores the input from the eye. >> The brain may still fail to use that input properly. >> A temporary problem early in life can therefore leave a permanent effect, so the timing of the problem matters as much as the problem itself.
4|**Amblyopia occurs because of competitive dynamics between cortical inputs**. The two eyes send input to **visual cortex**, and those inputs **compete** for control of the neurons there. >> When both eyes work normally, they share the cortical neurons in balance. >> **Abnormal vision in one eye can lead to “colonization” of the cortical representation of that eye by the other eye**. The stronger eye takes over neurons that would have responded to the weaker eye. || The diagram of **monocular deprivation** in mice traces how this happens over time. **Monocular deprivation** means blocking or degrading input to one eye. >> After **2–3 days**, there is **increased spine motility**. **Dendritic spines** are small projections on neurons that receive connections from other neurons. Increased motility means they become unstable and move around. >> After **3–4 days**, there is **spine loss and Wallerian degeneration**. Connections from the deprived eye are lost, and **Wallerian degeneration** is the breakdown of nerve fibers that have been cut off from their source. >> After **more than a week**, there is **axon sprouting and new spine birth**. The open eye’s fibers grow new branches and form new connections in the space the deprived eye lost. || **When normal function is restored to the deprived eye, it remains at a competitive disadvantage**. >> The other eye now controls the connections. >> **Normal cortical responses cannot be restored by normal experience**, because ordinary vision with both eyes open keeps favoring the stronger eye.
5|**Treatments for amblyopia are based on competition**. **One of the most effective treatments involves intermittently patching the “good” eye**. >> A patch over the stronger eye blocks its input for part of the day. >> This forces the brain to rely on the weaker eye. || Patching **restores the competitive balance between the eyes** and **permits the brain’s own plasticity to restore normal function**. **Plasticity** is the brain’s ability to change its connections in response to experience. >> With the strong eye patched, the weaker eye’s input can win back cortical connections. >> The treatment does not repair the eye itself. It changes the experience the brain receives, and the brain’s plasticity does the rest. || **This treatment is most effective early in development**, and it **may be ineffective by late childhood, around 10 years**. >> Young brains are highly plastic, so rebalancing the input leads to rapid reorganization. >> As the visual system matures, the same treatment has less and less effect, so the timing of treatment matters as much as the treatment itself.
6|**Human adult plasticity after the critical period** is not entirely gone. **Levi and Polat (1996)** found that **plasticity is not abolished entirely, even well into adulthood**. **Some of these patients were in their 50s**, an age normally considered past the point where treatment works. || **Long-term, intensive training on specific psychophysical tasks** in the affected eye can lead to **improvements in both task performance and general visual function**. A **psychophysical task** measures the relationship between a physical stimulus and what a person perceives, such as the smallest contrast a person can detect. >> Participants practiced visual judgments with the amblyopic eye over a very long period. >> The benefits extended beyond the trained task to vision more broadly. || The graph plots the visual threshold against **kilotrials of training**, where one kilotrial is a thousand trials, from 0 to about 50 kilotrials. A **threshold** is the smallest stimulus a person can reliably detect, so a lower threshold means better vision. >> Over the first 19 kilotrials, the threshold dropped steadily, a highly significant change marked with p = 8.2 × 10⁻¹¹. >> From about 20 to 35 kilotrials, the threshold leveled off with little further change, marked with p = 0.97. >> Around 36 kilotrials, the threshold dropped again to a new, lower level and stayed there. || The adult visual system therefore keeps some **experience-dependent plasticity**. The right kind and amount of training can still produce change after ordinary late treatment stops working.
7|**Li, Ngo, Nguyen, Lam and Levi (2008)** found that **playing video games enhances visual acuity and visual attention in adult amblyopia**. Participants played an action video game, a first-person shooting game set in a battle scene, using their amblyopic eye. >> **Visual acuity** is the sharpness of vision, the ability to see fine detail. Improved acuity means sharper vision through the weak eye. >> **Visual attention** is the ability to select and focus on relevant parts of the visual scene, such as what matters in a busy battle scene. || Action games demand fast visual judgments and constant attention, which push the visual system to use the amblyopic eye’s input. >> The participants were adults whose childhood visual development was long over, so their improvement came from the training itself. >> Intensive visual experience improved function well after the age when patching usually works best. || Video game training adds to the evidence for adult **plasticity** in amblyopia. >> A demanding, engaging visual task can drive change in an adult visual system. >> An early optimal window can close without removing every possibility of improvement.
8|An **interim summary** brings the vision evidence together. **Amblyopia is a classic example of a sensitive period**. >> A **peripheral disturbance in early experience can lead to life-long disruption in visual function**. A problem with the eye itself during early development leaves a permanent effect on vision. >> **The long-term loss of function is the sequela of a temporary deficit during experience-dependent organization of the brain**. A **sequela** is a lasting consequence of an earlier condition. The brain organized itself around the abnormal input, and that organization persists. >> **Treatment is most effective during a limited developmental window**. Patching works best early and loses effectiveness by late childhood. || **New evidence suggests that the right kind of experience can reverse deficits late into adulthood**. >> Intensive perceptual training and video game play improved vision in adults. >> Later learning is harder and requires special conditions, but it still happens. || This pattern fits a **sensitive period** better than a strict critical period. >> A strict critical period would rule out any later improvement. >> Amblyopia combines a strong early window with some capacity for change later in life, which is what defines a sensitive period.
9|The question is **how the vision evidence relates to language**. Two lines of work connect them. >> **Lenneberg’s early attempts to combine developmental neurology with the notion of a “language acquisition device.”** **Developmental neurology** is the study of how the nervous system develops. A **language acquisition device** is a proposed innate mental capacity for learning language. >> **Studies of early deprivation**, which include **data from neglected children** and from **Deaf children with limited input to sign language**. || Lenneberg asked whether changes in the developing brain could open and close a window for acquiring language. >> He linked language milestones to brain development. >> If such a window exists, language learning would depend on the state of the brain as well as on the input. || Deprivation studies look at people who first encountered an accessible language unusually late. >> Neglected children were cut off from normal language input. >> Some Deaf children have no access to sign language in early childhood. >> Comparing these cases with typical development tests whether the timing of first exposure affects the final outcome.
10|In **Biological Foundations of Language (1967)**, **Lenneberg was attempting to combine contemporary linguistics with what was known about brain development**. **Studies of language development were starting to show a fairly robust and stage-like pattern**, with children around the world passing through similar stages in a similar order. || The graph plots the **percentage of children** reaching each milestone between **8 and 36 months** of age, combining data from Austrian, British and American children. >> The curve for **words** rises first, climbing steeply between about 10 and 18 months to near 100%. >> The curve for **two-word phrases** rises next, mostly between about 18 and 26 months. >> The curve for **spontaneous construction of sentences with five words or more** rises last, between about 28 and 36 months. || A table of milestones pairs motor development with language development at each age. >> At **8 months**, infants stand holding on and pick up small objects, and their **reduplicated** babbling, such as repeated syllables, becomes frequent, with intonation patterns that can signal emphasis and emotion. >> At **10 months**, infants creep efficiently and pull to standing, and their vocalizations mix with sound play such as gurgling and bubble-blowing. They try to imitate sounds and begin to tell words apart. >> At **12 months**, infants walk with one hand held, identical sound sequences are repeated more often, and words such as **mama** or **dada** emerge. They show definite signs of understanding some words and simple commands. >> At **18 months**, children have a definite vocabulary of more than three but fewer than fifty words. Much babbling continues, and comprehension progresses rapidly. || **The idea that language might have maturational milestones was seen as consistent with the unfolding of a biological program**. **Maturational milestones** are developmental achievements tied to physical growth. >> Language milestones line up with motor milestones, which suggests a shared biological timetable. >> Lenneberg proposed that brain maturation sets the pace for both, so the timing of language learning depends on the developing brain.
11|**Data on typical brain development were sparse** in Lenneberg’s time, **because they were almost entirely based on post-mortem analyses of tissue**. **Post-mortem** means after death. >> Researchers could not follow the same children’s brains as they grew. >> They had to piece together development from the brains of children who had died at different ages. || The graph of **neuron size** plots the **cell body volume** of neurons in the cerebral cortex against age. Separate curves are drawn for neurons in cortical **layers III, IV and V**. >> Neuron size grows rapidly in the first two years and then levels off. >> The lines are broken between ages **2 and 18**, because **no measurements were available** for that period. A shaded band marks the period of **language acquisition**. || Lenneberg observed that **abrupt changes in neuron size and packing density before age two might be permissive for the beginning of language acquisition**. >> **Packing density** is how closely neurons are crowded together in the tissue. >> **Permissive** means these changes make language acquisition possible without directly causing it. || On this view, **the critical period seems to open after this initial period of rapid development**. >> The brain first goes through a burst of physical growth, and once that growth settles, the window for learning language opens. >> Lenneberg filled the gap in the data with a reasonable guess about how the brain matures, which later research would need to test directly.
12|**More extensive data were available from studies of recovery from stroke**. **Aphasia is surprisingly rare in children with brain lesions**. A **lesion** is an area of damaged brain tissue. >> In adults, damage to language areas often causes lasting aphasia. >> In children, similar damage often has much milder effects on language. || In extreme cases, a **hemispherectomy** was performed. This is surgery that removes or disconnects one entire **cerebral hemisphere**, typically **to relieve seizures in people with epilepsy**. >> **Children in whom the left hemisphere is removed nonetheless seem to develop typical language abilities**. >> **Adults do not recover language after this procedure**. || Lenneberg’s table of hemispherectomy outcomes, based on Basser (1962), sorts cases by when the lesion was acquired. >> **Before the teens**, of the children whose **left** hemisphere was removed, **49** had speech that was **not affected or improved** after the operation. Only **3** had permanent aphasia, and all 3 had aphasia before the operation. >> **Before the teens**, of the children whose **right** hemisphere was removed, **38** had speech not affected or improved, and **5** had permanent aphasia, all of whom had aphasia before the operation. >> **During puberty**, in a single case of **left** removal, there was **slow improvement**, with some residual aphasia to the end of life, 27 months after surgery. >> In **adults** with **left** removal, **none** had speech unaffected or improved, and **6** had permanent aphasia. >> In **adults** with **right** removal, **25** had speech unaffected or improved, and **none** had permanent aphasia. || These findings led Lenneberg to hypothesize that **a key factor in closing the critical period was the development of adult-like cerebral dominance**. >> **Cerebral dominance** is the specialization of one hemisphere, usually the left, for language. >> In young children, language is not yet fixed in the left hemisphere, so the right hemisphere can take over. Once dominance is established in adulthood, removing the left hemisphere leaves permanent aphasia.
13|**As always, things are more complicated**. **Systematic studies of children with perinatal strokes** add important detail. A **perinatal stroke** occurs between the **prenatal period and 28 days after birth**. >> **Outcomes are clearly much better than for adults with similar injuries**. >> Children still show some effects of the damage, however. || **Early language deficits appear in both right- and left-hemisphere patients**. >> In adults, language problems mainly follow left-hemisphere damage. >> In young children, damage to either side causes early language delays. >> **Some deficits are worse for children with right-hemisphere damage**, which is the opposite of the adult pattern. || **Language outcomes are generally in the low-normal range**. >> This suggests **a persistent delay rather than catching up**. >> Children recover far better than adults, but they often do not reach the level of children without injury. || Early plasticity is therefore real but limited. The young brain can reorganize to support language, but the reorganized system may not work as well as the original one would have.
14|**Curtiss and colleagues (1974)** studied the **effects of early deprivation** in the case of **Genie**. >> Genie was born in **April 1957**. >> When researchers first met her, she was **13 years and 7 months old**, a painfully thin child who looked six or seven. || Her early history involved extreme isolation and abuse. >> **From the age of 20 months**, Genie was confined to a small room under conditions of increasing physical restraint. >> Most of the time she was harnessed to an infant’s potty chair. Otherwise she was confined in a homemade sleeping bag in an infant’s crib covered with wire mesh. >> She received minimal care from a mother who was rapidly losing her sight, and she was fed only infant food. >> She was **physically punished by her father if she made any sounds**. Her father and older brother did not speak to her, but at times barked at her like dogs. >> There was no television or radio in the home, and her contact with other people was extremely limited. || When she was hospitalized for malnutrition, Genie **could not stand up straight or chew food**, was **not toilet trained**, and **did not speak, cry or produce any vocal sounds**. >> Her case reveals what happens when first-language learning starts in adolescence. >> Her lack of language came together with severe physical, social and emotional deprivation, so language deprivation cannot be separated from her other hardships.
15|**During the four years that her language was observed, Genie learned quite a bit**. >> She came to **comprehend hundreds of words**. >> She came to **comprehend some aspects of morphology**, and she was **particularly good with plurals**. **Morphology** is the system of meaningful word parts, such as the plural ending “-s.” >> She came to **comprehend some prepositions**, words for spatial relationships between objects. || A test of prepositions used **buttons and plastic boxes** of different colors and sizes. Genie’s task was to move the buttons and boxes according to the instructions. >> A sample item was **“Put the red box behind the blue box.”** >> The test covered **under, over, in, on, behind** and **in front of**, along with **in back of** and **next to**. || The results, collected between October 1972 and August 1973, show more correct than incorrect responses for every preposition. >> **On** had 36 correct and 9 incorrect, and **in** had 37 correct and 4 incorrect. >> **Over** had 11 correct and 2 incorrect, and **under** had 17 correct and 7 incorrect. >> **In front of** had 20 correct and 6 incorrect, **in back of** had 7 correct and 3 incorrect, **next to** had 7 correct and 1 incorrect, and **behind** had 12 correct and 5 incorrect. || Some language can therefore be acquired late. This learning took place after puberty, long after the window Lenneberg proposed, but it was uneven across different parts of language.
16|There were also things Genie **did not learn**. **Genie’s production lagged severely behind her comprehension**. >> **Some lag is typical of development**. All children understand more than they can say. >> Genie had been **punished for making sounds** as a child, which could also limit how much she spoke, so her speech showed less than she actually knew. || **Her grammatical abilities developed much more slowly than her vocabulary**. A test of **conjunctions** placed **five familiar objects in a row** in front of Genie and asked her to point to one or more of them. >> Sample items included **“Show me the fork and pencil,” “Show me the crayon or the knife”** and **“Show me either the spoon or the crayon.”** >> **And** requires picking every named object. **Or** requires picking one of them, which depends on understanding a logical relationship. || **Early responses** treated **or** sentences the same as **and** sentences, so Genie pointed to both objects. **Later responses** distinguished and from or, but she still did not understand the meaning of **either/or**. >> Between October 1971 and December 1973, **and** sentences produced **100 correct**, **3 incorrect** and **0 unanalyzable** responses. >> **Or** sentences produced only **7 correct**, **51 incorrect** and **6 unanalyzable** responses. >> Genie grasped and almost perfectly but failed or almost entirely, despite a vocabulary of hundreds of words. Her strong vocabulary and weak grammar fit the idea that grammar depends more on early exposure than vocabulary does.
17|Genie’s case raises **lots of questions**. >> **Was Genie learning disabled?** She might have had difficulties from birth that would have affected her language regardless of deprivation. >> **What were the residual effects of neglect and abuse on her social development?** Severe trauma could affect learning in many ways beyond the lack of language input. || A further limit is that **after four years, funding to study her was pulled**. >> **We cannot know what she might have learned after a longer time**. >> The evidence covers only what she learned within that four-year window. || **Nonetheless, the disparity between her vocabulary development and her grammatical development is widely interpreted as suggesting that grammar learning in a first language is specifically impaired after a sensitive period**. >> She learned hundreds of words but made little progress with grammar. >> On this interpretation, vocabulary can be learned at any age, while grammar depends on early exposure. >> Disability, abuse and late exposure could each affect grammar learning, and no single case can separate them. Even so, her pattern became an influential piece of evidence for a sensitive period in first-language learning.
18|**Mayberry, Lock and Kazmi (2002)** studied deaf people who acquired a first language late. **Many deaf children do not get sign language input but have an otherwise typical upbringing**. >> Unlike Genie, these children grow up in ordinary, caring homes. >> Their only unusual experience is a lack of accessible language early in life, which makes them a cleaner test of first-language timing. || **Children aged 9 to 15 have difficulty learning American Sign Language, or ASL, if it is their first time learning a language**. >> A **comparison group** of children who **went deaf after initially learning a spoken language** **do much better** at learning ASL. >> Both groups learned ASL late, but only one group had an earlier language. >> **These differences persist into adulthood**. || The results come from two tasks. >> **(a) ASL performance** of deaf adults was measured with a task requiring **recall of complex ASL sentences**. Adults with **no early language** scored about **36%**, while adults with **early spoken language** scored about **78%**. >> **(b) English performance** was measured with a task requiring **judgments of whether complex English sentences were grammatically correct**, where **chance is 50%**. Deaf adults with **no early language** scored about **52%**, close to chance. Deaf adults who had **early signed language**, ASL in infancy, scored about **80%**. Hearing adults with an **early spoken language** other than English scored about **80%**. || The advantage comes from **having any language early**, whatever its type. >> Early ASL helped later English learning as much as an early spoken language did. >> People with no early language struggled with both ASL and English. Early accessible language, signed or spoken, provides the foundation for learning languages later.
19|The evidence bears on whether there is a **“critical period”** or a **“sensitive period”** for a first language, or **L1**. >> **Recovery from stroke, and certainly hemispherectomy, looks very, very different in childhood and adulthood**. Children can develop language after damage that would leave adults with permanent aphasia. >> This supports a strong effect of age on the brain’s ability to reorganize for language. || **Deprivation of input does not appear to extend the critical period**. >> One might expect that a child without language input would keep the window open until input arrives. >> Instead, Genie and the late-signing deaf adults show lasting difficulty, especially with grammar. >> The window seems to follow a maturational schedule, whether or not language has been experienced. || **All of these data come from highly unusual cases with many other things going on**, so it is **difficult to reach firm conclusions**. >> Stroke, brain surgery, abuse and neglect each bring complications beyond the timing of language input. >> There is also a **lack of animal models**. Animals do not learn human language, so their development cannot directly answer the question. >> Experiments such as **gene knockouts**, which disable specific genes to test their role, **cannot be done with people**.
