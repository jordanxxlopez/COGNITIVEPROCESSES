2|The first principle is the simplest. **As mass goes up, frequency goes down, and as stiffness goes up, frequency goes up.** That principle alone invites a **false argument**, because if something raises both the mass and the stiffness of a system, it can seem that the two effects cancel and nothing audible changes. >> That is only true of a system with a single mass. With one mass, more mass and more stiffness really can cancel. >> In a system with many masses and springs, the effects do not cancel, because of a fact about where things happen. || **The places with the most motion are the places with the least springiness, and the places with the most springiness have the least motion.** So the location of a change decides which effect counts. >> At a spot with no movement but lots of stretching, a heavier spot does nothing, while a stiffer spot raises the frequency, so raising both makes the frequency go up. >> At a spot with lots of movement but no stretching, a heavier spot lowers the frequency, while a stiffer spot does almost nothing, so raising both makes the frequency go down. || The rest of the principles build on this one, and **they all interact with each other**. A system with N masses has N modes, each with a higher frequency, because more springs are active in the higher modes. >> Increasing a mass lowers frequency in proportion to how much that mass moves, so a mass that is not moving has zero effect. >> Increasing a spring's stiffness raises frequency in proportion to how much it stretches and compresses, so stiffening a spring in a place with lots of motion has no effect. >> In a fixed-fixed system the harmonics are multiples of the base frequency, and in a fixed-free system the formants are odd multiples of the base frequency.
3|Chains from one mass up to six masses all follow the same rule, **N masses give N independent modes**. Each chain has fixed ends, equal masses and equal springs, and each row of the grid holds every mode of its chain. || Moving to higher modes, the pattern has **more reversals and a higher vibration frequency**. In the lowest mode all the masses move together and the springs barely stretch, so the frequency is low. In the highest mode every neighbor moves against the next, so every spring is working and the frequency is highest. >> In chains of separate masses like these, the frequencies are generally not whole-number multiples of the lowest one. >> Exact multiples belong to the ideal string, which has no separate masses.
4|The **vocal folds** are the main fixed-fixed system in speech. They sit right behind the Adam's apple and are a membrane of many masses and springs, fixed on all sides, so their vibrations are called **harmonics**. The bottom one is the **base frequency**, and each following harmonic is a multiple of it. >> If the base is 100 Hz, the next harmonics are 200, 300, 400 and 500. >> If another person's base is 250 Hz, their harmonics are 500, 750, 1000 and so on. || The multiples come from **counting how many copies of the first mode fit into each higher mode**. On a string fixed at both ends, the first mode is half a wave, one section going up and down, and that piece from one still point to the next is a **half-wave packet**. >> The second mode holds two packets, one going up while the other goes down, so it vibrates at twice the base frequency. >> The fifth mode holds five packets, so it is five times the base frequency. >> Higher harmonics have more half-wave packets in the same length, so each wave is shorter and vibrates faster.
5|A string fixed at both ends with modes at 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz vibrates in all five at once. Its combined motion is the sum of all five modes, so the string takes on a changing, complicated shape built from five simple patterns. >> Higher modes complete more cycles in the same time, so while the first mode swings once, the fifth swings five times. >> Measured at one point, its spectrum has five equal spikes, because each mode's strength was set to give the same size there.
6|A **membrane** fixed on all edges has modes named with two numbers that count its moving regions in each direction. In the (1, 1) mode one region rises and falls as a whole, in the (2, 1) and (1, 2) modes two regions go against each other, in the (2, 2) mode four regions alternate, and in the (3, 1) mode three regions go against each other along one direction. || The same counting works here. The first mode fits into the second mode twice, so the second is 200 Hz, and it fits into the fourth mode four times, so the fourth is 400 Hz. >> The five modes are given 100 to 500 Hz for teaching, so these are not the natural frequencies of that rectangle. >> The combined surface is the exact sum of all five motions, and its spectrum at one pickup point has five equal spikes.
7|A **building** is fixed on one side at the ground and free on the other at the top. Engineers want it to sway in the first mode, all together, which is mildly annoying on a high floor but safe. A higher mode, with some floors going one way and others going the other way, would shatter the structure. || A fixed-free system has a **different counting rule**. A fixed-fixed system's basic piece goes from nothing to lots of motion and back to nothing, while a fixed-free system's basic piece goes from nothing to lots of motion, with no still point at the free end. >> If the first mode is 100 Hz, the next mode fits three of those pieces, so it is 300 Hz, and the third fits five, so it is 500 Hz. >> The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called **formants**, and their major property is that they are **odd multiples** of the first. || The string fixed at the bottom and free at the top has a **node** at the fixed end, a point with no motion, and an **antinode** at the free end, a point of largest motion. Its four modes are at 100, 300, 500 and 700 Hz, so only odd harmonics occur. >> For the same length and wave speed, freeing one end halves the lowest frequency, so this string was retuned to keep its lowest mode at 100 Hz. >> The big, important fixed-free system in speech is the air in the vocal tract, whose vibration makes all the vowels and consonants.
8|The basic piece from a node to an antinode is a **quarter-wave packet**, and each fixed-free mode holds an odd number of them. The first mode holds one packet, the second holds three, the third holds five and the fourth holds seven. || The counts skip the even numbers because of the ends. Adding one packet would leave a node at the free top, which is not allowed, but adding two packets keeps the antinode at the top. >> So one fixed end and one free end give only odd multiples, while two fixed ends give every multiple. >> In both cases, more packets in the same length means shorter waves and higher frequencies.
9|The quarter-wave packets can be counted one mode at a time, from the fixed bottom up to the free top. The first packet appears in every mode, the next two appear from the second mode on, the next two from the third mode on, and the last two only in the fourth mode. >> That is why the completed counts come out as one, three, five and seven rather than one, two, three and four. >> Each pair of packets adds another node and antinode inside the string while keeping the free top as an antinode.
10|The air in the vocal tract can be modeled as a **fixed-free chain of masses and springs**, the same as any other vibrating system. The masses stand for the air's inertia, its tendency to keep moving, and the springs stand for its springiness when squeezed. The chain is fixed at one end, where there is no motion, and completely free at the other. >> Masses and springs are easier to see than air, but the model and the air itself behave the same way. || The first mode is **F1-like**, the lowest fixed-free air mode, with motion still at the fixed end and growing toward the free end. The second mode is **F2-like**, and it fits three of the no-motion-to-lots-of-motion pieces, while the first mode fits one. >> In a uniform tube the second mode vibrates three times faster than the first, so if F1 is 500 Hz, F2 is 1500 Hz. >> F stands for formant, so F1 is the first formant and F2 is the second. >> Three spots are marked along the tube, with /a/ one third of the way from the fixed end, /i/ two thirds of the way, and /u/ at the free end.
11|The air inside the mouth is **fixed at the vocal folds and free at the lips**. At the vocal folds the folds are closed, so the air cannot go back and forth, and at the lips the mouth is open, so the air goes back and forth freely. Air that goes out also comes back in, just as a mass goes back and forth. >> Each blue circle stands for billions of air molecules. || Two kinds of motion tell the masses and springs apart. Where neighboring lines of air come really close together and then really far apart, the air is stretching and compressing, which is a sign of **springiness**. Where the lines of air just move back and forth together without their spacing changing, the air is acting as a **mass**. >> The easiest place to see pure mass is the free end at the lips, where the air just moves back and forth with no compression at all. || **The first mode of the air is the same concept as a building swaying in its first mode.** There is no motion at the bottom but lots of compression and expansion there, and there is lots of motion at the top but no compression. >> Higher modes have more fixed points, with one in the first mode, two in the second and three in the third. >> Around each point with no motion there is lots of compression, so motion and springiness alternate along the tract. || The formants follow the odd-multiple rule. If the first formant is 200 Hz, the second is three times that and the third is five times that, and the actual base number depends on how heavy and how springy the air is. >> A recording of schwa, the vowel in hut, came out with formants near 500, 1400 and 2500 Hz, close to the prediction. >> The same thinking was first developed to understand how planets move, how electricity works and how buildings vibrate, and was then applied to speech.
12|Vowels differ in **where the air is squeezed** in the vocal tract. A **constriction** is a place where the tongue or lips narrow the passage and squeeze the air. >> Done has no constriction, so the airflow is unimpeded everywhere. >> Dean, a high front vowel, squeezes the air under the roof of the mouth, two thirds of the way from the **glottis**, the tiny space between the vocal folds at the fixed end. >> Don, a low back vowel, squeezes the air one third of the way from the glottis, far in the back, while Dune, a high back vowel, also has rounding with the lips very close together, and Dan is low and front. || **/u/ has two constrictions**, one with the tongue far in the back and one at the lips. The constriction at the back has no effect on any of the formants, even though it is large, because of where it falls in the modes. >> Japanese has a rare vowel like English /u/ but without lip rounding, so it has only the back constriction and sounds as if the constriction is barely there. >> So the lip rounding is the part of /u/ that actually changes the formants.
13|**Constricting air makes it stiffer**, just as squeezing the air in a tire makes the tire stiffer. **Constricting air also makes it more massive**, because squeezing air into a smaller volume raises its density, and the smaller the volume, the higher the density. >> So a constriction raises both the effective mass and the effective stiffness of the air. || The false conclusion is that the two effects cancel. They do not, since speech is full of changing frequencies even though all a speaker does is raise mass and stiffness in different parts of the tract. The real answer comes from location. >> At a point with no motion but lots of stretching and compression, the added mass does nothing and the added stiffness raises the frequency. >> At a point with lots of motion and no compression, the added mass lowers the frequency and the added stiffness does nothing. >> So whether a formant goes up or down depends on whether its mode is moving or compressing at the constriction.
14|Watching the air column again with constrictions in mind, each spot along the tube can be asked one question for each mode, namely whether the air there is mostly moving or mostly compressing. **Where the air mostly moves, an added constriction lowers that formant. Where it mostly compresses, an added constriction raises it.** >> Near the closed end the air is always springy, because air can be squeezed against something closed. >> Near the open end the air always acts like a mass, because there is nothing to squeeze against.
15|The fixed-free mass-spring chain makes the same point with visible masses and springs. Near the lips both the first and second modes move a lot, while farther inside the tube the second mode has an extra change in its pattern. >> So a spot can be moving in one mode and compressing in the other. >> The one-third and two-thirds marks are the spots used for the /a/ and /i/ constrictions.
16|For **/u/**, the masses at the free end get heavier, which is **free-end loading**. Rounding the lips squeezes the air between them and raises its mass and stiffness. **At the free end, both modes are all motion**, with hardly any compression or expansion, so the added mass matters and the added stiffness does not. >> Added mass where there is motion lowers frequency, so F1 goes down and F2 goes down. || **A recording confirms the prediction.** Starting from schwa, with formants near 500, 1500 and 2500, a student moved into /u/, and both the first and second formants dropped. >> The increased density of the air between the lips is what lowered them.
17|For **/i/**, the air is squeezed two thirds of the way from the fixed end, and at that spot the two modes are doing different things. In the first mode there is lots of motion and hardly any compression, so the spot is mass-dominated. In the second mode there is lots of compression and expansion, so the spot is stiffness-dominated. >> So F1 goes down, because added mass where there is motion lowers the frequency. >> And F2 goes up, because added stiffness where there is compression raises the frequency. || **A recording confirms this as well.** Starting from schwa and moving into /i/, the first formant dropped a lot and the second went up. >> Speech is masses and springs from end to end. The vocal folds vibrate, the air in the tract is shaped by the tongue and lips, the sound travels into the listener's ear, which also has a mass-spring structure, and the brain itself oscillates in the form of brain waves.
18|For **/a/**, the constriction is one third of the way from the glottis, closer to the fixed end. In the first mode springiness dominates at that spot, so the added stiffness wins and F1 goes up. In the second mode mass motion dominates at that spot, so the added mass wins and F2 goes down. >> The result is F1 up and F2 down, the reverse of /i/. >> The same rule decides both cases, since stiffness where the air compresses raises a formant and mass where the air moves lowers it.
19|Across the uniform schwa tube and the first three modes, motion and compression keep trading places along the tract. **That alternation is what lets one constriction lower one formant and raise another.** >> A spot that is mostly moving in the first mode can be mostly compressing in the second mode. >> So the effect of a constriction has to be worked out separately for each formant.
20|In **source-filter theory**, a vowel comes from two systems working at the same time. The **source** is the glottis, the vibrating vocal folds, and **the glottal source gives a harmonic comb**, a set of evenly spaced harmonics, here every 100 Hz up to 4000 Hz. Before the vocal tract, these are just evenly spaced lines of equal height, so nothing in the source marks one vowel from another. || The **filter** is the air in the vocal tract, and **the vocal tract filter sculpts the heights of those harmonics**. For schwa, the filter places formants at 500, 1500, 2500 and 3500 Hz. >> Harmonics near a formant come out tall, and harmonics far from any formant come out short. >> So the filtered spectrum rises into peaks around each formant and dips between them. || **The harmonics stay the same and only their heights change, so the envelope is the vowel.** The envelope is the smooth outline traced by the tops of the harmonics. >> Changing the vowel does not move the harmonics. It reshapes the envelope by moving the formants.
21|The same 100 Hz harmonic comb goes through the filter for **/i/**, which has a **low F1** and a **high F2**. The harmonics near the bottom become tall, almost nothing survives in the middle range, and another group of tall harmonics rises up high. >> Compared with schwa, F1 is lower and F2 is much higher. >> That matches the prediction of F1 down and F2 up for a constriction near two thirds of the way from the glottis.
22|For **/a/**, the filter gives a **higher F1** and a **lower F2**, so the two formants sit close together and the tall harmonics form one broad group fairly low down. >> Compared with schwa, F1 is higher and F2 is lower, which matches the prediction for a constriction near one third of the way from the glottis. >> In both vowels the harmonics stay at the same frequencies, and only their heights change.
23|**A spectrum is one instant**, with frequency along one axis and amplitude along the other. But speech is time-varying, since constrictions are made and unmade as the tongue and lips move, so one spectrum is not enough. **A spectrogram stacks many spectra over time.** Time runs along the horizontal axis, frequency runs up the vertical axis, and amplitude becomes darkness, with high amplitude black, medium gray and low white. >> So a formant appears as a dark band at its frequency. || In a spectrogram of **/ia/**, the bands follow the formants over time. During /i/, F1 is very low and F2 is very high, so there is a dark band low down, a gap, and another dark band high up. >> Moving into /a/, the formants come close together, as the low band rises and the high band falls.

25|**Formants can only be seen through the harmonics they shape**, so the spacing of the harmonics matters. With harmonics at 100, 200, 300 and so on, and F1 at 300 and F2 at 2100, the formants have lots of harmonics to work on shaping, so each formant band is easy to see. With harmonics at 250, 500, 750 and so on, far fewer harmonics fall near each formant, and it becomes very hard to tell harmonics from formants. || Holding the filter fixed and changing only **F0**, the base frequency of the vocal folds, low F0 gives dense harmonics that trace each formant band well, while higher F0 gives sparser harmonics, so a formant can fall between them. At very high F0 the formants may look like isolated harmonics instead of bands. >> Formants are the smooth envelope, and harmonics are samples of it, so higher F0 means fewer samples. >> Raising F0 does not move the formants. It only leaves fewer harmonics to reveal them. || High voices make this a real problem for listeners too. A toddler's harmonics could be at 400, 800 and 1200, so it is hard to tell where their formants are unless the listener is used to how that toddler speaks. >> A soprano's first harmonic could be as high as 800 or 900 Hz, so most people cannot follow the words at an opera, even while watching the singer's lips.
