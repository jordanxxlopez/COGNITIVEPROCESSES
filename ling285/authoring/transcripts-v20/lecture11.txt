2|A **mass-spring system** is a mass hooked up to a spring, and it vibrates because of what each part wants. **The spring wants to be at its equilibrium position**, its resting length, so whenever it is stretched or squished it pulls or pushes to get back there. **A mass that is moving keeps on moving**, so once it gets going it does not stop on its own. >> When the mass is pulled out and let go, the spring pulls it back toward equilibrium. >> The mass is moving when it gets there, so it keeps on moving past equilibrium and squishes the spring, which then pushes it back the other way. >> The mass overshoots again and again, so the system vibrates. Friction from the table would slowly stop it, but the model ignores friction, so it vibrates forever. || The same system can be pictured in **two directions**, and it is important to be able to picture it both ways. In **longitudinal** motion the mass moves left and right along the spring. In **transverse** motion the mass sits between two walls with a spring on each side and goes up and down. >> Pulling the mass up in the transverse picture stretches the springs, and springs hate being extended, so they pull the mass back down. >> When the mass passes the middle, the springs are not stretched at all, but the mass is moving, so it keeps on moving and stretches them on the other side. || **Both pictures move in exactly the same way** and at the same rate, one cycle every two seconds, because they have the same mass and the same total stiffness in the direction of motion. Some systems are easier to think about longitudinally and others transversely, but both describe the same vibration. >> So the rate of vibration is set by the mass and the stiffness, not by which direction the mass happens to move.
3|Two things set how fast a mass-spring system vibrates, the **mass** and the **stiffness** of the spring. The number of back-and-forth cycles in each second is the **frequency**. >> Each comparison changes only one of these two things, so its effect is easy to see. || **A heavier mass vibrates more slowly.** This matches everyday experience, since heavy branches sway back and forth slowly while little branches sway back and forth quickly. A heavy mass is hard to get moving and hard to turn around, so each trip back and forth takes longer. >> When the mass is made four times heavier and the spring stays the same, the system vibrates at half the rate. || **A stiffer spring vibrates faster.** Stiffness is harder to picture, but the stiffer something is, the faster it wants to go back, because a stiff spring pulls back hard and turns the mass around sooner. >> When the spring is made four times stiffer and the mass stays the same, the system vibrates at twice the rate. >> Both rules hold whether the system is pictured longitudinally or transversely. || The two rules point in **opposite directions**. Raising the mass lowers the frequency, and raising the stiffness raises the frequency. >> These two rules are what explain everything about modes, strings and, eventually, speech.
4|With **two masses**, the system has two walls, a spring from each wall to a mass, and one spring between the two masses. All the masses are equal and all the springs are equal, and this system can vibrate in **two different ways**, called **modes**. **A mode is just another word for a way of vibrating**, and the two modes are told apart by asking whether the masses go together or against each other. >> In the first mode, both masses are pulled up and let go, and they go up and down together. Longitudinally, they move left and right together. >> In the second mode, one mass is pulled down and the other up, and they move against each other, so when one goes up the other goes down. || A system can also vibrate in **both modes at the same time**. If both masses are pulled up but one higher than the other, both start falling together, which is the first mode, while the middle spring also pushes them against each other, which is the second mode. >> The result moves together and against each other at once, and real systems, including the ones used in speech production, do this all the time. || **The second mode vibrates faster than the first**, even though both use the same two masses and the same three springs. The explanation comes from asking what each spring is actually doing. >> In the first mode the masses move together, so the middle spring never stretches or compresses. It just rides along like a rigid bar, so effectively the first mode has only two springs. >> In the second mode the masses move against each other, so the middle spring is stretched and squished over and over, and all three springs are working. >> More springs working means more stiffness, more stiffness means more pull, and more pull means a higher frequency. Nothing about the springs changed. The modes simply use the springs differently. || This foundation has to be followed step by step rather than memorized, because each later idea builds on it. >> The general rule is that the more the masses go against each other, the more the springs stretch and compress, so the higher the frequency of that mode.

6|With **three masses**, the system has **three modes**, one more than with two masses, and each mode is a different way the masses move relative to each other. In the first mode all three go back and forth together, and this mode has the lowest frequency. In the second mode the middle mass stays perfectly still while the two outer masses move against each other, and this is the middle frequency. In the third mode the middle mass moves opposite to the two outer masses, and this is the highest frequency. >> The second mode can be started by carefully leaving the middle mass untouched and pulling the two outer masses apart. >> The third mode is easiest to see in the transverse picture, with the middle going one way while the sides go the other. || The frequencies follow the same reasoning as with two masses. The first mode is very slow because the masses move together, so the two middle springs are doing nothing. Their job is to stretch and compress, which is what raises frequency, and when they just ride along they add no stiffness. >> In the second mode the springs do stretch and compress, but not as much as they could. >> In the third mode everything is going against each other, so there is lots of stretching and lots of compression, which uses the most stiffness and makes it the fastest. >> The masses and springs are identical in all three modes. Only the way the masses move relative to each other changes the frequency. || All three modes can also happen **at the same time**, and any way the system is started, it vibrates as some mix of its pure modes. **Learning a musical instrument means learning how to make the masses and springs vibrate in certain modes and not others.** >> How a string is plucked or a violin is bowed decides which modes get excited, and those favored modes are heard as notes. >> A trained singer controls the masses and stiffness of the vocal folds in the same way to carry a tune. >> With four masses the same logic continues. All four can go together, the two left can go against the two right, the two middle can go against the two outer, or every mass can go against its neighbors.
7|The pattern holds for any number of masses, since **N masses give N modes**. One mass has only one way to vibrate, two masses have two ways, three have three, and so on. Moving to higher modes, more parts move against each other, so there are more reversals in the pattern and the frequency gets higher. >> In chains of separate masses like these, the mode frequencies are generally not whole-number multiples of the lowest frequency. || Comparing the same mode across chains of different lengths reveals the same idea repeating. Every first mode has all the masses going up and down together. Every second mode has the left side going against the right side, and every third mode has the middle going against the sides. >> When the number of masses is odd, the middle mass in the second mode is frozen while the sides go against each other around it. || Packing in more and more masses and springs, all the way to an infinite number, gives a **string**, like a guitar or piano string. Every little piece of a string has mass, and every little piece stretches like a spring, so a string vibrates in exactly the same kinds of modes. >> In the lowest mode the whole string goes up and down together with hardly any stretching between its pieces, and that mode gives the lowest note. >> In higher modes more and more parts of the string move against each other, and a string plucked without any skill vibrates in many modes at once. || A system can also be **fixed on one side and free to sway on the other**, and a **building** is like this. It is fixed to the ground at the bottom and free to sway at the top in high winds or earthquakes. >> Civil engineers design buildings to sway in the first mode, all together, and never in higher modes, where some floors would move one way while others move the other way. >> Buildings are not made so stiff that they cannot move at all. They are designed to sway safely in their lowest mode.
8|A **string fixed at both ends** vibrates in many modes, and here five of them are shown at 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz. **Hertz** means times per second, so a mode at 100 Hz goes back and forth one hundred times every second. Each of these frequencies is a **whole-number multiple** of the lowest one, so they are called **consecutive harmonics**. >> In the lowest mode the whole string goes up and down together, while each higher mode has more sections moving against each other and completes more cycles in the same time. >> The motion is slowed way down so that it can be followed by eye. || A real string vibrates in **all of its modes at the same time**, so its actual motion is the sum of all five. Adding the five motions together at every point gives one complicated, changing shape, which is what an actual plucked string does. || The **spectrum** of a sound says which frequencies are in it and how much of each there is. It is the same display that DJs use on their equipment. >> For this string, the spectrum has five spikes, one at each of the five frequencies, because all five modes are moving. >> If the string vibrated only in its third mode, there would be no movement in the other modes, so the spectrum would have one spike at 300 Hz and nothing else. That pattern is like a one-hot encoding, with one active slot and every other slot at zero. >> So the spectrum turns a complicated motion into a simple list of the modes that are moving.
9|**The vocal folds are even more complicated than strings.** They are a bunch of muscle forming a surface, which is called a **membrane**, and a membrane spreads its masses and springs across two directions instead of along one line. The same mode logic still applies. >> In the first mode all the masses go up and down together. >> In the second mode the left half goes against the right half. >> In the third mode the middle is stuck while the sides go against each other, and higher modes divide the surface into more and more parts. || The five membrane modes are given 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz. These values are assigned for teaching, so they are not the natural frequencies this particular rectangle would really have. >> Adding all five motions together gives one combined surface, and measured at one point, its spectrum has five equal spikes. || In real vocal folds, **all the modes happen at the same time**. Producing a voice makes the vocal folds vibrate in many modes at once, and that is what creates a spectrum full of spikes.
10|**The scientific method depends on very clear statements about how systems work.** These statements are usually written as **differential equations**, which describe how quantities change over time, and solving the equations gives a prediction about how the world will behave when it is actually measured. >> The simple spring equation is one example. Solving it predicts that a pulled spring goes back and a pushed spring also goes back. || For the voice, the theory says that **the vocal folds are a membrane** built from many masses and springs, so they vibrate in many modes at once. Their equations are the same type as the spring equations, only more of them and slightly more complicated, so the intuition built from masses and springs is enough. The prediction is that **any laryngeal vibration signal will have a spectrum with equidistant spikes**, and these evenly spaced spikes are called **harmonics**. || **This prediction was tested in class.** Two students each made a sound with the mouth wide open, so no particular vowel was being made, and the spectrum of each voice was examined in the program Praat. >> In Jack's voice the first real spike was at about 100 Hz, and the next spikes fell at about 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz, evenly spaced just as predicted. >> In Eliza's voice the first spike was higher, at about 220 Hz, and the next spikes were about double and about triple that, also evenly spaced but farther apart. >> So the prediction held for both voices. A prediction built from intuition about masses and springs turned out to be precise and real. || The difference between the two voices follows from the mass rule. Eliza's voice has a higher pitch, so her vocal folds are probably smaller and thinner, and thinner folds have less mass, so they vibrate at a higher frequency. >> This is part of how people are recognized by their voices, since becoming familiar with someone means becoming familiar with the spectrum of their voice. >> Identical twins can be hard to tell apart on the phone because they have very similar vocal folds. How a person says particular vowels also matters, and that depends on the air above the vocal folds rather than the folds themselves.
11|A string can also be **fixed at one end and free at the other**, like a building that is fixed to the ground and sways at the top. At the fixed bottom the string cannot move, and a point that never moves is called a **node**. At the free top the string moves the most, and a point of largest motion is called an **antinode**. >> Every mode has to start with a node at the bottom and end with an antinode at the top. || This changes which modes are possible. The four modes here are at 100, 300, 500 and 700 Hz, so **only the odd harmonics occur**, meaning one, three, five and seven times the lowest frequency. The modes at two and four times the lowest frequency are missing because those patterns cannot end with free motion at the top. >> The first mode has no still point inside the string, and its motion grows steadily from the fixed bottom to the free top, like a building swaying as a whole. >> Each higher mode adds more still points inside, with more sections swinging against each other. || Freeing one end also lowers the whole system. For a string of the same length and wave speed, switching from fixed at both ends to fixed at one end and free at the other **halves the lowest frequency**, so this string was retuned to keep its lowest mode at 100 Hz. >> Both ends fixed gives every whole-number multiple of the lowest frequency. >> One end fixed and one end free gives only the odd multiples.
