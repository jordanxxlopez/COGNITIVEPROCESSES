2|The **2026 Nobel Prize in economics** went to **Robert Hall** and **Michael Woodford** for the microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic **stabilization**. Stabilization means getting a system to equilibrium and keeping it there, which is the same **balancing feedback** a spring has. Pull a spring and it goes back to equilibrium, and push it and it also goes back. >> The prize was described as work on **boom-bust cycles**, the economy's repeated swings between good times and bad times. || In the Nobel committee's picture, **unemployment and inflation go in reverse of each other**, rising and falling like two oscillating curves. These are the same two curves as in a mass-spring system. >> One curve starts big, becomes negative and then positive again, just like **x**, the position of the mass. >> The other curve is its flip, just like **v**, the velocity. In the economy, x behaves like inflation and v behaves like unemployment, so the math is exactly the same in a completely different domain. || Woodford's theory is called **Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium**, or DSGE, and each word connects to ideas from masses, springs and SGD. **Dynamic** means it describes how the economy changes over time. **Stochastic** means it handles shocks, things that happen unpredictably, like a war starting, a factory burning down or a CEO caught embezzling. >> Stochastic works the way it does in stochastic gradient descent. If SGD gets one wildly off number, it gets knocked off course, but as it keeps learning from more numbers it comes back to the average. >> **General equilibrium** means the goal is an economy that settles into balance instead of jumping around, and stabilization policy tries to damp the swings so the oscillations get smaller and smaller. >> Micro means individual decisions and macro means whole-economy quantities. Speech has the same structure, since many tiny oscillations in the mouth add up to a meaning that a listener understands without tracking each one.
3|A mass-spring system rests on two facts. **The spring wants to be at its equilibrium position**, and **a moving mass keeps on moving**. Pull the mass and let go, and the spring pulls it back, the mass overshoots because it keeps moving, and the system vibrates. >> The model ignores gravity and friction and only cares about the springs pulling toward equilibrium. || The same system can be pictured **longitudinally**, with the mass moving left to right, or **transversely**, with the mass moving up and down between two springs. Both move in exactly the same way and at the same rate. >> Both views matter, because one will help with understanding the vocal folds and the other will help with the air in the vocal tract.
4|**When the mass goes up, the frequency goes down, and when the stiffness goes up, the frequency goes up.** A heavier mass is harder to turn around, so it goes back and forth fewer times per second, and a stiffer spring pulls back harder, so it goes back and forth more times per second. >> Making the mass four times heavier halves the rate of vibration. >> Making the spring four times stiffer doubles the rate of vibration. || The two directions of motion keep matching frequencies in every comparison. Whether the mass moves longitudinally or transversely, changing its mass or its stiffness changes the rate in exactly the same way. >> Once vibration is understood this way, the mass rule feels natural, and the stiffness rule follows from the fact that the stiffer something is, the faster it wants to go back.
5|With two masses, the system can vibrate in **two modes**, and a mode is a way of vibrating defined by whether the masses go together or against each other. Lift both masses and let go, and they go up and down together. Pull one up and the other down, and they go against each other. Lift both but one higher than the other, and both modes happen at the same time. || **The first mode is slow and the second mode is fast**, even though both use the same masses and the same three springs. The difference is how many springs are actually working. >> In the first mode the middle spring never stretches or compresses, so it is not behaving like a spring at all. Effectively, the first mode has two springs. >> In the second mode all three springs stretch and compress, so effectively the second mode has three springs. >> More springs working means more stiffness, and the stiffer the system is, the faster it has to go back to equilibrium, so the frequency rises. || So **multiple masses give multiple modes, each with its own frequency**. The higher the mode, the more springs are being used, and the higher its frequency. >> The two frequencies here are in the ratio of one to the square root of three, so the second mode is about one and a half to two times as fast as the first.
6|With **three masses** there are three modes. In the first mode all three go back and forth together, the springs hardly stretch, and the frequency is lowest. In the second mode the middle mass stays frozen while the two outer masses go against each other around it. In the third mode all three go against each other, which has the most stretching and compression and so the highest frequency. || Changing a single part of the system affects the modes differently, depending on what that part is doing in each mode. **Making the middle spring stiffer** affects only the modes that stretch it. >> The first mode stays the same, because its middle spring never stretches or compresses, so it does not matter how stiff it is. >> The second mode rises, because its middle spring is stretched and squished, so a stiffer spring pulls harder. The more stiffness is packed into that one spring, the more the second mode rises while the first never changes. || **Making the middle mass heavier** affects only the modes where that mass moves. >> The first mode goes down, because the middle mass moves and a heavier moving mass slows the system. >> The second mode stays the same, because the middle mass is frozen there, and a mass that does not move has no effect on frequency however heavy it gets. >> The third mode goes down even more than the first, because the middle mass moves even more in the third mode. || **A mass's influence on frequency depends on its movement.** If it does not move at all, it has no effect, if it moves a little it has a little effect, and if it moves a lot it has a big effect. >> These principles are worked out by asking what each part is doing in each mode, not by memorizing a list.
7|Adding masses keeps the same patterns. One mass has one mode, two masses have two, three have three, and so on. Every first mode is the same idea, with everything going up and down together. Every second mode divides the system into two parts, one going up while the other goes down, and when the number of masses is odd, the middle one is frozen. Every third mode divides the system into three parts, with the sides going up while the middle goes down. >> Lower modes go up and down slowly because the system is not very stiff there. Higher modes go faster because more springs are stretching and compressing. || Packing in more and more masses and springs gives a **string**, like a guitar or piano string. This is the **Lagrangian string**, named after Lagrange, who discovered that a string is an infinite number of masses and springs. >> Every little piece of a string has weight, which is its mass, and every little piece hates being stretched or compressed, which is its springiness. >> In musical instruments, the frequencies of these modes are heard as notes. || A real string vibrates in many modes at the same time, so its motion is the sum of all the modes. Hearing just a single mode would be a very annoying sound, a pure tone like an ambulance siren, so real sounds always have many modes together. >> The ear can tell many instruments apart at once because it is made of masses and springs that are well matched to the masses and springs vibrating in the world.
8|A string fixed at both ends has modes at 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz. These frequencies are **harmonics**, exact multiples of the **base frequency**, which is the frequency of the lowest mode. || **The multiples come from how many copies of the first mode fit into each higher mode.** The first mode is a single bump that goes up and down. The second mode fits two of those bumps, one going up while the other goes down, so it vibrates twice as fast. >> The third mode fits three bumps, so it vibrates three times as fast. >> The fifth mode fits five bumps, so if the first is 100 Hz, the fifth is 500 Hz. The frequency multiplies by how many times the first mode fits into the whole string. || The **spectrum** says which frequencies a vibration has and how much amplitude is in each one. This string's spectrum has spikes at all five harmonics added together, and because many frequencies happen at once, the actual motion looks erratic, like the waveform of speech. >> Measured at one marked point, the five spikes come out equal, because each mode's strength was set to give the same size there.
9|Everything about mass-spring systems can be summed up as a short list of principles, and each one comes from the same two facts, that mass resists being turned around and that springs hate being stretched. **If you raise the mass, the frequency goes down.** The printed list says stiffness up, frequency down, but that was corrected in class to **stiffness up, frequency up**, and the stiffness principle further down the same list agrees. >> A system of N masses has N modes of vibration, each with a higher frequency. || **Increasing a mass lowers frequency in proportion to how much that mass moves, which depends on where it is.** A mass that does not move has no effect however heavy it gets, and a mass that moves a lot lowers the frequency a lot. >> At the very edges of a string there is no motion, so adding mass there changes nothing. >> In the middle of the first, third and fifth modes there is lots of motion, so adding mass there drops the frequency a lot. || **Where there is maximal movement there is no stretching, and where there is maximal stretching there is no movement.** **Increasing a spring's stiffness raises frequency in proportion to how much that spring stretches and compresses**, which also depends on where it is. >> With no stretching or compression, stiffening the spring has no effect, and with lots of stretching it raises the frequency a lot. || The last two principles are about the ends of the system. In a fixed-fixed system the harmonics are multiples of the base frequency, because the base mode fits a whole number of times into each successive mode. In a fixed-free system the frequencies are odd multiples, because the base mode fits an odd number of times into each successive mode.
10|Looking again at the string fixed at both ends, the lowest mode has the **most movement in the middle**, where the string just goes up and down without being stretched. The **most stretching is at the sides**, near the fixed ends, and there the string barely moves at all. || In higher modes the same thing happens over and over along the string. Lots of movement with no stretching is followed by lots of stretching with no movement, and then lots of movement again. >> Where there is the most movement there is the least stretching, and where there is the most stretching there is the least movement. >> This alternation does not have to be memorized, since it can be worked out by watching what each piece of the string is doing.
11|A string has a fourth mode at 400 Hz and a fifth mode at 500 Hz, and one small section is marked. In that section, the **fourth mode is moving a lot**, while the **fifth mode has zero motion but lots of stretching**. Raising both the mass and the stiffness of that section has a different effect on each mode, so each effect has to be reasoned out separately. || For the fourth mode, the section is mostly moving. **Raising the mass lowers the frequency**, because the mass is moving there, while raising the stiffness has no effect, because the string is just moving up and down without stretching. >> So the fourth mode's frequency goes down. || For the fifth mode, the section is mostly stretching. **Raising the mass has no effect**, because there is no motion, while **raising the stiffness raises the frequency**, because there is lots of stretching. >> So the fifth mode's frequency goes up. || **Increasing both k and m at that spot lowers the fourth mode's frequency and raises the fifth mode's frequency.** Where there is no motion, added stiffness raises frequency, and where there is motion, added mass lowers frequency. >> This method of changing one small part and predicting its effect is called **perturbation**. It was first worked out on drums in the late 19th century by adding wax to different spots. Wax where the drum vibrated the most made the frequency drop, and wax where there was no motion had no effect.
12|**The larynx is a whole membrane**, a surface built from many masses and springs, a bit like a drum. It has many modes of vibration, and they follow the same patterns as before. >> In the first mode all the masses go up and down together. >> In the second mode the left half goes against the right half, and in the third mode the middle is stuck while the sides go against each other. || If the first mode is at 100 Hz, the next is at 200 Hz, and so on, just like the string. The membrane picture assigns 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz to its five modes for teaching, so those are not the natural frequencies of that rectangle. >> Adding all five motions together gives the combined surface, and its spectrum at one pickup point has five equal spikes.
13|**The scientific method depends on very clear theories** about how the world works, usually written as differential equations, and solving the equations gives a prediction of what will be found when the world is actually measured. The theory here is that **the vocal folds are a membrane**, so when they vibrate, the spectrum will have equidistant spikes called **harmonics**. The prediction is that **any laryngeal vibration signal will have a spectrum with equidistant harmonics**. || The experiment checks this in Praat by taking a spectral slice of a voice. The first spike came out at about 130 Hz, the next at about double that, and the one after at about triple. >> The spikes came out evenly spaced, exactly as predicted. Every person's voice has its own base frequency, but the spikes are always multiples of it. || The same reasoning explains how **smoking changes the voice**. Heavy smokers can develop nodules on their vocal folds, and a nodule is like adding a little mass to one spot. >> Even without a full nodule, smoking changes the stiffness and mass of different parts of the folds. >> Those local changes shift different modes by different amounts, just like the wax on the drum, so some harmonics stop being perfect multiples of the base frequency and the voice sounds different.
14|A **building** is a system fixed on one side and free on the other. Structural engineers design it to sway in the first mode, all together, and never in higher modes, where some floors would go one way while others go the other way. **The air inside the vocal tract is also fixed on one side and free on the other.** >> At the bottom, at the vocal folds, the air cannot move because the folds are closed and the air is stuck there. >> At the top, at the lips, the air moves in and out easily. Unlike a building, the air vibrates in all its modes, and changing the masses and springs of different parts of the tract is how listeners know which sound is being made. || A fixed-free system has a different spectrum. Instead of 100, 200, 300 and 400, its frequencies jump 100, 300, 500 and 700, which are **odd multiples** of the base frequency. The reason again comes from how many copies of the first mode fit. >> The first mode goes from no motion at the fixed end to lots of motion at the free end. >> The second mode fits three of those no-motion-to-lots-of-motion pieces, so it is three times the base frequency, and the third mode fits five, so it is five times the base. The pieces always come in odd numbers because the system must start with no motion and end with lots of motion. || A string fixed at the bottom and free at the top follows the same rule. The fixed bottom is a **node**, a point with no motion, and the free top is an **antinode**, a point of largest motion. >> For the same length and wave speed, freeing one end halves the lowest frequency, so this string was retuned to keep its lowest mode at 100 Hz.
15|The air in the vocal tract can be modeled as a **fixed-free mass-spring chain**. The masses stand for the air's inertia and the springs stand for its springiness, with the chain fixed at the glottis and free at the lips. The air moves lengthwise, back and forth, so when neighbors move together the air between them is compressed, and when they move apart it expands. || The first two modes matter most for vowels. The first mode is **F1-like**, the lowest fixed-free air mode, with motion still at the glottis and growing toward the lips. The second mode is **F2-like**, and it vibrates three times faster than the first. >> **F1 and F2** are **formants**, the resonances of the air in the vocal tract. >> Three spots are marked, measured from the glottis toward the lips. One third of the way is labeled /a/, two thirds is labeled /i/, and the lip end is labeled /u/.
16|Vowels differ in the **shape of the vocal tract**, which MRI images of five vowels make visible. High and low describe tongue height, front, central and back describe how far forward the tongue is, and together they locate a **constriction**, a narrowing of the tract. >> Dean is high and front, with a constriction two thirds of the way from the glottis. >> Dune is high and back, with a constriction at the lips. >> Done is central with no constriction, Don is low and back with a constriction one third of the way from the glottis, and Dan is low and front with no constriction position marked. || These spots match the marks on the air chain, so each vowel's constriction lands on a different part of each air mode. Where a vowel squeezes the air decides which formants go up and which go down.

18|In the fixed-free air chain, each marked spot takes part in both modes, but it does not play the same role in both. Near the lips both modes move a lot, while farther inside the tube the second mode has an extra change in its pattern, so a spot can be moving in one mode and compressing in the other. >> The one-third and two-thirds marks are the spots used to compare the back and front constrictions of /a/ and /i/. >> F1 and F2 are resonances of the air in the tract, not harmonics of the vocal folds.
19|For **/u/**, the masses near the lips get heavier, which is called **free-end loading**. **Motion dominates there in both modes**, because the lip end moves a lot in the first mode and in the second mode. >> Added mass where the air moves a lot slows the vibration down. >> So for /u/, F1 goes down and F2 goes down.
20|For **/i/**, the constriction is near two thirds of the way from the glottis. In the first mode **mass motion dominates** at that spot, so the added mass wins and F1 goes down. In the second mode **springiness dominates** at that spot, so the added stiffness wins and F2 goes up. >> This is the same logic as the fourth and fifth modes on the string. >> One local change lowers one mode and raises another, because the spot moves in one mode and stretches in the other.
21|For **/a/**, the constriction is near one third of the way from the glottis. In the first mode **springiness dominates** at that spot, so F1 goes up. In the second mode **mass motion dominates** at that spot, so F2 goes down. >> The result is the reverse of /i/. >> The rule never changes, since dominant stiffness raises a formant and dominant mass lowers it. Moving the constriction is what changes which effect wins.
22|Taken together, the principles predict what happens to any mode when a system is changed. To know whether a change raises or lowers a mode's frequency, three things matter, namely **what changed**, mass or stiffness, **where it changed**, and **whether that spot moves or stretches in that mode**. >> Added mass at a moving spot lowers that mode's frequency. >> Added stiffness at a stretching spot raises it, and a change at a spot that does neither leaves the mode alone. >> The same three questions explain the string's fourth and fifth modes and the vowels /u/, /i/ and /a/.
23|In the uniform tube labeled **Schwa** and in the separate rows for the first, second and third modes, some places have air **moving a lot** and others have air **compressing a lot**, and the two alternate along the tube. **Motion** is how far an air element travels back and forth, while **compression** is how much the spacing between neighbors changes. >> In the first mode, air hardly moves near the closed end, where compression is largest, and moves most near the open end. >> Higher modes fit more alternating regions into the tube, so strong motion and strong compression trade places along it. >> That alternation is why one constriction can push two formants in opposite directions.
