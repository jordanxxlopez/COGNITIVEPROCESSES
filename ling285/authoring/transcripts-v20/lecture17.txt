2|A **neural network** takes in information about one moment, uses its weights to process it, and makes a decision, and it learns those weights by making errors and correcting them through backpropagation. The idea began in 1943 with a paper by **McCulloch and Pitts**, which proposed that simple mathematics could model how the brain works. >> The paper starts from the **all-or-none** character of nervous activity, where all means active and none means inactive, so a neuron is either firing or not. >> That is the same idea as the sigmoid, so the very first paper on artificial neural networks starts from biology about how real neurons behave. >> Warren McCulloch was a psychiatrist, and the earliest work on neural networks was about modeling how the mind and mental illness work, not about making money. || By 1986, the ideas had matured into the two **Parallel Distributed Processing** books, written by a group at UC San Diego. **Parallel** means many neurons compute their activations at the same time, and **distributed** means the information from F1 and F2 is spread across all the neurons. >> By then forward propagation and backpropagation were well formed, and one contributor, Geoffrey Hinton, later won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work. || **Given some static vector of formants, these networks could make a decision about a few possible categories.** A vector is just a list of numbers, and static means one fixed list from one moment. But **human cognition is far more complex** than taking in one list of numbers and giving one decision at the end. >> Speech is incredibly time-varying, because every little moment in time gives a new formant. >> Language is time-varying too, since a sentence is a whole bunch of words, one after another. >> Giving a network one bunch of numbers can never model cognition that unfolds in time.
3|In 1985, **Michael Jordan** developed **recurrent neural networks** to make sense of **speech production**, meaning how the tongue, velum and lips move over time. He realized that a regular neural network could never handle something that complex and time-varying, so he designed a new kind of network, and that line of work became large language models and then agentic AI. The technology at the center of modern AI was born from thinking about speech. >> His network is not just sequential, one layer after another, because it has two layers working in parallel. >> Plan units say what sequence to produce, hidden units combine information, output units give the response, and the new state units hold what the network has just produced. || **The network is called recurrent because it takes its output and sends it back into part of its input.** It produces something at one moment, that output loops back into the state units, and the network uses it to predict the next moment, and then the moment after that. >> In the measured performance on a sequence of speech sounds, the rounding feature rises early and stays high across many sounds, because outputs fed back from one moment carry into the next. >> GPT works the same way one word at a time. It predicts the next word, feeds the prompt plus that word back in, predicts the next word again, and keeps going. || Science and technology evolve from other people's ideas over long stretches of time. An idea from 1943 was refined into the networks of 1986, and Jordan's idea from 1985 was refined into the large language models of 2017. >> The people who came up with these ideas were mostly not the ones who made a lot of money from them. >> These years give a sense of the history, but they will not be asked about.
4|AI systems build up in steps, and **each step extends the one before it**. **A single neuron takes in a list of numbers and gives back one number.** It multiplies each input by its weight and adds them all up, which is the inner product, and the symbol sigma just means add them up. >> This uses nothing but second-grade multiplication and addition to model the electrochemical activity of a real neuron, and a profound idea can start out that simple. || **A neural network takes in one list of numbers and gives back a whole list of decisions.** Its layers are linked by sets of weights, and each set turns one list of numbers into the next through many inner products, until the last layer gives one value for each possible answer. >> The vowel network is this kind of network, and it still takes only one list of numbers. || **A large language model takes in many lists of numbers, one for each moment in a sequence, and gives back one decision about what comes next.** Here it takes 15 vectors, each standing for something at one moment in time, such as one word of a sentence, and its job is to predict the sixteenth. >> In real systems the vectors number in the hundreds of thousands to a million, and that set is called the **context window**. Each vector is also thousands of numbers long, which is why huge data centers are needed. >> The sixteenth vector is a probability distribution over the next word, giving the likelihood that each word comes next. || **A large language model is made of neural networks**, since each block of weights is a layer, and real systems have hundreds of layers. The knowledge is still in the weights, so an LLM's knowledge of how to write an essay is stored in its weights. >> An LLM is also a **dynamical system**, which is why the Nobel Prize for AI was awarded in physics. A mass-spring system starts from initial conditions and the system decides what happens next, and an LLM's initial condition is its set of input vectors, which each layer turns into better vectors until the last layer can predict the next word. >> The calculation at every step is the inner product, multiplying and adding billions or trillions of times. || **An LLM learns by predicting the next word and correcting its mistakes.** Given the cat sat on the, with mat as the real next word, a new LLM with random weights predicts something silly like in, and backpropagation adjusts its trillions of weights so the prediction moves toward mat. >> Repeating this with about a trillion sentences, the system surprisingly ends up knowing how to speak English. >> The first problem LLMs solved was machine translation, and the same design then worked for speech and many other problems. || **An agentic system is a graph of LLMs**, and an agent in AI today is just an LLM. One LLM outlines the answer, another writes it out in smooth sentences, a **critic** LLM criticizes the draft, a revision LLM sends it back to the critic if it is not good enough, and a final LLM produces the output. >> The critic is what fixed **hallucination**, the system making things up, and the back-and-forth is why many systems take a while to respond. >> Neurons make neural networks, neural networks make LLMs, and LLMs make agentic systems, much as molecules make cells and cells make organs. >> Designing a graph of LLMs to solve a problem is a real job, but the connections can also form circuits nobody planned that let the system do things that were never part of the design. || **The chief insight behind all of this is Gestalt completion.** People see a triangle in the Gestalt figure even though none is drawn, and training a system only to predict the missing thing turns out to emulate human cognition. >> Underneath everything, **inference is inner products**, and **learning is SGD plus dynamic programming**.
5|**An LLM takes 15 vectors and predicts the sixteenth**, and since a vector is just a list of numbers, those numbers can stand for almost anything. Whatever they stand for, the machine plays the same game of predicting the next one. || The vectors can be **words**. In the cat that the dog chased ran up the old tree and sat on the blank, the sixteenth vector is the missing next word, and a speaker of English knows a likely completion, such as branch or mat. >> Getting it right means using the whole sentence, since it was the cat, not the dog, that sat. || The vectors can be **slices of sound**. A spectrogram is cut into little frames, each frame becomes one vector of frequencies and amplitudes, and the system learns to predict the next little piece of speech from all the previous pieces. >> This gave the best speech recognition in 75 years, and it is also the basis for synthetic voices that are now very hard to tell from humans. >> The newest systems can hold a spoken conversation, and the only giveaway left is a slight delay before each response, so as that gap shrinks, scams become a real danger. || The vectors can be **pieces of a picture**. A picture is cut into 16 patches read row by row, and the system predicts the missing patch, which across huge numbers of pictures teaches it what a dog, a dolphin or a boat is. >> This became the basis of modern computer vision. || The vectors can even be **amino acids**. A protein is a string of amino acids, which come in 20 kinds, and treating a protein as just a sequence and playing the same predict-the-next game pushed the problem of how proteins fold many years ahead. >> That work won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. || **The LLM only ever sees vectors.** Words, sounds, pixels and molecules all reach it the same way, so one machine asks one question, what comes sixteenth, using the same architecture every time. >> Because these systems predict from past data, something new that the past data does not cover can make them hallucinate.
6|English allows only **certain sequences of sounds** in its words, and knowing which sequences are allowed is called **phonotactic knowledge**. **A simple syllable in English can have a consonant, then a vowel, then a consonant**, as in Don, mat, sit, cup, dog and fish. >> The pattern is about sounds, not letters, so the silent e in shave, kite and rope does not count, and two letters can spell one consonant sound, as in ship, chin and bath. || Syllables can also have **clusters**, groups of consonants together. A cluster before the vowel is an **onset**, as in split, flat and black, and a cluster after the vowel is a **coda**, as in desk, help and lamp. >> Each real word has an impossible twin marked with a star, and many twins use the same letters in the other order, such as flat and lfat, help and hepl, and lamp and lapm, so order matters. >> **The order mirrors around the vowel.** In flat the l sits right next to the vowel with the f farther out, and in help the l again sits next to the vowel, now on the other side, with the p farther out. Each impossible twin puts the wrong consonant against the vowel. || **A long word is a chain of syllables**, such as ba-na-na, At-lan-tic and ath-lete. The sounds t and l are fine across a syllable break, but tl cannot start a syllable, which is why Atlantic is a word but tlantic is impossible. >> The sound ng can end a syllable, as in ring, but it can never start one. >> So whether a sequence of sounds is allowed depends on where the syllable breaks fall.
7|**Phonotactic knowledge can be revealed through GPT learning.** Each item is the start of an English word, and the task is to guess the next letter. People agree on their guesses quickly, and how much they agree changes from item to item. || **After a vowel, almost anything can follow.** After ra, likely letters include t, n, r and l, and many others are possible too, because once a vowel has come the onset is over. >> **At the start of a word, the choices narrow sharply** to exactly what an English onset allows. After sp the next letter is l, r or a vowel and never n, and after k it is a vowel or n, as in knee. >> After b and f it is r, l or a vowel, after sh it is a vowel or r, as in shrimp, and after skr or m at the start of a word only a vowel can come. >> After bram, the letter b is almost forced, as in bramble, though a new syllable could also start there. || **Guessing the next letter is predicting the sixteenth item**, the same next-token game an LLM plays. A GPT trained only on this game learns English sounds and spelling, because its predictions can only get good by matching the patterns of English syllables. >> Its prediction is a probability distribution over the next letter, and the shape of that distribution carries the knowledge. >> After fa the distribution is flat, spread over many letters, while after skr it is sharply peaked on a few vowels, because English allows nothing else there. >> The more the letters so far restrict what can come next, the more the probability piles onto a few letters.
