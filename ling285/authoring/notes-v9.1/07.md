## p2
- **A harmonic oscillator is a dynamical system with two variables that feed into each other, position, written x, and velocity, written v.** Position tells where something is, and velocity tells how fast and in which direction it is moving. The system is fully described by two rules of change, one for each variable, and each rule depends on the other variable.
  - The dot above a letter is the notation for change over time. So ẋ is the **increase or decrease in x with time**, the rate at which position is changing at a given moment, and v̇ is the rate at which velocity is changing.
- **The first rule says that the rate of change of position equals the velocity, so ẋ is v.** In other words, velocity drives position. When v is positive, x grows, and when v is negative, x shrinks. When v is exactly zero, x holds still for that instant. In the loop diagram this is the red arrow from v to x, which marks v as the cause and x as the thing being changed.
- **The second rule says that the rate of change of velocity equals the negative of the position, so v̇ is minus x.** Position drives velocity, but with a minus sign. When x is positive, velocity is pushed downward, and when x is negative, velocity is pushed upward. This is the blue arrow from x to v. Together the red and blue arrows form a **closed loop of mutual influence**. The value v changes x, and x changes v, over and over.
- **Stepping through the loop by hand from x at 1 and v at 0 works out what it does.** At the very start, position is at its highest value and nothing is moving yet.
  - Since x is 1, the second rule gives v̇ of negative 1, so velocity starts decreasing and becomes negative. Once v is negative, the first rule says ẋ is negative, so position begins to fall, slowly at first and then faster and faster as v grows more negative.
  - Position keeps falling until it crosses zero. At the moment x is 0, the second rule gives v̇ of 0, so velocity stops changing. This is the moment when velocity is at its most negative, which means position is falling at its fastest right as it passes through zero.
  - After the crossing, x is negative, so v̇ is now positive. Velocity starts climbing back toward zero, which means position is still falling but more slowly. When velocity reaches zero, position stops falling at its lowest point, negative 1. From there velocity turns positive, position starts to rise, and the whole sequence runs in reverse until x is back at plus 1 with v at 0.
- **Plotting both variables against time point by point gives two waves.** Position starts at its peak of plus 1, drops through zero, bottoms out at negative 1, and comes back up to its peak. Velocity starts at zero, dips negative, returns to zero, rises positive, and comes back to zero.
  - The two waves are offset by a quarter of a cycle. **Velocity is zero whenever position is at a peak or a trough**, and **velocity is at its largest size whenever position is crossing zero**. This offset is a direct consequence of the two rules. Position turns around only when velocity reaches zero, and velocity turns around only when position reaches zero.

## p3
- **An oscillation, also called a vibration, is a repeating back-and-forth motion around a central point.** The two-variable loop between x and v is the basic dynamical system that produces it. Each variable pushes on the other, and the result is that neither one ever settles down.
  - When the loop is drawn with one arrow from x to v and another from v back to x, the influence is **circular**. There is no single cause at the start and no single effect at the end.
  - Graphed smoothly over time, x traces a wave that starts high, swings down below zero, swings back up, and continues through repeated cycles. The value v traces a wave of the same shape that starts at zero and heads negative first, staying a quarter cycle out of step with x.
- **The key property of this system is that it never comes to rest on its own.** In the systems from the first dynamical-systems material, variables moved toward a resting value and stopped there. Here, every time one variable reaches its target, the other variable is at its largest value and pushes it past the target again.
  - Position overshoots zero because velocity is at its maximum at the moment position reaches zero. Velocity overshoots zero because position is at its maximum at the moment velocity reaches zero. That is why the motion keeps repeating.

## p4
- **Physically, vibration requires two effects working together.** Neither one alone can produce a back-and-forth motion.
- **The first effect is a spring, more precisely a viscous spring.** A spring is the springiness found in many objects, their tendency to return to a natural shape or position after being pushed or pulled. The word "viscous" refers to a frictional, slowing quality, and it is set aside here to focus on the springiness itself.
  - A spring has a property called **stiffness**. Stiffness measures how strongly the spring pulls back toward its natural position when it has been moved away from it. A very stiff spring pulls back hard, and a loose spring pulls back gently.
- **The second effect is mass.** Mass is the amount of matter in an object, and it determines how the object carries motion once it is moving.
- **Vibration matters because it shows up across many different systems, both physical and abstract.**
  - **The jolt we feel when a car hits a bump** is a vibration, produced by the car's suspension springs and the car's mass together. **Music** is vibration, since strings, air columns and drum skins all vibrate to produce sound. **Boom and bust in economics** is a vibration, with an economy swinging above and below a steady level in repeating cycles.
  - **Oscillation in SGD** is a vibration. SGD stands for **stochastic gradient descent**, a method used to train machine-learning models by repeatedly nudging a value toward a target. Under some settings, that value swings back and forth around the target rather than settling onto it.
  - **The motion of the air in the vocal tract and the motion of the vocal folds during speech** are vibrations. Every sound produced in talking is built from these vibrations.

## p5
- **The air inside the vocal tract can be treated as a small slice of material made of many particles.** During speech, the particles in the slice stay the same, and each particle stays the same size. **What changes is the distance between the particles.**
  - At **equilibrium spacing**, the particles sit at their natural, resting distance from one another. A chosen pair of neighboring particles is at 100 percent **of its equilibrium distance**. The equilibrium size of the slice is the reference that all other states are compared with.
- **Compression is the state in which the particles are pushed closer together than their resting distance.** In a compressed slice, the chosen pair sits at only 56 percent of its equilibrium distance, and the whole slice shrinks. **High pressure means air that is packed very close together**, so compression is a high-pressure state.
- **Rarefaction is the state in which the particles are spread farther apart than their resting distance.** In a rarefied slice, the chosen pair sits at 144 percent of its equilibrium distance, and the whole slice expands. **Low pressure means air that is spread very far apart**, so rarefaction is a low-pressure state.
- **During speech, a slice of air in the vocal tract alternates between compression and rarefaction, shrinking and expanding around its equilibrium size again and again.** This alternation is a vibration of the air. The sizes in this model are idealized and exaggerated so that the change in spacing is visible. Real air in the vocal tract changes its spacing by much smaller amounts.

## p6
- **The spring effect on its own can be written as a single rule of change, ẋ equals minus one third of x.** Here x is the distance of the spring from its natural position, and ẋ is how fast that distance is changing. The rate of change is always **one third of the current distance, with the opposite sign**.
  - **If x is positive, ẋ is negative, so x falls.** A spring stretched in the positive direction moves back toward zero. **If x is negative, ẋ is positive, so x rises.** A spring compressed in the negative direction also moves back toward zero, just from the other side.
- **The rule can be followed step by step from an initial condition of x equal to 1.** The initial condition is the value the system starts from before any change has happened.
  - With x at 1, x will **fall by one third of its current size**. One third of 1 is one third, so x drops to two thirds. One third of two thirds is two ninths, so x drops to four ninths. One third of four ninths is four twenty-sevenths, so x drops to eight twenty-sevenths.
  - The process repeats **again and again**, and each drop is smaller than the one before it. The first drop was one third, the second was two ninths, and the third was four twenty-sevenths.
- **When x is smaller, ẋ, its speed of falling, is smaller too.** The spring moves quickly when it is far from its natural position and slows down as it gets closer.
  - Graphed against time, x starts at 1 and curves downward toward zero. The slope is steeply negative at the start and becomes **smaller and smaller negative** as time goes on. The curve gets closer and closer to zero but **never crosses it**, since the speed shrinks to nothing as the distance shrinks to nothing.
  - If the initial condition is negative, the same logic runs the other way. Starting below zero, x rises toward zero, quickly at first and then more slowly, with a slope that is **smaller and smaller positive**, and again never crosses it.
- **This kind of system has a natural resting condition, which here is x at 0.** When the system's position is at a **discrepancy** with its natural position, meaning it has been moved away from where it rests, it **feeds back on itself** through the rule to cancel that discrepancy. This is **negative feedback**, also called **balancing feedback**, from Meadows' work on systems.
  - The feedback is called negative because the change always opposes the discrepancy. A positive discrepancy produces a negative change, and a negative discrepancy produces a positive change. It is called balancing because its effect is to bring the system back into balance at its resting point.
  - **These are systems that are in motion only in order to achieve their equilibrium.** An **equilibrium** is a state where the system has no reason to change any further. A spring moves only because it is away from its natural position, and once it arrives there, all motion stops.
- **The number minus one third in the rule is the stiffness, and its size controls how quickly the spring returns to equilibrium.** A **higher stiffness**, a larger fraction than one third, means the spring closes a bigger share of the remaining distance at every step, so it returns to zero faster. A **lower stiffness** means it closes a smaller share at every step, so it returns more slowly.

## p7
- **Mass behaves very differently from a spring. If a mass is moving, it just keeps on moving.** A mass does not need a discrepancy or a target to keep going, and it has no natural resting position that it is trying to reach.
- **This principle was worked out by Galileo, Descartes and Newton. A mass in motion stays in motion.** Without an outside force acting on it, a moving object continues at the same speed in the same direction.
  - Motion of this kind is called **constant velocity**. An object with constant velocity covers **the same distance in every equal interval of time**. A car rolling along a straight street at constant velocity passes the same amount of scenery in every moment. It does not speed up, slow down or stop on its own.
- **This property is exactly what the spring lacks.** A spring slows down as it nears its resting point and stops there, while a mass carries its motion forward regardless of where it is.

## p8
- **A spring stops at a place, while a mass stops after a process.** The spring's stopping point is a location, its natural position. A mass does not stop at a location at all. It keeps moving until something removes its motion over time.
- **Consider a spring attached to a wall at one end, lying on a table, with no mass on its free end.** Its displacement x is **measured from the spring's natural position**, set as x equal to 0, and the scale runs from negative 1 to plus 1.
  - When the free end is pulled out to plus 1 and released, the spring **returns and stops at zero**. The end approaches zero **without crossing it**. Graphed over time, this return is a curve that starts at plus 1 and bends down toward zero, flattening as it gets close, the same shape as the curve produced by the one-third rule.
  - When the free end is instead pushed to negative 1 and released, **x rises toward zero without crossing it**. Pulling the spring out again gives the exact same return each time. A spring alone always settles at its natural position and never overshoots.
- **When a mass is attached to the free end of the same spring and the system is released from plus 1, the result is completely different.** The spring still pulls the mass back toward zero, but when the mass reaches zero it is moving at its fastest, and **the mass carries that motion through the crossing**.
  - The mass continues past zero into negative displacement. Now the spring is stretched the other way, so it pulls back toward zero from the negative side. The mass slows down, stops near negative 1, and is pulled back through zero toward the positive side again. This repeats through **every crossing**.
- **Graphed over time, the spring-plus-mass system produces a lightly damped oscillation.** The curve swings from plus 1 down below zero, back up above zero, and continues through repeated cycles.
  - **Damping** is the gradual loss of motion over time, which comes from the viscous, frictional part of the spring. In a lightly damped system the damping is small, so each swing is only a little smaller than the one before and the oscillation continues for many cycles. Eventually the damping would bring the mass to rest at zero, which is what it means for a mass to stop after a process.
- **Putting the two effects together recovers the harmonic oscillator.** The spring supplies the pull back toward the natural position, the rule v̇ equals minus x, since the farther the mass is from zero, the harder its velocity is pushed back. The mass supplies persistent motion, the rule ẋ equals v, since position keeps changing as long as there is velocity, including at the moment the mass passes through zero.
  - A spring alone gives balancing feedback that ends at equilibrium. A mass alone gives motion that never turns around. **A spring and a mass together give vibration**, since the spring keeps turning the motion around and the mass keeps carrying it past the resting point.

## p9
- **The vibration of a slice of air in the vocal tract can be described with a single mathematical function, the cosine.** The value x measures the **normalized expansion** of the slice, how far the particles are spread relative to their equilibrium spacing, scaled so that the extremes are plus 1 and minus 1. So x equals the cosine of the phase.
  - An x of plus 1 means the particles are **farthest apart**, **maximum rarefaction**. An x of 0 means the particles are at their **equilibrium** spacing. An x of minus 1 means the particles are **closest together**, **maximum compression**.
- **The phase is the input to the cosine, and it tracks where the slice is within its cycle of motion.** One full cycle of the cosine takes a phase of 2π, so a phase running from 0 to 6π covers exactly **three cycles**.
  - At a phase of 0 the cosine equals plus 1, so the slice starts at maximum rarefaction. At a phase of π the cosine equals minus 1, so the slice has reached maximum compression halfway through the first cycle. At a phase of 2π the cosine is back at plus 1, completing Cycle 1. The same pattern repeats for Cycle 2, ending at 4π, and Cycle 3, ending at 6π.
  - The slice therefore moves from **maximum rarefaction to compression and back to rarefaction** in every cycle, following the cosine curve exactly. Partway through a swing it is in an in-between state. At plus 0.84 the particles are still rarefied but not at their maximum spread, and at minus 0.84 they are compressed but not at their tightest.
- **This cosine is the same wave that position traced in the harmonic oscillator**, starting at its peak and swinging down through zero to its lowest point and back. The air in the vocal tract behaves as a harmonic oscillator, with the springiness of the air pushing it back toward equilibrium spacing and the mass of the air carrying it past equilibrium each time.
  - The sizes of the slice in this model are exaggerated and its motion is slowed, so that each stage of the cycle is visible. The dashed boundary marks the slice's equilibrium size.

## p10
- **Different vowels are made by placing the tongue in different positions inside the vocal tract.** Side-view MRI images of the head during five words show how the tongue body moves to create each vowel. The vowels are organized along two dimensions, **height**, how high or low the tongue body sits in the mouth, and **front or back**, how far forward or backward it sits.
- **High vowels are made with the tongue body raised close to the roof of the mouth.** In **"dean,"** the vowel is **high and front**. The tongue body is pushed up and forward toward the hard palate, leaving a wide open space in the throat behind the tongue. In **"dune,"** the vowel is **high and back**. The tongue body is raised but bunched toward the back of the mouth, closer to the soft palate.
- **Low vowels are made with the tongue body lowered away from the roof of the mouth.** In **"Dan,"** the vowel is **low and front**. The tongue sits lower while staying toward the front, leaving a large open space above it. In **"Don,"** the vowel is **low and back**. The tongue is lowered and pulled back toward the throat, which narrows the space behind the tongue.
  - In **"done,"** the vowel is **central**. The tongue body sits in a middle position, neither pushed far forward nor pulled far back.
- **Each tongue position gives the air inside the vocal tract a different shape.** A different shape changes how the air vibrates, and those differences in vibration are what make one vowel sound different from another.

## p11
- **The frequency of a vibration is how many complete cycles it goes through in a given amount of time.** Three vibrations can be compared over the same 12-second interval, each covering the same range of particle spacing and each starting at **maximum rarefaction**, where x is plus 1. As before, plus 1 is maximum rarefaction, 0 is equilibrium, and minus 1 is maximum compression.
- **The low-frequency vibration completes 3 cycles in 12 seconds.** Each cycle takes 4 seconds, so its peaks fall at 0, 4, 8 and 12 seconds, and its frequency is 3 cycles in 12 seconds, or 0.25 cycles per second.
- **The mid-frequency vibration completes 6 cycles in 12 seconds.** Each cycle takes 2 seconds, so the slice swings between rarefaction and compression twice as often as the low-frequency one, at 0.5 cycles per second.
- **The high-frequency vibration completes 9 cycles in 12 seconds.** Each cycle takes 12 ninths of a second, about 1.33 seconds, so this slice swings the most rapidly of the three, at 0.75 cycles per second.
- **All three vibrations reach the same extremes of rarefaction and compression. The only thing that differs among them is how quickly the cycle repeats.** Frequency is a separate property from how far the particles spread or squeeze, and a vibration can be made faster or slower without changing its range.
  - One cycle per second is called **one hertz**, written **Hz**. This unit is used for the frequencies in the vowel models.

## p12
- **A vowel is not a single cosine. A vowel can be modeled as a sum of vibrations at different frequencies happening at the same time in the same slice of air.** The simplest model uses two frequencies of equal size added together, and the two frequencies chosen give each vowel its own pattern of motion.
  - The frequencies in these models are small example values, chosen so the combined wave is easy to follow. They are not the actual frequencies found in speech.
  - A cosine at a frequency of f hertz is written as the cosine of 2π times f times t, where t is time in seconds. Since one cycle takes a phase of 2π, multiplying 2π by f gives f full cycles for every second that passes. A 1 Hz vibration is the cosine of 2πt, which completes one cycle at 1 second. A 4 Hz vibration is the cosine of 8πt, which completes four cycles at 1 second.
- **The vowel /i/, the vowel in "dean," is modeled as a 1 Hz vibration plus a 4 Hz vibration, the cosine of 2πt plus the cosine of 8πt.** At time 0 both cosines equal plus 1, so the sum is 2. The combined wave starts at plus 2, **maximum rarefaction** for the combined motion, and the range of x is now from plus 2 to minus 2, since two waves of size 1 are stacked together.
  - At 0.5 seconds, the 1 Hz wave is halfway through its cycle and the 4 Hz wave has completed exactly two cycles, so the sum is the cosine of π plus the cosine of 4π, which is minus 1 plus 1, or 0.
  - Between its large peaks, the /i/ wave has a busy pattern. It rises to smaller peaks near plus 1, dips to troughs near minus 1.7, and touches zero at the halfway point. The fast 4 Hz wave rides on top of the slow 1 Hz wave, putting several smaller wiggles inside each slow cycle. The whole pattern repeats **once every second**, returning to plus 2 at 1, 2 and 3 seconds.
- **The vowel /a/, close to the vowel in "Don," is modeled as a 2 Hz vibration plus a 3 Hz vibration, the cosine of 4πt plus the cosine of 6πt.** At time 0 both cosines equal plus 1, so /a/ also starts at plus 2, maximum rarefaction.
  - At 0.5 seconds, the 2 Hz wave has completed exactly one cycle and the 3 Hz wave one and a half cycles, so the sum is the cosine of 2π plus the cosine of 3π, which is 1 plus negative 1, or 0.
  - The /a/ wave has a different shape from /i/. Between each large peak at plus 2, it drops to a deep trough near minus 1.6, rises to a pair of small humps near plus 0.7 separated by a dip to zero, drops to another deep trough, and returns to plus 2. This pattern also repeats **once every second**.
- **In both vowels, positive x means rarefaction and negative x means compression.** Both vowels start at the same value and repeat at the same rate, yet the air moves in a clearly different way within each second. **The combination of frequencies is what distinguishes one vowel from another.** The motion in these models is played back at one quarter of its speed so that the particle spacing can be followed.

## p13
- **Perturbation theory is the study of how a system's behavior changes when one of its parameters is nudged up or down.** A **parameter** is a fixed setting of a system, such as how heavy the mass is or how stiff the spring is. For a mass-spring system, the behavior of interest is its **frequency**, how many oscillations it completes per second.
- **If the mass m of a mass-spring system is increased, the frequency decreases.** A heavier mass carries its motion more strongly, so the spring takes longer to slow it down and turn it around at each end of its swing. Each cycle takes longer, and fewer cycles fit into each second. **More mass means a lower frequency.**
- **If the stiffness k of the spring is increased, the frequency increases.** A stiffer spring pulls back harder on the mass for the same displacement, so it turns the mass around more quickly at each end of its swing. Each cycle takes less time, and more cycles fit into each second. **More stiffness means a higher frequency.**
- **These two results are the core perturbation-theory relationships for a mass-spring system.** Increasing mass lowers frequency, and increasing stiffness raises frequency. They give a way to predict how a vibrating system will sound or behave just from knowing whether it is heavy or light and stiff or loose.

## p14
- **The mass-spring relationships explain how the ear separates sounds by frequency.** This explanation comes from the work of **Georg von Békésy**, who received the **Nobel Prize in 1961** for discovering how the inner ear responds to sound.
- **Inside the inner ear is a long, tapered membrane called the basilar membrane.** It is wide at one end and narrow at the other, and its physical properties change gradually along its length.
  - At the **wide end**, the membrane has **large mass** and **small stiffness**. It is heavy and loose. At the **narrow end**, it has **small mass** and **large stiffness**. It is light and stiff. Moving from the wide end to the narrow end, mass steadily decreases and stiffness steadily increases.
- **Applying perturbation theory to each location on the membrane gives each location its own preferred frequency.** At the wide end, large mass lowers the frequency and small stiffness also lowers it, so this end vibrates most strongly for **low-frequency** sounds. At the narrow end, small mass raises the frequency and large stiffness also raises it, so this end vibrates most strongly for **high-frequency** sounds.
  - Every point in between has an intermediate mass and stiffness, so every point is tuned to an intermediate frequency. The membrane is effectively a long row of mass-spring systems, each set to a slightly different frequency.
- **Nerve fibers leave the membrane at many locations along its length.** When a particular spot vibrates, the fibers at that spot become active, and which fibers are active tells the brain which frequencies are present in the sound.
  - When a vowel made of two combined frequencies reaches the ear, such as the 1 Hz and 4 Hz combination for /i/, two different places along the membrane vibrate, one for each frequency. The membrane separates the combined vibration back into its component frequencies. This is how the ear can tell /i/ from /a/, since the two vowels are built from different frequency combinations and set different places on the membrane into vibration.

## p15
- **In machine learning, a model is trained by repeatedly updating a value to move it toward a target.** The **learning rate**, written γ, sets how large each update is, as a fraction of the remaining distance to the target. Each full round of updates through the training data is an **epoch**.
- **The effect of γ can be seen by training three runs that start near 130, 80 and 10 and all head toward a target of about 80, tracked over 300 epochs at four learning rates.**
  - At a **learning rate of 1 percent**, the runs move smoothly and slowly. The run starting at 130 is still at about 82 after 300 epochs, and the run starting at 10 has only reached about 76. The motion is very steady, but the runs have not fully reached the target.
  - At a **learning rate of 10 percent**, the runs reach the neighborhood of 80 within roughly the first 30 to 50 epochs and then stay close to it with small wiggles of a few units.
  - At a **learning rate of 50 percent**, the runs jump to the region of 80 within a few epochs and then bounce noisily, roughly between 70 and 95. At **80 percent**, they arrive almost immediately but bounce even more widely, roughly between 68 and 97, for all 300 epochs.
- **These results are the fundamental learning tradeoff.** **When γ increases, speed increases and accuracy decreases.** Large updates get the value to the target region quickly, but the same large updates keep throwing it past the target, so it oscillates around the answer instead of settling. **When γ decreases, speed decreases and accuracy increases.** Small updates keep the value on a smooth, steady path but take many more epochs to arrive.
  - This is the **oscillation in SGD** that appears among the everyday examples of vibration. A high learning rate turns training into a back-and-forth swinging motion around the target.
- **The tradeoff is unfortunate, since in practice both fast learning and accurate learning are wanted at the same time.** **Thousands of people, over generations, have made their living by finding better ways to optimize**, meaning better training methods that raise both speed and accuracy together. The goal is a method where **speed increases** and **accuracy increases** at once, escaping the tradeoff that a single fixed learning rate forces.
