## p2
- Every vibrating system in speech follows a small set of principles, and each one connects to the others.
  - **Raising the mass lowers the frequency, and raising the stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - **A system with n masses has n modes**, and each higher mode has a higher frequency, because more parts go against each other and more springs stretch and compress.
  - **An increase in mass lowers frequency in proportion to how much that mass moves**, and **an increase in stiffness raises frequency in proportion to how much that spring stretches and compresses.** Both depend on where the change is made.
  - **Where there is maximal movement there is no stretching, and where there is maximal stretching there is no movement.**
  - In a **fixed-fixed system**, the frequencies are called **harmonics** and are **whole-number multiples** of the base frequency, and in a **fixed-free system** they are called **formants** and are **odd multiples**.
- If the mass and the stiffness of a system are both raised, it might seem the two effects simply **cancel out**. That is only true for a **single mass on a single spring**. In a system with many masses and springs, a spot that is moving a lot is hardly stretching, and a spot that is stretching a lot is hardly moving, so only one of the two changes matters at that spot.
  - Picture a spot on a string that never moves but is stretched hard. Extra mass there does nothing, while extra stiffness raises the frequency, so the frequency goes up.
  - Picture a spot that swings up and down the most. Extra stiffness there does nothing, while extra mass slows it down, so the frequency goes down.

## p3
- Adding masses adds modes, and the same shapes keep repeating. **Mode 1** always has everything moving together, **Mode 2** always splits the system into two halves going against each other, and **Mode 3** always has the middle going against the sides.
  - The more the parts go against each other, the more the springs work, so **higher modes are stiffer and faster**. In plain terms, a higher mode is not made of stiffer springs. It just makes the same springs do more stretching.

## p4
- In a string fixed at both ends, the first mode is **one half-wave**, a single bump that goes from no motion at one end, to the most motion in the middle, to no motion at the other end. Each higher mode packs **more of these half-wave packets** into the same length.
  - **Mode 2 holds two packets**, with one half going up while the other half goes down. Each packet behaves like a copy of Mode 1, so Mode 2 has **twice the stiffness** and **twice the frequency**.
  - **Mode 3 holds three packets**, and **Mode 5 holds five**, so the fifth mode has five times the frequency of the first.
  - The short version for an exam answer is that **the fifth mode has the first mode fitted into it five times**, so it has five times the stiffness and five times the frequency.
- The numbers do not have to start at 100. A voice whose base frequency is **250 hertz** has its next harmonics at 500, 750 and 1000 hertz, because each one is still a whole number of copies of the first.

## p5
- A string fixed at both ends vibrates in all of these modes at once, and its **spectrum**, with **frequency** along the bottom and **amplitude** going up, has **evenly spaced spikes**, one for each harmonic. If the first harmonic is 100 hertz, the next ones are 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz.
  - The **vocal folds** are fixed on all sides, just behind the Adam's apple, so they behave like this kind of fixed-fixed system and produce harmonics.

## p6
- The vocal folds form a **membrane**, and the same packet counting works on a surface. The first mode is a single bump across the whole membrane, the second mode squeezes two of those bumps in, so it has twice the frequency, and the fourth mode squeezes in four ups and downs, so it has four times the frequency.

## p7
- A **building** is **fixed at the bottom** and **free at the top**. In high winds or an earthquake, engineers want it to sway only in **Mode 1**, all together, which is mildly unpleasant on a high floor but safe. A higher mode would send some floors one way and other floors the other way, and the whole structure could shatter.
- A string can be set up the same way, fixed at the bottom and free at the top. Its first mode is not a full half-wave anymore. It goes from **no motion at the fixed end** to **lots of motion at the free end**, which is only **one quarter of a wave**.

## p8
- In a fixed-free system, each mode is built from **quarter-wave packets**, and each packet runs from no motion to lots of motion. **Mode 1 holds one packet**, **Mode 2 holds three**, **Mode 3 holds five** and **Mode 4 holds seven**, so with a base of 100 hertz the modes come at 100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz.
- **Two packets can never fit.** Stacking two quarter-wave packets gives no motion, then lots of motion, then no motion again, which puts a still point at the top as well as the bottom and makes the system **fixed at both ends**. Adding one more packet, for **three** in total, ends with lots of motion at the top again, so that is the next possible mode.
  - The simple version is that the free end must always be moving, and only an odd number of quarter-waves lands on a moving end.
- The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called **formants**. **A formant is a frequency of vibration of a system that is fixed on one side and completely free on the other**, and formants are **odd multiples** of the lowest one, written **F1**, **F2** and **F3**.

## p9
- Counting quarter-wave packets gives every formant from the first. If **F1 is 500 hertz**, then **F2 is three times that, 1500 hertz**, and **F3 is five times that, 2500 hertz**.
  - Each step between formants is twice the first formant, because each new mode adds two more quarter-wave packets.

## p10
- The air inside the mouth and throat forms a tube. At one end are the **vocal folds**, and because they are closed, the air there **cannot go back and forth**, so that end is **fixed**. At the other end the **lips are open**, so the air can go back and forth freely, and that end is **free**.
  - So **the motion of air in the vocal tract is like a building**, and the math describing them is almost exactly the same, and its frequencies are **formants**.
  - Speaking changes the stiffness and mass of the air in different parts of the tract, which moves the formants, and that is how listeners hear different vowels and consonants.

## p11
- The air in the tube can be modeled as lines of particles, where each particle stands for billions of air molecules.
  - Where the lines come **really close together and then really far apart**, the air is compressing and stretching, so it is acting like a **spring**.
  - Where the lines just **move back and forth** without changing their distance, the air is acting like a **mass**, which is easiest to see at the **free end at the lips**.
  - An easy way to read it is that bunching and spreading means springiness, and sliding without bunching means mass.
- The air's modes match a building's modes. In **Mode 1**, the only point of no motion is the fixed end, in **Mode 2** there are **two**, and in **Mode 3** there are **three**, and around each of them there is lots of compression and expansion.
- In **Mode 4** of a building, **the most springiness is at the fixed bottom and at the three points where the building's line crosses its resting position**, four places in all, because those points do not move.
  - Raising the stiffness at a place of maximal movement changes **nothing**, because the structure is not acting like a spring there, while raising it at a place of no movement makes the frequency **shoot up**.

## p12
- A **constriction** is a place where the tongue or lips squeeze the air, measured from the **glottis**, the tiny space between the vocal folds at the fixed end.
  - In **done**, the central vowel, there is **no constriction**.
  - In **dean**, a **front high vowel**, the air is squeezed under the roof of the mouth, about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **Don**, a **low back vowel**, the air is squeezed far back, about **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **dune**, a **back high vowel**, the lips are rounded close together, which squeezes the air **at the lips**, and **Dan** is a **front low vowel**.

## p13
- **Compressing air raises its stiffness**, just as squeezing a tire makes it stiffer, so a constriction at a place that experiences lots of compression **raises frequency**. **Bunching up air raises its mass**, because it raises the air's **density**, so a constriction at a place where the air moves a lot **lowers frequency**.
- So every constriction **increases both mass and stiffness**, but that does not mean constrictions have no effect on frequency. **The parts of a vibrating medium that move the most alternate with the parts that compress and expand the most.**
  - A useful way to answer any constriction question is to ask two things in order. First, is the air at this spot mostly moving or mostly compressing in this mode? Second, which change counts there, the extra mass or the extra stiffness?
- The same two-step reasoning answers a question about a string fixed at both ends. Take a small spot where **Mode 3 crosses its middle line** while **Mode 4 is at the top of a bump**, and raise both the mass and the stiffness of that spot.
  - In **Mode 3**, the string does not move at that spot but is stretched the most, so only the extra stiffness counts, and **Mode 3's frequency goes up**.
  - In **Mode 4**, the string moves the most at that spot and hardly stretches, so only the extra mass counts, and **Mode 4's frequency goes down**.

## p14
- Before reasoning about any vowel, it helps to know where the air moves and where it compresses in the first two modes.
  - In **Mode 1**, the air near the closed glottis end compresses and expands a lot, and the air near the open lip end just moves back and forth.
  - In **Mode 2**, the air compresses a lot at the closed end and again at a second region about two thirds of the way along, and in between, about one third of the way along, it mostly moves.
  - **The closed end is always springy and the open end is always massy, in every mode**, because air can be squeezed against a closed end but has nothing to squeeze against at an open end.

## p15
- The mass-spring chain marks the three vowel locations along the tube, **one third of the way from the glottis** for **/a/**, **two thirds of the way** for **/i/**, and the **lip end** for **/u/**. Each vowel adds mass and stiffness at its own spot, and the effect on F1 and F2 depends on whether the air there is moving or compressing in each mode.
- Adding mass and stiffness at the **very top of a building** lowers the frequency of **every mode**, including Mode 4. The top is the free end, so in every mode it has the most motion and almost no springiness, and only the extra mass counts there.
  - **The top of the building corresponds to the lip end of the vocal tract**, which is why rounding the lips lowers every formant.

## p16
- **/u/** rounds the lips, which squeezes the air at the **free end**. The free end moves a lot and hardly compresses in both Mode 1 and Mode 2, so the extra **mass** counts in both, and **F1 and F2 both go down**.
  - A speaker who moved from the central vowel into /u/ produced exactly this, with **both formants dropping** in the recording.

## p17
- **/i/** squeezes the air about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **Mode 1**, that spot has lots of motion, so the extra mass counts and **F1 goes down**.
  - In **Mode 2**, that spot has lots of compression, so the extra stiffness counts and **F2 goes up**.
  - A speaker who moved from the central vowel into /i/ showed the **first formant dropping a lot** and the **second formant going up**.

## p18
- **/a/** squeezes the air about **one third of the way from the glottis**, which reverses the /i/ pattern.
  - In **Mode 1**, that spot near the fixed end compresses a lot, so the extra stiffness counts and **F1 goes up**.
  - In **Mode 2**, that spot moves a lot, so the extra mass counts and **F2 goes down**.
- The reasoning also runs backward. **If a constriction raises a formant a great deal, the air at that spot must be very springy and hardly moving in that mode**, and **if a constriction lowers a formant a great deal, the air at that spot must be moving a lot**.

## p19
- A measured **schwa**, made with no constriction, had formants near **500, 1500 and 2500 hertz**, which are the odd multiples a uniform fixed-free tube predicts. Every vowel moves these formants away from the schwa values by changing where the air moves and where it compresses.

## p20
- Speech combines **two vibrating systems at the same time**.
  - The **vocal folds** are a fixed-fixed system, so they are the **source** and produce **harmonics**. The lowest harmonic is called the **base frequency**, or **F0**, and here the harmonics are **100 hertz apart**.
  - The **air in the vocal tract** is a fixed-free system, so it acts as a **filter**, and its frequencies are the **formants**. For the schwa, the formants sit near **500, 1500, 2500 and 3500 hertz**.
- **The formants shape the heights of the harmonics.** The harmonics are all still there, but **harmonics near a formant are tall, and harmonics far from any formant are short**, so the outline of their heights, called the **envelope**, rises into a peak at each formant.
  - **The harmonics stay the same from vowel to vowel, but their heights change, and the envelope is what makes the vowel.**
  - In plain terms, the vocal folds decide which frequencies are present, and the shape of the mouth decides which of them are loud.

## p21
- For **/i/**, the filter keeps **F1 low and F2 high**. The tall harmonics are bunched at the very bottom of the spectrum, then a long stretch of short harmonics follows, and the next tall group appears much higher up, so the two lowest formants are **far apart**.
  - The constriction two thirds of the way from the glottis is what puts the formants there, lowering F1 and raising F2.

## p22
- For **/a/**, the filter **raises F1 and lowers F2**. The first two groups of tall harmonics sit **close together** in the lower part of the spectrum.
  - The same source harmonics are under both /i/ and /a/. Only the filter differs, and moving the constriction is what moves the formants and reshapes which harmonics are loud.

## p23
- **A spectrum describes one instant**, but speech is **time-varying**, because the vocal tract keeps moving and constrictions are made and unmade. **A spectrogram stacks many spectra side by side over time.**
  - **Time** runs along the **horizontal axis**.
  - **Frequency**, which was the horizontal axis of each spectrum, becomes the **vertical axis**.
  - **Amplitude**, which was the height of each spike, becomes **darkness**, so the louder a frequency is, the darker it looks.
- The formants appear as **dark bands** that move as the speaker moves. In **/ia/**, the two lowest bands start **far apart** during /i/, and as the speaker moves into /a/, **F1 rises and F2 falls**, so the bands **come close together**.
  - Reading a spectrogram means following the dark bands, because those bands are the formants changing over time.

## p24
- **Where were you a year ago?** is a good sentence for seeing source and filter working together over time. The vocal folds, as the source, keep producing harmonics, while the vocal tract, as the filter, keeps changing shape, so the envelope over the harmonics changes from moment to moment.
  - On a spectrogram, these changes show up as **dark bands that rise and fall**, since the dark bands are the formants and darkness shows amplitude.
  - Where the vowel sounds like **/i/**, the two lowest bands spread far apart, and during **you**, all the bands drop because the lips are rounded.
- **A spectrum shows one frozen instant, but a spectrogram shows how the formants move**, and real speech is all movement. Reading a sentence like this one means following each band and asking what constriction moved it.

## p25
- Formants can only show up through the harmonics, so the spacing of the harmonics matters. With harmonics every **100 hertz** and formants at **300 and 2100 hertz**, **many harmonics** sit under each formant peak, so the formants are easy to see.
- With a higher base frequency, such as **250 hertz**, fewer harmonics land on each formant peak and a formant can fall **between two harmonics**, so **it becomes very hard to tell the harmonics from the formants**.
  - A **toddler's** harmonics can be at 400, 800 and 1200 hertz, and a **soprano's** can start at 800 hertz, which is why their vowels can be very hard to identify.
- **Formants are the smooth envelope, and harmonics are samples of it.** A higher F0 means fewer samples, so the spectrogram stops looking like clean formant bands.
  - A simple way to see it is that the harmonics are like the points used to trace a curve. With many points the curve is obvious, and with only a few points it is hard to see.
