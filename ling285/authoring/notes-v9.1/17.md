## p2
- A **neural network** takes in information about one moment, uses its **weights** to process it and makes a decision, and it learns those weights by making errors and correcting them through **backpropagation**. The idea began in **1943** with a paper by **McCulloch and Pitts**, which proposed that simple mathematics could model how the brain works.
  - The paper starts from the **all-or-none** character of nervous activity, where all means active and none means inactive. That is the same idea as the **sigmoid**, so the very first paper on artificial neural networks starts from biology about how real neurons behave.
  - Warren McCulloch was a **psychiatrist**, and the earliest work on neural networks aimed to model how the mind works, including mental illness, not to make money.
- By **1986**, the ideas had matured into the two **Parallel Distributed Processing** books, written by a group at UC San Diego. **Parallel** means many neurons compute their activations at the same time, and **distributed** means the information from the inputs is spread across all the neurons.
  - Forward propagation and backpropagation were well formed by then, and one contributor, **Geoffrey Hinton**, later won the **2024 Nobel Prize in Physics** for this work.
- **Given some static vector of formants, these networks could make a decision about a few possible categories.** A **vector** is just a list of numbers, and static means one fixed list from one moment. But **human cognition is far more complex** than taking in one list of numbers and giving one decision at the end.
  - **Speech is incredibly time-varying**, because every little moment in time gives a new set of formants.
  - **Language is time-varying too**, since a sentence is a whole bunch of words, one after another.
  - So giving a network one bunch of numbers can never model cognition that unfolds in time.

## p3
- In **1985**, **Michael Jordan** developed **recurrent neural networks** to make sense of **speech production**, meaning how the tongue, the velum and the lips move over time. Features such as nasality, backness and lip rounding each switch on and off at different moments across a sequence of sounds, and he realized that a regular network could never handle something that complex and time-varying.
  - His network is **not just sequential**, one layer after another, because it has **two layers working in parallel**.
  - It is called **recurrent** because it **takes its output and sends it back into part of its input**. It produces something at one moment, then uses that to predict the next moment, and then the moment after that.
- **GPT works the same way, one word at a time.** It takes the words of the prompt and predicts the next word, which becomes the first word of its answer. Then it feeds the prompt plus that word back in and predicts the next word again, and keeps going, so the idea behind GPT starts in 1985 with thinking about how speech unfolds in time.
  - People kept refining the recurrent network from 1985 until **2017**, when the **large language model** was invented.
- **Science and technology evolve from other people's ideas over long stretches of time.** An idea from 1943 was refined into the networks of 1986, and Jordan's idea from 1985 was refined into the large language models of 2017, and the people who came up with these ideas were mostly not the ones who made a lot of money from them.
  - These years give a sense of the history, but they will not be asked about.

## p4
- AI systems build up in steps, and **each step is made of the one before it**.
  - **A single neuron takes in a vector and gives back one number.** It multiplies each input by its weight and adds them all up, which is the **inner product**, and the symbol **sigma** just means add them up. This uses nothing but second-grade multiplication and addition to model the electrochemical activity of a real neuron.
  - **A neural network takes in one vector and gives back a whole decision vector.** Its layers are linked by sets of weights, and each set turns one vector into the next through many inner products.
- **A large language model takes in many vectors, one for each moment in a sequence, and gives back one decision vector about what comes next.** Picture 15 vectors, each standing for something at one moment, such as one word of a sentence, and the job of the model is to **predict the sixteenth**.
  - In real systems, the vectors number in the hundreds of thousands to a million, and that set is called the **context window**. Each vector is also thousands of numbers long, which is why huge data centers are needed.
  - The sixteenth vector is a **probability distribution over the next word**. With about 30,000 English words, each entry gives the likelihood that one particular word comes next.
- **A large language model is made of neural networks.** Each set of weights is a **layer**, and real systems have about a hundred or more layers. **The knowledge is still in the weights**, so an LLM's knowledge of how to write an essay is stored in its weights.
- **An LLM is a dynamical system**, and that is why the Nobel Prize for AI was awarded in physics. A mass-spring system starts from **initial conditions** and the system decides what happens next, and an LLM's initial condition is its set of input vectors, which each layer turns into **better vectors**, step after step, until the last layer can predict the next word.
  - The calculation at every step is the **inner product**, multiplying and adding billions or trillions of times.
  - In plain terms, an LLM is like the oscillator, except that instead of two numbers changing over time, thousands of numbers are refined layer by layer.
- **An LLM learns by predicting the next word and correcting its mistakes.** Given "the cat sat on the," with **mat** as the real next word, a new LLM with random weights might predict something impossible like "in," and backpropagation adjusts its trillions of weights so the prediction moves toward mat.
  - Repeating this with about a trillion sentences, the system surprisingly ends up knowing how to speak English.
  - The first problem this design solved, in 2017, was **machine translation**, and the same design then worked for speech and many other problems.
- **An agentic system is a graph of LLMs**, and an **agent** in AI today is just an LLM.
  - An **outline LLM** receives the prompt and is instructed only to outline the answer, and a **writing LLM** turns the outline into smooth sentences.
  - A **critic LLM** criticizes the draft, and a **revision LLM** decides whether it is good enough or sends it back to the critic, usually for only a few rounds, before a **final LLM** produces the output.
  - The critic is what greatly reduced **hallucination**, the system making things up, and the inner conversation between LLMs is why many systems take a while to respond.
- **Neurons make neural networks, neural networks make LLMs, and LLMs make agentic systems**, much as molecules make cells and cells make organs.
  - Designing a graph of LLMs to solve a problem is a real job today, and it needs problem solving more than knowledge of programming languages.
  - The connections between LLMs can also form circuits nobody planned, which let a system do things that were never part of the design, sometimes harmful ones.
- **The chief insight behind all of this is Gestalt completion.** People see a triangle in the Gestalt figure even though none is drawn, because a big part of human knowledge is **completing** what is missing, and training a system only to **predict the missing thing** turns out to emulate human cognition.
  - The same idea is behind the **cloze procedure**, a test introduced by **Wilson Taylor** in **1953**, in which words are deleted from a text and people fill them in. The name comes from **closure**, the Gestalt term for completing a familiar pattern that is not quite finished, such as seeing a broken circle as whole.
  - **Knowledge can be tested by asking someone to complete a sequence, and for AI, completion is all that knowledge is.** An LLM predicting the next word is playing a cloze game over and over.
  - Underneath everything, **inference is inner products**, and **learning is stochastic gradient descent plus dynamic programming**.

## p5
- **An LLM takes 15 vectors and predicts the sixteenth**, and since a vector is just a list of numbers, those numbers can stand for almost anything. Whatever they stand for, the machine plays the same game of predicting the next one.
- The vectors can be **words**. In "The cat that the dog chased ran up the old tree and sat on the," the sixteenth vector is the missing next word, and a speaker of English knows a likely completion, such as branch or mat.
  - Getting it right means using the whole sentence, since it was the cat, not the dog, that sat.
- The vectors can be **slices of sound**. A spectrogram is cut into little frames, each frame becomes one vector of frequencies and amplitudes, and the system learns to predict the next little piece of speech from all the previous pieces.
  - This approach, in 2019, gave the **best speech recognition in 75 years**, and it is also the basis for synthetic voices that are now very hard to tell from humans.
  - The newest systems can hold a spoken conversation, and the only giveaway left is a slight delay before each response. As that delay shrinks, phone scams become a real danger.
- The vectors can be **pieces of an image**. An image is cut into **16 patches** read row by row, and the system predicts the missing patch, and trained on huge numbers of images, it learns what a dog, a dolphin or a boat is. This became the basis of modern **computer vision**.
- The vectors can even be **amino acids**. A protein is a string of amino acids, which come in **20 kinds**, and the order of the string decides how the protein folds into its shape. Treating a protein as just a sequence and playing the same predict-the-next game pushed the **protein folding problem** many years ahead, which matters for designing drugs and vaccines.
  - That work won the **2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry**.
- **The LLM only ever sees vectors.** Words, sounds, pixels and molecules all reach it the same way, so one design asks one question, what comes sixteenth, every time.
  - Because these systems predict from past data, something new that the past data does not cover can make them **hallucinate**.

## p6
- English allows only **certain sequences of sounds** in its words, and knowing which sequences are allowed is part of every speaker's knowledge of the language. **A simple English syllable can have a consonant, then a vowel, then a consonant**, as in Don, mat, sit, cup, dog and fish.
  - The pattern is about **sounds, not letters**. The silent e in shave, kite and rope does not count, and two letters can spell one consonant sound, as in ship, chin and bath.
- Syllables can also have **clusters**, which are groups of consonants together. A cluster before the vowel is an **onset**, as in split, flat and black, and a cluster after the vowel is a **coda**, as in desk, help and lamp. **Only certain sequences are allowed before the vowel and after the vowel.**
  - Each real word has an impossible twin marked with a star, such as **flat** and **lfat**, **help** and **hepl**, and **lamp** and **lapm**. Many twins use the same letters in the other order, so order matters.
  - **The order mirrors around the vowel.** In flat, the l sits right next to the vowel with the f farther out, and in help, the l again sits next to the vowel, now on the other side, with the p farther out. Each impossible twin puts the wrong consonant against the vowel.
- **A long word is a chain of syllables**, such as ba-na-na, At-lan-tic and ath-lete. The sounds t and l are fine across a syllable break, but **tl cannot start a syllable**, which is why Atlantic is a word but tlantic is impossible.
  - The sound **ng** can end a syllable, as in ring, but it can never start one, so whether a sequence of sounds is allowed depends on where the syllable breaks fall.
- **Every language has its own rules.** Mandarin allows only nasal sounds at the end of a syllable, Cantonese also allows a final p, Russian and Polish allow clusters like zb or dl at the start of a word, and Spanish speakers turn a word like stadium into one that starts with e.

## p7
- **Phonotactic knowledge can be revealed by the same next-item game that GPT plays.** Each item is the start of an English word, and the task is to guess the next letter. People agree on their guesses quickly, and how much they agree changes from item to item.
- **After a vowel, almost anything can follow.** After "ra," likely letters include t, n, r and l, and many others are possible too.
- **At the start of a word, the choices narrow sharply** to exactly what an English onset allows.
  - After **sp**, the next letter is l, r or a vowel, and never n, and after **k**, it is a vowel or n, as in knee, and never t.
  - After **b** and **f**, it is r, l or a vowel, and never n, and after **sh**, it is a vowel or r, as in shrimp.
  - After **skr**, or after **m** at the start of a word, only a vowel can come.
  - After **bram**, the letter b is almost forced, as in bramble, though a new syllable could also start there.
- **Guessing the next letter is predicting the sixteenth item**, the same next-token game an LLM plays. A GPT trained only on this game learns English sounds and spelling, because its predictions can only get good by matching the patterns of English syllables.
  - Its prediction is a **probability distribution** over the next letter, and the shape of that distribution carries the knowledge.
  - After "ra," the distribution is **flat**, spread over many letters, while after "skr" it is **sharply peaked** on a few vowels, because English allows nothing else there.
  - In plain terms, the more the letters so far restrict what can come next, the more the probability piles onto a few letters.

## p8
- English syllables are a good test case for understanding LLMs. English has about 30,000 words, so training on words needs enormous data, but it has only about 26 letters, or a few dozen sounds, so a small LLM, with thousands of weights instead of trillions, can be trained to learn the knowledge of English syllables that every speaker already has.
- Instead of writing that program by hand, which would take months, the plan is to **design an agentic system using instructions written in English**. The agentic system builds the LLM, trains it to predict each next letter, and shows what it has learned along the way, so it can be compared with the knowledge of English syllables that speakers carry in their heads.
