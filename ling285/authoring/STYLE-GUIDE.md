# Jordan's writing style for study material (LING285, all courses)

Built Nov 11, 2026 from Jordan's own feedback across past sessions and from the transcript-note
versions Jordan liked (v9.3, v10.1, also 9.4 and 8.2). Full evidence:
scratchpad style-feedback.md and style-notes-analysis.md.

## Never
1. No "Label: sentence" lines ("Why it vibrates: …", "Step 1:", "Comparison:", "Answer first, then the reason: …"). Jordan called this unacceptable. Write the sentence itself.
2. No mention of the lecture, slides, class, course, the professor or the TA in teaching text ("In the lecture's account", "the lecture example", "he said"). Just teach.
3. No meta framing: "this shows", "the key point", "the question is", "note that", "let's", "remember that", "start with", "rather than an arbitrary list", "the reasoning works backward".
4. No analogies or invented comparisons. Apply the idea directly. Only images the professor himself used (building, classroom of experts, spring that hates being stretched) may stay.
5. No homemade stand-ins for real terms: never "squeeze", "frozen", "massy". Use constriction, compression, inertia.
6. No formal or literary filler: since, thus, hence, fares worse, mediated, digitizes, downstream, "with no other change", passive "was heard to", stacked noun phrases.
7. No colons, semicolons, em dashes, equals signs or arrows in prose.
8. "Picture" only as a verb meaning imagine ("Picture a spot…"). Never as a noun.
9. No telegraphic or reference-sheet fragments ("One neuron. Vector in, one number out.").
10. No filler background that does not teach.

## Always
1. Set up the system before explaining it. Open with what the thing is or does, as the subject with an active verb ("In a mass-spring system, a mass is attached to a spring…", "Perturbation theory predicts how a small change…"). Never start abruptly mid-mechanism.
2. One idea per sentence, about 10 to 18 words, almost never over 25. Split "…, and …, and …" chains.
3. Spell out every cause-to-effect link with "so" or "because": extra mass → vibrates more slowly → lower frequency (pitch).
4. Use the real course term, bold it once, and explain it right away in plain words ("constrictions (narrowings)", "frequency (pitch)", "**inertia** is the air's resistance to being moved").
5. Give each case its own full sentence in the same pattern: "In **Mode 1**, the spot moves, so the extra mass counts, and **the frequency goes down**."
6. Bold the takeaway sentence that opens a block, key terms and result words. Reading only the bold should give the answer. Do not bold things that do not matter.
7. Order within a block: takeaway (bold) → setup (the system, the spot, the change) → what happens, case by case, with "because" → the general rule → example or evidence → "So…"/"That is why…" closing sentence.
8. Results in words. Keep a number only when it matters (200 Hz Mode 1 → Mode 5 is 1,000 Hz; 20 percent chance).
9. Neutral, plain register. Readable aloud. Not chatty, not academic.
10. Keep the depth when rewriting. Cut only what does not teach.

## Answers to questions
- Always give the answer. Write it the way Jordan would say it on the exam: 2 to 4 full sentences, the direct answer first as a normal sentence, then the reason with "because"/"so". No label box, no "The answer is".

## Layout (Module I "What to know by the end")
- Topic heading, then "I can explain and apply these ideas" with blocks. Each block has a lecture-page tag, a bullet whose first sentence is bold and continues into the explanation, a sub-bullet with the why, and a closing bullet. Then "What you can be asked" with each question in a dropdown that shows the answer.

## Model sentences Jordan approved (verbatim)
- "A building is not designed to be completely stiff because buildings that cannot bend or move slightly are more likely to be damaged during an earthquake."
- "Perturbation theory predicts how a small change in one location affects how something vibrates. In the late nineteenth century, researchers tested this by placing small amounts of wax on different parts of a drum. When wax was placed where the drum vibrated a lot, the extra mass made the drum vibrate more slowly, causing its frequency (pitch) to decrease. When wax was placed where the drum did not vibrate, it had no effect on the drum's frequency or sound."
- "Images taken while people speak show where constrictions (narrowings) occur in the vocal tract, which extends from the closed glottis to the open lips."
- "A single artificial neuron takes in a vector of numerical inputs and produces one numerical output. Each input is assigned a weight, and the neuron combines these weighted inputs before applying an activation function. The result is a single number."
- v10.1: "**Two masses have two modes, and Mode 2 is faster because more springs are stretching.** In Mode 1, the masses move together…"
- v9.3: "For Mode 4, the piece is moving, so the extra mass **lowers** the frequency. The piece is barely stretched, so the extra stiffness does nothing."

## Simpler explanations (added Nov 12, 2026, from Jordan's own rewrites)
Jordan said the v11 writing was still "making it so much harder". These are the rewrites Jordan
liked, set beside what was rejected. Follow these over anything above that conflicts.

1. **Say what is literally happening, never a borrowed idea.** Rejected: "the order of assigning blame",
   "each layer's blame is reused". Liked: "calculating how much each weight contributed to the error.
   It starts with the last layer and uses those calculations to work backward through the earlier layers."
   No "blame", "credit", "pushes", "votes", "keeps the sign", "flips" unless spelled out.
2. **Direct answer first, as a full plain sentence that repeats the question's words.** "All three modes
   have lower frequencies when mass 1 becomes heavier."
3. **Then "This is because…", one step per sentence, with no step skipped.** Rejected: "a heavier mass
   that is moving lowers the frequency." Liked: "When a mass becomes heavier, it is harder to move, so
   the system vibrates more slowly." Explain the middle step (harder to move) before the result.
4. **Tie it back at the end.** "Since mass 1 moves in every mode, all three modes slow down, and their
   frequencies decrease." ("Since" is fine in this everyday sense.)
5. **Everyday verbs.** updating, figuring out, determines how big, makes it go up/down, harder to move.
   Not "sets", "follows from", "yields", "carries".
6. **Keep the key term, then explain it in parentheses right there.** "formant transitions (changes in
   formant frequencies as your mouth moves from one sound to another)", "formants (frequency regions with
   strong acoustic energy)", "amplitude (strength)".
7. **Clear up the obvious confusion a reader would have.** "the vowel that comes after it (the vowel
   sound immediately following the consonant within a word, not a vowel in the next word)". "/n/ is a
   consonant".
8. **Use a small worked number example when the idea is about signs or amounts.** "If the input is +3,
   a weight of +2 gives +6, so it stays positive. A weight of −2 gives −6, so it becomes negative."
9. **Separate paragraph for each part of a multi-part question**, each opening with the part's name
   ("The SGD part is…", "The dynamic programming part is…", "The small problem is…").
10. **Spell out the case that confuses.** "If the weight is negative, increasing the positive activation
    makes that output's probability go down."
11. Still never: "Label: sentence" lines, "Basically:", arrows, equals signs in prose, lecture/professor
    mentions, analogies of my own, telegraphic fragments.
