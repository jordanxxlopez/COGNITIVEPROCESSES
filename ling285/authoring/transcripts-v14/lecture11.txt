2|A spring holding a mass has a resting point called **equilibrium**. Moving the mass away from it deforms the spring, and the spring answers with a **restoring force** that points back toward rest. >> That force accelerates the mass toward equilibrium. >> Thanks to **inertia**, the mass overshoots, deforms the spring on the opposite side and gets pulled back again. This repeated swing is an **oscillation**. || The swing can run in two directions. >> In **longitudinal motion**, the mass moves along the spring, and the spring lengthens and shortens. >> In **transverse motion**, the mass moves across the spring. The taut segments tilt as the mass leaves their line, and the tilted pull points back toward the line. || The force reverses each time the mass crosses equilibrium. >> On either side, the spring acts toward the center from that side. >> Inertia carries the mass through, the force brings it back, and the exchange repeats. || Both directions repeat at **0.5 Hz**, one cycle every two seconds. >> Their mass and total restoring stiffness are equal, so their **frequency** is equal. >> With **no damping**, the motion keeps its full size.
3|Three oscillators run side by side, each changing one property from a reference. >> The **reference** has mass **m** and stiffness **K** and runs at **0.5 Hz**. >> With mass **4m**, it runs at **0.25 Hz**. >> With stiffness **4K**, it runs at **1 Hz**. || **Adding mass lowers the frequency.** >> More inertia means the same spring force changes the motion more slowly. >> Each turnaround takes longer, so four times the mass halves the rate. || **Adding stiffness raises the frequency.** >> A stiffer spring pulls back harder at the same displacement. >> The mass reverses sooner, so four times the stiffness doubles the rate. >> Mass resists changes in motion, and stiffness drives the return. || Frequency is found by counting full cycles in equal time. >> **Amplitude**, the distance traveled, is a different property from rate. >> The longitudinal and transverse versions match because they share the same **effective restoring stiffness** in the direction they move. Across the spring, that stiffness comes from tension and geometry, so a spring that is harder to stretch lengthwise is not automatically stiffer sideways.
4|Two equal masses, held between fixed ends by three equal springs, have **two normal modes**. A **normal mode** is a pattern in which every part oscillates at one frequency and keeps a fixed relationship to the rest. || **Mode 1** moves the two masses together in the same direction. >> Their spacing hardly changes, so the middle spring barely works. >> It is the slower mode, at **0.4 Hz**. || **Mode 2** moves them against each other. >> The middle spring now compresses and stretches strongly. >> The extra restoring force raises the frequency to about **1.73 times** Mode 1, higher but not double. || Each mode involves both masses. >> The masses and springs stay the same, and only their relationship differs, together in one mode and opposed in the other. || Both modes can run at once. >> Their motions **add** at each mass, reinforcing where they agree and canceling where they disagree. This is **superposition**. >> The combined row is the exact sum of the two modes and carries both frequencies. The longitudinal and transverse chains behave alike.

6|Three equal masses held by four equal springs between fixed ends have **three normal modes**. || **Mode 1** moves all three masses one way. >> The middle mass travels farthest, and the outer ones travel less. >> It is the lowest mode, at **0.32 Hz**. || **Mode 2** sends the outer masses in opposite directions. >> The two pulls on the middle mass cancel, so it stays at equilibrium, a still point called a **node**. >> It runs at about **1.85 times** the lowest frequency. || **Mode 3** moves the outer masses together and the middle mass against them. >> Every mass opposes its neighbor, so all the springs work hard. >> It is the fastest mode, at about **2.41 times** the lowest frequency. || Stronger opposition between neighbors gives a faster mode. >> Opposed neighbors stretch the springs more, which raises the restoring force. >> All three masses take part in every mode. Colors track each mass, and resting marks give the zero position. || These ratios belong to this chain and are not whole numbers. >> In the mixture, the modes add at each mass. >> The middle mass moves in the mixture through Modes 1 and 3, even though Mode 2 holds it still.
7|In the atlas, rows hold chains of one to six equal masses and columns hold mode numbers. Each movable mass adds one **normal mode**, so a chain of N masses has N modes. >> Smaller chains leave later columns empty, for **21** patterns in total. >> Every chain has equal masses, one more spring than masses and fixed ends. || **Mode 1** is the broadest pattern in every chain, with all masses moving together. >> Each higher mode adds one more group moving the opposite way. >> More reversals mean more opposed neighbors, more spring stretch, a stronger restoring force and a higher frequency. || The fixed ends never move. >> A mass that stays still in one mode is a **node** of that mode while the others move. >> Higher modes rearrange the same parts. The mode number names the pattern, not a particular mass. || Colors mark mode number and rows mark chain size. >> Every pattern shares the same largest displacement, so frequency must be read from repetition rate, not height. >> **Amplitude** is movement size, and frequency is repetition rate. >> Separate-mass chains lack the exact whole-number spacing of an ideal continuous string.
8|A **fixed–fixed string** is held at both ends, so both ends are **nodes**. >> **Mode 1** fits one half-wave. >> Modes 2 to 5 fit two to five half-waves. || More half-waves in the same length means shorter waves, and shorter waves at the same speed repeat faster. >> The modes run at **100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz**. >> These are consecutive **harmonics**, whole-number multiples of the **fundamental**, with both odd and even multiples present. || All five modes can share the string. >> Their motions add at each point, so the outline keeps changing while each component stays regular. >> A point may go nearly flat through cancellation, then move strongly again, with every component still present. || Playback is slowed **250** times, so 100 Hz displays at **0.4 Hz**. >> The equal slowdown keeps the relative rates intact. || A **pickup** at **17%** of the length records one point. >> Its **spectrum** has one peak per mode, with frequency on one axis and amplitude on the other. >> The mode strengths were chosen to make the peaks equal at this point. Another position would give different heights, so equal peaks do not mean equal motion everywhere.
9|A **membrane** is a vibrating surface with fixed edges. Its modes have two-number labels counting moving regions along the two directions. >> **(1,1)** is one region. >> **(2,1)** and **(1,2)** split the surface in two along different directions. >> **(2,2)** makes four regions, and **(3,1)** makes three along one direction. >> Neighboring regions move oppositely, divided by still **node** lines. || The five modes add at every point into one changing surface. >> They reinforce in some places and cancel in others. >> The combined panel uses a smaller vertical scale, so its height cannot be compared with the single panels. || A node stays still all cycle. >> A moving region crosses the resting level for an instant and keeps going, so the crossing is not a node. >> The two numbers describe the division of the surface, not two frequencies or two membranes. || A **pickup** at one point gives a **spectrum** with one peak per mode. >> Mode strengths were set to make the peaks equal there. >> The frequencies **100 to 500 Hz** were **assigned for the illustration**, not calculated as this rectangle’s natural resonances. Their even spacing models how frequencies combine, without claiming every membrane is harmonic.
10|The **scientific method** relies on clear theories about how the world works, usually written as **differential equations**. >> These equations describe how a system’s quantities change. >> Solving them gives a **prediction** of what a measurement should find. || The theory here is that the **vocal folds are a membrane**. >> A vibrating membrane produces a spectrum with **equidistant peaks**, called **harmonics**. >> Its equations resemble those for springs and membranes, with somewhat more complexity. || That theory predicts what a recording should contain. >> A person’s **laryngeal vibration** signal should have **equidistant harmonics**, peaks separated by equal intervals. >> The test is to record real vocal-fold vibration and check the spacing of its peaks. >> Even spacing supports the theory, and other spacing challenges it. || The spectrum is the decisive part. >> The folds vibrate either way, so only the spacing tests the theory. >> The membrane animation used assigned frequencies, so it pictures the idea without testing it. The experiment is proposed here, with no reported outcome yet.
11|A **fixed–free system** is fixed at one end and free at the other. >> The fixed end must be a **node**. >> The free end must be an **antinode**, the point of greatest movement. || The allowed shapes fit odd numbers of **quarter-waves**. >> The first fits one, and the next fit three, five and seven. >> They are the **first, third, fifth and seventh harmonics**, so the second allowed mode is the third harmonic. || Only odd counts satisfy both ends. >> An even count would place a node at the free end. >> More quarter-waves mean shorter waves and higher frequencies. || The modes run at **100, 300, 500 and 700 Hz**, slowed **250** times for display. >> With equal strengths, the free-end spectrum shows four equal peaks. >> An antinode swings through rest to the far side, while the fixed end stays still. || The fixed–fixed and fixed–free strings were **tuned separately** to the same fundamental. >> Freeing one end with length and wave speed unchanged would halve the fundamental. >> The matching labels therefore do not isolate the effect of the free end.
