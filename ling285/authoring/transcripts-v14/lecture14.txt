4|A **quarter-wavelength** joins a **node**, where nothing moves, to an **antinode**, where movement is greatest. A fixed–free string has a node at its fixed bottom and an antinode at its free top, so its shapes are built from quarter-waves. >> **One quarter-wave** fits exactly between the two ends. >> It is the simplest pattern, with no still point inside the string, and it sets the fundamental. || The next pattern needs care. >> Adding only one more quarter-wave would end the string on a node. >> That is the wrong condition for the free top, so the pattern is not allowed. >> Adding **two** returns the top to an antinode. || The allowed patterns therefore hold **one, three, five and seven quarter-waves**. >> Each step adds one inner node and one inner antinode. >> More sections in the same length give shorter waves, and shorter waves at the same speed repeat faster. || The demonstration adds one packet at a time to every mode that contains it. >> The modes hold **1, 3, 5 and 7** packets, matching the odd harmonics. >> The numbered pieces divide the standing shape and do not travel up the string. >> The string moves back and forth while the pattern’s still points stay in place. || The boundaries alone produce the odd pattern. >> Only odd counts start at a node and end at an antinode. >> Even frequencies are absent because the ends rule them out.

6|Every point in a vibrating air column has two properties, and they are easy to mix up. >> **Movement** is how far an air element travels. >> **Compression** is how much the spacing between neighbors changes. >> Elements can travel far together with nearly constant gaps, while elsewhere gaps close and open strongly around a point that barely moves. || Each property has its own reading. >> To see movement, follow one circle through a full cycle. >> To see compression, compare the gaps between neighboring circles, which shrink where air is squeezed and widen where it spreads. || Together the two keep the air oscillating. >> Changing compression gives a **spring-like restoring effect**, because crowded air pushes back toward normal spacing. >> **Inertia** keeps moving air going while that push changes, so the air overshoots and the cycle repeats. || The tube’s ends set where each region falls. >> The **closed end** restricts movement. >> The **open end** permits it. >> Each higher mode fits more alternating regions into the tube, in its own positions. || The moving **Schwa** reference and the **F1, F2 and F3** rows are resonances of one air system, not separate streams. >> A constriction can act mainly on moving mass in one mode and mainly on compression in another. >> Its effect depends on the mode as well as the location.
7|This **pop quiz** marks six spots, **A** through **F**, along the air column and asks what each is doing. >> Each spot must be followed through a **complete cycle**. >> Its air element’s travel gives its movement. >> The spacing changes around it give its compression. >> One still frame cannot settle it, since a fast-moving element may be caught passing through rest. || Strong **movement** is where added mass matters. >> More **effective mass** is harder to speed up and reverse. >> The cycle lengthens, and the resonance tends to **fall**. || Strong **compression change** is where added stiffness matters. >> More **stiffness** returns the air toward its spacing more forcefully. >> When this dominates, the cycle shortens, and the resonance tends to **rise**. || **Modes 1, 2 and 3** place these regions differently. >> Extra reversals in the higher modes add more alternating regions. >> A spot that moves strongly in one mode can be squeezed strongly in another. || Predicting a local change takes two pieces of information. >> The **position** of the change. >> The **mode** being considered. With both, a narrowing can be judged to add mainly moving mass or mainly stiffness for that resonance.

10|**Mode 1** and **Mode 2** are two resonances of a single air column, held at the glottis and open at the lips. >> Both move the air strongly near the open lips. >> Mode 2 has one extra reversal inside the tube, so the rest of its movement and compression is arranged differently. || Markers sit at the **back**, the **front** and the **lips**. >> Each marker is the same physical place in both modes. >> Narrowing the tract at a marker changes that region for both modes together. >> What differs is how each mode uses the region. || The narrowing’s effect depends on each mode’s activity there. >> Strong **movement** makes the mass-like effect important, and the extra load slows the vibration. >> Strong **compression** makes the spring-like effect important, and the stronger return speeds it up. >> Because the two modes can be doing different things at one marker, a single narrowing can move **F1** and **F2** in opposite directions. || Their ratio is fixed only in the ideal case. >> In the uniform reference tube, Mode 2 runs at **three times** the frequency of Mode 1. >> Any change in tract shape moves the formants and changes that ratio. || Formants and harmonics are separate things. >> **Formants** are tract resonances that shift with tract shape. >> **Harmonics** are source components whose spacing comes from vocal-fold repetition, which shifts with pitch.

15|How fast the vocal folds repeat decides how the voiced **source** looks. That repetition rate is **F0**, and it fixes the gap between neighboring harmonic lines. || A membrane offers the model for this. >> The **fixed–fixed membrane** combines five modes, labeled (1,1), (2,1), (1,2), (2,2) and (3,1). >> They were assigned the evenly spaced frequencies **100 to 500 Hz** for the illustration. >> Measured at a pickup point, their sum gives a spectrum of evenly spaced peaks, the pattern of a harmonic source. || Three source spectra, each covering **0 to 4000 Hz**, compare different vocal folds before any filtering. >> With an F0 of **100 Hz**, many lines crowd into the window. >> **Tighter, lighter vocal folds** raise F0 to **250 Hz**, so lines appear only at 250, 500, 750 and onward. >> **Very tight and light vocal folds** raise F0 to **350 Hz**, spacing the lines at 350, 700, 1050 and onward. || The rule is that **lower F0 means denser harmonic sampling and higher F0 means sparser sampling**. >> The vocal folds obey mass–spring logic. >> Heavy, loose folds have more mass and less restoring force, so they repeat slowly. >> Light, tight folds have less mass and more restoring force, so they repeat quickly. || One voiced vowel carries **many frequencies**. >> Which harmonics exist depends on the source. >> Which harmonics are strong depends on the tract filter. >> Pitch and vowel shape therefore change different parts of the same sound.
16|The vocal tract is the **filter**, a **fixed–free** tube closed at one end and open at the other. It selects **formants** by one core rule. >> **A formant encourages the closest harmonics to be audible.** >> **Harmonics far from that formant are muted.** >> The source supplies harmonics, and the filter chooses which survive, so the vowel shape is the filter. || Each vowel shape puts its formants in its own places. >> **Schwa** has separated first and second resonances, near **500** and **1500 Hz**. >> **/u/** places both lower, near **300** and **1000 Hz**, and marks a third near **2000 Hz**. >> **/i/ or /ɪ/** has a low F1 near **300 Hz** and a much higher F2 near **2200 Hz**. >> **/a/** puts its first two targets close together, at **800** and **900 Hz**. >> These values belong to this illustration. || Moving the tongue and lips reshapes the tract and moves these favored regions. >> Each target lifts its nearest harmonics. >> Harmonics between targets stay weak, forming valleys between the peaks. || Targets and lines are different things. >> A **formant target** is a resonance of the tract. >> A **harmonic line** is a component of the source. >> A target can sit between two lines and still strengthen both. Filtering changes heights without adding a component at each target.
17|This grid compares **one vowel filter at several pitches**. Source rows cross vowel filter columns, with **dashed red ticks** for formant targets and **colored stems** for harmonics after filtering. || Each row fixes the source. >> The top row has **heavy, loose** folds and dense harmonics. >> The middle row has **lighter, tighter** folds at **F0 = 250 Hz**. >> The bottom row has the **lightest, tightest** folds at **F0 = 350 Hz**. || Each column fixes the filter. >> Down a column, only F0 changes, so the ticks stay put while the stems spread. >> Across a row, only the filter changes, so the stems stay put while the ticks move. >> The grid pulls source effects apart from tract-shape effects. || **Dense harmonics trace the formant shape well.** >> At low F0, many stems sit near each resonance. >> Their heights draw the **envelope**, the smooth outline of favored strengths. >> More harmonics give a smoother, easier-to-read spectrum. || **Sparse harmonics can miss a formant or make it look ambiguous.** >> At high F0, fewer stems sample each formant. >> A strong stem may sit beside a target instead of on it, though the target has not moved. >> The two close /a/ targets can blur into one region. || A hard-to-read formant has not vanished. >> The ticks hold their positions down each column. >> Unchanged targets with wider-spaced stems mean the source changed and the filter did not.

21|Speech science now leads **back to AI**. A **neural network** will be trained on students’ **formants** and will decide which vowel is being spoken. || The inputs are **F1** and **F2**. >> They are the first two resonance regions that the vocal tract emphasizes. >> Together they form a compact pair of measurements for each vowel, instead of every point in the waveform. >> Tongue and lip positions move these two formants in characteristic ways, so the pair carries much of what separates vowels. || The **input** and **output** carry different information. >> The input is a pair of sound measurements. >> The output picks one of the candidate vowel categories. >> The pair is evidence about the sound, while the true label says which category the example belongs to. >> Recognition means learning to relate the measured pattern to the right category. || The exercise joins the physics of speech to machine learning. >> The mass–spring account explains where F1 and F2 come from. >> The network learns how those two numbers relate to vowel categories, using students’ measurements. >> For now, the inputs and the task are set. Training and testing will show how well the network recognizes vowels.
22|The vowel network is **purely linear**, running **2 inputs** through **hidden layers of 3, 2 and 4 neurons** to **5 outputs**, one per word, with **no activation functions**. >> The inputs are **F1** and **F2**, kept in that order so each position means one formant. || Every layer is **fully connected**. >> Each receiving neuron links to every neuron in the previous layer. >> Every link has a **weight** that sets how strongly that value counts. >> A receiving neuron multiplies each incoming value by its weight and adds the products to get its own value. || The hidden layers hold intermediate combinations. >> They have **three**, **two** and **four** neurons. >> Each hidden value is a weighted blend of the layer before, not a new formant and not a finished category. >> Different weights let neurons blend the same inputs in different ways. || The outputs stand for five words. >> From top to bottom, **/done/, /dune/, /dean/, /Don/** and **/Dan/**. >> A fixed order keeps each value tied to its word. || **Purely linear** means no activation function sits between layers. >> Each layer passes its weighted sums straight on. >> The whole network is one chain of weighted sums. >> That chain does not by itself make the five outputs into probabilities.
23|An example input enters, **F1 = 320** and **F2 = 2000**, and **every edge gets a random weight**. || Inputs and weights play different roles. >> The inputs **measure** the sound. >> The **weights** are the network’s **settings**, deciding how the measurements combine. >> New weights change every internal result, even for the same input pair. || Every connection supplies one weight. >> A neuron multiplies incoming values by their weights and adds the products. >> A first-layer neuron receives F1 and F2, so it has two weights. >> The three first-layer neurons form three different combinations of that pair. || **Before training, every weight is a random number between −1 and 1.** >> Edges carry values such as **0.95**, **−0.91** and **0.28**. >> The heading marks **blue for positive** and **orange for negative**, though negative edges render green. The written sign gives direction, and the size gives strength. >> A negative weight flips its input’s sign, so contributions can reinforce or oppose each other. || These are **random starting values**, with no learned vowel knowledge. >> A **forward pass** runs an example through the current weights. >> **Learning** is what changes the weights. >> Naming the outputs does not make the untrained network recognize them.
24|In the **forward flow**, the input moves **layer by layer**, and the output is **a distribution over the five words**. >> The animation carries the input pair along the connections, one layer at a time. >> Each layer combines the previous values with its current weights. >> This is the **forward pass**, and learning would be a separate step that changes the weights. || The illustrated distribution gives each word a probability. >> **/done/** gets **0.5**. >> **/Dan/** gets **0.2**. >> **/dune/**, **/dean/** and **/Don/** get **0.1** each. >> /done/ is favored, and the others keep smaller support. || Valid probabilities lie between zero and one and add to one. >> These five values meet both conditions. || The bars illustrate the intended output. >> A purely linear network has no step that guarantees a distribution. >> So the bars picture the goal, not a verified result of the shown weights. || The **predicted category** is the model’s favorite. >> The **true category** is the example’s actual label. >> Only the true label can show whether the prediction is right, so the tallest bar and the smiley face are not enough on their own.
