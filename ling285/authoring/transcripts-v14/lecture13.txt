4|Both ends of a **fixed–fixed string** are held, so both must be **nodes**. Every allowed shape therefore fits a whole number of **half-waves** between them. || **Mode 1** fits one half-wave. >> The string moves as one broad region, biggest in the middle. >> Its frequency is the **fundamental**, the lowest. || **Mode 2** fits two half-waves. >> A node appears at the center. >> The halves move in opposite directions. || **Modes 3 to 5** fit three to five half-waves. >> Each added half-wave shortens the **wavelength**. >> At the same wave speed, shorter waves repeat faster. >> Since each mode holds a whole number of base half-waves, each frequency is a **whole-number multiple** of the fundamental. Odd and even counts both keep the ends still, so both appear. || Numbered sections mark the half-wave packets inside each shape. >> Section one appears in every mode, section two from Mode 2 upward, and section five only in Mode 5. >> The sections stay in place as the string moves. They do not count elapsed cycles or travel along the string. >> Higher harmonics simply hold more half-wave packets in the same length.

8|A **quarter-wave** spans from a **node** to an **antinode**. On a **fixed–free string**, fixed at the bottom and free at the top, those two conditions set every allowed shape. >> The bottom must be a node. >> The top must be an antinode. || **One quarter-wave** meets both conditions, giving the **fundamental**. || Quarter-waves are added in pairs. >> A single extra one would end on a node at the free top. >> A pair keeps both conditions and adds one internal reversal. >> The four allowed modes hold **one, three, five and seven** quarter-waves. || More sections mean shorter waves, and shorter waves at the same speed vibrate faster. >> The modes are the **first, third, fifth and seventh harmonics**. >> So the second mode is the third harmonic, the third is the fifth and the fourth is the seventh. || Even counts would put a node at the free top, so even harmonics never appear. || Numbered pieces count the quarter-wave sections. >> Extra pieces appear only in higher modes, and pieces six and seven appear only in the fourth. >> They mark fixed positions in a standing pattern, and their counts of 1, 3, 5 and 7 match the odd harmonics.
9|This **pop quiz** asks for the quarter-wave count of each **fixed–free pattern**, read from its ends. >> The fixed bottom is a **node**. >> The free top is an **antinode**. >> Each step between a node and an antinode covers **one quarter-wave**. || The four patterns hold **one, three, five and seven** quarter-waves. >> Each higher pattern adds one node and one antinode inside the string. >> More sections shorten the wavelength, so higher patterns repeat faster. >> The numbered divisions stay fixed as the material moves through them. || Two numbers describe each pattern. >> **Mode number** counts allowed patterns in order. >> **Harmonic number** compares frequency with the fundamental. >> On a fixed–free string they separate, because only odd counts fit both ends. >> The fourth mode is the **seventh harmonic**, not the fourth.

13|A **constriction** narrows the airway and changes two properties of the air there. || It raises **stiffness**. >> Compressed air resists further compression more strongly. >> Where a mode **compresses** the air a lot, a constriction **raises its frequency**. || It raises **mass**. >> Bunching air up increases its density. >> Where a mode **moves** the air a lot, a constriction adds moving mass and **lowers its frequency**. || Mass and stiffness rise together, yet their effects do not cancel. >> The parts of a vibrating medium that **move most** alternate with the parts that **compress most**. >> Each mode places these regions differently, so one spot can be a moving region in one mode and a compressing region in another. || To tell the effects apart, follow the air through a cycle. >> One element’s travel reveals the mass being carried. >> The closing and opening gaps beside it reveal compression and the restoring force. >> A tongue-height label or a single frozen frame cannot reveal both. || One narrowing can act mainly through **mass** for one formant and mainly through **stiffness** for another. >> One formant falls while the other rises. >> Both rules still hold. Mass lowers frequency where air moves, and stiffness raises it where air compresses.

20|**Source–filter theory** separates two parts of a vowel. The **source** is vocal-fold vibration, and the **filter** is the vocal tract that shapes it. || The source is a **harmonic comb**. >> It repeats at a **fundamental frequency**, **F0**, with harmonics at whole-number multiples. >> Here the harmonics are **100 Hz** apart and reach **4000 Hz**. >> Before the tract acts, they are evenly spaced lines of equal height. || The filter **sculpts the heights** of the harmonics. >> Harmonics near the tract’s resonances, the **formants**, become stronger, and the rest weaker. >> The lines keep their positions, and only their heights change. >> The outline of the heights is the **spectral envelope**, and the envelope is the vowel. || The schwa example labels four formants, **F1 to F4**, with the strongest group at low frequency. || Frequency runs across the spectrum, and amplitude runs up. >> A taller line is stronger, not higher in frequency. >> **F0** is the source’s repetition rate, while **F1** is the tract’s lowest resonance. >> The vowel contains many harmonics, and the formants decide which are strong. The values belong to this example, not to every speaker.
21|The **/i/ filter** pairs a **low F1** with a **high F2** on the same 100 Hz comb. >> One strong group of harmonics sits at the low end. >> The next sits far higher, past a weak gap. >> More groups follow around **F3** and **F4**. || The harmonics keep their even spacing and positions. >> Only their heights change. >> So the filter’s effect is visible apart from the source’s repetition. || This pattern follows from the front constriction of /i/. >> Mode 1 moves the air at the narrowing, so added mass lowers F1. >> Mode 2 compresses the air there, so added stiffness raises F2. >> F1 and F2 end up far apart. || Each line needs two readings. >> Its horizontal position is its **frequency**. >> Its height is its **amplitude**. >> A tall low line is still low in frequency, even beside a weaker high line. || The harmonic lines sample the broad formant regions. >> The envelope their heights trace is the vowel. >> These values describe one schematic /i/, not every speaker.
22|The **/a/ filter** raises F1 and lowers F2 compared with /i/, bringing the two close together. >> **F1** sits at **750 Hz** and **F2** near **1200 Hz**. >> **F3** and **F4** stay higher. >> The source is unchanged, and the new tract shape alone makes a new vowel. || The filter reshapes harmonic **strengths**, and two kinds of change appear. >> Moving a formant shifts the favored region along the frequency axis. >> Changing a harmonic’s amplitude changes its height. || A formant can fall **between two harmonics**. >> The 750 Hz F1 lies between the **700** and **800 Hz** harmonics. >> Those neighbors grow stronger, and no new line appears at 750 Hz, since the filter can only reshape what the source supplies. || The two terms stay distinct. >> A **formant** is a resonance of the filter. >> A **harmonic** is a component of the source. >> The filter sets each harmonic’s strength, and the harmonics reveal where the formants are. The drawing’s values belong to this simplified case.
23|A **spectrum** shows **one instant**, while a **spectrogram** **stacks many spectra over time**. || A spectrum describes one short slice of sound. >> Frequency runs across, and amplitude runs up. >> The example takes three spectra from **/i/** and three from **/a/**. || Building the spectrogram takes two steps. >> First, each spectrum is turned on its side, so frequency runs upward. A peak at the right of a spectrum becomes a mark near the top. >> The slices are lined up left to right in time, so moving right means later, not higher. || Second, **height becomes darkness**. >> **Black is high amplitude, gray is medium and white is low**. >> Vertical position gives frequency, and darkness gives strength. >> A darkening band is getting stronger, and a rising band is moving higher in frequency. || From **/i/** to **/a/**, **F1 rises and F2 falls**. >> The two lowest bands move toward each other. >> The higher bands stay above with smaller changes. >> The spectrogram tracks the tract’s resonances as they change over time.

25|Formants can only be seen through the source harmonics, so their visibility depends on harmonic spacing. || **Close spacing** makes formants easy to find. >> With harmonics at **100, 200, 300, 400 Hz** and onward, a vowel with **F1 at 300** and **F2 at 2100 Hz** shapes many harmonics. >> Several lines cluster near each formant, and their heights trace its shape. || A higher **F0** widens the spacing. >> With harmonics at **250, 500, 750** and onward, fewer lines fall near each formant. >> A formant can fall between harmonics, and a single strong line may sit near a formant without marking its center. >> Telling harmonics from formants becomes very hard. || High voices push the spacing wider still. >> A **toddler’s** harmonics could be **400, 800** and onward. >> A **soprano’s** could be **800, 1600, 2400** and onward. >> Across the three spectra and spectrograms, dense components thin out into isolated traces. || The filter has not changed. >> The tract keeps the same resonances in every case. >> The higher source simply supplies fewer frequencies to reveal them.
