3|**Forward propagation** carries the input forward, layer by layer, to the outputs. The example input is **F1 = 320** and **F2 = 2000**. || The top first-layer neuron computes **w₁ times F1 plus w₂ times F2**. >> Its weights are **+0.005** and **−0.001**. >> F1 adds a positive amount, F2 adds a larger negative amount, and the sum is **−0.40**. || Multiply-then-add is an **inner product**. >> The other two first-layer neurons, with their own weights, give **6.64** and **2.72**. >> A negative value is still a usable value for the next layer. || Each later layer repeats the same operation. >> The second layer gives about **5.50** and **−5.93**. >> The third gives about **1.57, −1.95, −1.95** and **−2.88**. >> Order matters, since each layer needs the finished results of the one before. || The five final sums are **logits**, scores that are not yet probabilities, **0.622, −1.398, −1.300, −0.478** and **−1.076**. >> **Softmax** raises e to each logit and divides by the total of all five. >> The resulting probabilities are positive and add to 1, even where logits are negative. || **Done** gets the most, about **55.7%**, and **dean** gets about **8.1%**. >> Don, Dan and dune fall in between or below, at about 18.5%, 10.2% and 7.4%. >> These are current responses, not the true word. The hidden activations are linear, and the weights stay fixed during the pass.

6|**Backpropagation** starts by setting the network’s answer beside the right answer. >> The **computed output** gives **done 55.7%** and **dean** only **8.1%**. >> The **desired output** puts **1** on **dean** and **0** on the rest, because dean was spoken. >> The target names the correct word, so it can disagree with the network’s favorite. || **Cross-entropy loss** measures the mismatch through the true word’s probability. >> Here it is the negative log of the probability of dean. >> More probability on dean lowers the loss, and less raises it. || The calculation runs **backward**, from outputs to inputs. >> The final weights’ contribution is found first. >> That result feeds the calculation for hidden layer 3, then 2, then 1. >> Each step swaps in updated weights, such as **+0.1** becoming **+0.083** and **+0.6** becoming **+0.622**. || A **gradient** says how the loss changes when a weight changes slightly. >> The update subtracts the learning rate, **η**, times the gradient. >> A positive gradient lowers a weight, and a negative one raises it, so learning does not simply enlarge positive weights. || Stored forward activations stay fixed through the backward pass. >> Every gradient uses the original weights, forming one simultaneous update. >> A new forward pass is needed to see the updated predictions. >> Input connections get a smaller learning rate, because their inputs are raw hertz values.
7|**If we increase this w, will the error go up or down?** The target is **dean**, but the network favors **done**, so the question is what a small change does to dean’s probability. || The **pink-marked** connection joins the top neuron of the last hidden layer to **done**. >> The weight is positive, and the neuron’s activation is about **1.57**. >> Increasing the weight raises done’s score. >> Softmax shares one total among five words, so a stronger done leaves dean less, and the loss **rises**. >> The update should therefore decrease this weight. || The **blue-marked** connection joins **F1** to the top first-layer neuron. >> Increasing it raises that neuron’s value, since F1 is positive. >> That change then flows through many signed connections to every output. >> The first connection’s sign alone cannot predict whether the error rises or falls. || The full backward calculation decides it. >> For this network and the dean target, the blue connection’s gradient is also positive. >> A small increase raises the error, so the update decreases this weight too. >> Both marked weights fall here. The late one can be read almost straight from the output, while the early one needed every downstream path combined, which is the work backpropagation does.
8|Why **backpropagate**? Comparing the **last** weights with the **first** answers it. || The **last weights, W5**, determine the output probabilities directly. >> The error depends very directly on them. >> The gap between computed and desired outputs gives better W5 values right away. || The **first weights, W2**, sit far from the output. >> They shape the first activations, which pass through two more layers and other weights first. >> Their culpability for an error made at the very end is really hard to work out. >> Their effect also **includes** W5’s effect, since W5 is downstream. || So **W5 is the easy problem**, and the W2 problem contains it. >> Solving small problems first and reusing their answers for bigger, dependent ones is **dynamic programming**. >> **Backpropagation is Bellman–Viterbi dynamic programming.** >> It computes output-side effects once and reuses them for each earlier layer, instead of redoing the downstream work for every early weight. >> Working backward matches the structure, because an early layer’s effect includes every layer after it. || **Backpropagation = SGD + dynamic programming.** >> **SGD**, stochastic gradient descent, adjusts the weights. >> Backpropagation supplies its gradients efficiently through dynamic programming.
9|Training needs examples paired with answers, held in two **training matrices**. || **Formant_Inputs** is **110 × 2**. >> Each row is one recording’s **F1** and **F2** in hertz. >> The two columns are sound measurements, and each row is one example. || **TrueVowel_Outputs** is **110 × 5**. >> Its columns are **done, dune, dean, Don** and **Dan**. >> Each row has a **1** under the correct word and **0** elsewhere, a **one-hot** target. || Illustrative rows pair formants with words. >> **500, 1500** is **done**, **350, 1000** is **dune** and **320, 2000** is **dean**, the network’s example. >> **700, 1100** is **Don**, and **800, 1700** is **Dan**. >> These rows show the layout, not the full data. || The two matrices must stay aligned row by row. >> The third input row, 320 and 2000, matches the third target row, dean. >> Moving an input row without its target would pair sounds with wrong answers. >> Equal row counts are not enough, since the order must agree too. >> Five target columns list the categories. Each recording is still one example. || The network’s output is separate from the targets. >> Before backpropagation, it favors **done**, at 55.7%, for the dean example. >> Training compares the output with the target and never replaces the target with the network’s guess.

11|The **sigmoid activation function**, **σ(z) = 1 / (1 + exp(−z))**, turns an inner product z into a value between 0 and 1. || Its two limits catch the extremes. >> A **large positive** inner product gives a sigmoid **near 1**. >> A **large negative** one gives a sigmoid **basically 0**. >> **σ(−4) = 0.0180**, and **σ(4) = 0.9820**. || The curve is an S. >> **Linear activation**, the weighted sum, runs across, and **sigmoid activation** runs up. >> It is steep through the middle and flat at both ends. >> On the flat ends, big input changes make small output changes, so the response is gradual, not a pure switch. || The formula explains the shape. >> Large positive z makes exp(−z) tiny, so the output nears 1. >> Large negative z makes exp(−z) huge, so the output nears 0. || **Lord Edgar Adrian**, **Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, 1932**, gives biological context. >> **Neurons are nearly digital**, on or off, roughly. >> His work concerned firing rates, so the sigmoid models graded activity that acts almost like a switch at its extremes. || **Animal perception** shows the same pattern. >> Some things that seem alike are perceived as different, and some very different things are perceived as the same. >> Inputs on opposite sides of the steep middle give very different outputs, and inputs near a flat end give nearly equal ones.

Wrapping sigmoid around the old stored numbers would give a different, wrong result. || With the first layer done, its outputs are about **0.401, 0.999** and **0.938**. >> The second layer, recalculated and squashed, gives about **0.713** and **0.223**. >> The third ends at about **0.599, 0.609, 0.609** and **0.481**. || The outputs still use **softmax**. >> With every hidden neuron on sigmoid, **Dan** leads at about **32.0%**. >> **Dean** reaches about **23.0%**, while **done** drops to about 16.2%. >> Inputs and weights never changed, yet the output pattern shifted a great deal. || The weights were not trained. >> Dean improves over 8.1% but still trails Dan. >> So sigmoid can change the network’s favorite without the network having learned the right answer.
