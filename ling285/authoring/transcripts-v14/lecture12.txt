2|A **dynamic system** changes over time because its parts keep acting on one another. Economists use the same idea to describe how an economy rises and falls. >> **Woodford’s theory** is **Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium**, shortened to **DSGE**. >> The name packs three ideas together. The economy changes over time, random shocks keep disturbing it, and its linked markets settle toward balance together. || The stabilization picture plots two quantities through time. >> The **black curve** is **unemployment**. >> The **gray curve** is **inflation**. >> Without stabilization, both swing through tall, jagged peaks and deep troughs. With stabilization, the swings shrink and smooth out. || **Stabilization** is about the size of the swings. >> Both quantities keep changing, and they stay separate quantities. >> The aim is to avoid harmful extremes, such as very high unemployment or runaway inflation. || A spring and an economy pose the same basic question. >> What drives the fluctuation, and what can calm it? >> In a spring, inertia and the restoring force drive the swing. In an economy, shocks and the responses of its parts drive it. >> The curves picture the goal of smaller swings, without testing any particular policy.

9|Seven **principles of mass–spring systems** sum up how oscillators behave. || **Mass lowers frequency, and stiffness raises it.** >> A heavier load turns around more slowly. >> A stiffer spring pulls back harder and reverses the motion sooner. || **An n-mass system has n modes**, each with a higher frequency than the last. || **Where a change happens decides how much it matters.** >> Added mass lowers a mode’s frequency in proportion to how much that mass moves in the mode. >> Added stiffness raises a mode’s frequency in proportion to how much that spring stretches or compresses. >> Where movement is greatest, there is no stretching, and where stretching is greatest, there is no movement. Neighbors that move together keep their spacing, so the two peaks fall in alternating places. || One change can therefore have very different effects in different modes. >> It counts where the mode moves or stretches strongly. >> It barely counts at a node of that mode, and that node can still move in other modes. || **Endpoints set the harmonics.** >> In a **fixed–fixed system**, harmonics are whole-number multiples of the base frequency, because the base shape fits a whole number of times into each higher mode. >> In a **fixed–free system**, harmonics are **odd multiples**, because the base shape fits an odd number of times into each higher mode. >> Separate-mass chains follow the mode-counting rule, but their frequencies are not exact harmonics.

11|The **pop quiz** places extra mass or stiffness at one spot on a string, just left of the midpoint, and asks how **Mode 4** and **Mode 5** respond. || The two modes treat that spot very differently. >> **Mode 4** moves it strongly, and its neighbors move with it, so there is little stretching there. >> **Mode 5** barely moves it, while its neighbors move differently, so there is strong stretching there. >> Both descriptions refer to a full cycle, not a single frozen frame. || **Extra mass** acts through movement. >> Mode 4 must carry the added load through a big swing, so its frequency drops. >> Mode 5 hardly moves the spot, so its frequency barely changes. >> Mass lowers frequency only where the mass actually moves. || **Extra stiffness** acts through stretching. >> Mode 5 stretches the spot strongly, so the stiffer spring adds restoring force and raises its frequency. >> Mode 4 hardly stretches the spot, so its frequency barely changes. || Increasing **both** at that spot **lowers Mode 4 and raises Mode 5**. >> The location is the same in both modes. >> Its role differs, mainly movement in one mode and mainly stretching in the other, so one local change pushes the two modes in opposite directions.

15|The air in the vocal tract can be modeled as masses linked by springs. >> Moving air has **inertia**, so it tends to keep moving. >> Squeezed air pushes back, and the spring-like links stand for that restoring effect. >> The air elements move **longitudinally**, back and forth along the tube around their usual places. They do not flow steadily from the glottis to the lips. || The tube is **fixed–free**. >> The **glottis** is the fixed end. >> The open **lips** are the free end. || Its two lowest patterns match the first two formants. >> **Mode 1 is F1-like**, the lowest resonance. >> **Mode 2 is F2-like**, with one more reversal inside the tube. >> In the uniform tube, Mode 2 runs **three times** as fast as Mode 1, as fixed–free systems require. A different tract shape can change that ratio. || Each mode is a coordinated motion of the whole column. >> F1 and F2 are not separate air streams or separate sound sources. >> In places, neighbors travel together with steady gaps. Elsewhere, their gaps keep opening and closing. || Three markers locate the vowel constrictions. >> **/a/** is about **one-third** of the way from the glottis. >> **/i/** is about **two-thirds** of the way. >> **/u/** is at the **lips**. >> Each marker is the same spot in both modes. Mass matters where a mode moves strongly, and springiness matters where it compresses strongly, so one narrowing can affect F1 and F2 differently.
16|Vowels are described by **tongue height** and **front–back position**, and each one narrows the airway at a particular point. Five words fill the chart. || The high vowels narrow at different places. >> **Dean** is **high and front**, narrowing about **two-thirds** of the way from the glottis to the lips. >> **Dune** is **high and back**, narrowing at the **lips**. >> Same height, different constriction place. || The low vowels sit at the bottom. >> **Dan** is **low and front**, with no numbered constriction position. >> **Don** is **low and back**, narrowing about **one-third** of the way from the glottis. || **Done** is **central** and labeled **“No constriction,”** so it is the open reference shape. || Each label names a separate feature. >> High and low describe tongue height. >> Front and back describe the tongue’s position along the mouth. >> The fraction gives distance along the air path from the glottis. >> One shared label, such as high, leaves the other features free to differ. || The constriction’s location links articulation to sound. >> A narrowing changes the air’s local mass-like and spring-like behavior. >> Each formant’s response depends on how its mode moves and compresses the narrowed spot.

19|The **/u/ model** loads the open lips, the **free end** of the air column. Larger elements there stand for more **effective mass**, meaning more air to move at that spot, not heavier molecules. || Both lowest modes move strongly at the free end. >> In **Mode 1**, **motion dominates** at the lips. >> In **Mode 2**, **motion dominates** there too. || Added moving mass slows any mode that moves it. >> A bigger load is harder to speed up and reverse, so each cycle takes longer. >> Both modes slow, giving **F1 down and F2 down** relative to the uniform tube. || The two rows compare one lip location in two resonance patterns. >> Their internal shapes differ, since Mode 2 has an extra reversal. >> Both still move strongly at the lips, so the load slows both. >> F1 stays the lower formant and F2 the higher one, and each change is judged against its own uniform reference. || The shared direction comes from the lip region’s role in both modes. >> Motion dominates in both, so loading lowers both. >> Other constrictions sit where the modes behave differently, so not every constriction lowers every formant. The air keeps oscillating around its usual positions throughout.
20|The **/i/ constriction** sits near the front, about **two-thirds** of the way from the glottis to the lips. One region is narrowed, and it plays a different part in each mode. || In **Mode 1**, **mass motion dominates** there. >> The air at that spot moves strongly in this mode. >> Narrowing adds effective moving mass, which is slower to reverse. >> **F1 goes down**. || In **Mode 2**, **springiness dominates** there. >> The air at that spot is strongly squeezed and stretched in this mode. >> Narrowing strengthens the restoring response, which speeds the reversal. >> **F2 goes up**. || The region stays put, and its role changes with the mode. >> Where neighbors travel together, moving load counts most. >> Where neighbors keep pressing together and pulling apart, restoring force counts most. >> Comparing both rows prevents treating the narrowing as one instruction for every formant. || The outcome is **lower F1 and higher F2** than the uniform tube. >> The mass and spring effects do not cancel. >> Each mode follows the effect that dominates in its own pattern, so one constriction sends the two formants in opposite directions.
21|A narrowing about **one-third** of the way up from the glottis produces **/a/**. This back spot plays the opposite roles from the /i/ spot, although mass and stiffness work just as before. || For the F1-like **Mode 1**, **springiness dominates** here. >> This mode squeezes and stretches the air at the back strongly. >> The narrowing makes that air push back harder, the cycle gets quicker, and **F1 rises**. || For the F2-like **Mode 2**, **mass motion dominates** here. >> This mode carries the air at the back through a large swing. >> The narrowing adds moving load, the cycle gets slower, and **F2 falls**. || Why does one location behave two ways? >> In a compression role, the spot itself hardly travels while the air on either side crowds in and thins out. >> In a movement role, the spot travels far while the gaps around it stay about the same. >> At the one-third point, the first mode gives the spot a compression role and the second gives it a movement role. || So /a/ ends with **F1 higher and F2 lower**, the mirror image of /i/. >> Shifting a constriction changes which role each mode gives it. >> The arrows mark the predicted direction of change, not its measured size.

23|Each place in an air column has two properties, **movement** and **compression**. >> **Movement** is how far an air element travels. >> **Compression** is how the gaps between neighbors change. Shrinking gaps mean compression, and widening gaps mean **rarefaction**, or thinned air. >> Neighbors can travel together with steady gaps, or squeeze and spread around an element that hardly moves. || The animation separates one air column into its patterns. >> The moving **Schwa** row is the uniform reference. >> The **F1, F2 and F3** rows are the first three resonances of that column. >> The closed left end restricts movement, and the open right end allows it. Higher modes fit more alternating regions of movement and compression into the tube. || Compression and movement act as spring and mass. >> Crowded air pushes back toward its usual spacing. >> Moving air keeps going because of inertia. >> Together they keep the air oscillating around its usual positions. || Strong movement and strong compression sit in different places. >> Large movement means a lot of moving mass. >> Large spacing changes mean strong restoring force, even where the central element barely moves. || Following one element over a cycle gives its movement, and following its gaps gives compression. >> Added mass matters where movement is strong. >> Added stiffness matters where compression is strong. >> These places shift from mode to mode, and each row is a resonance of the same air, not a separate stream.
