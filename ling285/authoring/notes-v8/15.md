**Section 1: Two Formants Feed a Fully Connected Network**

- A **neural network** can act as a very simple **speech recognizer**. It takes the **two formants, F1 and F2**, of a vowel and decides which of **five words** it heard, **done, dune, dean, Don or Dan**. The networks studied here are the kind used fifteen or twenty years ago, and modern **large language models** use networks like these as their **atoms**, building them into much bigger structures.

- The network has **2 input neurons**, **three hidden layers** with **3, 2 and 4 neurons**, and **5 output neurons**. Every neuron connects to every neuron in the next layer, so it is **fully connected**.
  - The **input neurons** just take numbers in from the environment, such as one speaker's F1 of 400 and F2 of 1900 for some vowel.
  - **The knowledge of the network is in its weights.** Each connection has a weight, which is a positive or negative number, and the whole network is nothing more than a machine for processing numbers with those weights.

**Section 2: Forward Propagation Combines Weighted Inputs**

- **Forward propagation** carries one input through the network to a decision. The example input is **F1 of 320 and F2 of 2000**, which has the pattern of **dean**.
  - Each neuron computes an **inner product**. For the top neuron of the first hidden layer, the weight on F1 is multiplied by 320, the weight on F2 is multiplied by 2000, and the two products are added.
  - The result of that inner product, with no other change, is called the neuron's **linear activation**, and here it comes out slightly below zero.
  - The other neurons do the same with their own weights. Their activations then become the inputs to the next layer, which does more inner products, and so on until the output layer.

- At the end, the output numbers are turned into a **probability distribution**, a set of numbers between zero and one that **add up to one** and stand for the network's **decision**. The operation that does this is called **softmax**, and all that matters about it is that it converts numbers into probabilities, with a bigger number giving a bigger probability. Probabilities and percentages are the same idea, since a probability of 0.55 is a 55 percent chance.
  - Here the network gives **done about 56 percent**, and much less to each of the other words. So the network says, in effect, **"I heard done,"** even though the input was dean.

- **Knowledge has been reduced to positive and negative numbers, and inference has been reduced to multiplying and adding**, which are operations from second grade. **Inference** means working out a decision, and the systems people chat with every day make their inferences with nothing but these elementary operations.
  - This is less surprising than it seems, because brains are chemical machines. The chemistry of ions such as sodium, potassium and calcium inside neurons performs operations close to adding and multiplying.

**Section 3: Raising an Activation or a Weight**

- Because every step is an inner product, the effect of raising one number can be worked out without any arithmetic. Consider the top neuron of the last hidden layer, whose activation is about 1.57, and its weight of positive 0.1 into the **done** output.
  - If that **activation goes up**, its contribution to the done output, which is the activation times a positive weight, goes up, so the **probability of done increases**.
  - If that **weight goes up**, from 0.1 to 0.2, the contribution of the positive activation also grows, so the **probability of done increases** again.
  - **A positive number times a positive number is positive.** So raising either one of them makes that neuron push the output up more.

- The questions here are about understanding what happens, never about doing the multiplication. A good answer names the direction, up or down, and then the reason, such as "the probability of done goes up because the weight and the activation are both positive, so their product adds more to the done output."
  - In plain terms, each neuron votes for each output through its weight, and a positive vote from an active neuron always helps that output.

**Section 4: Backpropagation Uses the Difference Between Prediction and Target**

- **Every network starts out stupid**, because its weights start out **random**. It becomes smarter only by being told the right answer.
  - The right answer is given as a **desired output**. For a recording of dean, the desired output is **0, 0, 1, 0, 0**, which says there is a 100 percent chance it was dean and zero for everything else. This kind of list, with a single one and zeros everywhere else, is called a **one-hot encoding**.
  - The output the network actually produced is the **computed output**, and the gap between the computed output and the desired output is the network's **error**.
  - The desired outputs come from real data, such as a table of each student's formants together with the vowel that student actually said.

- **Backpropagation** is the algorithm that changes the weights so the network makes better decisions. If the same 320 and 2000 were given again after learning, the probability of done should go down and the probability of dean should go up.
  - **Learning happens gradually, one step at a time.** Just as with stochastic gradient descent, the weights do not jump to the right values in one step. They are nudged a little, then the next piece of data nudges them again, and so on.
  - Each weight moves a small step in whichever direction makes the error smaller, and the **learning rate** sets how big that step is.
  - The weights are updated **starting from the end**. First the weights into the output change, then the weights into the third hidden layer, then the second, then the first. The activations from the forward pass stay as they were while this backward pass happens.

- This is why AI needs huge **data centers**. A modern chatbot can have **trillions of weights**, and backpropagation has to nudge all of them, over and over, for months.

**Section 5: Predicting How a Weight Changes the Error**

- For the **last weight**, the one of 0.1 that links the top neuron of the last hidden layer to **done**, the effect is easy to see.
  - That weight multiplies an activation of about 1.57, so raising the weight would raise the probability of done.
  - The desired probability of done is **zero**, so raising the weight would make the error **worse**.
  - So that weight should be **lowered**, which shrinks its contribution and pulls the probability of done down toward zero. The decision takes only one inner product to reason out.

- For the **first weight**, the one of 0.005 near the input, the effect is very hard to see.
  - Changing it changes one activation in the first hidden layer. That changes the activations in the second hidden layer, which change the third, which finally change the output probabilities.
  - **Its effect on the decision is mediated by all the intermediate calculations**, and it also depends on all the later weights. So just by looking at it, there is no way to tell whether it should go up or down. Backpropagation still works it out.

**Section 6: Why Backpropagation Starts at the End**

- **The last weights directly determine the output probabilities**, so the error is very directly determined by them, and the difference between the computed and the desired output can be used right away to improve them.

- **The first weights affect their activations, which affect two more layers of activations along with other weights.** It is really hard to figure out how much an early weight is to blame for a mistake made all the way at the end. Its effect on the error also **includes the effect of the last weights**, since the last weights are downstream of it.

- So **improving the last weights is the easy problem**, and improving the first weights is a much bigger problem that already contains the easy one. The method for solving a series of problems like this, where small problems are solved first so they can be used for bigger problems that depend on them, is **dynamic programming**, developed by **Bellman** and **Viterbi**.
  - **Backpropagation is stochastic gradient descent plus dynamic programming.** Stochastic gradient descent changes the numbers gradually, and dynamic programming supplies the **back** in backpropagation.
  - Backpropagation was worked out in the **mid-1980s**, and in 2024 a Nobel Prize recognized it, because there would be no AI without it.
  - Research over the last ten years suggests that single neurons in the brain also work in a forward-then-backward way, using their chemistry, so this clean two-phase process may not be so different from what brains do.
  - The short version of the exam answer is that the last weight's effect on the decision is one simple step, while the first weight's effect goes through many layers and depends on the later weights, so the easy problem is solved first and its answer is reused for the harder ones.

**Section 7: Pairing Each Recording with Its Target**

- Training needs two tables of numbers, which are called **matrices**. Each row pairs **one recording** with **the vowel it really was**.
  - The **input matrix** has one row per recording and **two columns**, F1 and F2, because the network takes two numbers in. With about 110 recordings, it is 110 by 2.
  - The **output matrix** has one row per recording and **five columns**, one for each word, with a **1 marking the true vowel** and 0 in the other columns. It is 110 by 5.
  - So the network is told, row by row, that if it is given formants like 800 and 1700, the desired answer is one particular vowel, and if it is given 700 and 1100, the desired answer is another.

**Section 8: One Training Step in PyTorch**

- A short **Python** program, using the **PyTorch** library, builds and trains this network.

```python
import torch
from torch import nn

X = torch.load("Formant_Inputs.pt").float()
Y = torch.load("TrueVowel_Outputs.pt").float()

net = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Linear(2, 3),
    nn.Linear(3, 2),
    nn.Linear(2, 4),
    nn.Linear(4, 5)
)

sgd = torch.optim.SGD(net.parameters(), lr=0.1)
sgd.zero_grad()
loss = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()(net(X), Y)
loss.backward()
sgd.step()
```

- The **import** lines tell the program to use the **torch library**, which does all the background work so that the network can be described in just a few lines. The two **load** lines bring in the two matrices, the formants as X and the true vowels as Y.

- **Sequential** means the network is a **sequence of layers**, one after another. **Linear** means **do inner products**, since all a linear layer does is multiply and add.
  - **nn.Linear(2, 3)** describes the **first hidden layer**, which takes **2 inputs and provides 3 outputs**. The next lines take 3 and give 2, take 2 and give 4, and take 4 and give 5.
  - The network has **five layers**, but there are only **four lines**, because the **input layer does no processing**. It just receives numbers, so its only trace in the code is the 2 in the first line.

- The last lines carry out **one training step** using all 110 rows at once. **SGD** is stochastic gradient descent, and **lr** is the **learning rate**, which sets the size of each step. The **loss** measures the network's mistakes, comparing its outputs with the true vowels, and **backward** runs backpropagation to work out how each weight should change. **step** then actually changes the weights.
  - In plain terms, the program describes the network, shows it the data, measures how wrong it is, works backward to find who is to blame, and then adjusts the weights a little.

**Section 9: The Sigmoid Activation Function**

- **Lord Edgar Adrian** won the **Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1932** for his discovery that **neurons are nearly digital**. A real neuron is roughly **on or off**, either firing or not firing, instead of taking any value at all. The neurons in the network so far are not like that, because their activations are any real numbers, such as slightly below zero, about 6.6 or about 2.7.

- Knowing this discovery, people added an **activation function** called the **sigmoid** to neural networks. The sigmoid takes the inner product and squashes it.
  - If the inner product is a **large negative number**, the sigmoid gives about **0**, so the neuron is **off**. An inner product of minus 4 gives almost exactly zero, and minus 10, minus 100 or minus 1000 all give about zero too.
  - If the inner product is a **large positive number**, the sigmoid gives about **1**, so the neuron is **on**. An inner product of 4 gives almost exactly one, and 6 or 100 also give about one.
  - Only inner products **near zero** get values in between, so the sigmoid **digitizes** the neuron, much like the chemistry that keeps real neurons from firing without limit.

- The sigmoid also matches something deep about **perception** in animals and humans. **Some things that are very different are perceived as the same, and some things that are very similar are perceived as different.**
  - A friend is recognized as the same person whether they wear a hat or not, or a different shirt on a different day. Perception ignores many real differences, just as the sigmoid gives minus 1000, minus 100 and minus 20 all the same value.
  - Two universities can be close to each other, both large, and alike in their labs and their football, yet USC and UCLA are perceived as completely different. Small differences can matter enormously, just as two inputs just below and just above zero land on opposite sides of the sigmoid's jump.
  - So the sigmoid lets the same difference in input count for nothing in one place and count for everything in another. In plain terms, the flat parts of the curve lump different things together, and the steep middle splits nearly identical things apart.

**Section 10: Applying the Sigmoid One Neuron at a Time**

- Adding the sigmoid to each hidden neuron changes every number downstream. The inputs stay as formants, but each hidden neuron now passes on its sigmoid value instead of its raw inner product.
  - The first hidden neuron's inner product, slightly below zero, becomes about **0.4**.
  - The second, about 6.6, becomes about **1**, so that neuron is fully on.
  - As each neuron is changed, the activations in the later layers are recalculated, and the output probabilities shift.

- With the same random weights and the sigmoid on all nine hidden neurons, the decision moves away from done. The network now spreads its probability more evenly and gives its highest probability to **Dan**, around one third, so it is **still wrong** about dean. The sigmoid changes how the network computes, but only training with backpropagation can give it good weights.

**Section 11: The Same Program with the Sigmoid**

- The program changes in only one way. **nn.Sigmoid** is inserted after each **hidden** linear layer, which means "apply the sigmoid," or make that layer digital.

```python
net = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Linear(2, 3), nn.Sigmoid(),
    nn.Linear(3, 2), nn.Sigmoid(),
    nn.Linear(2, 4), nn.Sigmoid(),
    nn.Linear(4, 5)
)
```

- The **last layer has no sigmoid**, because its outputs go straight into the loss calculation, which turns them into probabilities with softmax. The rest of the training program, with SGD at a learning rate of 0.1, stays the same.

- Whether adding the sigmoid makes the vowel recognizer better or worse is something to test by training both versions. Other activation functions that look somewhat like the sigmoid, such as one called **ReLU**, can be swapped in to see whether they improve recognition. **Many of the most basic ideas in AI come from how real brains work**, such as neurons, inner products that gather input from many neurons, and the near-digital firing captured by the sigmoid.
