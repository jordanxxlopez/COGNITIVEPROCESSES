**Section 1: The Elements and the Two Rules of the Harmonic Oscillator**

- **A harmonic oscillator is built from only two quantities, x and v.** The quantity x is how far something is displaced from its resting point, and v is its **velocity**, how fast and in which direction x is moving. Neither variable is interesting alone. The whole system is about **how x and v change over time as a result of their relation to each other**.

- **A dot over a variable means its change over time, in Isaac Newton's notation.** So ẋ is the change in x as time increases, and v̇ is the change in v as time increases.
  - The sign of the dotted quantity gives the direction of change. When ẋ is positive, x is rising, and when ẋ is negative, x is falling. The same holds for v.
  - The size of the dotted quantity gives how quickly the change happens. A large positive ẋ means x is climbing quickly, and a small positive ẋ means it is climbing slowly.

- **The first rule says that the rate of change of x equals the current value of v, so ẋ is v.** Whatever sign v has at a given moment is the direction x moves at that moment. The quantity v directly steers x.

- **The second rule says that the rate of change of v equals the negative of x, so v̇ is minus x.** The **minus sign** is the heart of the system. It means v is always pushed in the direction opposite to wherever x currently is.
  - The defining feature of the harmonic oscillator is that **x and v change each other**. The rate at which x changes is set by v, and the rate at which v changes is set by x. Neither variable can change on its own terms, and this two-way dependence is what produces repeating back-and-forth motion.


**Section 2: A Worked Example with x and v**

- **Suppose that at some moment v equals negative 5.** The first rule gives ẋ equal to v, so ẋ is negative 5. The rate of change of x is negative, so x **will go down**. The value of x itself does not matter for this conclusion. Only the sign of v decides whether x is rising or falling.

- **Now suppose that at some moment x equals negative 5.** The second rule gives v̇ equal to minus x, and minus negative 5 is positive 5. The two minus signs cancel, so the rate of change of v is positive, and v **will go up**.
  - This is the restoring behavior in action. When x is below zero, v is pushed upward, and an upward v then lifts x back toward zero through the first rule.
  - The example is why x and v must be read together. Knowing v tells what x is about to do, and knowing x tells what v is about to do.

- **The same reading works for the positive cases.** When v is positive 5, ẋ is positive and x rises. When x is positive 5, v̇ is negative and v falls. These are statements about the direction of change, not about whether the current value is positive or negative. A velocity that is going up can be moving from a large negative value toward zero before it becomes positive.


**Section 3: Two Sign Relationships and the Restoring Loop**

- **The two rules treat the sign differently.** The rule ẋ equals v keeps the sign. Positive velocity makes position rise, and negative velocity makes position fall. The rule v̇ equals minus x reverses the sign. Positive position makes velocity fall, and negative position makes velocity rise.

- **Putting the two rules together, whenever x moves away from zero in either direction, the second rule turns v around, and the first rule then carries x back toward zero.** This **restoring relationship** is what keeps the system from drifting off and instead makes it swing back and forth.
  - The quantity changed by v̇ is velocity, not position. A positive position can go with a decreasing velocity even while the object is still moving toward larger positions. Keeping each quantity separate from its rate of change makes the back-and-forth motion understandable.


**Section 4: The x-v System Traced Through Time**

- **The system can be followed moment by moment, starting with x at its highest positive value and v equal to zero.**
  - At the start, x is positive, so v̇ is negative and v begins to drop below zero. As soon as v is negative, ẋ is negative, so x starts to decrease, slowly at first since v is only slightly negative.
  - While x is still positive, v keeps getting more negative. A more negative v makes x fall faster, so x drops more and more steeply.
  - When x reaches zero, v̇ equals zero, so v stops decreasing. At this instant v is at its most negative value, which means x is crossing zero at its fastest speed.
  - Once x is below zero, v̇ becomes positive, so v starts rising back toward zero. The value x is still falling, but more and more slowly.
  - When v returns to zero, x has reached its lowest, most negative value. At that point x stops moving down and turns around.
  - With x negative, v keeps rising and becomes positive, so x climbs. The value v reaches its largest positive value just as x crosses zero on the way up, and then x returns to its maximum as v comes back to zero. The system is back where it started, and the whole cycle repeats.

- **Computing the system at a series of separate moments gives a sequence of points, where each new value of x and v is worked out from the values just before it.** Connecting these points produces two smooth waves.
  - The x wave starts at the top, dips below zero to a lowest point, and climbs back up past zero to the top again. The v wave starts at zero, dips below zero to its lowest point, returns to zero, and then rises above zero.
  - The two waves have the same shape but are shifted in time by a quarter of a cycle. The value v is at its extremes exactly when x crosses zero, and v is zero exactly when x is at its extremes.


**Section 5: The Oscillation and Vibration Dynamical System**

- **A dynamical system is a set of variables together with rules for how those variables change over time.** The x-v system is a specific kind called an **oscillation** or **vibration dynamical system**. Oscillation and vibration mean the same thing here, a quantity repeatedly swinging back and forth around a middle value.

- **The relationship can be drawn as a loop of influence, with x and v each in a circle.** One arrow runs from v to x, standing for ẋ equals v, since v determines how x changes. A second arrow runs from x back to v, standing for v̇ equals minus x, since x determines how v changes. The loop is closed, so a change in v changes x, and that change in x goes on to change v again.
  - The loop between x and v is enough, by itself, to generate the repeated swinging. Once the system is set in motion it keeps cycling without anything outside it having to push it again.
  - Plotted against time, x traces a wave that starts at its peak, falls below zero, rises back to the same peak, and repeats. Each cycle reaches the same height and the same depth, and each cycle takes the same amount of time. The value v follows the same kind of repeating wave over the same stretch of time.


**Section 6: Everything's a Harmonic Oscillator, If You're Brave Enough**

- **The same two-variable structure of ẋ equals v and v̇ equals minus x shows up in a huge range of systems**, including the air in the vocal tract, the vocal folds, economies, music, and matter itself.

- **Calling a system a harmonic oscillator means identifying something in it that plays the role of x, a displacement away from a resting state, and something that plays the role of v, the rate at which that displacement changes.** It also means accepting a simplification, since real systems are usually messier than two perfectly linked variables. The harmonic oscillator is still useful as a model, since it captures the basic back-and-forth pattern that all of these systems share.


**Section 7: A Slice of Air in the Vocal Tract**

- **The vocal tract is the air-filled passage from the vocal folds in the throat up through the mouth to the lips.** A thin slice of air inside it contains a large number of air particles, and **pressure** in this slice depends on how close together the particles are.
  - **High pressure** means the air particles are very close together. **Low pressure** means the air particles are very far apart. The particles themselves stay the same, in number and in size. The only thing that changes is the distance between them.

- **Compression is the state in which particles are squeezed closer together than their normal spacing, and rarefaction is the state in which they are spread farther apart.** The normal spacing, where the air is neither squeezed nor stretched, is the **equilibrium spacing**.
  - Tracking one pair of neighboring particles, at equilibrium they sit at 100 percent of their equilibrium distance. At the strongest compression the pair is squeezed to about 56 percent of that distance, a little more than half as far apart as usual. At the strongest rarefaction the same pair is stretched to about 141 percent.
  - The slice cycles through these states continuously. It compresses, passes back through equilibrium, rarefies, and passes back through equilibrium again. In real speech the motion is much smaller than these exaggerated values, and the slice is an idealized piece of the air.

- **The slice of air behaves like a harmonic oscillator.** The spacing of the particles, measured relative to equilibrium, plays the role of x. Air that is squeezed together tends to push itself apart, and air that is spread out tends to be pulled back together, so any departure from equilibrium spacing is pushed back toward equilibrium. This is the same restoring relationship as v̇ equals minus x, and it is why the slice keeps swinging between compression and rarefaction.


**Section 8: There's a Cosine in Your Mouth**

- **The back-and-forth motion of the air slice follows a cosine, so x equals the cosine of the phase.** Here x measures the **normalized expansion** of the slice. Normalized means the values are scaled so that the largest stretch equals plus 1 and the largest squeeze equals minus 1.
  - An x of plus 1 means the particles are **farthest apart**, maximum rarefaction. An x of 0 means the particles are at **equilibrium** spacing. An x of minus 1 means the particles are **closest together**, maximum compression. Any positive x means some degree of rarefaction, and any negative x means some degree of compression.

- **The phase is the position within a cycle, and one complete cycle corresponds to the phase increasing by 2π.** Running the phase from 0 to 6π covers exactly three full cycles, ending at 2π, 4π and 6π.
  - At phase 0 the cosine equals plus 1, so the slice is at maximum rarefaction. As the phase increases, x decreases. A value such as plus 0.74 means the particles are still farther apart than equilibrium, but less so than at the start.
  - At phase π over 2 the cosine equals 0, so the slice passes through equilibrium. A value such as minus 0.54 means the slice is now compressed. At phase π the cosine equals minus 1, so the slice reaches maximum compression.
  - At phase 3π over 2, x returns to 0, and at phase 2π, x is back at plus 1, completing the pattern of maximum rarefaction, then compression, then rarefaction again. Over the next two cycles x passes through values such as plus 0.31, minus 0.05, minus 0.21 and plus 0.45, and each value's sign says whether the slice is rarefied or compressed at that moment.

- **The cosine is the same wave that the x-v system produces when x starts at its maximum and v starts at zero.** In both cases the curve begins at the top and swings downward. So the vibrating air in the vocal tract follows the harmonic oscillator relationship, and its expansion over time traces a cosine.


**Section 9: Vowels and the Shape of the Vocal Tract**

- **Vowels differ from one another in where the tongue sits inside the vocal tract.** These positions can be imaged with magnetic resonance imaging, which produces a side view of the head cut down the middle, with the speaker facing left, the tongue as the large bright mass in the mouth, and the dark regions as air.

- **Vowels are organized along two dimensions, height and frontness.** **Height** is whether the tongue body is raised toward the roof of the mouth, **high**, or lowered, **low**. **Frontness** is whether the tongue body is pushed forward toward the teeth, **front**, pulled back toward the throat, **back**, or held in between, **central**.
  - In **"dean,"** the vowel is **high and front**. The tongue body is raised and pushed forward, close to the hard roof of the mouth, which leaves a large open air space in the throat behind the tongue.
  - In **"dune,"** the vowel is **high and back**. The tongue is still raised, but its highest point is pulled back toward the soft rear part of the roof of the mouth.
  - In **"Dan,"** the vowel is **low and front**. The tongue is lowered and the mouth is more open, with the tongue body still toward the front. In **"Don,"** the vowel is **low and back**. The tongue is lowered and pulled back into the throat, which narrows the air space behind it.
  - In **"done,"** the vowel is **central**. The tongue rests in the middle of the mouth, neither strongly raised nor lowered and neither pushed forward nor pulled back.

- **Each tongue position gives the air in the vocal tract a different shape.** A differently shaped column of air vibrates in a different way, and that difference in vibration is what makes one vowel sound different from another.


**Section 10: Vowels as Combined Vibrations**

- **A single cosine describes air vibrating at one frequency, but a vowel is more complex.** The air in the vocal tract vibrates at **several frequencies at the same time**, and the vowel is the **sum** of those vibrations. Since each vibration is a cosine, and each cosine is the behavior of a harmonic oscillator, a vowel can be modeled as **harmonic oscillators added together**.
  - **Frequency** is the number of complete cycles a vibration makes per second, measured in **hertz (Hz)**. A vibration at f hertz is written as the cosine of 2π times f times t, where t is time in seconds. Multiplying by 2π converts the number of cycles into phase, since one full cycle equals a phase of 2π.

- **The vowel /i/, the vowel in "dean," is modeled as a 1 Hz vibration plus a 4 Hz vibration, the cosine of 2πt plus the cosine of 8πt.** The first term is the 1 Hz vibration, since 2π times 1 is 2π, and the second is the 4 Hz vibration, since 2π times 4 is 8π. The result is a slow wave with fast ripples riding on top of it. Over 3 seconds, the slow component completes 3 cycles while the fast component completes 12.

- **The vowel /a/, the vowel in "Don," is modeled as a 2 Hz vibration plus a 3 Hz vibration, the cosine of 4πt plus the cosine of 6πt.** Over 3 seconds the 2 Hz component completes 6 cycles and the 3 Hz component completes 9. The two frequencies are close together, so the sum produces a different repeating pattern of tall and short swings than /i/ does.

- **These frequencies are example values, not measured vowel frequencies.** They are chosen low enough that the motion can be watched, and the two components in each vowel have **equal amplitudes**, each swinging between plus 1 and minus 1.
  - Since each component reaches at most plus 1 and at least minus 1, their sum ranges between plus 2 and minus 2. The sum reaches plus 2 only when both cosines peak at the same moment, which for both vowels happens at 0, 1, 2 and 3 seconds, since every component completes a whole number of cycles in each second.
  - At other moments the components partly cancel or partly reinforce each other. At one moment /i/ can be at minus 1.21 while /a/ is at plus 0.58, and at another /i/ can be at plus 0.62 while /a/ is at minus 1.62.

- **The same sign rule from the single cosine still applies.** **Positive x means rarefaction**, with particles farther apart than equilibrium, and **negative x means compression**, with particles closer together. For /i/ and /a/, the slice of air is squeezed and stretched in two different time patterns, and those two patterns are what distinguish the two vowels. Watching them at one-quarter speed makes the different rhythms visible.


**Section 11: Praat Demonstration**

- **Praat is a free computer program used in phonetics to record and analyze speech.** It displays the **waveform** of a recorded sound, the air pressure changing over time, and it can break that sound into the frequencies it contains.

- **Recording vowels such as those in "dean" and "Don" in Praat shows the combined vibrations directly.** Each vowel produces its own repeating waveform, and each waveform is made of several frequencies added together, the same idea as the two-frequency models of /i/ and /a/.


**Section 12: Two Things Are Vibrating, the Vocal Tract Air and the Vocal Folds**

- **During speech, two things vibrate.** The first is the air in the vocal tract, the vibration modeled by the cosines and sums of cosines. The second is the **vocal folds**.

- **The vocal folds are two folds of tissue in the larynx, the voice box in the throat.** When air from the lungs pushes up against the closed folds, pressure builds until the folds are forced apart. Once they open and air rushes through, the pressure between them drops and the folds come back together. The cycle repeats many times per second.
  - This repeated opening and closing is itself an oscillation, with the folds swinging away from their closed position and being pulled back again.
  - Each opening lets a puff of air through, so the vocal folds chop the steady airflow from the lungs into a rapid series of puffs. Those puffs set the air in the vocal tract vibrating, and the shape of the vocal tract, set by the tongue position, determines which combination of frequencies that air vibrates at.

- **Speech sounds such as vowels therefore come from one oscillator driving another.** The vocal folds vibrate, and the air in the vocal tract vibrates in response. The folds are moving tissue, while the air is undergoing changing compression and rarefaction, so an explanation of speech must account for both.


**Section 13: The Harmonic Oscillator in Economics**

- **The harmonic oscillator pattern also appears in the business cycle, the repeated rise and fall of economic activity over time.** The business cycle is plotted with **time** on the horizontal axis and the **level of real output** on the vertical axis. **Real output** is the total amount of goods and services an economy produces, adjusted so that price changes do not distort the comparison across years.
  - At a **peak**, output is at a high point. During a **recession**, output falls. At a **trough**, output reaches a low point. During a **recovery**, output rises again until the next peak. The sequence then repeats from the start.

- **The swings happen around a rising straight line labeled trend or growth.** Over the long run output tends to increase, so each later peak can be higher than the one before. The oscillation is the part of output that swings above and below this trend line, so repeated fluctuation can coexist with an overall increase.
  - To treat the business cycle as a harmonic oscillator, the distance of output above or below the trend plays the role of x. Output above the trend is followed by a fall back toward and below it, and output below the trend is followed by a rise back toward and above it, the same back-and-forth pattern produced by ẋ equals v and v̇ equals minus x.


**Section 14: Music and Harmonic Oscillators**

- **Music is built from harmonic oscillators.** Every musical note is a vibration at a particular frequency. A plucked string, the column of air inside a wind instrument, and a vibrating reed each swing back and forth around a resting state, just as x does in the x-v system.

- **The pitch of a note corresponds to its frequency.** A higher-pitched note vibrates more times per second, and a lower-pitched note vibrates fewer times per second.
  - Sheet music is a written record of which vibrations to produce and when. When several notes sound together, their vibrations add, the same way the 1 Hz and 4 Hz cosines add to form the model of /i/.


**Section 15: Matter and the Quantum Harmonic Oscillator**

- **The harmonic oscillator appears in physics at the scale of atoms as the quantum harmonic oscillator.** In quantum physics a vibrating system cannot vibrate with just any amount of energy. It can only take certain specific energy levels, which is what **quantized** means.

- **Matter is made up of atoms with different numbers of electrons, some vibrating at a low frequency and some at a high frequency.** In the quantum description, electrons in an atom are not tiny balls on tracks but **wave modes**, specific allowed patterns of vibration.

- **Magnesium shows two ways of drawing an atom.** A neutral magnesium atom has 12 **electrons**, and its nucleus contains 12 **protons**. Its **ground-state configuration**, the arrangement of electrons at lowest energy, is written 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s².
  - In the older **solar system** drawing, also called the **Bohr-style shell** drawing, electrons are particles that travel on circular paths around the nucleus, arranged in shells of 2, 8 and 2. This is a historical model of how the shells are arranged.
  - In the **quantum orbital** description, each occupied wave mode, called an **orbital**, contributes a cloud of **electron density** around the nucleus. Darker shading is greater density, a region where an electron is more likely to be found.
  - The **total electron density** of magnesium is built by adding together the densities of all its occupied orbitals, 1s², 2s², 2p⁶ and 3s². The superscript counts the electrons in each orbital group, and 2p⁶ includes all three filled p orbitals. The sum is a sum of **probability densities**, and the sizes and shading contrasts are drawn schematically rather than to exact scale. The innermost 1s electrons form the densest cloud close to the nucleus, the 2s and 2p electrons form a middle layer, and the 3s electrons form the faint outermost layer.

- **Atomic orbitals are quantized wave modes, which connects them to the idea of vibration, but magnesium is not a harmonic-oscillator atom.** The quantum harmonic oscillator is a specific model, and real atoms like magnesium follow related but different rules.


**Section 16: The x-v System in Python**

- **The x-v system can be computed step by step in Python, producing the same sequence of points traced through time.** The system starts with x at its maximum and v at zero, and time is divided into many small steps of size dt.
  - At each step, ẋ is set equal to the current v, following the first rule, and v̇ is set equal to the negative of the current x, following the second rule.
  - The new x is the old x plus ẋ multiplied by the step size, and the new v is the old v plus v̇ multiplied by the step size.
  - Repeating this many times builds up the full x wave and v wave one small step at a time. Smaller steps make the computed points follow the true smooth waves more closely.

- **The program uses numpy for the arrays and matplotlib for the plot.** It sets dt to 0.01 and the number of steps to 1000, fills two arrays with zeros, sets x[0] to 1.0 and v[0] to 0.0, and then loops over the steps. Inside the loop, x_dot takes the value of v[n], v_dot takes the value of minus x[n], and the next entries are x[n] plus x_dot times dt and v[n] plus v_dot times dt. Finally it plots x and v against t.

- **The plotted output reproduces the behavior of the harmonic oscillator.** The value x starts at the top and traces a cosine wave, and v starts at zero, drops negative first, and traces the same wave shape shifted by a quarter of a cycle. The value v reaches its extremes whenever x crosses zero, and v passes through zero whenever x reaches its extremes.
