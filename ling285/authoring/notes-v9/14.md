## p2
- Speech uses **two major vibrating systems**. The first is the **vocal folds**, a **membrane** that is **fixed on all sides** and made of a whole bunch of masses and springs. It vibrates in many modes at once, with all the masses going up and down together in Mode 1, half going up while the other half goes down in Mode 2, and more complicated patterns above that, and all five modes add together into one combined motion.
  - Because the vocal folds are a fixed-fixed system, their frequencies are **harmonics**, which are whole-number multiples of the lowest one.
  - The **bottom-most harmonic** is called **F0**. Each person has a different F0, but the other harmonics are always its multiples.

## p3
- The second system is the **air in the vocal tract**. It is **fixed at the glottis**, because the closed vocal folds keep the air there from moving back and forth, and it is **free at the lips**, where the air can go in and out. A fixed end and a free end select only the **odd harmonics**, so its frequencies are one, three, five and seven times the lowest one.

## p4
- Each fixed-free mode is built from **quarter-wavelength packets**, each running from no motion at a still point to the most motion at a moving point. **Mode 1 holds one packet, Mode 2 holds three, Mode 3 holds five and Mode 4 holds seven.**
  - Two packets would end with a still point at the free end, which is impossible, so only odd numbers of packets fit, and that is why the formants are odd multiples of the first.

## p5
- In the vocal tract, the air moves **longitudinally**, back and forth along the tube, not up and down. Each formant is one mode of this longitudinal motion, with F1 the lowest mode and F2 the next.
  - The modes look like the building's modes turned on their side, with the fixed glottis end at the left and the free lip end at the right.

## p6
- **The closed end is always springy and the open end is always massy, in every mode.** Air can be squeezed against a closed end, but there is nothing to squeeze against at an open end.
  - Near the **closed end**, the slivers of air come very close together and then far apart, so the air **compresses and expands** and acts like a **spring**.
  - Near the **open end**, the slivers stay equally far apart and simply go back and forth, so the air acts like a **mass**.
  - In plain terms, the lip end swings like a heavy door, and the throat end squeezes like a pillow.
- In higher modes, springy places and massy places **alternate** more and more times along the tube.

## p7
- A constriction can be judged at any spot, in any mode, with the same chain of reasoning. Take a spot inside the tube where, in **Mode 3**, the lines of air are bunching up and spreading apart while hardly moving back and forth. Constricting the tract at that spot raises **F3**.
  - The first step is that constricting air always **increases both its mass and its stiffness**, just as squeezing a tire makes it stiffer.
  - The second step is that at this spot in Mode 3, the air is acting like a **pure spring**, with compression and expansion but no movement back and forth, so the extra mass does not matter.
  - The third step is that only the extra stiffness counts, and **raising stiffness raises frequency**, so **F3 goes up**.
- The answer is never memorized. It is **built one claim at a time**, with each claim resting on the one before it, which is the same way any good argument is built in science or in art.

## p8
- Images taken while people speak show where each vowel squeezes the air in the tube that runs from the closed glottis to the open lips.
  - The vowel in **done** has **no constriction** at all.
  - **dean** squeezes the air near the front, about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - **Don** squeezes the air far back in the throat, about **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - **dune** squeezes the air **at the lips** by rounding them, and **Dan** is a low front vowel.

## p9
- A constriction changes both the **inertia** of the air, which is its mass, and its **restoring stiffness**. In the mass-spring model, a constriction is drawn as **heavier masses and stiffer springs** at that spot.
  - It always raises both, but because moving places and springy places alternate, **its effect depends on what the air is doing there in each mode**.
  - **Whenever stiffness is what matters at a spot, the frequency goes up, and whenever mass is what matters, the frequency goes down.**

## p10
- The same location in the tube can act differently in the two modes, so both modes have to be checked separately. A spot can be massy in Mode 1 and springy in Mode 2, and then one constriction lowers F1 while raising F2.
  - That is why a vowel is defined by the pair of formants, not by one formant alone.

## p11
- **/u/** squeezes the air at the lips. At the open end the air goes back and forth with hardly any compression in **every** mode, so the air there is a pure mass. The extra stiffness does not matter, the extra mass does, and **F1 and F2 both go down**.
  - Compared with schwa's reference values of about **500 and 1500 hertz**, a measured /u/ had an F1 down in the low two hundreds, well below 500.

## p12
- **/i/** squeezes the air about two thirds of the way from the glottis.
  - In **Mode 1**, the air there moves back and forth, so it acts like a mass, the extra mass counts, and **F1 goes down**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the air there compresses and expands, so it acts like a spring, the extra stiffness counts, and **F2 goes up**.
  - A speaker moving from schwa into /i/ showed exactly this, with **F1 going down and F2 going up**.

## p13
- **/a/** squeezes the air about one third of the way from the glottis.
  - In **Mode 1**, that spot is close enough to the closed end that the air is mostly springy, so the extra stiffness counts and **F1 goes up**.
  - In **Mode 2**, that spot sits between two springy regions, so the air there is mostly moving, the extra mass counts, and **F2 goes down**.
  - A speaker producing /a/ showed **F1 going up and F2 going down**, and a typical /a/ has its F1 and F2 fairly close together.

## p14
- Across all three vowels, the logic is the same. **Find whether the air at the constriction is mostly moving or mostly compressing in each mode, then decide whether the extra mass or the extra stiffness counts.**
  - At the lips, the air is massy in both modes, so /u/ lowers both formants.
  - Two thirds along, the air is massy in Mode 1 and springy in Mode 2, so /i/ lowers F1 and raises F2.
  - One third along, the air is springy in Mode 1 and massy in Mode 2, so /a/ raises F1 and lowers F2.

## p15
- The vocal folds are the **source**, and their mass and stiffness set **F0** and therefore the spacing of the harmonics.
  - **Big, heavy, loose vocal folds** have a large mass and small stiffness, so their **F0 is low**, around 100 hertz, with harmonics at 200, 300 and so on.
  - **Tighter, lighter vocal folds** have a higher F0, such as 250 hertz, and **very tight, light vocal folds** have an even higher F0, such as 350 hertz.
- **F0 is not fixed while people talk.** Speakers keep changing F0 by tightening and loosening the vocal folds, and listeners use those changes to get meaning.
  - **The dog slept here** said as a statement has F0 falling at the very end, while the same words as a question have F0 rising at the end.
  - The way F0 moves also tells listeners whether a person is being sarcastic, pretending, or even lying.

## p16
- The air in the vocal tract is the **filter**, and its formants depend on **where the constriction is**. Schwa has F1 near 500 and F2 near 1500, /u/ has lower formants, /i/ has F1 and F2 very far apart, and /a/ has them very close together.
- **The core rule is that a formant encourages the closest harmonics to be audible, and harmonics far from that formant are muted.**
  - **The vowel shape is the filter. The voiced source supplies the harmonics, and the filter chooses which harmonics survive.**

## p17
- **The lower F0 is, the more harmonics the formants can shape.** With harmonics every 100 hertz, many harmonics sit under each formant, so the shaped spectrum traces the formant peaks smoothly.
- With very light, tight vocal folds, a formant at 500 hertz may have **no harmonic near it to amplify**, so sparse harmonics can miss a formant or make it look ambiguous.
  - An **opera singer** is an extreme case, which is why sung vowels are hard to identify, and singers are even trained to shape the mouth so the formants line up with the harmonics.
  - A **small child** has an F0 near 500 hertz, so very few harmonics fall near the formants, and only caretakers used to the child's speech understand it easily.
  - A simple way to see it is that the formants are the same in every case, but a high voice gives the listener only a few harmonics to hear them through.

## p18
- A **spectrogram** maps many spectra onto one display over time. **Time** runs along the horizontal axis, **frequency** runs up the vertical axis, and **darkness** shows how much energy, or amplitude, there is.
- F0 can be measured in **Praat** by taking a little piece of speech, viewing a spectral slice and picking the lowest harmonic. One measured voice had F0 around 170 hertz, and the next harmonic sat at about twice that value.

## p19
- Saying **Where were you a year ago?** brings every idea about speech together in one short sentence. The vocal folds supply harmonics the whole time, and every new sound changes where the tongue and lips squeeze the air, so the formants keep moving from one moment to the next.
  - On a spectrogram of the sentence, **time** runs along the bottom, **frequency** runs up, and the **dark bands are the formants**.
  - Where the vowel sounds like **/i/**, **F2 rises high and F1 stays low**, because the squeeze lands where Mode 2 is springy and Mode 1 is massy.
  - During **you**, the formants **drop**, because rounding the lips adds mass right where the air moves the most, and that lowers all of them.
  - Through **a year ago**, F2 goes up and then down again as the tongue moves from one vowel to the next.
- **All the formants are constantly changing during speech, so speech is not a string of frozen vowels.** Every rise and fall of a formant can be predicted with the same mass and spring reasoning, by asking where the constriction is and whether the air there is moving or compressing in each mode.
  - This constant change is why a network that sees two formants from one frozen moment cannot understand real speech, and why speech recognition needs systems that take in whole stretches of sound over time.
  - In plain terms, a spectrogram of a real sentence is a moving record of the tongue and lips, and reading it means following the dark bands as they rise and fall.

## p20
- A spliced recording reveals how complex speech perception is. The **m** part of a recorded **ma** is joined to the **a** part of a recorded **na**, and listeners clearly hear **na**, not ma.
  - **The perception of m or n comes more from the vowel than from the nasal sound itself.** After m, one formant of the vowel stays fairly **stationary**, and after n, that formant shows a **big drop**.
  - So listeners decide what consonant they heard **during the vowel**, after the consonant is over. This was one of the first striking results in speech perception, published in the **1950s** by the linguist **André Malécot**.
- Listeners **take in whole stretches of speech and then decide** about the sounds that came first. For about seventy years, speech recognition machines that treated each slice as its own sound **failed**, and modern speech recognition relies on large language models that use what follows to decide about what came before.

## p21
- A **neural network** can be trained to recognize vowels from formants. Its **two inputs** are **F1 and F2**, measured from real recordings of five vowels, and its job is to decide which vowel was spoken.
  - **The knowledge of the network is in its weights**, which are learned from the data.

## p22
- The network has **five output neurons**, one for each word, **done, dune, dean, Don and Dan**, and **three hidden layers** with **3, 2 and 4 neurons**. Every neuron in one layer connects to every neuron in the next, so the network is **fully connected**.
  - This version is **purely linear**, meaning it has **no activation functions**, so each neuron just passes on the result of its inner product.

## p23
- Before training, **every weight is a random number between minus one and one**. A **positive** weight makes its input push the next neuron up, and a **negative** weight makes its input push the next neuron down.
  - The example input is **F1 of 320 and F2 of 2000 hertz**, a very low F1 with a very high F2, which is the pattern of **/i/**, so the correct answer is **dean**.

## p24
- **Forward propagation** carries the input through the network one layer at a time, and each neuron computes an **inner product**, multiplying each incoming number by its weight and adding the results.
- The output is a **probability distribution** over the five words, with values of **.5, .1, .1, .1 and .2**, which add up to one. With random weights the network puts its highest probability on **done**, so it is wrong.
  - The correct answer is given as the target distribution **0, 0, 1, 0, 0**, and **stochastic gradient descent** changes each weight so that next time the inner products lead to a high probability for dean.
  - In plain terms, the network starts out guessing, the target says what the right guess was, and learning nudges every weight so the right guess wins next time.

## p25
- **PyTorch** declares the network as a list of layers. **Sequential** means the network has **one layer after another, in a sequence**, and **Linear** means **do inner products** of weights and previous activations.
  - **nn.Linear(2, 3)** reads as **take input from 2 neurons, and have 3 neurons**. The next lines read 3 in and 2 out, then 2 in and 4 out, then 4 in and 5 out.
  - Turning off the **bias**, by setting it to False, keeps every layer purely linear, matching the diagram of the network.
- **Only layers that do calculations get a line.** The **input layer** has no weights coming into it and does no inner products, so it never gets its own line, and the first line describes the **first hidden layer**.
  - This network has **five layers in total** but **three hidden layers**, and the **last line is always the output layer**, so the question has to be read carefully.
- To **add a hidden layer of 3 neurons right before the output**, the old last line, 4 in and 5 out, becomes **4 in and 3 out**, and a new last line takes **3 in and gives 5 out**.
  - A quick check is that each line's first number must match the previous line's second number.
- A network declared as 4 in and 3 out, 3 in and 8 out, 8 in and 4 out, 4 in and 4 out, and 4 in and 5 out takes **4 numbers as input**, has **four hidden layers** with 3, 8, 4 and 4 neurons, and decides among **5 categories**.
  - When the first category is correct, the target distribution puts a **1 in the first slot and 0 everywhere else**.
