## p2
- A **neural network** takes in information about one moment, uses its **weights** to process it and makes a decision, and it learns those weights through **backpropagation**. The idea began in **1943** with a paper by **McCulloch and Pitts**, which starts from the **all-or-none** character of nervous activity, the same idea as the **sigmoid**.
  - Warren McCulloch was a **psychiatrist**, and the earliest work on neural networks aimed to model how the mind works, not to make money.
- By **1986**, the two **Parallel Distributed Processing** books had matured these ideas. **Parallel** means many neurons compute at the same time, and **distributed** means the information is spread across all the neurons, and one contributor, **Geoffrey Hinton**, later won the **2024 Nobel Prize in Physics**.
- **Given some static vector of formants, these networks could make a decision about a few possible categories**, but **human cognition is far more complex**.
  - **Speech is incredibly time-varying**, because every moment gives a new set of formants, and **language is time-varying too**, since a sentence is many words in a row.
  - So giving a network one list of numbers can never model cognition that unfolds in time.

## p3
- In **1985**, **Michael Jordan** developed **recurrent neural networks** to make sense of **speech production**, meaning how the tongue, the velum and the lips move over time. Features such as nasality, backness and lip rounding each switch on and off at different moments, and a regular network could never handle something that complex and time-varying.
  - His network is **not just sequential**, because it has **two layers working in parallel**.
  - It is called **recurrent** because it **takes its output and sends it back into part of its input**, using what it produced at one moment to predict the next.
- **GPT works the same way, one word at a time.** It predicts the next word, feeds the prompt plus that word back in, and predicts the next word again, so the idea behind GPT starts with thinking about how speech unfolds in time.
  - People refined the recurrent network until **2017**, when the **large language model** was invented. These years give a sense of the history, but they will not be asked about.

## p4
- AI systems build up in steps, and **each step is made of the one before it**.
  - **A single neuron takes in a vector and gives back one number** through an **inner product**, multiplying each input by its weight and adding them up.
  - **A neural network takes in one vector and gives back a whole decision vector**, through many inner products.
- **A large language model takes in many vectors, one for each moment in a sequence, and gives back one decision vector about what comes next.** Picture 15 vectors, each standing for one word, and the job is to **predict the sixteenth**.
  - In real systems, the **context window** holds hundreds of thousands to a million vectors, each thousands of numbers long.
  - The sixteenth vector is a **probability distribution over the next word**, with one entry for each of about 30,000 English words.
- **An LLM is a dynamical system**, which is why the Nobel Prize for AI was awarded in physics. Its input vectors are its **initial condition**, and each layer turns them into **better vectors**, until the last layer can predict the next word.
  - In plain terms, an LLM is like the oscillator, except that thousands of numbers are refined layer by layer instead of two numbers changing over time.
- **An agentic system is a graph of LLMs**, such as an outline LLM, a writing LLM, a **critic LLM**, a revision LLM and a final LLM. The critic is what greatly reduced **hallucination**, and the back-and-forth is why many systems take a while to respond.
- **The chief insight behind all of this is Gestalt completion.** People see a triangle in the Gestalt figure even though none is drawn, and training a system only to **predict the missing thing** turns out to emulate human cognition.
  - The same idea is behind the **cloze procedure**, a test introduced by **Wilson Taylor** in **1953**, in which words are deleted from a text and people fill them in. The name comes from **closure**, the Gestalt term for completing a familiar pattern that is not quite finished, such as seeing a broken circle as whole.
  - **Knowledge can be tested by asking someone to complete a sequence, and for AI, completion is all that knowledge is.** An LLM predicting the next word is playing a cloze game over and over.
  - Underneath everything, **inference is inner products**, and **learning is stochastic gradient descent plus dynamic programming**.

## p5
- **An LLM takes 15 vectors and predicts the sixteenth**, and the vectors can stand for almost anything.
  - They can be **words**, as in "The cat that the dog chased ran up the old tree and sat on the," where getting the next word right means knowing it was the cat that sat.
  - They can be **slices of sound** from a spectrogram, which in 2019 gave the **best speech recognition in 75 years** and now powers synthetic voices that are hard to tell from humans.
  - They can be **patches of an image**, which became the basis of modern **computer vision**.
  - They can be **amino acids** in a protein, and that work on **protein folding** won the **2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry**.
- **The LLM only ever sees vectors**, so one design asks one question every time, what comes sixteenth. Because it predicts from past data, something the past data does not cover can make it **hallucinate**.

## p6
- English allows only **certain sequences of sounds** in its words. **A simple English syllable can have a consonant, then a vowel, then a consonant**, as in Don, mat, sit, cup, dog and fish, and the pattern is about **sounds, not letters**.
- Syllables can also have **clusters**. A cluster before the vowel is an **onset**, as in split, flat and black, and one after the vowel is a **coda**, as in desk, help and lamp.
  - Each real word has an impossible twin, such as **flat** and **lfat**, **help** and **hepl**, and **lamp** and **lapm**, so order matters.
  - **The order mirrors around the vowel.** The l sits right next to the vowel in both flat and help, and each impossible twin puts the wrong consonant against the vowel.
- **A long word is a chain of syllables.** The sounds t and l are fine across a syllable break, as in Atlantic, but **tl cannot start a syllable**, and **ng** can end a syllable, as in ring, but never start one.

## p7
- **Guessing the next letter of an English word reveals this sound knowledge.** After a vowel, as in "ra," almost anything can follow, but at the start of a word the choices narrow sharply.
  - After **sp**, the next letter is l, r or a vowel, and never n, and after **k** it is a vowel or n, as in knee.
  - After **skr**, or after **m** at the start of a word, only a vowel can come.
  - After **bram**, the letter b is almost forced, as in bramble.
- **Guessing the next letter is predicting the sixteenth item**, the same game an LLM plays. Its prediction is a **probability distribution** that is **flat** after "ra" and **sharply peaked** after "skr," because English allows nothing else there.
  - In plain terms, the more the letters so far restrict what can come next, the more the probability piles onto a few letters.

## p8
- English syllables are a good test case, because English has only about 26 letters or a few dozen sounds, so a small LLM can learn the syllable knowledge every speaker has. The plan is to **design an agentic system using instructions written in English**, which builds the LLM, trains it to predict each next letter, and shows what it has learned along the way.
