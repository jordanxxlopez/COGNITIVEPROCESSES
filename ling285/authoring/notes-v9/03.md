## p3
- **A dynamical system is a system whose state changes over time according to rules.** This course meets them in **animal behavior**, such as an octopus defending its home or a person speaking, and in **AI learning**, where a program changes its internal numbers as it is trained.
- **Every dynamical system has three parts, elements, relations and a goal.** The **elements** are the pieces, the **relations** are how the pieces are connected and coordinated, and the **goal** is what the whole system works to achieve. The same three parts describe an animal's body, a vocal tract or a learning program.

## p4
- **An octopus defending its den is a dynamical system.** Its **elements** are its eight **arms** and the **ground**. Its **relations** are the coordinated connections between each arm, the ground and the fish, **planned by a neural system** in the octopus's head and throughout its body. Its **goal** is to **keep the den**.

## p5
- **Human speech is a dynamical system with many more elements.** The **elements** are the **many parts of the tongue**, the **lips**, the **velum**, the **larynx**, the **palate** and the **pharyngeal wall**. The tongue counts as many parts because its tip, body and root move somewhat independently.
- **The relations between all these parts are planned by the brain, and the goal is to convey a thought.** Producing a single word requires the lips, tongue, velum and larynx to move at precisely coordinated times. The MRI recording shows these parts moving together continuously over about three seconds of speech.

## p6
- **All daily voluntary actions and the involuntary actions of the heart, pancreas and neurons are dynamical systems.** Their elements range from **organs** down to **cells** and the **chemicals** inside cells, so **biology, chemistry and physics** all study dynamical systems. The **speech system** is a **very complex** one.
- **Because there are so many dynamical systems, one can be used to study another.** An octopus arm and a human tongue look extremely different yet share an **underlying similarity** in how their elements, relations and goals are organized. This is **going up Diotima's ladder**, from individual cases to the general principles they share.

## p7
- **The octopus example comes from a 2023 Marine Biology paper by Jean Alupay, Jennifer Mather and Khalil Iskarous on the octopus arm slap.** An **Abdopus** octopus filmed in **tidal pools in Okinawa, Japan**, slapped at fish, and 19 slaps were analyzed in an **external reference frame** and an **animal-centered reference frame**.
- **Each slap was built from two kinematic primitives, rotation and translation of the hydrostatic arms**, combined **serially** and **in parallel**. A **muscular hydrostat** has no bones and is supported by muscles pushing against each other, so the arm can bend anywhere along its length.
  - Combining a few primitives keeps movement flexible while controlling only a few **degrees of freedom**, independent ways of moving. The authors call this **flexible rigidity**.
- **The slap supports Flash and Hochner's embodied organization view, and the idea that motor primitives combine syntactically**, by rules, the way words combine into sentences. The octopus combines **sensory feedback** about the moving fish with **feed-forward** pre-planned primitives, staying in **dynamic equilibrium** with its environment.

## p8
- **AI is a model of five animal psychological skills, knowing, recognizing, attending, acting and learning.** Knowing is stored information, recognizing is identifying what arrives, attending is focusing on what matters, acting is producing an output, and learning is changing knowledge from experience.
- **The cloze procedure, introduced by Wilson Taylor in Journalism Quarterly in Fall 1953, measured one of these skills in humans.** Words are removed from a passage and readers fill the blanks. Predicting a word from the words around it is the same skill modern language AI performs.

## p9
- **In GPT each word is a vector, a list of thousands of numbers, called an x or a token.** All of the AI's knowledge is stored in a few trillion numbers called **weights**, or **w's**, and the AI combines the W's with the x's using mostly **addition, subtraction, multiplication and division**.
- **The calculations produce a probability distribution function over possible next words**, which all add up to one. For "chickens cackle and ducks," the tallest peak sits over **"quack."** The way the W's combine with the x's is itself a dynamical system, and learning is a simpler one, so it comes first.

## p10
- **The weights come from training that starts with no knowledge, summed up as "Fake it till you make it!"** The system starts with random W's, is given "chickens cackle and ducks," and predicts the next word. With random weights the peak may sit over "cat." The **target distribution** puts all the probability on "quack," so the system changes its W's to look more like the target. Repeated **billions of times** over more sequences, this is how LLMs learn and know.

## p11
- **AI learning depends on two tricks, shown in an image generated by ChatGPT.** **"Fake It Till You Make It"** means starting with random weights and predicting right away. **"Learn by Making Mistakes"** means each wrong prediction produces an **error** that is used to adjust the weights.

## p12
- **The average of 10, 12 and 14 is easy, 36 divided by 3 gives 12, but this formula does not work for speech, language, biological molecules and scenes.** For those, an estimate must be improved step by step.
- **The AI method starts with a wild guess x and improves it through exposure to the data**, moving x a bit closer to each observed number in turn. This is **observation-based error learning**, the same logic as training an LLM with random weights corrected toward the right next word.

## p13
- **SGD, stochastic gradient descent, learns the average, the essence, of [78, 67, 98, 85, 76, 68, 98, 72] one observation at a time.** The true average is 80.25, but SGD starts by picking a random estimate, 130 at **time 0**, written x[0]. The bracket is the **time step**.
- **The learning rate γ is 25 percent**, the fraction of the error the estimate moves each step. At update 0 the estimate sits at 130 with the average far below it.

## p14
- **One observation is enough to create error.** The first random pick is 98, so d[0] is 98. The **discrepancy**, or **loss**, is the estimate minus the observation, 130 minus 98, which is 32. The positive sign means the estimate is 32 too high, and since the average should be close to the data, the estimate needs to change.

## p15
- **The correction subtracts 25 percent of the discrepancy.** A quarter of 32 is 8, so x[1] is 122. The estimate moves toward the observation but only a quarter of the way, which keeps any single observation from taking over.

## p16
- **The procedure repeats with a new random observation each time.** On the second update the observation is 85, the discrepancy is 37, the correction is 9.25, and x[2] is 112.75.

## p17
- **On the third update the observation is 76.** The discrepancy is 36.75, the correction is 9.1875, and x[3] is about 103.563.

## p18
- **On the fourth update the observation is 68.** The discrepancy is 35.563, the correction is about 8.891, and x[4] is 94.672. Every observation so far has been below the estimate, so the estimate has fallen steadily, 130, 122, 112.75, 103.563, 94.672.

## p19
- **On the fifth update the observation, 98, is higher than the estimate of 94.672, so the discrepancy is negative, 3.328 below zero.** Subtracting 25 percent of a negative number is the same as adding, so the estimate rises to 95.504. The same rule moves the estimate toward the observation from either side, and the path bends upward for the first time.

## p20
- **On the sixth update the observation is 72.** The discrepancy is 23.504, the correction is 5.876, and x[6] is 89.628.

## p21
- **On the seventh update the observation is 78.** The discrepancy is 11.628, smaller now that the estimate is close to the data, the correction is 2.907, and x[7] is 86.721.

## p22
- **On the eighth update the observation is 67, the lowest in the list.** The discrepancy is 19.721, the correction is about 4.930, and x[8] is 81.791, only about 1.5 above the true average.

## p23
- **On the ninth update 98 is drawn again, the third time, since random draws can repeat.** The discrepancy is negative 16.209, so the estimate rises from 81.791 to 85.843. A single high observation pushes the estimate away from the average, because each step responds only to the one observation drawn.

## p24
- **On the tenth update the observation, 85, is extremely close to the estimate.** The discrepancy is 0.843, the correction is about 0.211, and the estimate barely moves, to 85.632.

## p25
- **On the eleventh update the observation is 76.** The discrepancy is 9.632, the correction is 2.408, and x[11] is 83.224.

## p26
- **On the twelfth update the observation is 68.** The discrepancy is 15.224, the correction is 3.806, and x[12] is 79.418, less than 1 below the true average of 80.25 after starting at 130.
  - Over all twelve updates the estimate went 130, 122, 112.75, 103.563, 94.672, 95.504, 89.628, 86.721, 81.791, 85.843, 85.632, 83.224 and 79.418. The path falls steeply, bumps upward twice when 98 is drawn, and ends up hovering near the average.

## p27
- **SGD has two steps, make an initial guess and use data to improve it.** **Stochastic** means a random observation is used each time. **Gradient** means the loss gives the direction, down for a positive discrepancy and up for a negative one. **Descent** means reducing the mistake.
- **The path jitters, but its tendency is toward the average.** The random pushes up and down balance out over many updates. The same method applied to trillions of weights is how LLMs learn to predict the next word.

## p28
- **A second data set, [62, 70, 78, 82, 90, 98], with average 80, makes the link between estimate and loss easier to track.** The **mean motion of SGD** replaces the random observation with the average, so each new estimate is the old one minus 25 percent of its distance from 80.
- **The loss is one half of the discrepancy squared.** Squaring makes the loss positive on either side of 80, and its rate of change as x changes is the discrepancy itself, which is why the discrepancy is the **gradient**.
- **Starting at 140, the estimate goes 125, 113.75 and onward, while the loss goes 1800, 1012.5, about 569.5 and toward zero.** Each update keeps 75 percent of the discrepancy, the curve is smooth with no jitter, and the **estimate goes down as the loss goes down**.

## p29
- **With the same data and the same 25 percent rate but an initial condition of negative 100, the estimate rises toward 80**, to negative 55, then negative 21.25, reaching about 74 by update 12. The loss starts at 16200 and falls toward zero.
- **The direction changes, but the learning does not.** From above, the estimate falls. From below, the estimate rises. In both cases the loss decreases, so learning is defined by the loss going down.

## p30
- **Runs of 300 epochs from initial conditions of 130, 80 and 10 all settle around 80, and the learning rate decides how.** At **10 percent**, the runs reach 80 within 25 to 50 epochs and then wiggle between about 75 and 85. At **50 percent**, they arrive within a few epochs and then jump between about 70 and 95. At **1 percent**, they move slowly and smoothly and are still short of 80 after 300 epochs. At **80 percent**, they arrive at once and then jump between about 68 and 98, tracking individual data points.
  - The initial condition affects only the first part of each run.

## p31
- **The learning rate creates a fundamental learning tradeoff.** Raising γ raises speed and lowers accuracy. Lowering γ lowers speed and raises accuracy. The 1 percent run is accurate but slow, and the 50 and 80 percent runs are fast but noisy.
- **Thousands of people, for generations, have made their living finding ways to optimize learning** so that speed and accuracy both increase.
