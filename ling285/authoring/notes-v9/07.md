## p2
- **A harmonic oscillator has two variables that feed into each other, position x and velocity v.** A dot means change over time, so ẋ is the rate at which position changes and v̇ is the rate at which velocity changes.
- **The first rule is that ẋ equals v, so velocity drives position.** Positive v makes x grow, negative v makes x shrink, and v of zero holds x still. This is the red arrow from v to x. **The second rule is that v̇ equals minus x, so position drives velocity with a minus sign.** Positive x pushes velocity down and negative x pushes it up. This is the blue arrow from x to v, and together the arrows form a **closed loop of mutual influence**.
- **Stepping through from x at 1 and v at 0**, velocity turns negative, so position falls, slowly at first and then faster. When x crosses zero, v̇ is zero and velocity is at its most negative, so position is falling fastest. Below zero, v̇ turns positive and velocity climbs back, position bottoms out at negative 1 when velocity reaches zero, and the sequence runs in reverse back to plus 1.
  - Plotted against time, position and velocity are two waves offset by a quarter of a cycle. **Velocity is zero whenever position is at a peak or a trough, and largest whenever position is crossing zero.**

## p3
- **An oscillation, or vibration, is a repeating back-and-forth motion around a central point, and the x-v loop is the basic system that produces it.** The influence is **circular**, with no single cause at the start and no single effect at the end.
- **The key property is that the system never comes to rest on its own.** Every time one variable reaches its target, the other is at its largest value and pushes it past the target again. Position overshoots zero because velocity is at its maximum there, and velocity overshoots zero because position is at its maximum there.

## p4
- **Vibration requires two physical effects working together.** The first is a **spring**, strictly a **viscous spring**, the springiness that returns things to a natural shape after a push or pull. Its **stiffness** is how strongly it pulls back. The second is **mass**, the amount of matter, which determines how an object carries motion once it is moving.
- **Vibration shows up in many different systems.** It is the jolt from a car hitting a bump, it is music, it is boom and bust in economics, it is **oscillation in SGD**, where a trained value swings around its target, and it is the motion of the air in the vocal tract and of the vocal folds during speech.

## p5
- **A slice of air in the vocal tract is many particles whose number and size stay the same. What changes is the distance between them.** At **equilibrium spacing** a chosen pair sits at 100 percent of its equilibrium distance.
- **Compression pushes the particles closer than their resting distance**, to 56 percent for the chosen pair, and it is a high-pressure state. **Rarefaction spreads them farther apart**, to 144 percent, and it is a low-pressure state. During speech the slice alternates between the two around its equilibrium size, which is a vibration of the air. The sizes are exaggerated so the change is visible.

## p6
- **The spring effect alone is the rule that ẋ equals minus one third of x.** The rate of change is always one third of the current distance with the opposite sign, so positive x falls and negative x rises, both toward zero.
  - From an **initial condition** of 1, x drops by one third to two thirds, then by two ninths to four ninths, then by four twenty-sevenths to eight twenty-sevenths. Each drop is smaller than the one before.
- **When x is smaller, its speed of falling is smaller too.** The curve starts steep and flattens as it nears zero, and it **never crosses zero**, since the speed shrinks to nothing as the distance does. From a negative start it rises toward zero the same way.
- **The system has a natural resting condition at zero, and a discrepancy from it feeds back on itself to cancel that discrepancy.** This is **negative feedback**, also called **balancing feedback**, from Meadows. The change always opposes the discrepancy, and the system is in motion only to reach its **equilibrium**, where it has no reason to change further.
- **The number minus one third is the stiffness.** A higher stiffness closes a bigger share of the remaining distance each step and returns faster. A lower stiffness returns more slowly.

## p7
- **If a mass is moving, it just keeps on moving.** A mass needs no discrepancy or target and has no natural resting position. **Galileo, Descartes and Newton** worked out that a mass in motion stays in motion, at **constant velocity**, covering the same distance in every equal interval of time, like a car rolling past the same amount of scenery each moment.
- **This is exactly what the spring lacks.** A spring slows and stops at its resting point, while a mass carries its motion forward regardless of where it is.

## p8
- **A spring stops at a place, while a mass stops after a process.** A spring alone on a table, with displacement x measured from its natural position at zero, returns from plus 1 and stops at zero **without crossing it**, and from negative 1 rises to zero without crossing it. The return curve flattens as it arrives, the same shape as the one-third rule.
- **With a mass on the spring, the mass is moving fastest when it reaches zero and carries that motion through the crossing.** The spring then pulls it back from the other side, it stops near negative 1, and it is pulled back through zero again, through **every crossing**.
  - Graphed over time this is a **lightly damped oscillation**. **Damping** is the gradual loss of motion from the viscous part of the spring, so each swing is a little smaller, and the mass would come to rest only at the end of a long sequence of swings.
- **Together the two effects recover the harmonic oscillator.** The spring supplies the pull back toward zero, v̇ equals minus x. The mass supplies persistent motion, ẋ equals v. A spring alone gives balancing feedback, a mass alone gives motion that never turns around, and **a spring and a mass together give vibration**.

## p9
- **The vibration of the air slice is described by a cosine, so x equals the cosine of the phase.** Here x is the **normalized expansion**, with plus 1 **maximum rarefaction**, 0 **equilibrium** and minus 1 **maximum compression**.
- **The phase tracks where the slice is in its cycle, and each 2π is one cycle, so 0 to 6π is three cycles.** At 0 the slice is at maximum rarefaction, at π at maximum compression, and at 2π back at maximum rarefaction. In between, plus 0.84 is still rarefied and minus 0.84 is compressed but not at the tightest.
- **This is the same wave position traced in the harmonic oscillator.** The springiness of the air pushes it back toward equilibrium spacing, and the mass of the air carries it past equilibrium each time. The model's sizes are exaggerated and its motion slowed.

## p10
- **Different vowels are made by placing the tongue in different positions**, seen in side-view MRI images during five words. Vowels are organized by **height** and by **front or back**.
  - **"Dean"** is high and front, with a wide open space in the throat behind the tongue. **"Dune"** is high and back, bunched toward the soft palate. **"Dan"** is low and front. **"Don"** is low and back, narrowing the space behind the tongue. **"Done"** is central, in the middle of the mouth.
- **Each tongue position gives the air a different shape, which changes how it vibrates**, and those differences are what make one vowel sound different from another.

## p11
- **Frequency is how many complete cycles a vibration goes through in a given time.** Three vibrations over the same 12 seconds all start at maximum rarefaction and cover the same range. The low-frequency one completes 3 cycles, 0.25 cycles per second. The mid-frequency one completes 6, 0.5 cycles per second. The high-frequency one completes 9, 0.75 cycles per second.
- **All three reach the same extremes. The only difference is how quickly the cycle repeats.** Frequency is separate from how far the particles spread or squeeze. One cycle per second is **one hertz**, written **Hz**.

## p12
- **A vowel is a sum of vibrations at different frequencies happening at once in the same slice of air.** A cosine at f hertz is the cosine of 2π times f times t, so a 1 Hz vibration is the cosine of 2πt and a 4 Hz vibration is the cosine of 8πt. The model frequencies are small example values, not the actual frequencies of speech.
- **The vowel /i/ in "dean" is modeled as 1 Hz plus 4 Hz.** The sum starts at plus 2, maximum rarefaction, ranges from plus 2 to minus 2, and is 0 at 0.5 seconds. The fast wave rides on the slow one, giving smaller wiggles inside each slow cycle, and the pattern repeats every second.
- **The vowel /a/, close to the vowel in "Don," is modeled as 2 Hz plus 3 Hz.** It also starts at plus 2 and is 0 at 0.5 seconds, but between peaks it drops to a deep trough near minus 1.6, rises to a pair of small humps, and drops to another trough.
- **Positive x is rarefaction and negative x is compression in both.** They start at the same value and repeat at the same rate, yet the air moves differently within each second. **The combination of frequencies is what distinguishes one vowel from another.**

## p13
- **Perturbation theory studies how a system's behavior changes when a parameter, a fixed setting such as mass or stiffness, is nudged.** For a mass-spring system the behavior of interest is its **frequency**.
- **Increasing the mass lowers the frequency**, because a heavier mass carries its motion more strongly and the spring takes longer to turn it around. **Increasing the stiffness raises the frequency**, because a stiffer spring pulls back harder and turns the mass around sooner. Knowing whether a system is heavy or light and stiff or loose predicts how it will vibrate.

## p14
- **The mass-spring relationships explain how the ear separates sounds by frequency**, the work of **Georg von Békésy**, **Nobel Prize in 1961**. The **basilar membrane** in the inner ear is wide at one end and narrow at the other. The **wide end** has large mass and small stiffness, heavy and loose. The **narrow end** has small mass and large stiffness, light and stiff.
- **By perturbation theory, the wide end vibrates most for low-frequency sounds and the narrow end for high-frequency sounds**, with every point between tuned to an intermediate frequency. The membrane is a long row of mass-spring systems.
- **Nerve fibers at each spot become active when that spot vibrates, telling the brain which frequencies are present.** A vowel built from two frequencies, like /i/, sets two different places vibrating, so the membrane separates the combination back into its components, which is how the ear tells /i/ from /a/.

## p15
- **The learning rate γ sets how large each training update is, and an epoch is one full round through the training data.** Runs starting near 130, 80 and 10 head toward a target of about 80 over 300 epochs. At 1 percent they move smoothly but are still short of 80 at the end. At 10 percent they arrive within 30 to 50 epochs and wiggle only a little. At 50 percent they arrive in a few epochs but bounce between about 70 and 95. At 80 percent they bounce between about 68 and 97.
- **This is the fundamental learning tradeoff.** Raising γ raises speed and lowers accuracy, because large updates keep throwing the value past the target so it oscillates around it. Lowering γ lowers speed and raises accuracy. This is the **oscillation in SGD** from the list of vibrations.
- **Thousands of people, over generations, have made their living finding better ways to optimize**, so that speed and accuracy increase together.
