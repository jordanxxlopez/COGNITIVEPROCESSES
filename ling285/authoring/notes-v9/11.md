## p2
- A **mass-spring system** is a mass hooked up to a spring, and its behavior comes from what each part "wants." **The spring wants to be at its equilibrium position**, which is its resting length, so if it is stretched or squished it pulls or pushes to get back there. **A mass that is moving wants to keep on moving**, so once it gets going it does not stop by itself.
  - When the mass is pulled out and let go, the spring pulls it back toward equilibrium. The mass is moving when it gets there, so it **keeps on moving** past equilibrium and squishes the spring.
  - The squished spring now pushes the mass back the other way, the mass overshoots again, and the cycle repeats. That repeating back and forth is **vibration**.
  - On a real table, **friction** slowly brings the motion to a stop. The model leaves friction out, so in the model the system vibrates forever.
- The same system can move in **two directions**, and it is important to understand both kinds of motion, because some systems are easier to think about one way than the other.
  - In **longitudinal** motion, the mass moves **left and right**, back and forth along the line of the spring.
  - In **transverse** motion, the mass sits between **two walls** with a spring on each side, and it moves **up and down**, across the line of the springs.
- **Both kinds of motion work in exactly the same way, and for the same reason.** Pulling the transverse mass up **stretches the springs**, and since springs hate being stretched, they pull the mass back down. As the mass passes the middle, the springs are not stretched at all, but the mass is still moving, so it keeps going and stretches them on the other side.
  - Both versions vibrate at the **same rate**, because they have the same mass and the same total stiffness in the direction of motion.

## p3
- **Frequency** is the number of back-and-forth cycles a system completes each second, and two things set it, the **mass** and the **stiffness**. Each change has the same effect in longitudinal motion and in transverse motion.
- **A heavier mass vibrates more slowly.** A heavy mass is hard to get moving and hard to turn around, so each trip takes longer. **Heavy branches sway back and forth slowly, while little branches sway quickly.**
  - Making the mass **four times heavier cuts the rate in half**.
- **A stiffer spring makes the system vibrate faster.** The stiffer something is, the harder it pulls back toward where it started, so it turns the mass around sooner.
  - Making the spring **four times stiffer doubles the rate**.
- **Raising the mass lowers the frequency, and raising the stiffness raises the frequency.** Each part has its effect only while it is doing its job.
  - **A mass affects frequency by moving, and a spring affects frequency by stretching and compressing.** A mass that never moves could be made a thousand pounds heavier without changing the frequency at all, and a spring that never stretches adds no stiffness.

## p4
- With **two masses**, the system has two walls, a spring from each wall to a mass, and **one spring between the two masses**. All the masses are equal, and all the springs have the same stiffness. This system can vibrate in **two different ways**, which are called **modes**, and **a mode is just another word for a way of vibrating**.
  - In **Mode 1**, the masses move **together**. Both are pulled up and let go, and they rise and fall side by side, or in longitudinal motion they move left and right as a pair.
  - In **Mode 2**, the masses move **against each other**. One is pulled down and the other up, so whenever one rises the other falls.
  - A system can also vibrate in **both modes at the same time**. Pulling both masses up, but one higher than the other, makes them move together and against each other at once, which real movement and real speech production do all the time.
- **When the masses go together, they move back and forth slowly, and when they go against each other, they move back and forth quickly**, so **Mode 2 has the higher frequency**. Both modes use the same masses and the same springs, so the explanation comes from asking **what each spring is actually doing**.
  - In **Mode 1**, the middle spring never stretches or compresses. It just rides along like a rigid bar, so **effectively Mode 1 has only two springs**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the middle spring is stretched and squished over and over, so **all three springs are working**, and Mode 2 has three units of stiffness instead of two.
  - **More stiffness means more pull back toward equilibrium, and more pull means a higher frequency.** In plain terms, a higher mode is not built from stiffer springs. It just makes the same springs do more stretching.

## p5
- Adding a third mass adds a third way to vibrate, so **a system with three masses has three modes**. The modes are told apart the same way as before, by asking which masses move together and which move against each other.
  - All three masses can go back and forth together.
  - The two outer masses can move against each other while the middle mass stays still.
  - The middle mass can move against the two outer masses.
  - The more the masses go against each other, the more the springs stretch, so these three modes go from the lowest frequency to the highest.

## p6
- With **three masses** and four springs, the system has **three modes**, and each has its own frequency.
  - In **Mode 1**, all three masses go back and forth **together**, and this mode has the **lowest frequency**. The two middle springs just ride along, so they add no stiffness.
  - In **Mode 2**, the **middle mass stays still** while the two outer masses move against each other, and this mode has the **middle frequency**. To start it, the outer masses are pulled apart very carefully without touching the middle one.
  - In **Mode 3**, the **middle mass moves opposite to the two outer masses**, so **everything goes against everything else**. It has the most stretching and compressing, the most stiffness, and the **highest frequency**.
- Because a mass acts only by moving, **changing one mass affects only the modes in which that mass moves**. Making the **middle mass heavier lowers the frequency of Mode 1 and Mode 3**, since the middle mass moves in both of them. **Mode 2 stays the same**, because in Mode 2 the middle mass does not move at all.
- All three modes can also happen **at the same time**, and however the system is started, it vibrates as some mix of these **pure modes**.
  - **Learning a musical instrument means learning how to make the masses and springs vibrate in certain modes and not others.** How a string is plucked or a violin is bowed decides which modes get excited, and the favored modes are heard as **notes**.
  - A **trained singer** does the same with the vocal folds, controlling their mass and stiffness well enough to carry a tune.

## p7
- The pattern holds for any number of masses. **N masses give N independent modes**, so one mass has one way to vibrate, two masses have two, and six masses have six. Moving to higher modes, the pattern has **more reversals**, which are places where neighboring masses head in opposite directions, and more reversals mean more stretching, so **higher modes have higher frequencies**.
  - In chains of separate masses like these, the mode frequencies are generally **not whole-number multiples** of the lowest frequency.
- The same mode looks alike no matter how many masses the chain has.
  - **Every Mode 1 is alike**, because all the masses go up and down together.
  - **Every Mode 2 is alike**, because the **left side goes against the right side**. With an odd number of masses, the middle one stays still.
  - **Every Mode 3 is alike**, because the **middle goes against the sides**.
- Packing in more and more masses and springs, all the way to an **infinite number**, gives a **string**, like a guitar or piano string. **Every little piece of a string has mass, and every little piece stretches like a spring**, so a string has an infinite number of modes.
  - In the **lowest mode**, the whole string goes up and down together with hardly any stretching between its pieces, so it gives the **lowest note**.
  - A **building** is like a string that is fixed at the ground and free to sway at the top. Engineers design buildings to sway only in **Mode 1**, because in higher modes some floors would move one way while others moved the other way.

## p8
- A **string fixed at both ends** with its lowest mode at **100 hertz** has its next modes at **200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz**, and **hertz** means times per second. These frequencies are **whole-number multiples** of the lowest one, and modes that line up this way are called **harmonics**.
- The even spacing comes from how each mode is built out of the first one. **Mode 1 is a single bump**, and **Mode 2 is two copies of that bump**, with one half going up while the other half goes down.
  - With two copies, Mode 2 has **twice the stiffness** of Mode 1, so it has twice the frequency. Mode 3 fits three copies, and the fifth mode has **the first mode fitted into it five times**.
  - So with a lowest mode of 200 hertz, the fifth mode is five times 200.
- A real string vibrates in **all of its modes at the same time**, and adding the separate motions together gives one complicated, changing shape. The **spectrum** of a sound tells **which frequencies are in it and how much of each there is**, with **frequency** along the horizontal axis and **amplitude** going up the vertical axis.
  - For the string moving in all five modes, the spectrum has **five evenly spaced spikes**. In this example the spikes have **equal height**, since nothing has filtered the sound.
  - If the string vibrated **only in Mode 3**, the spectrum would have **one spike at the frequency of Mode 3** and nothing else, which is like a **one-hot encoding**.

## p9
- **The vocal folds are even more complicated than strings.** They are a sheet of muscle, which is called a **membrane**, so their masses and springs spread across a surface instead of along a line. The same logic of modes still applies.
  - In **Mode 1**, the whole surface goes up and down together.
  - In **Mode 2**, the **left half goes against the right half**.
  - In **Mode 3**, the surface splits the other way, and the two halves on either side of a still middle line go against each other.
  - In **Mode 4**, four sections go against each other, and still higher modes get more and more complicated.
- In real vocal folds, **all the modes happen at the same time**, so voicing produces a spectrum full of spikes. The evenly spaced frequencies attached to these membrane modes are teaching values, and the vocal folds are held fixed along their edges, so they **behave like a string**, which has evenly spaced harmonics.

## p10
- **The scientific method** depends on **very clear statements of how the world works**, usually written as **differential equations**. **Solving those equations gives a prediction** of what the world will be like when it is actually measured.
  - The **theory** is that the **vocal folds are a membrane**, so when they vibrate, the vibration has **equidistant peaks in its spectrum**, called **harmonics**.
  - The **prediction** is that **anyone's laryngeal vibration signal, when measured, will have a spectrum with equidistant harmonics.**
- The prediction was tested by recording two voices in **Praat**, each made with the **mouth wide open** so that no particular vowel was being made.
  - In the first voice, the lowest spike was near **100 hertz**, and the next spikes fell close to two, three, four and five times that value, **evenly spaced just as predicted**.
  - In the second voice, the lowest spike was near **220 hertz**, and the next ones fell near twice and three times that value, again evenly spaced but farther apart.
  - **The prediction held for both voices**, so a theory built from simple masses and springs made a precise and correct prediction about real speech.
- The second voice has a **higher pitch**, so those vocal folds are probably **smaller and thinner**. **Thinner folds have less mass, and less mass means a higher frequency**, so every harmonic is spaced farther apart.
  - This is part of how people are **recognized by their voices**, since each person's vocal folds give their voice its own spectrum.

## p11
- A string can also be **fixed at one end and free at the other**, just like a **building**, which is fixed to the ground and free to sway at the top. The **fixed bottom** cannot move, so every mode has a point of **no motion** there, called a **node**, and the **free top** moves the most, called an **antinode**.
- **Only the odd harmonics occur**, so the modes come at **100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz**, which are one, three, five and seven times the lowest frequency.
  - **Mode 1** goes from no motion at the bottom to the most motion at the top, like a building swaying as a whole.
  - Two copies of Mode 1 would put a still point at both ends, which would make the system **fixed at both ends**, so that pattern is impossible here.
  - **Three copies** do end with free motion at the top, so the next mode has three times the frequency of Mode 1, and the mode after that holds five copies.
  - The simple version is that the free end must always be moving, and only an odd number of quarter-waves lands on a moving end.
- When **both ends are fixed**, a string has **every whole-number multiple** of the lowest frequency, and when **one end is fixed and the other is free**, it has **only the odd multiples**.
