## p2
- **Real-time MRI films the inside of the head while a person talks**, so the hidden movements of speech can be watched directly. The view is a side view cut down the middle of the head, with the face pointing left. Air appears black, and soft tissue such as the tongue, lips and throat walls appears gray and white.
  - The **lips** sit at the far left. The **tongue** is the large rounded mass filling most of the mouth, and it is the most active structure in speech.
  - The roof of the mouth has a bony front part, the **hard palate**, and a soft movable back part, the **soft palate** or **velum**, which lifts to seal off the nose or lowers to let air out through the nose.
  - Behind the tongue, the **pharynx** runs down the throat to the **larynx**, which holds the **vocal folds**.
- **During speech all of these structures move at the same time and at high speed.** Each sound depends on a precise shape of the whole passage, and the shapes flow smoothly into one another. The two movies differ almost entirely in where the tongue and lips are placed.

## p3
- **We learn to move our speech organs in extremely complex ways with no instructions and almost no visual feedback.** A child cannot see their own tongue, soft palate or vocal folds, yet **by ages 2 to 4** children speak with a wide vocabulary and near-adult language.
  - **Reading and writing** whole words usually comes at **ages 6 to 7** and requires a great deal of instruction, even though the eyes and hands are fully visible. Speech is a deep human cognitive skill that the brain is prepared to acquire.
- **Articulatory phonetics** studies the movements of the different structures of the **vocal tract**, the full air passage from the larynx to the lips with a side branch into the nose. Real-time MRI is one of its main tools.
- **In the last two years, voice assistants and other speech technology have come to emulate human speech ability.** To **emulate** an ability means to reproduce its behavior. **LING 285 is about both human speech and AI speech**, so the human skill and the machine version can be compared directly.

## p4
- **Focus on the air between the tongue and the rest of the vocal tract.** Ignoring the curve of the real tract turns it into a simple **tube of air**, open at the **lips** and treated as closed at the **larynx**.
  - The tube is labeled **schwa**, the neutral relaxed vowel. For schwa the tongue does not narrow the passage much anywhere, so the air space is about the same width from end to end, and the straight even tube fits it best.
- **The air inside the tube vibrates by shifting back and forth, not by flowing out the end.** Each column of air shifts around its own resting position.
  - Where neighboring columns move toward each other, the air is packed tightly into a **compression**. Where they move apart, the air spreads out into a **rarefaction**.
  - These regions travel along the tube and reflect back from its ends. This repeating pattern is the **vibration of air** that makes up the sound of the vowel.
- **Acoustic phonetics** studies how air vibrates in the vocal tract and what sound results. Articulatory phonetics describes the shape of the tube, and acoustic phonetics describes the air inside it. Moving the tongue and lips changes the tube, and changing the tube changes how the air vibrates.

## p7
- **Diotima's ladder describes the climb from surface knowledge of particular things toward deep understanding of the general ideas behind them.** Diotima, around 440 BCE, taught **Socrates**, who taught **Plato**, who taught **Aristotle**.
- **The bottom of the ladder holds daily experience, habit, memorization, specialized skills, repetition, replication and textbooks.** Its rule is that **similar is same, and slight difference is different**.
- **The top of the ladder holds creativity, insight, thought, focus, attention, imagination, vision, problem-posing, problem-solving, critical reading and creative writing.** Its rule is the reverse of the first one. **Different can be same, and similar can be different.**
  - The sounds f and v sound different, yet they are made in the same place in the mouth. The two th sounds are spelled the same, yet one is voiced and the other is not.
  - **Problem-posing** means asking good questions in the first place. **Problem-solving** means answering questions once they have been asked.
- **A person climbs the ladder through repeated cycles of question and answer**, the method Socrates became famous for.
- **For about 2500 years, people could fake it** by memorizing, repeating and replicating. In 2026, interviews for jobs and graduate school look for the properties at the top of the ladder.

## p8
- **Produce the sounds and notice what the body does.** Hold the **f** in "fat" and switch to the **v** in "vat." Hold the **th** in "thing" and switch to the **th** in "that." Hold **s** and switch to **z**. Hold the **sh** in "mesh" and switch to the buzzing sound in the middle of "beige," written **j** here.
- **Within each pair, the difference is voice, which is whether the vocal folds vibrate.** With fingers on the throat, f, the first th, s and sh feel still and hiss, while v, the second th, z and the sound in "beige" buzz.
  - The **vocal folds** are two small folds of tissue inside the larynx. Held apart, they let air pass silently and the sound is **voiceless**. Brought close together, they vibrate in the airflow and the sound is **voiced**. The tongue and lips do not change within a pair.
- **From one line to the next, the difference is place of articulation, where the narrowing is formed.** The lower lip at the upper teeth for f and v, the tongue tip at the upper teeth for th, the tongue tip near the ridge behind the teeth for s and z, and the tongue a bit further back for sh and the sound in "beige."
- **Voice and place of articulation are two separate, independent properties of a speech sound.**

## p9
- **All of these sounds are fricatives**, made by bringing two parts of the vocal tract very close together without closing the passage. Air forced through the narrow gap becomes turbulent, which produces the hissing or buzzing noise, and the sound can be held as long as there is breath.
- **The fricative table has voice in the rows and place of articulation in the columns.** The places are **labial** (the lower lip at the upper teeth, for f and v), **dental** (the tongue tip at the teeth, for the two th sounds), **alveolar** (the tongue tip near the **alveolar ridge** just behind the upper teeth, for s and z) and **postalveolar** (the front of the tongue a little further back, for sh and the sound in "beige").
- **Two fricatives in the same column differ only in voicing, and two in the same row differ only in place.** English spells the two th sounds the same way even though one is voiceless and the other is voiced.

## p10
- **Stops close the passage completely for a brief moment.** Pressure builds up behind the closure, and the release lets the trapped air burst out, which makes a stop short and sharp. A stop cannot be held as a continuous sound.
- **The English stops form a grid of three places and three types.** The first row, **p, b and m**, closes the **lips**. The second row, **t, d and n**, presses the tongue tip against the **alveolar ridge**. The third row, **k, g and ng**, raises the **back of the tongue** against the **soft palate**.
  - Across each row, the place stays the same. The first sound is **voiceless**, the second is **voiced**, and the third is a **nasal**, which is voiced with the **soft palate lowered** so air flows out through the nose.
- **The columns follow the fricative table's logic.** The voiceless stops are **p, t and k**, the voiced stops are **b, d and g**, and the nasal stops are **m, n and ng**. Three places of closure combined with three settings of the vocal folds and soft palate give nine sounds.
