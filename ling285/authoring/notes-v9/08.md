## p3
- **A spring stops at a place, but a mass stops after a process.** The **displacement x** is how far the end of the spring is from its **natural position**, x at 0, with a stretch at plus 1 and a squeeze at minus 1.
- **A spring alone, pulled to plus 1 and released, pulls itself back and stops exactly at zero.** The curve drops quickly, then more slowly, and never crosses zero, because the pull shrinks as the stretch shrinks. Pushed to minus 1, it rises to zero and never goes above it. A massless spring **stops at a place**.
- **With a mass attached, the mass shoots right through zero.** It is moving fast when it gets there, compresses the spring on the other side, stops near minus 1, and is pushed back through zero again. **The mass carries motion through every crossing**, and x makes a wave, an **oscillation**.
- **Each swing is slightly smaller than the last, a lightly damped oscillation.** **Damping** is anything that drains motion, such as friction, so the mass **stops after a process** of many crossings. A mass-spring system has a **frequency**, cycles per second. A massless spring has none.

## p4
- **Perturbation theory asks what happens when one setting of a system is nudged.** For a mass-spring system the settings are the **mass m** and the **stiffness k**, and the behavior is the frequency f. **Increasing the mass decreases the frequency**, since a heavier mass is harder to speed up and slow down, so each swing takes longer. **Increasing the stiffness increases the frequency**, since a stiffer spring pulls back harder and each swing finishes sooner. Only the direction matters, not the exact number.
- **Speaking changes the effective mass and stiffness of air in the vocal tract.** Moving the tongue, jaw and lips squeezes some portions of the air in the tube and opens others, so each portion acts more or less like a mass or a spring. Set vibrating by the vocal folds, the air vibrates at **many frequencies at once**, and the set depends on **where** and **how much** the air is squeezed, so each vowel and consonant has its own.
  - MRI images of **dean, dune, done, Dan** and **Don** show the different squeezes. Dean is high and forward, dune high and back with the lips forward, done in the middle, Dan low and front, and Don low and back, squeezing the throat. Each shape gives a different set of frequencies.
- **The listener has to recover these frequencies from the sound.** The ear must find which low, middle and high frequencies are present and how strong each is, so the brain can tell **"scene"** from **"son."** How the ear does this is answered by the inner ear.

## p5
- **Sound is a wave of vibration in the air.** It arrives at the **external ear** and travels inward through the ear canal to the **inner ear**. The **cochlea**, the one name to know, is a small coiled tube like a snail shell deep in the skull. It sends the brain signals saying which frequencies are present.
- **The inner ear has two regions.** The upper one holds the **semicircular ducts**, the **utricle** and **saccule**, and the **cristae** in their **ampullae**, which are not the parts that separate frequencies. The lower one is the cochlea, with the **vestibular, cochlear** and **tympanic ducts** spiraling together inside it. Soft tubes, the **membranous labyrinth**, sit inside the hollows of the **bony labyrinth**, and the **vestibulocochlear nerve** carries the signals to the brain.

## p6
- **Sound reaches the cochlea through a chain.** It strikes the **tympanic membrane**, the eardrum, which moves the **malleus**, which moves the **incus**, which moves the **stapes**. The stapes pushes in and out on the fluid inside the cochlea.
- **The basilar membrane runs the length of the cochlea**, from the stapes end to the tip of the coil, the **helicotrema**. When the stapes pushes on the fluid, the fluid makes the basilar membrane vibrate, and the membrane turns that vibration into information about frequency.

## p7
- **The basilar membrane is wide and heavy at the helicotrema end and narrow and stiff at the stapes end.** The wide end has **large mass** and **small stiffness**, heavy and floppy. The narrow end has **small mass** and **large stiffness**, light and stiff.
- **Each spot acts like its own mass-spring system, so frequency increases steadily along the membrane.** Large mass and small stiffness both lower the frequency at the floppy end, so it vibrates at **low frequencies**. Small mass and large stiffness both raise it at the stiff end, so it vibrates at **high frequencies**.
- **A sound with many frequencies is sorted out by place.** Each frequency shakes the part of the membrane tuned to it. **Neurons** attached along the membrane signal **to the brain**, which tells the frequencies by **which neurons are active** and their strength by how strongly they signal. One pattern is "scene," another is "son." **Georg von Békésy won the Nobel Prize in 1961** for this mechanism.

## p8
- **The vocal folds are two small muscles, about 1 cm long and 1 mm high, sitting across the airway.** Muscle has mass and stiffness, so the folds behave like a mass-spring system. About **10 other muscles** can **stretch** them, making them **stiffer** and raising the frequency, or **bunch** them, making them **effectively heavier** and lowering it.
- **Controlling the frequency gives musical notes in singing, conveys questions, mistrust, belief, surprise and emotion in English, and distinguishes word meanings in tone languages.** The frequency is called **pitch**, F0, the **fundamental frequency**.
- **Pressure from the lungs blows the closed folds apart, and the air rushing through sucks them back together**, like a shower curtain pulled toward the water. That suction is the **Bernoulli force**, which also lifts airplanes. Pressure builds again, and the cycle repeats **hundreds of times per second**, each cycle one vibration.

## p9
- **A harmonic oscillator is a mass on a spring with no damping, described by ẋ equals v and v̇ equals minus k over m times x.** The dot means rate of change. The first rule says position changes at the velocity. The second says velocity is pushed back toward zero, harder for a stiffer spring and less for a heavier mass, so k over m holds both parts of perturbation theory.
- **With k over m at 1, a time step Δt of 0.5, and a start at x of 1 and v of 0, each step updates x first and then v using the new x.** The new x is the old x plus Δt times the old v, and the new v is the old v minus Δt times the new x. This is **position-first symplectic Euler**, and the order matters.
  - Step 1 gives x of 1.000 and v of minus 0.500. Step 2 gives x of 0.750 and v of minus 0.875. Step 3 gives x of 0.3125, shown as 0.313, and v of about minus 1.031. Later steps use the full-precision numbers.
- **After 12 steps, x falls through zero, bottoms out at minus 1.022 at step 7, and climbs back to 0.920, while v drops to minus 1.031 at step 3, crosses zero near step 6, and peaks at 1.019 at step 9.** The mass is fastest through zero and slowest at its farthest point, and the swing stays near a size of 1.
  - These values are an approximation from stepping in chunks of 0.5. In Python the loop appends the new position, then the velocity update reads x[-1], which is now the **new x**.

## p10
- **Explicit Euler, or forward Euler, updates both x and v from the previous pair.** The new x is the previous v times dt plus the previous x, and the new v is minus k over m times the **previous** x times dt plus the previous v, with k and m at 1 and dt at 0.5.
- **The difference shows at step 2.** Here v[2] uses x[1] of 1 and comes out to minus 1, where the position-first method gave minus 0.875. By step 3, x[3] is 0.250 instead of 0.313. Steps 4 to 12 give positions of minus 0.438, minus 1.188, minus 1.828, minus 2.172, minus 2.059, minus 1.402, minus 0.231, 1.290 and 2.869, and velocities up to 3.159.
- **With this time step, explicit Euler makes the oscillation grow.** The swings reach minus 2.172 and 2.869, far beyond the starting value of 1. A real mass cannot swing farther unless energy is added, so this growth is **numerical error** from the method at dt of 0.5. The position-first method kept the swing near 1, and the only difference is whether the velocity update uses the new position or the previous one.
  - In Python the loop counter n runs from 1 to 12, and x[n-1] and v[n-1] always refer to the previous pair.

## p11
- **Euler's explicit method solves differential equations using arithmetic.** A **differential equation** is an equation about rates of change, and Euler's method turns it into updates where the new value is the previous value plus a rate times a small time step. Every update is multiplication and addition, repeated many times, and the result is approximate. It is named for **Leonhard Euler**.
- **A Python loop carries out the updates for as many steps as wanted.** This is **Software 1.0**, software written as explicit instructions, where the programmer spells out every operation. A photograph of a woman beside a stack of printed code as tall as she is, and another of her receiving a medal, give a sense of how much hand-written code large Software 1.0 programs require.
