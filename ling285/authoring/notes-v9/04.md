## p3
- **The shortest path problem asks for the cheapest route through a network from a start to an end.** Each circle is a **state**, each arrow is a possible move, and each arrow carries a **cost**. The total cost of a route is the sum of its arrow costs.
- **The network runs from A to J through stages**, A, then B and C, then D, E and F, then G, H and I, then J, so every route makes exactly four decisions. From A, B costs 12 and C costs 7. From B, D, E and F cost 5, 6 and 9, and from C they cost 14, 10 and 11. From D, G, H and I cost 8, 7 and 10, from E 9, 7 and 9, and from F 10, 7 and 8. G, H and I reach J for 5, 9 and 8.
- **This is a model of decision-making over time.** Every choice has an immediate cost and also controls which future costs are available. A choice that looks good now can lock us into expensive moves later.

## p4
- **A greedy algorithm takes the cheapest arrow out of the current state and never looks further ahead.** From A it takes C for 7, from C it takes E for 10, from E it takes H for 7, and from H it must pay 9 to reach J. The greedy route A, C, E, H, J costs 33, while A, B, D, G, J costs only 30 despite its expensive first move of 12.
- **Greed is not good here.** The cheap move to C led into a part of the network where every continuation was costly, and the route ended at H with the large final cost of 9. Greed has no way of knowing about later costs.
- **A tiny network makes this obvious.** The top route costs 10 then 10, and the bottom route costs 2 then 100. Greed picks 2 and pays 102 instead of 20. Writing inside each middle state the cost of the rest of the journey, 10 or 100, removes the blindness of greed.

## p6
- **An exhaustive algorithm lists every route, adds up each total, and picks the smallest, so its answer is guaranteed correct.** Here there are 2 times 3 times 3 times 1, or 18 routes, needing 54 additions and 18 comparisons.
- **Its problem is how fast it grows.** Every added stage multiplies the number of routes, so with many stages and many states checking every route takes a very, very long time. Exhaustive search is accurate but impractical, and greedy search is fast but can be wrong. A method is needed that is both accurate and efficient.

## p7
- **Bellman began with an imaginary step.** Imagine the best cost from B to J and from C to J were already known, and call them VBJ and VCJ. V stands for **Value**, the lowest total cost from a state to the goal.
- **Then the best cost from A, VAJ, is one short calculation.** Through B the best total is 12 plus VBJ, and through C it is 7 plus VCJ. VAJ is the **minimum** of those two, written with min and braces. Each number in the set is one arrow cost plus the Value of the state the arrow leads to.

## p8
- **The difficulty is that VBJ and VCJ are not known.** The rule for VAJ is correct but depends on two numbers not yet found.
- **Bellman's strategy is to decompose a problem into subproblems you can solve.** Finding VAJ becomes finding VBJ and VCJ, and if those cannot be solved directly, they are broken down again.

## p9
- **The same reasoning applies at B.** If the best costs from D, E and F to J were known, VBJ would be the minimum of 5 plus VDJ, 6 plus VEJ and 9 plus VFJ, and VCJ the minimum of 14 plus VDJ, 10 plus VEJ and 11 plus VFJ.
- **The Values of D, E and F depend in turn on G, H and I**, and at that stage the subproblems become trivial. Each of G, H and I has one arrow straight to J, so VGJ is 5, VHJ is 9 and VIJ is 8.
  - The rules form a chain from the end of the network back to the start. Information about the cost of reaching J travels **backward**, one stage at a time.

## p10
- **With G, H and I known, the Values are calculated stage by stage.** VDJ is the minimum of 13, 16 and 18, so 13. VEJ is 14 and VFJ is 15, all through G, whose small Value of 5 outweighs the arrow costs. VBJ is the minimum of 18, 20 and 24, so 18, and VCJ is the minimum of 27, 24 and 26, so 24. VAJ is the minimum of 12 plus 18 and 7 plus 24, so 30.
- **Now go greedy on the Values, not the costs.** At A, B gives 30 and C gives 31, so go to **B** despite its expensive arrow. At B, D gives 18, so go to **D**. At D, G gives 13, so go to **G**, and then J. The best path is **A, B, D, G, J**, total 30.
- **Dynamic programming is this two-part method.** Calculate the Values backward from the goal, then choose moves greedily using those Values. It needed one small minimum per state instead of adding up all 18 routes.

## p11
- **Dynamic programming is foresight about the whole route ahead.** From D, E or F the cheapest arrow is always the 7 into H, but H leads to something terrible, the final 9, so H has the largest Value in its stage. At E, H gives 7 plus 9, which is 16, while G gives 9 plus 5, which is 14. The Value of H warns about the 9 before we ever reach it, however many stages away it lies.
- **The Values contain the future.** A late obstacle, the 9 out of H, made the early greedy choice of C a bad path. Because each Value already includes every obstacle ahead, going greedy on the Values makes present decisions optimal.
  - **Søren Kierkegaard** wrote that life can only be understood backwards but must be lived forwards. The Values are understood backwards, from the goal, and the decisions are lived forwards, from A.

## p12
- **Dynamic programming poses a problem, its subproblems and its sub-subproblems, down to problems that are trivial.** VAJ needs VBJ and VCJ, which need VDJ, VEJ and VFJ, which need VGJ, VHJ and VIJ, which are just single arrow costs. Then the bigger problems are solved one level at a time back up to the original.

## p13
- **The Values are propagated from each stage to the stage before it by Bellman's rule.** The Value at one state is the minimum of a set of numbers, each the sum of the cost to one of the next states and the Value at that state. Start at the stage before the goal, apply the rule to each stage before in turn, and stop when A has its Value.
- **Each Value is calculated once and reused by every state before it that connects to it**, such as VDJ of 13 feeding both the B rule and the C rule. That reuse keeps dynamic programming efficient.

## p14
- **Go through the bad 12 first to get onto a route that does not get stuck later at the 9.** It may seem to take a big brain to weigh the whole future against the present.
- **A lifeguard faces the same trade-off.** On sand alone, running at a **constant speed**, the fastest route is the **shortest path**, a straight line. With the person in the water, running is fast and swimming is slow, so the **path of least time** runs farther along the sand and swims a shorter distance, bending at the water's edge. A longer run now cuts the costly swim later, like paying 12 early to avoid the 9.

## p15
- **Decision-making is like physics, and light makes the same choice.** In **refraction**, light moves faster in air and slower in water, so its path bends at the surface just as the lifeguard's route bends, which is why a pencil in a glass of water looks broken at the water line.
- **Light has no brain, yet it takes the least-time route**, so accepting an early cost to avoid a larger later one does not require a big brain. Physical systems find these optimal paths on their own, and dynamic programming describes the same kind of route through stages.

## p16
- **Each state can be labeled with its Value and its best next state.** A is 30 and B. B is 18 and D, C is 24 and E. D is 13 and G, E is 14 and G, F is 15 and G. G, H and I are 5, 9 and 8, all to J. Following the labels from A gives A, B, D, G, J for 30.
- **The labels give the best route from any state**, so from C the route is E, G, J for 24. One backward calculation solves the problem for every starting point at once.

## p18
- **A learning process plotted against epochs, with a 10 percent learning rate, settles near 80 from any initial condition.** Runs starting at 130, 80 and 10 meet within about 25 to 50 epochs and then fluctuate together in a narrow band. The change is fastest when x is far from 80 and slows as it gets closer.
- **Coffee in a room at 18 degrees behaves the same way.** Hot cups starting at 100, 80 and 60 degrees cool toward 18, reaching about 29, 26 and 23 degrees after 8 minutes, and iced cups starting at 10, 5 and 0 degrees warm to about 16 or 17. The bigger the gap from room temperature, the faster the change.
- **In both systems many starting values move toward the same final value**, and the speed of change depends on how far the current value is from it.
