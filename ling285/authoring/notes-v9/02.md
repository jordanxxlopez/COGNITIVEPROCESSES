## p3
- **Real-time MRI films the inside of the head while a person is talking.** The images are **midsagittal**, a side-view slice down the middle of the head, with the face pointing left. **Air shows up black** and soft tissue shows up gray or white, so the dark spaces are the open airways, the mouth, the nasal passages and the **pharynx** behind the tongue.
  - The **tongue** is the large gray mass filling the mouth. Above it is the bony **hard palate**, and hanging from its back is the **velum**, or soft palate, which closes or opens the path from the throat into the nose.
- **The tongue, lips, jaw and velum all move at once and very quickly.** Every consonant and vowel is the result of shaping this tube of air in a particular way. **Phonetics** is the study of these speech sounds.

## p4
- **A fricative is made by bringing two parts of the mouth very close together without closing them completely.** Air pushed through the narrow gap becomes turbulent, which makes the hissing or buzzing noise, and the sound can be held as long as there is breath.
- **Fricatives are organized by place of articulation, where the gap is formed, and by voicing, whether the vocal folds vibrate.** A **voiceless** sound has only the hiss. A **voiced** sound adds a buzz that can be felt with a hand on the throat during "zzzz" but not "ssss."
  - **Labial** f and v raise the lower lip against the upper teeth. **Dental** th in "think" and th in "this" put the tongue tip at the upper teeth. **Alveolar** s and z raise the tongue tip toward the **alveolar ridge** behind the upper teeth. **Postalveolar** sh and j, the sound in "measure," form the gap a little farther back.
- **Each pair is made with the same place and the same manner.** The only difference between f and v, or s and z, is whether the vocal folds vibrate. The four MRI images show the constriction moving backward from the lips to behind the ridge.

## p5
- **A stop closes the vocal tract completely, so air cannot get out through the mouth.** Pressure builds behind the closure and bursts out when it is released. English has **oral stops** and **nasal stops**.
  - In an **oral stop**, or **plosive**, the velum is raised to seal off the nose, so pressure builds and is released as a burst. In a **nasal stop**, the mouth is closed the same way but the velum is lowered, so air flows out through the nose the whole time and the sound hums.
- **Each of three places has a voiceless oral stop, a voiced oral stop and a nasal stop.** At the **lips**, p, b and m. At the **alveolar ridge**, t, d and n. At the **velum**, k, g and ng, the sound at the end of "sing," which are called **velar**.
  - In the MRI images, m, n and ng have the velum hanging down away from the back of the throat, while p, t and k have it raised. A stop is an event in time. First the closure, then pressure or nasal airflow, then the release.

## p6
- **The fricatives and stops fit into one table built on manner of articulation, place of articulation and voice.** Manner is how the air is shaped, a narrow gap, a closure with the nose sealed, or a closure with the nose open. The places run from front to back, **labial, dental, alveolar, postalveolar** and **velar**.
- **Every consonant is a combination of three values.** The sound t is a **voiceless alveolar oral stop**, and z is a **voiced alveolar fricative**. Changing one value gives a different sound, so t becomes d by adding voice, and d becomes n by opening the nose.
- **The empty cells matter too.** English has no dental or postalveolar stops and no velar fricative. The sounds of English form a **highly structured system**, and every speaker uses it without ever having been taught the table.

## p7
- **Artificial intelligence is a model of five psychological skills that animals have, knowing, recognizing, attending, acting and learning.** Knowing is stored usable information, recognizing is identifying what arrives, attending is selecting what to focus on, acting is producing an output, and learning is changing with experience.
- **How AI models each skill is at least slightly based on how humans do it.** AI is not a technical innovation that came out of nowhere. It grew out of ideas about human and animal psychology.

## p8
- **How AI knows something connects to Wilson L. Taylor's 1953 paper "Cloze Procedure," subtitled A New Tool for Measuring Readability**, published in the Fall 1953 issue of **Journalism Quarterly**. It reported three pilot studies and two experiments comparing cloze results with two **readability formulas**.
- **Taylor asked whether people's ability to restore deleted parts of a message could measure how readable that message was.** A tool built to measure human understanding of language ended up describing what it means for a system to know something.

## p9
- **Taylor called cloze procedure a new psychological tool for measuring the effectiveness of communication.** The method is straightforward, the data are easily counted, and its results agreed with the **Flesch** and **Dale-Chall** readability formulas. He credited the experimental psychologists **Charles Osgood** and **Melvin Marks** for starting the work.
- **A cloze is the unit of measurement, pronounced like "close," and named after closure.** **Closure** is the Gestalt psychology term for the human tendency to **complete a familiar pattern that is not quite finished**, such as seeing a broken circle as whole.
  - The three black circles with wedges cut out produce a bright white triangle that is not actually drawn. The visual system completes the triangle from the partial information.

## p10
- **The same principle applies to language.** Given "Chickens cackle and ___ quack," almost anyone supplies "ducks." Supplying the removed word scores **one cloze unit**.
  - Filling that blank takes the **meanings** and **forms** of all five words, the meanings of their **combinations**, and the parallel structure that pairs an animal with its sound.
- **Taylor defined a cloze unit as a successful attempt to reproduce a part deleted from a message by deciding from the remaining context what is missing.** **Cloze procedure** intercepts a message from a transmitter, deletes parts, and gives the damaged message to receivers, whose repairs produce countable cloze units.
- **AI shows its knowledge of something by completing, or clozing, sequences.** A teacher tests a student the same way, by asking for the rest of a sequence. From the AI point of view, **knowledge is nothing more than completion**.

## p11
- **GPT takes in a sequence of words and predicts the next one.** To a computer, a word is **a bunch of random numbers**, with the one requirement that **no two words share the same numbers**. Such a list is a **vector**, and one word's vector could hold 1024 numbers, drawn as a tall bar for each of "chickens," "cackle," "and" and "ducks."
- **For "chickens cackle and ducks ___," a system that knows English outputs a vector of 10,000 numbers, each the probability that a given English word comes next.** "Quack" gets a high probability, while "cat" and "Zulu" get very low ones. Its knowledge of English shows up entirely in how well it completes the blank.

## p12
- **The list of probabilities is called a pdf, a probability distribution function, and the input vectors are the x's.** GPT turns the x's into the pdf by **recognizing by computing**, and the computing is mostly **addition, subtraction, multiplication and division**, grade school arithmetic done an enormous number of times.
- **The knowledge that transforms the x's into the pdf is stored in numbers called w's, or weights.** The x's change with every sentence, the w's stay fixed while the system is used, and changing the weights changes the knowledge. Today's systems have **about a couple of trillion weights**.

## p13
- **Trillions of weights are not too many to resemble human knowledge.** Knowing that chickens cackle but ducks quack is stored in the **synapses**, the connections between the **neurons** of the brain.
- **The brain has about 150 trillion synapses, roughly 75 times the 2 trillion weights of today's AI systems.** In both cases knowledge is stored in a huge number of connection strengths rather than in a list of facts.

## p14
- **Many people find "knowledge is completion" nonsensical, including Yann LeCun, who raised a billion dollars on that position.** Anyone who uses an AI system can judge for themselves whether its notion of knowledge is **totally different** from their own. One reasonable answer is that it is **not totally different**.
- **Another term for completion is prediction, and that word sits better with people.** Humans constantly **predict** and **expect** what comes next, so knowledge as completion is knowledge as prediction.

## p15
- **AI learns by making mistakes, and humans may not be that different.** In the Reddit cartoon "How much you learn," the stack of books "From mistakes" is far taller than "From theory" or "From practice."
- **The main learning algorithm in AI is Stochastic Gradient Descent, or SGD**, which can be understood through the very easy example of **averaging**.

## p16
- **The ordinary way to average 10, 12 and 14 is to add them and divide by three, which gives 12.** This direct method does not work for speech, language, biological molecules or scenes, where no formula gives the answer in one step.
- **The AI way starts with a wild guess x, then improves x by exposure to the data.** Take one number from the list, compare it with x, move x a bit closer to it, and repeat with another number. This is **observation-based error learning**.

## p17
- **SGD finds the average, the essence, of [78, 67, 98, 85, 76, 68, 98, 72] in five steps.** The true average is 80.25, but SGD never does that calculation.
  - Pick any random number as the estimate, so x[0] is 130. Randomly pick a data point, so d[0] is 98. Measure the mistake, the **Loss**, which is 130 minus 98, or 32. Subtract a small part of the Loss, 10 percent, so x[1] is 130 minus 3.2, which is 126.8. Repeat with another data point to get x[2], then x[3].
- **If the estimate starts too low, the Loss is negative and subtracting it raises the estimate.** From 10 with a draw of 78, the estimate rises by 6.8 to 16.8. The estimate is always pushed toward the data.
- **Stochastic means random, gradient refers to the Loss, and descent means reducing.** The algorithm makes an initial guess and uses data to improve it.

## p18
- **The fraction of the Loss subtracted at each step is the learning rate.** The four graphs plot the estimate x against 300 **epochs** for learning rates of 10, 50, 1 and 80 percent, each with runs starting from **initial conditions** of 130 (red), 80 (green) and 10 (blue).
  - At 10 percent, the red and blue runs reach the neighborhood of 80 within about 25 to 50 epochs and then wobble slightly. At 1 percent, the runs are smooth but very slow, still short of 80 after 300 epochs. At 50 percent, they arrive within a few epochs but then bounce between about 70 and 95. At 80 percent, the bouncing spreads from about 65 to 97.
- **The bouncing comes from the random single data points.** A large learning rate moves the estimate most of the way toward whichever number was drawn, so a draw of 98 from an estimate of 80 at 80 percent jumps the estimate to 94.4, and the next draw may throw it far back down.

## p19
- **This is the fundamental learning tradeoff, written with the learning rate γ (gamma).** Raising γ raises speed and lowers accuracy, because the estimate arrives fast but keeps jumping. Lowering γ lowers speed and raises accuracy, because the estimate creeps but settles close to the true value.
- **For generations, thousands of people have made their living by finding better ways to optimize learning**, so that speed and accuracy both increase at once.
