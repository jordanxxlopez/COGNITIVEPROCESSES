## p2
- The **2026 Nobel Prize in economics** went to **Robert Hall** and **Michael Woodford** for the **microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization**. **Stabilization** means getting a system to its equilibrium and keeping it there, which is the same job that **balancing feedback** does and the same thing a spring does when it pulls a mass back to rest.
  - In their model of the economy, **unemployment and inflation take turns rising and falling**, like the position and the velocity of a mass on a spring.
  - The aim of stabilization policy is to **make these ups and downs smaller**, so the swings shrink instead of producing high unemployment or runaway inflation.
- Woodford's theory is called **Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium**, or **DSGE**.
  - **Dynamic** means the economy changes over time according to rules.
  - **Stochastic** means the model can handle **random shocks**, such as a war starting or a large company's CEO being caught embezzling. This is similar to stochastic gradient descent, where one odd data point can push the estimate far off, but later data pull it back toward the average.
  - **Equilibrium** means the goal is a steady state without wild jumps.
- **Macroeconomic** quantities describe a whole economy, like inflation or the number of people unemployed, while **microeconomic** events are single choices, like one person buying something. Explaining large patterns from many tiny events is the same kind of problem as speech, where many tiny vibrations in the mouth add up to words that a listener understands without noticing any single vibration.

## p3
- A mass-spring system vibrates because **the spring wants to return to equilibrium** while **a moving mass wants to keep moving**. The same vibration can happen **longitudinally**, left and right along the spring, or **transversely**, up and down between two walls, and both kinds of motion behave in exactly the same way.
  - Understanding both is useful, because the vocal folds are easiest to understand with up-and-down motion, and the air in the vocal tract is easiest to understand with back-and-forth motion.
- **A part only changes the frequency while it is doing its job.** A mass changes the frequency only when it moves, and a spring changes the frequency only when it stretches or compresses. This one idea explains every result about modes.

## p4
- **Raising the mass lowers the frequency**, so the system goes back and forth fewer times per second, and **raising the stiffness raises the frequency**, so it goes back and forth more times per second.
  - A heavier mass is harder to turn around, and a stiffer spring pulls back harder, which is why the two changes push the frequency in opposite directions.

## p5
- With **two masses** there are **two modes**. In **Mode 1** the masses move together, and in **Mode 2** they move against each other, and the system can also do both at once.
  - **Mode 1 is slower** because the middle spring just rides along without stretching, so in practice it has **only two springs**.
  - **Mode 2 is faster** because all **three springs** stretch and compress, and more working springs means **more stiffness** and a **higher frequency**.
- Making the **middle spring stiffer** while the system vibrates leaves **Mode 1 unchanged**, since that spring never stretches in Mode 1. The same change makes **Mode 2 rise**, since in Mode 2 the middle spring stretches and compresses all the time.
  - In plain terms, a spring only matters in a mode where it is being stretched.
- The system can also vibrate in **both modes at once**, with the masses rising and falling together while also moving against each other, which is how real systems usually move.

## p6
- With **three masses** there are **three modes**. In Mode 1 all three move together, in Mode 2 the middle mass stays still while the outer two move against each other, and in Mode 3 the middle mass moves against both outer masses.
- Making **Mass 2**, the middle mass, heavier **lowers the frequency of Mode 1 and Mode 3**, because the middle mass moves in both. The same change has **no effect on Mode 2**, because in Mode 2 the middle mass does not move at all.
  - **A mass affects frequency only through its movement.** If it does not move, it has no effect. If it moves a little, it has a small effect. If it moves a lot, it has a large effect.

## p7
- **Every added mass adds one more mode**, and the same patterns repeat as the chain grows.
  - **Mode 1** is always everything going up and down together.
  - **Mode 2** always splits the system into **two parts** going against each other, and with an odd number of masses the middle mass stays still.
  - **Mode 3** always splits the system into **three parts**, with the middle going one way and the sides the other.
- Packing in more and more masses and springs gives a **string**. **Every little piece of a string has weight, which is its mass, and every little piece pulls back when it is stretched or compressed, which is its springiness.**
  - In musical instruments, these frequencies are heard as **notes**, and the ear can pick out many instruments at once because it is itself built of masses and springs.
  - Higher modes have more parts going against each other, so the springs stretch and compress far more, and those modes vibrate faster.
  - The same mode patterns appear in the string, with everything moving together in Mode 1, the two halves going against each other in Mode 2, and the middle going against the sides in Mode 3.

## p8
- A string fixed at both ends with its first mode at **100 hertz** has its next modes at **200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz**, so every mode is an **exact multiple** of the first. These multiples are called **harmonics**.
  - The second mode holds **two** copies of the first mode's bump, the third holds **three**, and the fifth holds **five**, so each mode's frequency is the first frequency times the number of copies.
- A single mode by itself is a **pure tone**, which sounds so annoying that it is used for sirens. What we normally hear has many modes at the same time, so a real string's motion looks messy and irregular, much like the waveform of speech.
- The **spectrum** shows the mixture, with **frequency** along the horizontal axis and **amplitude** up the vertical axis. For this string, it has spikes at 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz.

## p9
- All of the results so far follow from a short set of **principles of mass-spring systems**, and they all fit together.
  - **Raising the mass lowers the frequency, and raising the stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - **A system with n masses has n modes of vibration**, and each higher mode has a higher frequency.
  - **Adding mass lowers the frequency more when that spot moves more.** How much a spot moves depends on where it is.
  - **Where there is maximal movement there is no stretching, and where there is maximal stretching there is no movement.**
  - **Adding stiffness raises the frequency more when that spot stretches and compresses more.** This also depends on where the spot is.
  - In a **fixed-fixed system**, the harmonics are **multiples of the lowest frequency**, because each mode holds a whole number of copies of the lowest mode, and in a **fixed-free system** they are **odd multiples**, because it fits an odd number of times.

## p10
- Making one piece of a string **heavier**, such as thickening its very middle, changes some modes and not others.
  - In **Mode 1**, the middle moves up and down a lot, so the added mass **lowers** its frequency.
  - In **Mode 2**, the middle does not move at all, so the added mass changes **nothing**.
  - In **Mode 3** the middle moves again, so its frequency drops, and in **Mode 4** the middle is still again, so nothing changes. Going up the modes, the effect **alternates**.
- **Motion and stretching alternate along a vibrating string.** Where a piece moves the most, it is just carried up and down and is hardly stretched, and where the string is stretched the most, such as right next to a fixed end, there is **no movement** at all.
  - **The shortcut is that the springiest spots are the spots that do not move.** In **Mode 5** of a string fixed at both ends, the string does not move at the **two fixed ends** or at the **four points where it crosses its middle line**, so those **six places** have the most springiness.
  - The **most motion**, and so the **least springiness**, is at the tops of the **five bumps**.

## p11
- Changing one small piece of a string can push two modes in **opposite directions**. Take a spot where **Mode 4 is moving the most** and where **Mode 5 crosses its middle line**, so Mode 5 is not moving there at all, and raise both the **mass** and the **stiffness** of that piece.
  - **For Mode 4**, the piece is moving, so the extra mass **lowers** the frequency, and the piece is barely stretched, so the extra stiffness does nothing.
  - **For Mode 5**, the piece does not move, so the extra mass does nothing, and the piece is stretched the most, so the extra stiffness **raises** the frequency.
  - So the same change **lowers Mode 4's frequency and raises Mode 5's frequency**. Work out each mode separately by asking whether the spot is moving or stretching in that mode.
- Predicting what a small change at one spot will do is called **perturbation theory**. It was first worked out with **drums** in the late nineteenth century by adding small amounts of **wax** to different spots.
  - Wax on a spot predicted to vibrate a lot added mass where the drum moves, and the drum's frequency was heard to **drop**.
  - Wax on a spot predicted not to move at all made **no difference** to the sound.

## p12
- The **larynx** holds the **vocal folds**, which form a **membrane** that is a bit like a drum, so they have many modes of vibration. In Mode 1 all the masses go up and down together, in Mode 2 the two halves go against each other, and the higher modes divide the surface into more and more parts.
  - Because the vocal folds are fixed around their edges, they behave like a system fixed at both ends, so their frequencies are harmonics, whole-number multiples of the lowest one.
- Changes to the mass and stiffness of the vocal folds change the voice. People who smoke heavily can develop **nodules** on their vocal folds, which add mass at one spot and change the stiffness of nearby parts, so the folds vibrate differently and the voice sounds different.

## p13
- The **theory** is that the vocal folds vibrate in many modes at once, so a measured voice should have a spectrum with **equidistant harmonics**. A voice recorded and examined with **Praat** had its lowest harmonic at roughly 130 hertz, and the next harmonics fell close to twice and three times that value, so **the measurement matched the prediction.**
  - The **scientific method** works this way, with a clear theory written as **differential equations** whose solution gives a prediction that can be measured and checked.
  - Each person's lowest frequency is different, but the harmonics above it are always close to multiples of it.

## p14
- A **building** is **fixed on one side and free on the other**, because it is fixed to the earth at the bottom while the top can sway in very high winds or an earthquake. Engineers design it to sway only in **Mode 1**. That can feel unpleasant on a high floor, but it is safe. Swaying in a higher mode could break the building.
- The **air inside the vocal tract** is also a fixed-free system. At the bottom, the **vocal folds are closed**, so the air **cannot move** and that end is **fixed**, and at the lips the air can go in and out easily, so that end is **free**.
  - Unlike a building, the air in the vocal tract vibrates in **all of its modes**, and when we speak, we change the mass and stiffness of the air in different places.
- A fixed-free system has **frequencies at 100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz**, which are only the odd multiples of 100. The first mode goes from **no motion** at the fixed end to **lots of motion** at the free end, which is only a quarter of a wave, and the next possible modes hold **three** and then **five** of these pieces.
  - Two pieces would end with no motion at both ends, which is a fixed-fixed pattern, so even multiples never occur.

## p15
- The air in the vocal tract can be modeled as a **chain of masses and springs** that is fixed at the glottis end and free at the lip end. The masses stand for the air's tendency to keep moving, and the springs stand for its springiness.
  - The lowest mode is like the **first formant**, **F1**, and the next mode is like the **second formant**, **F2**. The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called **formants**, while those of a fixed-fixed system are called harmonics.
  - The second mode oscillates **three times faster** than the first, because it holds three of the first mode's quarter-wave pieces.
- Three places along the tube matter for vowels. **/a/** is constricted **one third of the way from the glottis**, **/i/** is constricted **two thirds of the way from the glottis**, and **/u/** is constricted at the **free end at the lips**.

## p16
- A **constriction** is a place where the tongue or lips narrow the tract and squeeze the air, and the **glottis** is the tiny space between the vocal folds at the fixed end, so distances are measured from there.
  - In **done**, the central vowel, there is **no constriction**.
  - In **dean**, a **high front** vowel, the air is squeezed under the roof of the mouth, about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **Don**, a **low back** vowel, the air is squeezed far back in the throat, about **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **dune**, a **high back** vowel made with **lip rounding**, the air is squeezed **at the lips**, and **Dan** is a **low front** vowel.

## p18
- **Constricting air raises both its stiffness and its mass.** Squeezing air is like squeezing the air in a tire, which makes it **stiffer**, and packing air into a smaller volume raises its **density**, which makes it more **massive**.
- It might seem that raising mass and stiffness together would **cancel out**. **That is false** for any system with many masses and springs, because each spot is either mostly **moving** or mostly **stretching** in a given mode.
  - At a spot with no motion and lots of stretching, only the extra stiffness counts, so the frequency **rises**.
  - At a spot with lots of motion and no stretching, only the extra mass counts, so the frequency **falls**.
  - Speech itself shows the effects do not cancel, because constrictions in different places clearly change the frequencies of the voice.

## p19
- For **/u/**, rounding the lips squeezes the air at the **free end**, which raises the mass and the stiffness of the air there.
  - In **Mode 1**, the free end has lots of motion and almost no compression, so **motion matters most** and the extra mass lowers **F1**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the free end again has lots of motion, so **motion matters most** and the extra mass lowers **F2**.
  - So **both F1 and F2 go down**.
- A speaker who started from the central vowel, with formants near 500, 1500 and 2500 hertz, and moved into /u/ showed **both formants dropping**, as predicted.
  - The rounded lips added mass to the air right where it moves the most, and the extra stiffness did nothing there, because the air at the lips is moving, not stretching.

## p20
- For **/i/**, the air is squeezed about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**, and that one location acts differently in the two modes.
  - In **Mode 1**, this spot has lots of motion and hardly any compression, so **motion matters most** and the extra mass **lowers F1**.
  - In **Mode 2**, this spot has lots of compression, so **springiness matters most** and the extra stiffness **raises F2**.
  - In plain terms, the same squeeze lands on a moving spot for F1 and a springy spot for F2, so one formant falls while the other rises.
- A speaker who went from the central vowel into /i/ showed the **first formant dropping a lot** and the **second formant going up**.
  - The two formants move apart because the same constriction is at a moving spot for one mode and a springy spot for the other.

## p21
- For **/a/**, the air is squeezed about **one third of the way from the glottis**, and the pattern reverses.
  - In **Mode 1**, this spot near the fixed end has lots of compression, so **springiness matters most** and the extra stiffness **raises F1**.
  - In **Mode 2**, this spot has lots of motion, so **motion matters most** and the extra mass **lowers F2**.
  - So for /a/, **F1 goes up and F2 goes down**, and the two formants come closer together.
- **If a constriction raises a formant a lot, the air at that spot must be springy and hardly moving in that mode**, and **if it lowers a formant a lot, the air there must be moving a lot**.

## p22
- The same principles explain the vowels. **Extra mass matters where the air moves, and extra stiffness matters where the air compresses and expands.** Because the vocal tract is fixed at the glottis and free at the lips, its formants are **odd multiples** of the lowest one.

## p23
- The air column can be modeled as **lines of air particles**, where each particle stands for billions of real air molecules, with the closed vocal folds at the **fixed end** and the open lips at the **free end**.
  - Where the lines bunch close together and spread far apart, the air is **compressing and expanding**, which is **springiness**.
  - Where the lines just move back and forth without changing their spacing, the air is acting as a **mass**, which is easiest to see at the **free end**.
- **F1** has a single point of no motion, at the fixed end, **F2** has two, and **F3** has three. For the **schwa**, with no constriction, a measured voice had formants near **500, 1500 and 2500 hertz**, the odd multiples the theory predicts.
  - Between every pair of no-motion points, the air alternates between places of most motion and places of most compression, just like on a vibrating string.

## p24
- A short question like **Where were you a year ago?** uses all of the constriction rules at once. Each sound squeezes the air at a different place, so the formants move up and down continuously instead of sitting at fixed values.
  - When the tongue makes an **/i/**-like squeeze near the front of the mouth, **F1 goes down and F2 goes up**, because that spot is moving in Mode 1 and springy in Mode 2.
  - When the lips round for **you**, **F1 and F2 both go down**, because the air at the lips is moving in every mode.
  - When the tongue moves back and down, as in an **/a/**-like sound, **F1 rises and F2 falls**, because that spot is springy in Mode 1 and moving in Mode 2.
- **Speech is a continuous series of constrictions, not a list of separate vowels**, so the frequencies of the air in the vocal tract are always changing. The same principles of masses and springs predict which way each formant moves at every moment.
