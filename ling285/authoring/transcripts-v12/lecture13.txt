4|A **fixed–fixed string** is held still at both of its ends. Because neither end can move, both ends must be **nodes** in every mode the string can take. That single requirement decides which shapes are allowed, and it explains why the frequencies of this kind of string line up as whole-number multiples. || The simplest shape fits **one half-wave** between the two fixed ends. >> The whole string bulges up and then down as a single broad region, with its largest movement in the middle. >> This is **Mode 1**, and its frequency is the **fundamental**, the lowest frequency the string can produce. || The next shape, **Mode 2**, fits **two half-waves** into the same length. >> A still point, a node, appears in the middle of the string. >> The two halves move in opposite directions, so while the left half rises, the right half falls. || **Modes 3, 4 and 5** continue the pattern, fitting **three, four and five half-waves** into the same length. >> Each added half-wave makes every section of the wave shorter, so the **wavelength** shrinks as the mode number rises. >> The wave speed along the string stays the same, so a shorter wave has to repeat more often. Shorter wavelength means higher frequency. >> Each higher mode contains a whole number of copies of the base half-wave, so its frequency is a **whole-number multiple** of the fundamental. >> Both odd and even counts are allowed, because any whole number of half-waves still leaves both ends at nodes. || The demonstration builds the half-wave sections up one at a time across all the modes. >> The first half-wave section appears in every mode, since every mode contains at least one. >> The second section appears only in Modes 2 through 5, the third only in Modes 3 through 5, and so on, until the fifth section appears in Mode 5 alone. >> These numbered sections are fixed places within each standing-wave shape. They stay where they are while the string moves up and down. They do not count how many cycles have passed, and they do not travel along the string one after another. >> The picture captures the central idea in a simple way. Higher harmonics have more half-wave packets fitted within the same length, and that is exactly what makes them higher.

8|A **fixed–free string** is fixed at the bottom and free at the top. Its allowed shapes are built from **quarter-waves**. A quarter-wave is the stretch of a standing wave that runs from a **node**, where there is no movement, to an **antinode**, where movement is greatest. >> The fixed bottom must be a node, since it cannot move. >> The free top must be an antinode, since nothing holds it back. || **One quarter-wave** satisfies both conditions at once. >> The movement starts at zero at the fixed bottom and grows steadily to its largest value at the free top. >> This is the lowest mode, and its frequency is the **fundamental**. || To build the next allowed shape, quarter-waves are added **two at a time**. >> Adding a single quarter-wave would end the string on a node, which the free top does not allow. >> Adding a pair keeps the bottom at a node and the top at an antinode while putting one more reversal inside the string. >> The four allowed modes therefore contain **one, three, five and seven quarter-waves**. || More sections packed into the same length make each wave shorter. >> With the wave speed unchanged, shorter waves vibrate faster. >> The frequencies are the **first, third, fifth and seventh harmonics**, odd multiples of the fundamental. >> This means the second mode is the third harmonic, the third mode is the fifth harmonic and the fourth mode is the seventh harmonic. Mode number and harmonic number are not the same here. || An **even** number of quarter-waves can never work for this string. >> An even count would always finish at a node at the top. >> A node is the wrong condition for a free end, so even harmonics are missing from a fixed–free system. || The demonstration reveals the quarter-wave packets one by one across all four modes. >> The first packet appears in every mode, while the later packets appear only in the higher modes. >> Only the fourth mode contains packets six and seven. >> Like the half-wave sections on a fixed–fixed string, these numbered pieces mark fixed positions within a standing pattern while the string itself moves back and forth. The counts of 1, 3, 5 and 7 packets match the odd harmonics directly.
9|This **pop quiz** asks how many quarter-waves each **fixed–free pattern** contains. The answer can be read directly from the conditions at the two ends of the string, without any calculation. >> The fixed bottom is always a **node**, a point with no movement. >> The free top is always an **antinode**, a point of greatest movement. >> Every change along the string from a node to an antinode, or from an antinode back to a node, spans exactly **one quarter-wave**. || Counting these sections in the four patterns gives **one, three, five and seven** quarter-waves. >> The simplest pattern goes directly from the node at the bottom to the antinode at the top, which is one quarter-wave. >> Each higher pattern adds two more sections, one more node and one more antinode inside the string. || Packing more sections into the same length shortens the wavelength. >> The wave speed does not change, so a shorter wave repeats faster. >> The higher patterns therefore have higher frequencies. >> The numbered divisions describe the shape of each standing wave. They remain at fixed positions as the material moves back and forth through them. || Two different numbers describe every pattern, and they should not be confused. >> **Mode number** simply counts the allowed patterns in order, first, second, third and fourth. >> **Harmonic number** compares each pattern’s frequency with the fundamental. >> On a fixed–fixed string the two numbers match, but here they do not, because only odd quarter-wave counts fit both endpoint conditions. >> The second allowed mode is the third harmonic, the third allowed mode is the fifth harmonic, and the fourth allowed mode is the **seventh harmonic**, not the fourth.

13|A **constriction** narrows the air passage of the vocal tract, and that narrowing changes two physical properties of the air at the narrowed spot. Understanding both changes, and where each one matters, explains how a single constriction can move two formants in opposite directions. || The first change is in **stiffness**. >> When air is compressed, as it is in a constriction, it resists being compressed further. Its stiffness rises. >> A stiffer region pushes back harder toward its usual spacing, so the vibration returns faster. >> Making a constriction at a place that experiences **lots of compression** therefore **raises the frequency** of that mode. || The second change is in **mass**. >> Bunching air together makes its density larger, which increases the effective mass at that spot. >> In a part of the vocal tract where the air **moves a lot**, the constriction adds moving mass. >> A heavier moving load is harder to turn around, so the vibration slows and the frequency **goes down**. || A constriction therefore increases **both mass and stiffness** at once. That might seem to suggest that the two effects simply cancel and leave the frequency unchanged, but they do not. >> The reason is that the parts of a vibrating medium that **move the most** alternate with the parts that **compress and expand the most**. >> At a spot where a mode moves strongly, the mass effect wins. At a spot where a mode compresses strongly, the stiffness effect wins. >> Each mode places these regions differently along the tract, so the same spot can be a moving region in one mode and a compressing region in another. || The two effects can be told apart by following the air through a full cycle. >> Follow one air element and watch how far it travels. That travel is the movement that carries the local mass. >> Compare the element with its neighbors and watch the gaps between them close and open. That change in spacing is compression, the source of the restoring force. >> A label such as tongue height, or a single frozen frame of an animation, cannot supply both pieces of information. The constriction’s position says where the air’s properties changed, and the mode’s motion says how that change affects that particular resonance. || The same narrowing can act mainly through **mass** for one formant and mainly through **stiffness** for another. >> One formant goes down while the other goes up. >> Neither physical rule has been reversed. Mass still lowers frequency where the air moves, and stiffness still raises frequency where the air compresses.

20|**Source–filter theory** explains vowel sounds by separating two contributions that combine in every vowel. The **source** is the vibration of the vocal folds at the glottis, and the **filter** is the vocal tract above them, which shapes the sound the source produces. Separating them makes it possible to change one while holding the other steady. || The glottal source produces a **harmonic comb**. >> Because the vocal folds vibrate in a repeating pattern, the source has a **fundamental frequency**, called **F0**, along with **harmonics** at every whole-number multiple of F0. >> F0 sets the spacing between the lines of the comb. In the example, the harmonics are **100 Hz** apart, so they sit at 100, 200, 300 Hz and so on, extending up to **4000 Hz**. >> Before the sound passes through the vocal tract, the spectrum looks like a row of evenly spaced vertical lines. In this example the lines are all the same height, which keeps the comparison simple. || The vocal tract filter **sculpts the heights** of those harmonics. >> The tract has its own resonances, called **formants**. Harmonics that fall near a formant are strengthened, and harmonics that fall far from any formant are weakened. >> The horizontal positions of the harmonics stay exactly where they were. Only their heights change. >> The broad outline traced by the tops of the harmonics is the **spectral envelope**. The harmonics are the same, their heights differ, and the envelope is the vowel. || The schwa example labels four resonance regions, **F1, F2, F3 and F4**. >> Its strongest group of harmonics sits at low frequency, around F1. || Reading the spectrum correctly depends on keeping its two axes separate. >> **Frequency** runs along the horizontal axis, and **amplitude** runs up the vertical axis. >> A taller line is a stronger component. It is not a higher-frequency component. >> **F0** is the repetition rate of the source, while **F1** is the lowest resonance of the tract. These are different quantities that happen to share the letter F. >> The vowel is made of many harmonics, not just four tones. The formants decide which of those harmonics are strong, and the values shown describe this one example rather than every speaker.
21|The **/i/ filter** shapes the same 100 Hz harmonic comb into the spectrum of /i/. Its defining pattern is a **low F1** combined with a **high F2**. >> The first strong group of harmonics sits near the low-frequency end of the spectrum, around F1. >> The next strong group sits much farther up the frequency axis, around F2, with a weak region of low-amplitude harmonics in between. >> Further strong groups appear around **F3** and **F4**, near **3000** and **3600 Hz**. || The source has not changed at all. >> The harmonics stay at the same evenly spaced positions they had before the vocal tract acted on them. >> Only their heights change when the tract takes the /i/ shape. >> Because the source lines stay put, the effect of the filter can be seen separately from the repetition of the source. || The low-F1, high-F2 pattern follows from the front constriction of /i/. >> The tongue narrows the tract about two-thirds of the way from the glottis to the lips. >> At that spot, the first mode moves the air strongly, so the added mass lowers F1. >> The second mode compresses the air strongly there, so the added stiffness raises F2. >> The result is two formants pushed far apart, which is the signature of /i/. || Reading this spectrum takes two separate judgments for every line. >> A line’s **horizontal position** gives its **frequency**. >> A line’s **height** gives its **amplitude**. >> A tall line near the low end of the spectrum is still low in frequency, even when a line farther up the axis is weaker. Height and position must not be confused. || The narrow harmonic lines act as samples of the broad formant regions. >> Each line is a single component of the source. >> The outline traced by their heights is the envelope, and that envelope is what identifies the vowel. >> The values describe one schematic /i/, not an exact pattern produced identically by every speaker.
22|The **/a/ filter** reverses the pattern of /i/. Compared with /i/, it has a **higher F1** and a **lower F2**, which brings the first two resonance regions close together. >> In the example, **F1 is at 750 Hz** and **F2 is near 1200 Hz**. >> **F3** and **F4** stay higher in frequency, near **2600** and **3500 Hz**. >> The source is the same 100 Hz harmonic comb. Only the shape of the tract has changed, and that change alone turns one vowel into another. || The filter works by changing the pattern of harmonic **strengths**, and two kinds of change have to be kept apart when reading the spectrum. >> **Moving a formant** shifts which frequency region is favored. That is a change along the horizontal axis. >> **Changing a harmonic’s amplitude** makes that line taller or shorter. That is a change along the vertical axis. >> When the tract moves from /i/ to /a/, F1 moves up and F2 moves down, and the heights of the harmonics change to follow the new envelope. || A formant does not have to land exactly on a harmonic. It can fall **between two source harmonics**. >> The F1 target of 750 Hz lies between the harmonics at **700 Hz** and **800 Hz**. >> The harmonics near the target become stronger, because they are close to the resonance. >> No new source line appears at exactly 750 Hz, because the filter can only strengthen or weaken the frequencies the source provides. It cannot create new ones. || This is why it is important to keep two terms separate. >> A **formant** is a resonance region of the vocal tract, a property of the filter. >> A **harmonic** is a frequency component of the repeating source, a property of the vocal folds. >> The filter determines how strong each harmonic is, and the harmonics, in turn, are what reveal where the formants lie. The values in the drawing belong to this simplified comparison.
23|A **spectrum** and a **spectrogram** are two different ways of displaying the frequency content of a sound. The key difference is time. || A **spectrum** shows **one instant** of sound. >> It describes the frequency components present during one short slice of time. >> Frequency runs along the horizontal axis and amplitude along the vertical axis, so each peak gives the strength of one frequency region at that moment. >> The example takes three successive spectra from the vowel **/i/** and three from the vowel **/a/**. || A **spectrogram** **stacks many spectra over time**. Building one from a series of spectra takes two steps. >> The **first step** turns each spectrum on its side. The spectrum’s horizontal frequency axis becomes the spectrogram’s vertical axis, so a peak near the right side of a spectrum becomes a mark near the top of a spectrogram strip. >> The strips are then placed side by side in order, so time runs from left to right. Moving to the right in a spectrogram means moving later in time, not moving to a higher frequency. || The **second step** turns **spectrum height into darkness**. >> In the grayscale display, **black means high amplitude, gray means medium amplitude and white means low amplitude**. >> A tall peak in the spectrum becomes a dark mark in the spectrogram, and a low dip becomes a pale one. >> Vertical position now gives frequency, and darkness gives strength. A band that becomes darker is growing stronger, while a band that rises is moving to a higher frequency. || The transition from **/i/** to **/a/** is easy to follow in the spectrogram. >> During the change, **F1 rises** and **F2 falls**. >> The two lowest dark bands move toward each other, which matches the /a/ pattern of F1 and F2 close together. >> The higher bands stay above them and change less. >> In this way, the spectrogram follows the changing resonances of the vocal tract through time, which no single spectrum can capture.

25|A practical problem arises when trying to see formants in a spectrum. Formants are properties of the vocal tract filter, but they can only be seen through the source harmonics, because the filter can only change the strength of the frequencies the source supplies. How easy it is to see a formant therefore depends on how closely spaced those harmonics are. || **Closely spaced harmonics** make formants easy to identify. >> Imagine harmonics at **100, 200, 300, 400 Hz** and so on, with **F1 at 300 Hz** and **F2 at 2100 Hz**. >> In that case, the formants structure the amplitudes of many harmonics. Several lines fall near each formant, and their heights rise toward the center of the formant and fall away on either side. >> With so many samples, the shape of each formant region is easy to trace. || Raising **F0** spreads the harmonics apart. >> Compare harmonics at **250, 500, 750 Hz** and so on. >> Now far fewer lines fall near each formant, so the shape of each resonance is sampled only a few times. >> A formant can easily fall between two harmonics, leaving no line at its center. >> A single strong line may sit near a formant without marking its exact location. In this case, it becomes very hard to tell harmonics from formants. || Some voices make the problem much worse because their fundamental frequency is very high. >> A **toddler’s** harmonics could be at **400, 800 Hz** and so on. >> A **soprano’s** could be at **800, 1600, 2400 Hz** and so on. >> Across the three spectra and their spectrograms, the picture moves from many closely spaced components to a handful of isolated traces, and the formants become increasingly hard to read. || It is important to see what has and has not changed. >> The vocal tract filter can be exactly the same in all three cases. It does not lose a resonance, and it does not move one upward. >> What has changed is the source. A higher-pitched source supplies fewer frequencies, and fewer frequencies give a sparser picture of the very same filter.
