2|Every oscillation in this course starts with the same simple system, a **mass** attached to a **spring**. The mass sits at **equilibrium**, the position where all the forces on it balance and it would stay at rest if nothing disturbed it. Pulling or pushing the mass away from equilibrium stretches or compresses the spring, and the spring responds with a **restoring force** that points back toward equilibrium. >> The farther the mass is displaced, the harder the spring pulls back. >> The restoring force speeds the mass up as it heads toward equilibrium. || The motion does not stop at equilibrium, because the mass has **inertia**. Inertia is the tendency of a moving object to keep moving. >> When the mass reaches equilibrium, it is moving at its fastest and carries straight past the resting point. >> Now the spring is displaced on the other side, so the restoring force reverses and slows the mass down. >> The mass stops at the far end, turns around and heads back, and the whole sequence repeats. That repeated back-and-forth movement is an **oscillation**. || The same mass and spring can oscillate in two different directions. >> In **longitudinal motion**, the mass moves along the line of the spring. The spring gets longer and shorter. >> In **transverse motion**, the mass moves across the line of the spring. Two spring segments hold the mass from either side and are already pulled tight. When the mass rises above or falls below their resting line, the segments tilt, and their pull now has a part that points back toward the line. >> In the demonstration, the transverse version appears three seconds after the longitudinal one, so the two directions can be compared side by side. || The two directions repeat at the same **frequency**, the number of complete cycles per second. Here both run at **0.5 Hz**, one full cycle every two seconds. >> They match because the moving mass is the same and the total restoring stiffness is the same, half from each transverse segment in the across direction. >> The direction of motion changes how the restoring force is produced, but not how fast the cycle repeats. >> The model has **no damping**, meaning nothing drains energy away, so the motion keeps its full size instead of fading out.
3|Two physical properties decide how fast a mass on a spring oscillates, how much **mass** is moving and how **stiff** the spring is. The demonstration runs three systems at the same time, in both longitudinal and transverse versions, and changes only one property in each comparison so that the cause of any change is clear. >> The **reference system** has mass **m** and stiffness **K**, and it oscillates at **0.5 Hz**. >> The **larger-mass system** has mass **4m** and the same stiffness **K**, and it oscillates at **0.25 Hz**. >> The **stiffer system** has the original mass **m** and stiffness **4K**, and it oscillates at **1 Hz**. || **Greater mass lowers the frequency** when the stiffness stays the same. >> A heavier mass has more inertia, so the same spring force changes its motion more slowly. It takes longer to speed up toward equilibrium, longer to slow down on the far side and longer to turn around. >> Each cycle therefore stretches out in time. Making the mass four times larger cuts the frequency to half, from 0.5 Hz to 0.25 Hz. || **Greater stiffness raises the frequency** when the mass stays the same. >> A stiffer spring produces a stronger restoring force for the same displacement, so it pulls the mass back toward equilibrium harder. >> The mass turns around sooner at each end and completes more cycles in the same amount of time. Making the stiffness four times larger doubles the frequency, from 0.5 Hz to 1 Hz. >> Mass and stiffness work in opposite directions on the rate. Mass sets how strongly the system resists changes in its motion, and stiffness sets how strongly the system is pulled back. || Reading the rate correctly means counting full repetitions over the same stretch of time. >> How far the mass travels is its **amplitude**, which describes the size of the motion, not its speed of repetition. A tall swing is not necessarily a fast one. >> Within each row, the longitudinal and transverse versions run at the same frequency because they share the same **effective restoring stiffness**, the restoring action in the actual direction of motion. >> For transverse motion, that stiffness comes from the tension and geometry of the spring segments. Making a spring harder to stretch along its length, without changing its tension, would not automatically raise its transverse frequency.
4|Adding a second mass changes what the system can do. Two equal masses are joined in a line by **three equal springs**, one from each fixed wall to a mass and one in the middle connecting the masses. A system like this has **two normal modes**. >> A **normal mode** is a coordinated pattern of motion in which every part of the system oscillates at the same single frequency and keeps a fixed relationship to every other part. >> The number of modes matches the number of masses that can move independently in the direction being modeled. || In **Mode 1**, both masses move in the same direction at the same time. >> Because they move together, the distance between them stays almost the same, and the middle spring is barely stretched or compressed. >> Only the outer springs do most of the restoring work, so the restoring force is relatively weak. This is the slower mode, at **0.4 Hz** in the demonstration. || In **Mode 2**, the masses move in opposite directions, toward each other and then away from each other. >> Now the middle spring is squeezed hard when they approach and stretched hard when they separate. >> That extra restoring action from the middle spring makes the cycle faster. Mode 2 runs at about **1.73 times** the frequency of Mode 1. It is clearly higher, but not exactly double. || A normal mode belongs to the whole system, not to a single mass. >> Both masses move in Mode 1, and both move in Mode 2. One mass does not carry the slow mode while the other carries the fast one. >> The physical parts never change between the modes. What changes is the relationship between the masses, together in one case and opposed in the other, and that relationship decides how hard the middle spring works. || The two modes can happen at the same time on the very same masses. >> At each mass, the motion from Mode 1 and the motion from Mode 2 simply **add**. Where both push the mass the same way, they reinforce. Where they push opposite ways, they partly or completely cancel. >> This adding of patterns is called **superposition**. The bottom row of the demonstration is the exact sum of the two modes, so its motion looks irregular even though it is built from two perfectly regular patterns. >> The combined motion contains both frequencies. The longitudinal and transverse chains show the same relationships, only in different directions of movement.

6|With **three equal masses** connected by **four equal springs** between two fixed ends, the chain has **three normal modes**, one for each movable mass. Each mode is a different way for the same three masses to move together at a single shared frequency. || In **Mode 1**, all three masses move in the same direction at the same time. >> The middle mass travels the farthest, while the two outer masses travel a shorter distance. Moving together does not require moving equal distances. >> Because no neighbors oppose each other, the springs between the masses change length only a little. This is the lowest-frequency mode, at **0.32 Hz** in the demonstration. || In **Mode 2**, the two outer masses move in opposite directions while the middle mass stays perfectly still at equilibrium. >> The middle mass stays put because the pull from its left side and the pull from its right side are always equal and opposite, so they cancel. >> A place that stays still throughout a mode is called a **node**. In Mode 2, the middle mass sits on a node. >> This mode runs at about **1.85 times** the lowest frequency. || In **Mode 3**, the two outer masses move together in one direction while the middle mass moves the opposite way. >> Every mass now moves against its neighbor, so all of the connecting springs are strongly stretched and compressed on every cycle. >> That strong restoring action makes Mode 3 the fastest pattern, at about **2.41 times** the lowest frequency. || The order of the modes follows how strongly neighboring masses oppose one another. >> In Mode 1, the masses broadly move together. In Mode 2, the outer masses oppose each other while the middle is balanced. In Mode 3, the middle opposes both outer masses. >> More opposition between neighbors means more stretching in the springs, a stronger restoring force and a faster cycle. >> These are three arrangements of the same system, not a sequence in which another mass starts moving at each step. The colors track each individual mass, and the resting marks give the position each displacement is measured from. || The frequency ratios of 1, about 1.85 and about 2.41 belong to this particular chain. They are not the simple 1, 2 and 3 of a continuous string. >> When the three modes run together, their motions add at each mass, and the bottom row is that sum. >> The middle mass still moves in the combined motion, because Modes 1 and 3 both move it, even though it stays still in Mode 2. The longitudinal and transverse chains follow the same relationships.
7|The **normal-mode atlas** gathers the pattern into a single grid. Each row is a chain with a different number of equal masses, from one up to six, and each column is a mode number. The rule behind the grid is simple, because each movable mass adds exactly one more **normal mode** in the direction being modeled. >> A chain of one mass has one mode, a chain of four masses has four, and a chain of six has six. >> Smaller chains leave the later columns empty, since they have no such modes. Counting every filled cell gives **21** patterns in all. >> Every chain in the atlas has equal masses, one more coupling spring than masses and two fixed endpoints. || Within any one chain, the modes rise in a regular way. >> **Mode 1** is always the broadest pattern, with all of the masses moving in the same direction. >> Each higher mode adds another group of masses moving in the opposite direction. Mode 2 has two groups, Mode 3 has three, and so on. >> More reversals across the chain mean more places where neighboring masses pull against each other. Those springs stretch more, the restoring force grows and the pattern repeats faster. || The fixed endpoints stay still in every mode, because they are attached to the supports. >> Inside a chain, a mass that stays still in one particular mode is a **node** of that pattern. It does not mean the chain has stopped, since the other masses keep moving. >> A higher mode does not use smaller masses or stronger replacement springs. The parts are identical within a row, and only the relationship between their movements changes. >> The mode number therefore names a pattern of the whole chain, not a particular mass that happens to be moving. || The colors in the atlas mark mode number, so the same color across rows means the same mode number in chains of different sizes. >> Every pattern is scaled to the same largest displayed displacement. A tall picture is not a fast picture, and frequency must be read from how often the pattern repeats. >> **Amplitude** is how far the parts move, while frequency is how often the motion repeats. >> The demonstration runs each mode with its own frequency, so the higher columns visibly cycle faster. Chains made of separate masses generally do not reach the exact whole-number frequency spacing of an ideal continuous string.
8|A **fixed–fixed string** is a continuous string held still at both ends. Because both ends cannot move, both are **nodes** in every mode. The string can only vibrate in shapes that fit a whole number of **half-waves** between those two fixed points. >> **Mode 1** fits a single half-wave, one broad arch from end to end. >> **Mode 2** fits two half-waves, with a node in the middle. >> **Modes 3, 4 and 5** fit three, four and five half-waves, adding one more node each time. || Fitting more half-waves into the same length makes each wave shorter. >> A wave of shorter **wavelength** traveling at the same speed along the string repeats more often, so its frequency is higher. >> The five modes run at **100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz**. The lowest, 100 Hz, is the **fundamental**, and the others are exact whole-number multiples of it. >> Frequencies spaced this way are called **harmonics**. Here they are consecutive harmonics, including both the odd ones and the even ones. || One string can carry all five modes at the same time. >> At every point along the string, the displacement from each mode adds to the others. Some contributions point the same way and reinforce, and some point opposite ways and cancel. >> As the faster modes run ahead of the slower ones, the pattern of reinforcement and cancellation keeps shifting, so the combined outline of the string keeps changing shape. >> A point can look nearly flat at one moment through cancellation and then move strongly a moment later. No mode has disappeared when that happens, since every component is still present and still regular. || The animation is slowed by a factor of **250**, so the 100 Hz mode is displayed at **0.4 Hz** and the 500 Hz mode at **2.0 Hz**. >> All five modes are slowed by the same factor, which keeps their relative rates exactly as they are in the real string. || A **pickup** placed **17%** of the way along the string measures the motion at that one point. >> Its **spectrum** plots frequency along the horizontal axis and amplitude along the vertical axis. Each of the five components produces its own peak at its own frequency. >> The five peaks are equal in height because each mode’s strength was chosen to give the same contribution at this particular pickup point. >> A pickup at a different spot would record different heights, since each mode moves different points by different amounts. Equal peaks at the pickup do not mean equal motion everywhere along the string.
9|A **membrane** is a vibrating surface held at its outer edges. Its modes are labeled with **two numbers** instead of one, because the surface can divide in two directions. The first number counts moving regions across one direction, and the second counts regions across the other. >> **(1,1)** has a single broad region, with the whole surface rising and falling together. >> **(2,1)** splits the surface into two regions along one direction, and **(1,2)** splits it into two regions along the other. >> **(2,2)** divides it into four regions, two by two. >> **(3,1)** divides it into three regions along one direction. >> Neighboring regions always move in opposite directions, and the lines between them are **nodes**, which stay still. All of the outer edges are fixed, so they stay still as well. || The five modes can all run at once on one membrane. >> At every point, their contributions add. Some reinforce and some cancel, producing one single surface that keeps changing shape. >> The combined panel is drawn with a smaller vertical scale than the single-mode panels so that it fits, which means its apparent height cannot be compared directly with theirs. || It is important to separate a **node** from a region that is simply passing through rest. >> A node stays still for the entire cycle of its mode. >> A moving region also crosses the flat resting surface for an instant on every cycle, on its way from up to down. That brief crossing does not make it a node. >> Watching one region over time makes the difference clear. It rises, crosses rest and falls, while the region next to it does exactly the opposite. >> The two numbers in a label describe how the surface is divided. They are not two separate frequencies, two different membranes or a count of how many cycles have passed. || A **pickup** at one marked point on the surface records the motion there, and its **spectrum** has one peak for each of the five modes. >> The strength of each mode was chosen so that the five peaks come out equal in height at that point. >> The frequencies **100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz** were **assigned for the illustration**. They were chosen for teaching and are not the natural resonant frequencies of this rectangular membrane, which would not be evenly spaced. >> The even spacing therefore models how separate frequencies combine on a surface. It does not establish that every membrane naturally vibrates with harmonic spacing.
10|The **scientific method** depends on stating very clear theories about what the world is like. In physics and in speech science, those theories usually take the form of **differential equations**. >> A differential equation describes how the quantities in a system change, such as how displacement and velocity change from one moment to the next. >> Solving the equations produces a **prediction**, a specific statement about what we should find when we actually measure the world. >> If the measurement matches the prediction, the theory is supported. If it does not match, the theory has a problem. || The theory proposed here is that the **vocal folds are a membrane**. >> A vibrating membrane, like the ones already modeled, produces a wave of vibration with **equidistant peaks** in its spectrum. >> These evenly spaced peaks are **harmonics**, frequencies at whole-number multiples of a lowest frequency. >> The equations for the vocal folds are of the same type as the spring and membrane equations, only somewhat more complicated. || The theory makes a clear, testable **prediction**. >> If anyone’s **laryngeal vibration** signal is measured, its spectrum should show **equidistant harmonics**, peaks separated by equal frequency intervals. >> The way to test this is an **experiment** that records real vocal-fold vibration and examines the spacing of the peaks in its spectrum. >> Evenly spaced peaks would agree with the theory. A different pattern would cast doubt on treating the vocal folds as a membrane, at least for that signal. || The prediction is more precise than simply saying the vocal folds vibrate. >> Many vibrating systems produce peaks that are not evenly spaced, so the spacing is what gives the prediction its force. >> The membrane animation used frequencies that were assigned to be harmonic, so watching it again cannot test anything about real vocal folds. It pictures the idea, while a recording of a real voice is evidence about it. >> The experiment is proposed here as the next way to find out whether the theory is true.
11|A **fixed–free system** is held still at one end and left free to move at the other. In the demonstration, the bottom end is fixed and the top end is free. The two ends impose two different conditions on every allowed shape. >> The fixed end must be a **node**, a point with no movement. >> The free end must be an **antinode**, the point that moves the most over a cycle. >> An ideal free end of this kind can be built as a frictionless sliding endpoint that keeps the string under tension. || The shapes that satisfy both conditions fit odd numbers of **quarter-waves** into the length. >> The simplest mode fits **one quarter-wave**, rising from the fixed bottom to its greatest movement at the free top. >> The next modes fit **three, five and seven quarter-waves**. >> These correspond to the **first, third, fifth and seventh harmonics**. The second allowed mode is therefore the third harmonic, not the second, and the third allowed mode is the fifth harmonic. || Only odd counts of quarter-waves work. >> An even count would end on a node at the free end, which would violate the free-end condition. >> As more quarter-waves are fitted into the same length, each wave gets shorter and repeats faster, so higher modes have higher frequencies. || The demonstration uses **100, 300, 500 and 700 Hz**, displayed at **0.4, 1.2, 2.0 and 2.8 Hz** after slowing everything by a factor of **250**. >> The four modes are given equal strengths, so the spectrum measured at the free end shows four equal peaks, with frequency on the horizontal axis and amplitude on the vertical. >> An antinode does not stay at its farthest position. It swings through rest and out to the other side on every cycle, while the fixed end never moves. >> Counting the allowed shapes and naming their harmonic numbers answer different questions, even though both use numbers. || The fixed–fixed and fixed–free examples were **tuned separately** so that both have a 100 Hz fundamental. >> If only one endpoint were changed from fixed to free, with the same length and wave speed, the fundamental would drop to half its value. >> The matching labels in the two examples come from separate tuning, so they do not isolate the effect of freeing one end.
