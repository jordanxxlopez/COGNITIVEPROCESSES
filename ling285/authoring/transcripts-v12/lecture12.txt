2|A **dynamic system** is any system whose state changes over time because its parts keep influencing one another. Masses and springs are one example, but the same kind of thinking is used far beyond physics, including in economics. >> **Woodford’s theory** of the economy is called **Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium**, or **DSGE**. >> Each part of the name describes the system. “Dynamic” means it changes over time. “Stochastic” means random shocks keep disturbing it. “General equilibrium” means many linked markets settle toward balance together. >> Seen this way, an economy is a system that keeps getting pushed and keeps responding, much as a spring system does. || The stabilization picture follows two economic quantities over time. >> The **black curve** tracks **unemployment**. >> The **gray curve** tracks **inflation**. >> In the unstabilized period, both curves rise and fall through large, sharp peaks and troughs. In the stabilized period, the swings become noticeably smaller and smoother. || **Stabilization** means reducing the size of those swings. >> It does not mean freezing the economy so that nothing changes, since both curves still move. >> It also does not mean turning unemployment and inflation into the same quantity, since the two curves remain separate. >> The practical goal is to avoid harmful extremes, such as unemployment rising very high or inflation running out of control. || The connection to physical systems lies in one shared question, what produces a fluctuation and what can make it smaller. >> In a spring system, inertia and a restoring force produce the oscillation. >> In an economy, shocks and the responses of the economy’s linked parts produce the swings, and policy aims to calm them. >> The curves picture the goal of smaller, smoother swings. They are an illustration of what stabilization means, not a measured test of any particular policy.

9|The **principles of mass–spring systems** collect the main ideas about oscillators into seven statements. Together they explain how a change in mass or stiffness, at a particular place, changes a particular mode. || **Principle 1** is about the whole system. **Raising the mass lowers the frequency, and raising the stiffness raises it.** >> A heavier moving load has more inertia, so the same restoring force takes longer to turn it around. >> A stiffer spring produces a stronger restoring force, so the motion reverses sooner and more cycles fit into each second. || **Principle 2** counts the modes. **A system with n masses has n modes of vibration**, and each mode has a higher frequency than the one before. >> One mass gives one mode, two masses give two, and so on. || **Principles 3, 4 and 5** add the idea that **location matters**. >> Added mass lowers a mode’s frequency in proportion to **how much that mass moves** in the mode, and how much it moves depends on where it sits. >> Added stiffness raises a mode’s frequency in proportion to **how much that spring stretches or compresses** in the mode, which also depends on position. >> **Where movement is greatest, there is no stretching, and where stretching is greatest, there is no movement.** When neighboring parts travel together, the spacing between them stays the same, so the spring between them does not stretch. When neighbors move in opposite directions, the spring between them stretches strongly, even if the point in the middle hardly moves. >> In these ideal patterns, the regions of greatest movement and greatest stretching therefore alternate. || These principles mean the same added mass can have very different effects in different modes. >> It matters a great deal in a mode that moves that spot strongly. >> It matters very little in a mode that barely moves that spot, such as at a node. >> A node of one mode is still only for that mode. The same point can move in other modes and in a mixture of modes. || **Principles 6 and 7** explain the harmonic patterns set by the endpoints. >> In a **fixed–fixed system**, the harmonics are **whole-number multiples** of the base frequency, because the base shape fits a whole number of times into each higher mode. >> In a **fixed–free system**, the harmonics are **odd multiples** of the base frequency, because the base shape fits an odd number of times into each higher mode. >> Chains of separate masses follow the same counting rule for modes, but their frequencies are generally not spaced as exact harmonics.

11|The **pop quiz** tests the location principle on a single string. A region just to the left of the midpoint is highlighted, and the question is how adding mass or stiffness there would change **Mode 4** and **Mode 5**. || The two modes use the highlighted region in very different ways. >> In **Mode 4**, the region moves strongly through each cycle. Neighboring points move in nearly the same way, so the string around the region barely stretches. >> In **Mode 5**, the region moves very little. Its neighbors move differently from one another, so the string around the region stretches and compresses strongly. >> These descriptions refer to a full cycle of each mode, not to a single frozen frame that might catch a point passing through rest. || **Adding mass** to the region depends on movement. >> In Mode 4, the extra mass has to be carried through a large swing on every cycle. The heavier load is harder to turn around, so **Mode 4’s frequency goes down**. >> In Mode 5, the region hardly moves, so the extra mass is barely carried anywhere. **Mode 5’s frequency is essentially unaffected.** >> In the quiz’s words, increasing mass lowers frequency only if the mass is moving. || **Adding stiffness** to the region depends on stretching. >> In Mode 5, the string around the region stretches strongly, so a stiffer spring there adds a lot of restoring force. **Mode 5’s frequency goes up.** >> In Mode 4, the string around the region barely stretches, so extra stiffness adds little restoring force. **Mode 4’s frequency is essentially unaffected.** || If both mass and stiffness are increased at that spot, the two effects separate cleanly between the modes. >> The mass increase **lowers Mode 4**. >> The stiffness increase **raises Mode 5**. >> One local change therefore moves two modes in opposite directions. The location is identical in both patterns, but its role is different, mostly movement in one mode and mostly stretching in the other.

15|The air inside the vocal tract can be modeled as a chain of masses joined by springs, just like the mechanical chains. >> Air has **inertia**. Once a parcel of air is moving, it tends to keep moving, which is the mass-like part of the model. >> Air also resists being squeezed. Compressing it raises its pressure, and the higher pressure pushes back toward normal spacing. The spring-like links in the model represent that restoring effect. >> The air elements move **longitudinally**, back and forth along the tube. They oscillate around their usual positions rather than flowing steadily from the glottis out through the lips. || The tube is treated as a **fixed–free** system. >> The **glottis**, the opening between the vocal folds at the bottom of the tract, is the **fixed end**, where the air has little room to move back and forth. >> The open **lips** are the **free end**, where the air can move most freely. || The two lowest patterns of this tube correspond to the first two formants of speech. >> **Mode 1 is F1-like**. It is the lowest resonance of the tube, with all of the air moving in one broad pattern. >> **Mode 2 is F2-like**. It adds one more reversal inside the tube, so some air moves one way while other air moves the opposite way. >> In the ideal uniform tube, Mode 2 repeats **three times** as fast as Mode 1, because a fixed–free system has harmonics at odd multiples. Changing the shape of the tract can alter that simple ratio. || Each resonance pattern is a coordinated motion of the whole column of air. >> F1 and F2 are not two separate air streams, and they are not two different places where sound comes out. >> At some locations in a mode, neighboring air elements travel together while their spacing hardly changes. At other locations, the gaps between neighbors keep closing and opening. >> A narrowing at one spot acts on both modes at that same physical place, but because each mode moves and compresses that spot differently, the narrowing plays a different role in each. || Three markers locate the constrictions of three vowels along the tube. >> The **/a/** marker is about **one-third** of the way from the glottis. >> The **/i/** marker is about **two-thirds** of the way from the glottis. >> The **/u/** marker is at the **lips**. >> Each marker is the same physical location in both modes. Added mass matters where a mode moves strongly, and added springiness matters where it compresses strongly, which is why one narrowing can affect F1 and F2 in different ways.
16|Vowels are described with two articulatory dimensions, **tongue height** and **front–back position**. Each vowel also narrows the air passage at a particular place along the path from the glottis to the lips. The chart arranges five words, dean, dune, Dan, Don and done, according to these dimensions. || The two **high** vowels sit at the top of the chart, but they narrow the tract in different places. >> **Dean** is **high and front**. Its constriction is marked about **two-thirds** of the way from the glottis to the lips. >> **Dune** is **high and back**. Its constriction is highlighted at the **lips**. >> Both vowels are high, yet their tract shapes are different. Sharing a tongue height does not mean sharing a constriction location. || The two **low** vowels sit at the bottom of the chart. >> **Dan** is **low and front**, and it has no numbered constriction position. >> **Don** is **low and back**, with its constriction marked about **one-third** of the way from the glottis. || **Done** is **central** and labeled **“No constriction.”** >> Its tract is relatively open and uniform, so it serves as the reference shape that the other vowels are compared with. || The labels describe related but different features of a vowel. >> **High versus low** concerns how high the tongue is. >> **Front versus back** concerns where the tongue sits along the mouth. >> The **fraction** concerns distance along the air path, measured from the glottis. >> Because these features are separate, one shared label such as high leaves the other features open. Dean and dune can both be high while narrowing at different places, and those places play different roles in the second resonance. || Locating the constriction connects articulation with acoustics. >> A narrowing changes the local mass-like and spring-like behavior of the air at that spot. >> Its effect on each formant depends on how much that mode moves the air there and how much it compresses the air there. >> The articulatory description tells what changed physically, and the air-mode description explains what that change does to the sound.

19|The **/u/ model** adds loading at the open lips, which is the **free end** of the air column. Loading the lips means that more air has to be moved back and forth there. In the model, the elements at the lips are drawn larger to represent this greater **effective mass**. >> The larger elements do not mean the air molecules themselves have become heavier. >> They mean that the moving load at that location is greater. || Both of the lowest resonance patterns move strongly at the free end. >> In **Mode 1**, the F1-like pattern, **motion dominates** at the lips. >> In **Mode 2**, the F2-like pattern, **motion also dominates** at the lips. >> Because the lips are the free end, both patterns have their greatest movement there. || Adding mass where motion dominates slows the oscillation. >> More moving mass is harder to speed up and harder to reverse. >> Each cycle takes longer to complete. >> Since both modes move strongly at the lips, both slow down, so the result is **F1 down and F2 down** compared with the uniform tube. || The two rows make it possible to compare the same lip location across two different resonance patterns. >> The internal arrangements of the two modes are different, since Mode 2 has an extra reversal along the tube. >> Yet both move strongly at the lips, so the extra load slows both. >> It does not make their shapes or frequencies identical. F1 stays the lower resonance and F2 stays the higher one. >> The important comparison is the direction of each change relative to its own uniform reference, not whether the two formants become equal. || The shared direction of change follows from the role that the lip region plays in both modes. >> Here, motion dominates in both modes, so added mass lowers both. >> It is not a general rule that every constriction lowers every formant, since other constrictions sit where the modes behave differently. >> The air keeps moving back and forth around its usual positions throughout, and the larger elements simply identify the load involved in that movement.
20|The **/i/ constriction** sits near the front of the vocal tract, about **two-thirds** of the way from the glottis to the lips. This narrowing changes one physical region, but that region plays a different role in each of the two lowest modes. || In **Mode 1**, the F1-like pattern, **mass motion dominates** at the narrowing. >> In this mode, the air at the two-thirds point moves strongly back and forth. >> Narrowing the passage there adds effective moving mass, and that extra moving load is harder to reverse. >> Each cycle takes longer, so **F1 goes down**. || In **Mode 2**, the F2-like pattern, **springiness dominates** at the same narrowing. >> In this mode, the air at the two-thirds point is strongly compressed and expanded, while its own movement is smaller. >> Narrowing the passage there strengthens the spring-like restoring response. >> The air is pushed back toward its usual spacing more forcefully, the cycle speeds up, and **F2 goes up**. || The narrowed region does not move to a new place when the mode changes. >> What changes is the job that the same region does within each coordinated pattern of motion. >> Where neighbors move together, the extra moving load is what matters. Where neighbors keep squeezing together and spreading apart, the restoring response is what matters. >> Looking at both rows keeps the narrowing from being treated as a single instruction to raise or lower every formant. || The result is **lower F1 and higher F2** than the uniform tube. >> Increasing the mass-like effect and the spring-like effect at the same time does not make them cancel. >> Each mode is governed by the effect that dominates at that spot in its own pattern. >> One constriction can therefore push the two formants in opposite directions. The two arrows summarize two separate causes, slower movement because of added mass for F1 and faster return because of added springiness for F2.
21|The **/a/ constriction** sits farther back in the vocal tract, about **one-third** of the way from the glottis to the lips. This changes a different part of each mode than the front /i/ narrowing does, while the basic effects of mass and stiffness stay exactly the same. || In **Mode 1**, the F1-like pattern, **springiness dominates** at the narrowing. >> In this mode, the air at the one-third point is strongly compressed and expanded. >> Narrowing the passage there strengthens the restoring response, so the air is pushed back toward its usual spacing more forcefully. >> The cycle speeds up, so **F1 goes up**. || In **Mode 2**, the F2-like pattern, **mass motion dominates** at the narrowing. >> In this mode, the air at the one-third point moves strongly back and forth. >> Narrowing the passage there adds effective moving mass, which is harder to reverse. >> The cycle slows down, so **F2 goes down**. || The narrowing affects the same back location in both rows, but the rows describe different coordinated movements of the air. >> A region can move very little itself while its neighbors repeatedly squeeze together and spread apart around it. That is a compression role. >> A region can also move strongly while the spacing around it changes very little. That is a movement role. >> At the one-third point, Mode 1 gives the region a compression role and Mode 2 gives it a movement role, which is why springiness dominates the first and moving mass dominates the second. || The result is **higher F1 and lower F2**, the reverse of the /i/ pattern. >> Changing where the constriction is located changes which role matters most in each mode. It does not change what mass or stiffness means. >> The full chain of reasoning runs from constriction location, to local movement or compression in each mode, to the effect on that mode’s frequency. >> The arrows describe the direction of change predicted by the model, not numerical measurements of how large each change is.

23|Every location in a vibrating air column can be described in two ways, by its **movement** and by its **compression**. These are related but different. >> **Movement** is how far an air element travels back and forth. >> **Compression** is how the gaps between neighboring elements change. When the gaps get smaller, the air is compressed. When they get wider, the air is in **rarefaction**, meaning it is more spread out than usual. >> Elements can travel a long way together while the spacing between them hardly changes. Elsewhere, neighbors can squeeze together and spread apart strongly around a point whose own movement is small. || The animation separates the resonance patterns of one air column. >> The moving **Schwa** row is the uniform-tube reference, the vowel with no constriction. >> The **F1, F2 and F3** rows each show one resonance pattern of that same column of air. >> The closed left end restricts movement, and the open right end allows it, making the column fixed–free. >> Each higher mode fits more alternating regions of strong movement and strong compression into the same tube. || Compression and movement together act like a spring and a mass. >> Compressed air pushes back toward its usual spacing, which supplies the restoring force. >> Moving air has inertia, so it keeps going while that restoring force changes. >> The two together keep the air oscillating around its usual positions, exactly as a mass and spring do. || The regions of strong movement and strong compression are found in different places. >> A region with large movement carries a lot of moving mass, but it is not where the air is squeezed most. >> A region with large spacing changes supplies strong restoring action, even though its central element does not travel far. >> These observations connect the visible circles and gaps in the animation with the mass-and-spring explanation of vowels. || Reading the animation means following two different things. >> Following a single element over a cycle reveals how much it moves. >> Following the gaps beside it reveals how much the air is compressed. >> Added mass matters most where movement is strong, and added stiffness matters most where compression is strong. These locations differ from one mode to another, and the rows are different resonances of one air column, not separate streams of air.
