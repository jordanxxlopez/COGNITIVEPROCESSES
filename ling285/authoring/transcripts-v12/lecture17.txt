It now stands for a whole era of network research. || Two sources mark the ends of that period. >> On the left is **McCulloch and Pitts (1943)**, **“A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity.”** >> Its abstract begins from the **“all-or-none” character of nervous activity**. A neuron either fires or does not, so neural events can be treated with propositional logic. >> On the right are the two **Parallel Distributed Processing** volumes from **1986**, which brought networks of many simple connected units, trained by adjusting their weights, into wide use. || The caption states what such a network can do. **Given some static vector of formants, the NN could make a decision about a few possible categories.** >> A **static vector** is one fixed list of numbers, measured at a single moment. >> Here the vector is F1 and F2, and the decision is a choice among five words. || The caption also states the limit. **Human cognition is far more complex.** >> Speech unfolds in time, so a word is a sequence of changing sounds rather than one fixed measurement. >> A sentence is a long sequence of words, and what comes next depends on what came before. >> A network that maps one fixed vector to a handful of labels has no way to take in a sequence or keep track of its earlier parts. || That gap marks the limit of these networks. >> To handle language, a network must take in many vectors in order and use the earlier ones when it responds to the later ones. >> That requirement separates these classifiers from recurrent networks and, later, large language models.
3|In **1985, Michael Jordan developed recurrent neural networks** to make sense of **speech production**. That work is traced forward to **LLMs** and then to **agentic AI**. >> The photo on the right is Michael Jordan. >> Speech production is a natural starting point, because speaking means producing a sequence of overlapping movements over time. || The network diagram has four kinds of units. >> **Plan units** receive input from outside and say which sequence the network should produce. >> **Hidden units** combine information, and **output units** produce the network’s response at each moment. >> **State units** are the new part. The outputs loop back along the long curved arrows into the state units, and each state unit also feeds back into itself. || Those loops are what make the network **recurrent**. >> At each moment the state units hold a trace of what the network has just produced. >> That trace goes into the hidden units together with the plan, so the next output depends on the plan and on the network’s own recent outputs. >> A feedforward classifier like the vowel network has no such memory, because each input passes straight through once and is gone. || The plot, **Performance on /istrstry/**, shows the trained network producing a sequence of sounds. >> Time runs along the bottom, from 0 to 32 steps. >> Each row is one articulatory feature, **Voice, Place, Sonorant, Sibilant, Nasal, Height, Back and Round**, and each line gives how much of that feature the output has at each moment. >> Sibilant rises in bumps where the s sounds occur, Voice and Sonorant dip and rise as consonants and vowels alternate, and Nasal and Height stay nearly flat. || The **Round** line rises early and stays high for most of the sequence. >> Rounding does not switch on and off for each sound. It spreads across neighboring sounds. >> This happens because each output is shaped by the outputs just before it, so the features blend smoothly from moment to moment. >> Producing a smooth, overlapping sequence is what recurrence adds, and the idea of feeding past outputs back into the network runs through to today’s language models.
