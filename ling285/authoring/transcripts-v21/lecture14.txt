2|Speech has **two major vibrating systems**, and the first is the **vocal folds**, a membrane fixed on all sides and made of a whole bunch of masses and springs. In the first mode all the masses go up and down together, in the second mode half of them go up while the other half go down, and higher modes split the membrane into more and more parts going against each other. >> This is literally what happens in the throat during speech, and measuring the spectrum of real voices confirms it. || Because the vocal folds are fixed on all sides, their frequencies are **harmonics**, whole-number multiples of the lowest one. **The bottom-most harmonic is called F0.** Each person has a different F0, but every voice has harmonics stacked at multiples of it. >> The membrane's five modes are given 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz for teaching, and these are assigned values rather than the membrane's natural frequencies. >> Added together, the five motions make one complicated surface, and its spectrum at a pickup point has five equal spikes.
3|The second vibrating system is fixed differently, so it follows a different rule. A **string fixed at the bottom and free at the top** is the simplest version. At the fixed bottom the string cannot move, so it is a **node**, and at the free top it moves the most, so it is an **antinode**. >> Every mode must have a node at the bottom and an antinode at the top. || The four modes are at 100, 300, 500 and 700 Hz, so **only odd harmonics occur**. With equal excitation, the spectrum at the free end has four equal spikes. >> For a string of the same length and wave speed, switching from fixed at both ends to fixed at one end halves the lowest frequency. >> This string was retuned to keep its lowest mode at 100 Hz.
4|A **quarter-wave packet** is the piece of a wave that runs from a node to an antinode. That piece is exactly what joins the fixed bottom to the free top, so each mode is built out of these packets. The first mode holds one packet, so it is one times 100 Hz. The second holds three, so it is three times 100 Hz, the third holds five and the fourth holds seven. || The counts are always odd, because the string must still end with an antinode at the top. Adding one packet would leave a node there, so packets get added two at a time. >> More packets in the same length means shorter waves and faster vibration. >> The packets are pieces of one standing pattern, so they do not travel along the string.
5|The second vibrating system is the **air in the vocal tract**. Picture the space inside the mouth and throat straightened out into a tube, with the flesh of the tongue, the palate and the throat forming its walls. The air is **fixed at the glottis**, the space between the vocal folds, because the folds are closed and the air cannot move back and forth there. The air is **free at the lips**, where it can go in and out. >> So the air in the vocal tract is a fixed-free system, and its frequencies are formants at odd multiples. || The air can be modeled as a fixed-free mass-spring chain of identical masses in a uniform tube. The first mode is **F1-like**, the lowest fixed-free air mode, and the second mode is **F2-like**, vibrating three times faster than the first. >> Three spots are marked, measured from the glottis, with /a/ one third of the way, /i/ two thirds of the way, and /u/ at the lip end.
6|The air does **two different things** at different places, and both can be seen directly. Where the slivers of air come very close together and then far apart, the air is compressing and expanding, which is **springiness**. That happens near the closed end, because air can be squeezed against something closed. Where the slivers stay equally spaced and just move back and forth, the air is acting like a **mass**. That happens near the open end, because there is nothing to squeeze against. || In the first mode, the air is very springy at the closed end, then less and less springy and more and more mass-like toward the open end. In higher modes springiness and mass alternate along the tube, and they repeat more times the higher the mode. >> For every mode, the air at the open end acts like a mass, going back and forth with hardly any expansion. >> The air near the closed end always acts like a spring.
7|Different sections of the vocal tract can be compared by asking, for each one, whether the air there is mostly springy or mostly massy. A section near the closed end is very springy, a section where the slivers just move back and forth together is mass, and a section where the slivers bunch up and spread out again is springy. >> It does not have to be memorized, since it can be read directly from how the air moves. || **Constricting the tract at a springy spot raises that mode's formant.** A constriction always increases both mass and stiffness, because squeezing air makes it stiffer, the way squeezing a tire does, and bunching it up makes it effectively heavier. >> At a spot in the third mode where the air is acting like a pure spring, there is no back-and-forth movement, only compression and expansion. >> So the added mass does not matter there, and only the added stiffness counts, which means F3 goes up. >> This chain of reasoning has to be built fresh at each spot, because the situation is slightly different everywhere.
8|Real-time **MRI** images of people speaking reveal where each vowel squeezes the air. >> Dean is high and front, with a constriction two thirds of the way from the glottis. >> Dune is high and back, with a constriction at the lips, and Done is central with no constriction. >> Don is low and back, squeezing the air far back in the throat, one third of the way from the glottis, and Dan is low and front. || **Combining these images with the model** is where theory meets reality. The model treats air as masses and springs, fixed on one side and free to move on the other, and a constriction is drawn with stiffer springs and heavier masses at that spot. >> Where each vowel puts its constriction decides which formants go up and which go down.
9|**Compressing air raises its stiffness**, so constricting at a place with lots of compression raises the frequency. **Bunching up air raises its mass, or density**, so constricting where air moves a lot raises the mass and lowers the frequency. >> So making a constriction increases both mass and stiffness. || That does not mean the effects cancel and constrictions have no effect on frequency. **The parts of a vibrating medium that move the most alternate with the parts that compress and uncompress the most**, so at any given spot in a mode, one effect wins. >> If the air there is mostly moving, the added mass wins and the formant goes down. >> If the air there is mostly compressing, the added stiffness wins and the formant goes up. >> The same constriction can do opposite things to different formants, because each mode has its own pattern of motion and compression.
10|The same spot along the tract can play different roles in the first and second modes. Near the lips both modes allow strong motion, but farther inside the tube the second mode has an extra change in its pattern, so a spot can be moving in one mode and compressing in the other. >> That is why the effect of a constriction has to be worked out separately for F1 and for F2. >> F1 and F2 are resonances of the air in the tract, not harmonics of the vocal folds.
11|For **/u/**, the lips squeeze the air at the free end, which is **free-end loading**, and that raises both the mass and the stiffness of the air at the lips. **At the open end, the air acts like a mass in every mode**, going back and forth with hardly any squeezing, because there is nothing to squeeze against. >> So the added stiffness does not matter, because there is no compression or expansion there. >> The added mass does matter, because the air there is moving a lot, so F1 goes down and F2 goes down. || This is what happens in the mouth for the vowels in dune, moon and through. Measurements match it, since starting from schwa, near 500 and 1500 Hz, a student's /u/ had an F1 around 235 Hz, far below 500.
12|For **/i/**, the air is squeezed two thirds of the way from the closed end, and the two modes are doing different things at that spot. In the first mode, the air there is mostly moving back and forth, so it acts like a **mass**. In the second mode, the air there is mostly compressing, so it acts like a **spring**. || A constriction always raises both mass and stiffness, so the outcome depends on which one matters in each mode. F1 goes down because the air is moving and the added mass wins, while F2 goes up because the air is compressing and the added stiffness wins. >> Wherever stiffness is what matters, frequency goes up, and wherever mass is what matters, frequency goes down. >> Moving from schwa into /i/ in a recording, F1 drops and F2 rises, exactly as predicted.
13|For **/a/**, the air is squeezed far back, about one third of the way from the closed end. In the first mode that spot is close to the closed end, and near the closed end there is always springiness, so the added stiffness wins and F1 goes up. In the second mode everything alternates, with compression near the closed end, compression further up, and mass motion in between, which is right where /a/ squeezes, so the added mass wins and F2 goes down. >> /a/ is the opposite of /i/, with F1 up and F2 down. >> Many students measured their /a/ with F1 near 700 Hz and F2 near 900 to 1000 Hz, which fits the prediction.
14|Following one air element at a time separates the two features of each air mode. **Motion** is how far an element travels back and forth, while **compression** is how much the spacing between neighboring elements changes. >> A group of elements can travel far while staying evenly spaced, and elsewhere elements can barely travel while their spacing changes a lot. >> In the schwa tube and in the separate rows for the first three modes, these two features alternate along the length, which is what decides how each constriction affects each formant.
15|The **vocal folds** differ from person to person, and the mass and stiffness rules predict their F0. Big, loose, heavy vocal folds have high mass and low stiffness, so F0 is low, and with F0 at 100 Hz the harmonics are 100, 200, 300 and so on. Tighter, lighter vocal folds give a higher F0, such as 250 Hz, and very tight, light vocal folds give an even higher F0, such as 350 Hz. >> Lower F0 packs more harmonics into the same frequency window, and higher F0 spaces them farther apart. || **F0 is not fixed during speech.** Only a robot keeps F0 constant, while real speech changes F0 all the time, in every vowel. >> The dog slept here, said as a statement, ends with F0 going down, and said as a question it ends with F0 going up. >> Speakers change F0 by changing the stiffness of the vocal folds, and listeners use those changes to understand meaning, even to tell when someone is sarcastic or pretending.
16|The air in the vocal tract has its own formants, and for schwa, with no constriction, F1 is about 500 Hz and F2 is about 1500 Hz. Exact values vary with how long the vocal tract is and how heavy and stiff the air is. **Formants are all about where the constriction is in the vocal tract.** || The formants interact with the harmonics by one **core rule**. **A formant encourages the closest harmonics to be audible, and harmonics far from that formant are muted.** If there are harmonics near 500 Hz, they get amplified, and harmonics farther away get weaker until they cannot be heard. >> The vowel shape is the filter, the voiced source supplies the harmonics, and the filter chooses which harmonics survive. >> For /u/ the targets are 300, 1000 and 2000 Hz, for /i/ they are 300 and 2200 Hz, and for /a/ the two formants sit close together at 800 and 900 Hz.
17|**The interaction of source and filter is really important.** Picture a person with heavy, loose vocal folds, whose harmonics are at 100, 200, 300 and so on, saying schwa. There are lots of harmonics near 500, so the one closest to it is amplified and those farther away get weaker, and near 1500 the harmonics are amplified again. >> The lower F0 is, the more harmonics the formants have to shape. || Now picture very light, very stiff vocal folds, with harmonics at 350, 700, 1050 and 1400. The formant at 500 has no harmonic close by to amplify, so **sparse harmonics can miss a formant or make it look ambiguous**. >> **Opera singers** have such a high F0 that their formants barely line up with any harmonics, so listeners can hardly hear which vowels they are making. >> **Very small children** have an F0 around 500 Hz, so their formants and harmonics line up so poorly that only their caretakers can easily follow them. || **F0 can be measured in Praat** by taking a spectral slice of a vowel and selecting the lowest harmonic. A male voice measured around 170 to 180 Hz, and its next harmonic was about double that, so the theory holds.
18|**A spectrum is one instant, and a spectrogram stacks many spectra over time.** Time runs along the horizontal axis, frequency runs up the vertical axis, and darkness tells how much energy there is, with high amplitude black, medium gray and low white. So a formant appears as a dark band at its frequency, and its rise and fall over time can be followed. || Speech is not one steady vowel, so the formants are constantly changing. In the spectrogram of /ia/, the lowest band rises from /i/ to /a/, so F1 goes up, and the next band falls, so F2 goes down. >> These are the same directions as the vowel targets, since /i/ has a low F1 and a high F2 while /a/ has a high F1 and a lower F2.
19|A recording of **"Where were you a year ago?"** follows the same rules as single vowels, because real speech is a stream of changing sounds rather than one held vowel. Where F2 rises high, the speaker is making an /i/-like sound, since squeezing air near two thirds of the way up raises F2 and lowers F1. >> /i/ is the classic vowel where F1 is low and F2 is high. >> In you, all the formants go down, just as /u/ predicts, and across the whole phrase the formants keep going up and down as each sound is made.
20|**One experiment reveals how complex listening to speech is.** A speaker recorded the syllables ma and na. The m sound was cut out of ma, the a vowel was cut out of na, and the two pieces were pasted together. Since the consonant came from ma, the result should sound like ma, but **it sounds like na**. || **Listeners decide which consonant they heard mostly from the vowel that follows it.** In the a after m, the formants stay fairly stationary, while in the a after n, one formant drops sharply. That formant drop inside the vowel tells the listener n, so it overrides the m pasted in front. >> This was one of the first striking results about speech perception, discovered in the 1950s by the French linguist André Malécot. || **Speech perception does not decide sound by sound, slice by slice.** Listeners take in a whole stretch of speech and then decide what they heard, often deciding about a consonant later in time, during the vowel. >> For about 70 years, speech recognition machines failed because they were built to recognize each little piece separately, the way people think they listen. >> An AI that understands speech has to be complex enough to use later information to decide about earlier sounds, which is why large language models were needed for speech recognition.
21|A **neural network** can be trained on formants to decide which vowel is being spoken. The formants from each student's vowels in lab become the network's input, and the **W's**, the weights, contain the network's knowledge. >> Training adjusts the weights until the network makes good decisions about which vowel was produced. >> This is where the speech science and the AI come together, since F1 and F2 measured from spectrograms become the numbers the network learns from.
22|The network is **purely linear**, with two inputs, hidden layers of 3, 2 and 4 neurons, and 5 outputs, and there are no activation functions between the layers. The two inputs are F1 and F2, the frequencies of the first two modes of the air's vibration. >> Each hidden layer does **inner products**, multiplying the incoming numbers by its weights and adding them up, and makes a new representation. >> That is like information arriving at the ear and being processed by one part of the brain after another before the brain decides what it heard. >> The five outputs are /done/, /dune/, /dean/, /Don/ and /Dan/, one neuron per vowel.
23|The example input is F1 of 320 and F2 of 2000. **F1 very low and F2 very high is the pattern for /i/**, so a smart network should decide dean. Before training, every weight is a random number between minus one and one. >> A positive weight pushes the next neuron up, and a negative weight pushes it down. >> The size of a weight sets how strongly its input counts, and these random weights carry no knowledge about vowels yet.
24|In **forward flow**, the input flows forward layer by layer. Each first-layer neuron multiplies F1 and F2 by its own weights and adds the products, and none of these calculations ever have to be done by hand. That gives three numbers, which pass through the next weights to give two, then four, and finally the output, a distribution over the five words. >> The output gives done 0.5, Dan 0.2 and the other three 0.1 each, which adds up to 1, so it is a good probability distribution. >> But it says done when the vowel was /i/, so the weights are bad, the network is stupid before training, and the result is met with an angry face. || **Training is what fixes the weights.** The network is told the right answer as the distribution zero, zero, one, zero, zero, a one for dean and zero for everything else, and **stochastic gradient descent** changes each weight. >> The next time 320 and 2000 come in, the inner products should make dean's value higher than all the others.
25|The same network can be written in **Python**, using the **PyTorch** library. The code imports torch and torch.nn as nn, and builds the network inside **nn.Sequential**, which means a sequence of layers, one after another. Each layer is an **nn.Linear**, which says how many numbers it takes in and how many it gives out. >> The first layer says that it takes two inputs and makes three outputs, the next takes three and makes two, the next takes two and makes four, and the last takes four and makes five, one per vowel. >> Each layer's output size has to match the next layer's input size, so the numbers can flow through. || **bias=False** removes the bias, an extra constant added to a neuron's sum, so each layer only multiplies and adds, matching the diagram. >> This code is a declaration only, with no training. It describes the network the way the machine sees it, without feeding in formants or changing any weights.
