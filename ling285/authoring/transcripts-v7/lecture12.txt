2|A **dynamic system** is any system that changes over time because its parts keep influencing one another. The same way of thinking that explains a mass on a spring also appears in economics. >> **Woodford’s theory** is called **Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium**, or **DSGE**. >> “Dynamic” means the economy changes over time, “stochastic” means random shocks keep disturbing it, and “general equilibrium” means its many markets are linked and settle toward balance together. || The stabilization picture tracks two economic quantities over time. >> The **black curve is unemployment**, and the **gray curve is inflation**. >> At first both curves swing through large, jagged peaks and troughs. >> After stabilization, the swings become smaller and smoother. || **Stabilization** means shrinking those swings. >> It does not mean stopping all change, and it does not merge unemployment and inflation into a single quantity. >> The aim is to avoid harmful extremes, such as very high unemployment or runaway inflation. || The link to physical systems is the shared question of what drives a fluctuation and what can damp it. >> A spring system oscillates because of inertia and a restoring force. >> An economy fluctuates because of shocks and the ways its parts respond to one another. The curves picture the goal of calmer swings without testing any particular policy.

9|Seven **principles of mass–spring systems** gather the main ideas into a short list. || The first principle is about the whole system. >> **Raising the mass lowers the frequency**, because a heavier load is harder to turn around. >> **Raising the stiffness raises the frequency**, because a stronger restoring force reverses the motion sooner. || The second principle counts modes. >> A system with **n masses has n modes** of vibration. >> Each mode has a higher frequency than the one before it. || The third, fourth and fifth principles say that **location matters**. >> Added mass lowers a mode’s frequency in proportion to **how much the mass moves** in that mode, and that depends on where it sits. >> Added stiffness raises a mode’s frequency in proportion to **how much the spring stretches or compresses** there, which also depends on position. >> Where movement is greatest, there is no stretching, and where stretching is greatest, there is no movement. Neighbors that travel together do not change their spacing, so strong motion and strong compression fall in different places. || The same added mass can therefore matter a great deal in one mode and very little in another. >> It matters where that mode moves strongly. >> It matters little where that mode hardly moves, such as near a node. A node of one mode can still move in another mode or in a mixture. || The last two principles concern harmonics. >> In a **fixed–fixed system**, the harmonics are whole-number multiples of the base frequency, because the base shape fits a whole number of times into each higher mode. >> In a **fixed–free system**, the harmonics are **odd multiples** of the base frequency, because the base shape fits an odd number of times into each higher mode.

11|A **pop quiz** applies the location principle to two modes of the same string. A region just left of the midpoint is highlighted, and the question is what happens when mass or stiffness is added there. || In **Mode 4**, the highlighted region moves strongly. >> Neighboring points move nearly together, so the string barely stretches there. >> In **Mode 5**, the same region barely moves, but neighboring points move differently, so the string stretches and compresses strongly there. || **Adding mass** to the region affects the two modes differently. >> Mode 4 has to carry the extra load through a large motion, so its frequency drops. >> Mode 5 hardly moves the region, so the extra mass has little effect on it. >> Increasing mass lowers frequency only where the mass is actually moving. || **Adding stiffness** reverses the pattern. >> Mode 5 strongly stretches the region, so a stiffer spring there adds restoring force and raises its frequency. >> Mode 4 barely stretches the region, so the extra stiffness has little effect. || Increasing **both** mass and stiffness at that spot therefore **lowers Mode 4 and raises Mode 5**. >> The location is the same in both modes. >> What differs is its role, mostly movement in one mode and mostly stretching in the other. These roles describe a full cycle of motion, not whether a single frozen frame happens to catch the region at rest.

15|The air inside the vocal tract can be modeled as a chain of masses and springs. >> Air has **inertia**, so air that is moving tends to keep moving. >> Squeezing air raises its pressure, so compressed air pushes back. The spring-like links in the model stand for that restoring effect. >> The air elements move **longitudinally**, back and forth along the tube. They oscillate around their usual places instead of flowing steadily from the glottis to the lips. || The tube is treated as **fixed–free**. >> The **glottis**, the opening between the vocal folds, is the fixed end. >> The open **lips** are the free end. || The two lowest patterns correspond to the first two formants. >> **Mode 1 is F1-like**, the lowest resonance of the tube. >> **Mode 2 is F2-like**, with one more reversal inside the tube. >> In the ideal uniform tube, Mode 2 repeats **three times** as fast as Mode 1, as a fixed–free system requires. A change in tract shape can alter that simple ratio. || Each mode is a coordinated back-and-forth pattern of the whole air column. >> F1 and F2 are not two separate air streams or two places where sound comes out. >> At some places, neighboring elements move together and their gaps hardly change. At others, the gaps keep closing and opening. || Three markers locate the vowel constrictions along the tube. >> **/a/** sits about **one-third** of the way from the glottis. >> **/i/** sits about **two-thirds** of the way. >> **/u/** sits at the **lips**. >> Each marker is the same spot in both modes. Added mass matters where a mode moves strongly, and added springiness matters where it compresses strongly, so one narrowing can affect F1 and F2 differently.
16|Vowels are described by **tongue height** and by **front–back position**, and each vowel narrows the air passage at its own place. The chart arranges five words by those two dimensions. || The high vowels narrow the tract in different places. >> **Dean** is **high and front**. Its constriction is marked about **two-thirds** of the way from the glottis to the lips. >> **Dune** is **high and back**. Its constriction is highlighted at the **lips**. >> Both are high, yet their tract shapes differ, so sharing a height does not mean sharing a constriction place. || The low vowels sit at the bottom of the chart. >> **Dan** is **low and front**, with no numbered constriction position. >> **Don** is **low and back**, with its constriction marked about **one-third** of the way from the glottis. || **Done** is **central** and labeled **“No constriction.”** >> It serves as the relatively open, uniform reference. || The labels describe related but separate features. >> High versus low is about tongue height. >> Front versus back is about where the tongue sits along the mouth. >> The fraction is about distance along the air path, measured from the glottis. || Locating the constriction links articulation to acoustics. >> A narrowing changes the local mass-like and spring-like properties of the air. >> Its effect on each formant depends on how that mode moves and compresses the narrowed region.

19|The **/u/ model** adds loading at the open lips, which is the **free end** of the air column. In the model, the lip elements are drawn larger to stand for greater **effective mass**. >> The larger elements do not mean heavier air molecules. >> They mean that more air has to be moved back and forth at that spot. || Both lowest modes move strongly at the free end. >> In **Mode 1**, motion dominates at the lips. >> In **Mode 2**, motion also dominates at the lips. || More moving mass is harder to speed up and reverse. >> Where motion dominates, the extra load makes each cycle take longer. >> Both modes slow down, so the result is **F1 down and F2 down** compared with the uniform tube. || The two rows compare the same lip location in two different resonance patterns. >> Their inner arrangements differ, but both have strong movement at the lips. >> The added load slows both without making their shapes or frequencies the same. F1 stays the lower resonance and F2 the higher one. >> Each change is measured against its own uniform reference. || The shared direction comes from the role of the lip region in both modes. >> It is not a rule that every constriction lowers every formant. >> The air still moves back and forth around its usual positions, and the larger elements mark the load carried in that movement.
20|The **/i/ constriction** sits near the front of the tract, about **two-thirds** of the way from the glottis to the lips. The narrowing changes one physical region, but that region plays a different role in each mode. || In **Mode 1**, **mass motion dominates** at the narrowing. >> The air there moves strongly in this mode. >> The constriction adds effective moving mass, which is harder to reverse. >> The cycle slows, so **F1 goes down**. || In **Mode 2**, **springiness dominates** at the same spot. >> The air there is strongly compressed and expanded in this mode. >> The constriction strengthens the spring-like restoring response. >> The cycle speeds up, so **F2 goes up**. || The region itself does not move to a new place when the mode changes. >> What changes is its job within each coordinated pattern. >> Where neighbors move together, extra moving load matters most. Where neighbors keep squeezing together and pulling apart, the restoring response matters most. || The result is **lower F1 and higher F2** than the uniform tube. >> The mass and spring effects do not simply cancel. >> Each mode uses the narrowed region differently, so one constriction pushes the two formants in opposite directions. The two arrows summarize two separate causes, slower movement from added mass and faster return from added springiness.
21|The **/a/ constriction** sits farther back, about **one-third** of the way from the glottis to the lips. It changes a different part of each mode than the /i/ narrowing, while mass and stiffness keep their usual effects. || In **Mode 1**, **springiness dominates** at the narrowing. >> The air there is strongly compressed in this mode. >> The constriction adds restoring force, so the cycle speeds up and **F1 goes up**. || In **Mode 2**, **mass motion dominates** at the narrowing. >> The air there moves strongly in this mode. >> The constriction adds effective moving mass, which is harder to reverse, so the cycle slows and **F2 goes down**. || The narrowing is in the same back location in both rows, but the rows are different coordinated motions of the air. >> A region can move very little itself while its neighbors squeeze together and spread apart around it. >> A region can also move a lot while its spacing barely changes. >> Those two roles explain why springiness wins in Mode 1 and moving mass wins in Mode 2 at this spot. || The result is **higher F1 and lower F2**, the reverse of /i/. >> Moving the constriction changes which role matters most. It does not change what mass or stiffness does. >> The arrows give the direction of each change predicted by the model, not a measured size.

23|An air column has two kinds of local behavior, **movement** and **compression**. >> **Movement** is how far an air element travels. >> **Compression** is how the gaps between neighboring elements change. Smaller gaps mean compression, and wider gaps mean **rarefaction**, air that is more spread out. >> Elements can travel together with almost no change in spacing, and neighbors can squeeze and spread strongly around an element that barely moves. || The rows of the animation separate the patterns of one air column. >> The moving **Schwa** row is the uniform-tube reference. >> The **F1, F2 and F3** rows show the first three resonance patterns of that same column. >> The closed left end prevents movement, and the open right end allows it. Each higher mode fits more alternating regions of movement and compression into the tube. || Compression and movement work together like a spring and a mass. >> Crowded air pushes back toward its usual spacing, which is the restoring response. >> Moving air has inertia, so it keeps going while the restoring response changes. >> Together they keep the air oscillating around its usual positions. || The two kinds of region are found in different places. >> A region with large movement carries a lot of moving mass but is not where the air squeezes most. >> A region with large spacing changes supplies strong restoring force even though its central element travels little. || Following one element over a cycle reveals its movement, and following the gaps beside it reveals compression. >> Added mass matters most where movement is strong. >> Added stiffness matters most where compression is strong. >> These places differ from mode to mode, and the rows are separate resonances, not separate air streams.
