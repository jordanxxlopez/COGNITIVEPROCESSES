4|A **fixed–fixed string** cannot move at either end, so both ends are **nodes** in every mode. That rule decides which shapes the string can take. || The simplest allowed shape fits **one half-wave** between the ends. >> The whole string bulges up and down as one broad region. >> This is **Mode 1**, the **fundamental**, the lowest frequency. || **Mode 2** fits **two half-waves**. >> A still point appears in the middle. >> The two halves move in opposite directions, one up while the other is down. || **Modes 3, 4 and 5** fit **three, four and five half-waves** into the same length. >> Each extra half-wave makes every section shorter, so the **wavelength** gets shorter. >> At the same wave speed, a shorter wave repeats faster. >> Because each mode holds a whole number of the base half-wave, its frequency is a **whole-number multiple** of the fundamental. Odd and even counts both work, since every count keeps both ends still. || The numbered half-wave sections mark places along the standing shape. >> The first section appears in every mode, while the later sections appear only in the higher modes, one more per mode. >> The sections stay in place while the string moves up and down. They do not count cycles that have passed, and they do not travel along the string one after another. >> Counting sections explains why higher harmonics fit more half-wave packets into the same length.

8|A **quarter-wave** is the stretch of a standing wave that runs from a **node**, where nothing moves, to an **antinode**, where movement is greatest. The **fixed–free string** is fixed at the bottom and free at the top. >> The fixed bottom must be a node. >> The free top must be an antinode. || **One quarter-wave** satisfies both conditions. >> It begins still at the bottom and reaches full movement at the top. >> This is the lowest mode, the **fundamental**. || Adding quarter-waves two at a time keeps both conditions true. >> Each added pair puts one more reversal inside the string. >> The four allowed modes contain **one, three, five and seven quarter-waves**. || More sections in the same length make the wavelength shorter. >> At the same wave speed, shorter waves vibrate faster. >> The modes run at the **first, third, fifth and seventh harmonics**. The second mode is the third harmonic, the third mode is the fifth and the fourth mode is the seventh. || An even number of quarter-waves would end on a node at the top. >> A node is the wrong condition for a free end, so even counts are not allowed. || The numbered pieces count the quarter-wave sections in each shape. >> Only the higher modes contain the extra pieces, and only the fourth mode contains pieces six and seven. >> The pieces mark fixed positions in a standing pattern while the string moves back and forth.
9|This **pop quiz** asks how many quarter-waves each **fixed–free pattern** contains, reading the answer from its endpoints. >> The fixed bottom is a **node**. >> The free top is an **antinode**. >> Every change from node to antinode, or from antinode back to node, spans **one quarter-wave**. || Counting those sections gives **one, three, five and seven** quarter-waves for the four patterns. >> Each pattern has two more sections than the one before. >> More sections in the same length shorten the wavelength. >> With the wave speed unchanged, the higher patterns repeat faster. || The numbered divisions describe the standing shape. >> They stay in fixed positions while the material moves. || Two different numbers describe each pattern. >> **Mode number** counts the allowed patterns in order, first, second, third and fourth. >> **Harmonic number** compares a pattern’s frequency with the fundamental. >> The two differ here because only odd quarter-wave counts fit both endpoint conditions. >> The fourth allowed mode is therefore the **seventh harmonic**, not the fourth.

13|A **constriction** narrows the air passage, and it changes two properties of the air at that spot. || Narrowing raises the air’s **stiffness**. >> When air is compressed, as it is in a constriction, it resists further compression more strongly. >> A constriction placed where the air is **compressed a lot** therefore **raises the frequency**. || Narrowing also raises the air’s **mass**. >> Bunching air up makes its density larger. >> In a part of the tract where the air **moves a lot**, constricting adds moving mass, which tends to **lower the frequency**. || A constriction increases mass and stiffness together, but that does not mean their effects cancel. >> The parts of a vibrating medium that **move the most** alternate with the parts that **compress and expand the most**. >> Each mode places those regions differently. || Telling the two effects apart means following the air through a cycle. >> Follow one air element to see how far it travels. That travel carries the local mass. >> Compare it with its neighbors to see their gaps close and open. That spacing change is compression, the source of the restoring force. >> A tongue-height label or a single frozen frame cannot give both pieces of information. || The same narrowing can work mainly through **mass** for one formant and mainly through **stiffness** for another. >> That explains how one constriction can lower one formant and raise another. >> Neither physical rule is reversed. Mass still lowers frequency where air moves, and stiffness still raises it where air compresses.

20|**Source–filter theory** separates the sound made by the vocal folds from the way the vocal tract shapes it. >> The **source** is the glottal vibration. >> The **filter** is the vocal tract. || The glottal source gives a **harmonic comb**. >> It repeats at a **fundamental frequency**, **F0**, and has harmonics at whole-number multiples of F0. >> In the example, the harmonics are **100 Hz** apart and extend to **4000 Hz**. >> Before the vocal tract acts, the source looks like evenly spaced vertical lines of equal height. Equal heights keep the example simple. || The vocal tract filter **sculpts the heights** of those harmonics. >> Harmonics near the tract’s resonances, the **formants**, become stronger, and others become weaker. >> The positions of the lines do not move. Only their heights change. >> The broad outline of the heights is the **spectral envelope**, and the envelope is the vowel. || The schwa example labels four resonance regions, **F1 through F4**. >> Its strongest group of harmonics sits at low frequency. || The spectrum puts frequency on the horizontal axis and amplitude on the vertical axis. >> A taller line is stronger, not higher in frequency. >> **F0** belongs to the source, and **F1** is the lowest resonance of the tract. >> The vowel still contains many harmonics, not just four tones, and the resonance values describe this one example rather than every speaker.
21|The **/i/ filter** has a **low F1** and a **high F2**. The same 100 Hz harmonic comb from the source passes through it. >> The first strong group of harmonics sits near the low-frequency end. >> The next strong group sits much farther up, with a weak region in between. >> Further strong groups appear around **F3** and **F4**, near **3000** and **3600 Hz**. || The source harmonics stay in the same evenly spaced positions. >> Only their heights change when the tract changes shape. >> That makes the filter’s effect visible separately from the repetition of the source. || The low-F1, high-F2 pattern matches the front constriction of /i/. >> The narrowing near two-thirds of the way from the glottis adds mass where Mode 1 moves and stiffness where Mode 2 compresses. >> F1 falls and F2 rises, which spreads the two lowest formants far apart. || Reading the spectrum takes two separate judgments. >> A line’s horizontal position gives its **frequency**. >> A line’s height gives its **amplitude**. >> A tall line near the low end is still low in frequency, even if a higher line is weaker. || The narrow harmonic lines sample the broad formant regions. >> Each line is a source component, and the envelope traced by their heights is the vowel. >> These values describe one schematic /i/, not an identical pattern for every speaker.
22|Compared with /i/, the **/a/ filter** has a **higher F1** and a **lower F2**. In the example, **F1 is at 750 Hz** and **F2 is near 1200 Hz**, so the first two resonance regions sit close together. >> **F3** and **F4** stay higher, near **2600** and **3500 Hz**. >> The same evenly spaced source produces a different vowel because the tract changed shape. || The filter changes the pattern of harmonic **strengths**. >> Moving a formant shifts which frequency region is favored, a change along the horizontal axis. >> Changing a harmonic’s amplitude changes its height, a change along the vertical axis. >> These are two different kinds of change in the spectrum. || A formant can fall **between two source harmonics**. >> The 750 Hz target for F1 lies between the harmonics at **700** and **800 Hz**. >> The nearby harmonics become stronger, but no new source line appears exactly at 750 Hz. || A formant and a harmonic are different things. >> A **formant** names a resonance region of the tract, the filter. >> A **harmonic** names a frequency component of the repeating source. >> The drawing’s values belong to this simplified comparison.
23|A **spectrum** and a **spectrogram** show the same sound in two different ways. || A **spectrum** is **one instant**. >> It describes the frequency components in one short slice of sound. >> Frequency runs horizontally and amplitude vertically. >> The example takes three spectra from **/i/** and three from **/a/**. || A **spectrogram** **stacks many spectra over time**. >> Time runs from left to right, and frequency runs upward. >> The first step turns the spectrum’s frequency axis on its side, so a peak near the right of a spectrum becomes a mark higher up in the spectrogram. >> Moving to the right in a spectrogram means later in time, not higher in frequency. || The second step turns **spectrum height into darkness**. >> In the grayscale display, **black means high amplitude, gray means medium and white means low**. >> Vertical position gives frequency, and darkness gives strength. >> A band that darkens is getting stronger, while a band that rises is moving to a higher frequency. || During the change from **/i/** to **/a/**, **F1 rises and F2 falls**. >> The two lowest bands move toward each other. >> The higher bands stay above them with smaller changes. >> The spectrogram follows the changing resonances of the tract through time.

25|Formants are easiest to see when the source harmonics are **closely spaced**. Each harmonic acts as a sample of the vocal tract’s response, and many samples trace the shape of each formant clearly. || With harmonics at **100, 200, 300, 400 Hz** and so on, a vowel with **F1 at 300** and **F2 at 2100 Hz** shapes the amplitudes of many harmonics. >> Many lines fall around each formant. >> Their heights rise and fall with the filter, so the formant regions are easy to pick out. || Raising **F0** spreads the harmonics apart. >> With harmonics at **250, 500, 750** and so on, fewer lines fall near each formant. >> A formant can land between two harmonics, so no line sits at its center. >> One strong line may be near a formant without marking its exact location. It becomes very hard to tell harmonics from formants. || Higher voices make the problem worse. >> A **toddler’s** harmonics could be **400, 800** and so on. >> A **soprano’s** could be **800, 1600, 2400** and so on. >> The three spectra and their spectrograms move from closely spaced components to a few isolated traces. || The tract has not lost or moved its resonances. >> The filter can stay exactly the same. >> What changed is the source, which now supplies fewer frequencies to reveal that same filter.
