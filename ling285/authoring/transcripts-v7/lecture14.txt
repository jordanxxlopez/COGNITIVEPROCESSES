4|A **quarter-wavelength** is the piece of a standing wave that connects a **node** to an **antinode**. On the fixed–free string, the fixed bottom must be a node and the free top must be an antinode. >> **One quarter-wave** fits exactly between them. >> It gives the simplest allowed pattern, with no still point inside the string. || The next pattern cannot add just one more quarter-wave. >> One more would end the string at a node. >> A node is the wrong condition for the free top. || Adding **two** quarter-waves keeps an antinode at the top. >> The allowed patterns therefore fit **one, three, five and seven quarter-waves** into the same length. >> More sections in the same length mean shorter wavelengths. >> At the same wave speed, shorter waves repeat faster, so each pattern has a higher frequency than the last. || The demonstration reveals one quarter-wave packet at a time in every mode that contains it. >> The four modes contain **1, 3, 5 and 7** packets, matching the odd harmonics. >> The numbered pieces divide the standing shape. They do not move up the string one after another. >> The string moves back and forth while the still points of the pattern stay in place. || The odd frequency pattern follows from the two endpoint conditions. >> Even frequencies are missing because no even count of quarter-waves can end on an antinode at the free top. >> Nothing has been chosen arbitrarily, since the boundaries themselves rule those patterns out.

6|An air column can be described by two separate properties at every point. >> **Movement** is how far an air element travels. >> **Compression** is how much the spacing between neighboring elements changes. >> Several elements can travel together while the gaps between them stay nearly the same. In other places, the gaps close and open strongly around a point that barely moves. || Each property is read in its own way. >> Following one circle through a cycle reveals how far it moves. >> Comparing the gaps between neighboring circles reveals which regions are squeezed and which are spread out. || The two properties combine like a spring and a mass. >> Changing compression supplies a **spring-like restoring effect**, since crowded air pushes back toward its usual spacing. >> The air’s **inertia** keeps it moving while that restoring effect changes. || The ends of the tube shape every pattern. >> The **closed end** restricts movement. >> The **open end** permits it. >> Higher modes place their alternating regions of movement and compression at different positions within the same tube. || The moving **Schwa** reference and the **F1, F2 and F3** rows are all resonances of one air system. >> They are not separate streams of air. >> A constriction can strongly affect moving mass in one mode and compression in another. >> Its effect therefore depends on the mode as well as on where the constriction is.
7|This **pop quiz** marks six locations along the air column, **A** through **F**, and asks what happens at each one. >> Each location has to be followed through a complete cycle. >> Watching its air element reveals how far it moves. >> Comparing its neighbors reveals how much the spacing around it changes. >> A single still frame cannot settle whether a location usually has large movement or large compression changes. || Strong **movement** means added mass matters. >> Greater **effective mass** makes the air harder to speed up and reverse. >> The cycle takes longer, so the resonance tends to **fall**. || Strong **compression change** means added stiffness matters. >> Greater **stiffness** gives a stronger return toward the usual spacing. >> When this effect dominates, the cycle gets faster and the resonance **rises**. || **Modes 1, 2 and 3** arrange these regions differently along the tube. >> A location that moves strongly in one mode can be squeezed strongly in another. >> Predicting the effect of a local change therefore requires knowing two things, the position and the mode.

10|**Mode 1** and **Mode 2** are two resonance patterns of the same air column. The glottal end is held, and the lips are open. >> Both modes move the air strongly near the lips. >> Mode 2 has one more reversal inside the tube, so its movement and compression are arranged differently. || Three markers sit at the **back**, the **front** and the **lips**. >> Each marker is the same physical place in both modes. >> Narrowing the tract at one marker changes that region for both modes at once. || The effect of the narrowing depends on what each mode is doing at that marker. >> Where the air **moves strongly**, the mass-like effect matters, and the vibration tends to slow. >> Where the air **compresses strongly**, the spring-like effect matters, and the vibration tends to speed up. >> One narrowing can therefore move **F1** and **F2** in different directions. || The two modes have a fixed relationship only in the ideal case. >> In the uniform reference tube, Mode 2 runs at **three times** the frequency of Mode 1. >> Changing the tract’s shape changes the formants and can break that ratio. || Formants and harmonics are different things. >> **Formants** are resonances of the tract, and they change when the tract changes shape. >> **Harmonics** are components of the source, and their spacing comes from how fast the vocal folds repeat.

15|The vocal folds supply the voiced **source**. Their repetition rate is **F0**, and F0 sets the spacing between the harmonic lines in the source spectrum. || The top row recalls the **fixed–fixed membrane**, with five modes and their sum. >> **Mode 1 (1,1)** is at **100 Hz**, **(2,1)** at **200 Hz**, **(1,2)** at **300 Hz**, **(2,2)** at **400 Hz** and **(3,1)** at **500 Hz**. >> A pickup point records the combined motion. >> These membrane frequencies were assigned for the illustration, not calculated as the natural resonances of every membrane. || The bottom row has **three source spectra** before the vocal tract filters them, all in the same **0 to 4000 Hz** window. >> With **F0 at 100 Hz**, the harmonic lines are packed closely together. >> With **F0 at 250 Hz**, the harmonics fall at **250, 500, 750** and onward, from **tighter, lighter vocal folds**. >> With **F0 at 350 Hz**, they fall at **350, 700, 1050** and onward, from **very tight and light vocal folds**. || A lower F0 gives **denser harmonic sampling**, and a higher F0 gives **sparser sampling**. >> The same window holds fewer lines as F0 rises. >> Heavier, looser folds repeat more slowly, and lighter, tighter folds repeat faster. Mass resists changes in movement, and tension supplies the return. || A voiced vowel contains **many frequencies** at once. >> Source repetition decides which harmonics are available. >> The vocal tract then strengthens some regions and weakens others. >> Changing pitch changes the source spacing, while changing vowel shape changes the tract’s favored regions. These are two separate contributions to one sound.
16|The **filter** is the vocal tract, modeled as a **fixed–free** tube with one end closed and one end open. It selects **formants**, and its core rule is simple. >> A formant **encourages the closest harmonics to be audible**. >> **Harmonics far from that formant are muted.** >> The voiced source supplies the harmonics, and the filter chooses which ones survive. The vowel shape is the filter. || Each vowel places its formants differently. >> **Schwa** has separate first and second resonances, near **500** and **1500 Hz**. >> **/u/** places both lower, near **300** and **1000 Hz**, and also marks a third formant near **2000 Hz**. >> The front vowel **/i/ or /ɪ/** has a low F1 near **300 Hz** and a much higher F2 near **2200 Hz**. >> **/a/** places its first two targets close together, at **800** and **900 Hz**. These values belong to this illustration. || Changing the tongue and lips changes these favored frequency regions. >> Each formant target pulls up the harmonics nearest to it. >> Harmonics between targets stay weak. || A formant target and a harmonic line are different things. >> The **target** identifies a resonance of the tract. >> The **line** identifies a component of the source. >> A target can sit between two lines and still strengthen the harmonics on either side. Filtering changes heights without creating a new component exactly at each target.
17|The grid crosses **source** rows with **vowel filter** columns, so the same filter can be compared at different pitches. **Dashed red ticks** mark the formant targets, and **colored stems** mark the actual harmonics after filtering. || Each row keeps the source fixed. >> The top row comes from **heavy, loose** vocal folds, with closely spaced harmonics. >> The middle row comes from **lighter, tighter** folds with **F0 at 250 Hz**. >> The bottom row comes from the **lightest, tightest** folds with **F0 at 350 Hz**. || Each column keeps the vowel filter fixed. >> Moving down a column changes only F0. >> Moving across a row changes only the vowel filter. >> These two directions separate the effects of the source from the effects of tract shape. || **Dense harmonics trace the formant shape well.** >> With a low F0, many harmonics fall near each resonance. >> Their heights describe the filter’s **envelope**, the smooth outline of favored strengths. >> More harmonics in the window give a smoother, easier-to-read spectrum. || **Sparse harmonics can miss a formant or make it look ambiguous.** >> A higher F0 leaves wider gaps between the stems. >> A strong stem may sit beside a formant target instead of on it, even though the target has not moved. >> The two close /a/ targets can then look like one grouped region. || Difficulty reading a formant does not mean the resonance disappeared. >> The ticks stay in the same place in each column. >> When the targets are unchanged but the stems spread apart, the source has changed and the filter has not.
18|A **spectrum** is **one instant**, and a **spectrogram** **stacks many spectra over time**. >> In a spectrum, frequency runs horizontally, and peak height gives amplitude for one short slice of sound. >> The sequence contains three slices of **/i/** followed by three slices of **/a/**. || The first step maps the spectrum’s frequency axis onto the spectrogram’s **vertical axis**. >> The slices are then placed from left to right in time. >> Frequency now runs upward. || The second step turns **spectrum height into darkness**. >> **Black** means high amplitude, **gray** means medium and **white** means low. >> A rising band is moving to a higher frequency. >> A darkening band is getting stronger at the frequency where it already is. || The two displays use the horizontal axis for different things. >> Moving right in a spectrum means a higher frequency. >> Moving right in a spectrogram means a later time. || During the **/i/ to /a/** transition, the first strong band **rises** and the second strong band **falls**. >> One upper band also moves down, while a fainter band stays in the high-frequency region. >> Connecting neighboring slices traces how the resonance pattern changes. >> Position and darkness carry different information about that change.

Recognition means relating the measured pattern to the right category. || The exercise links the physics of speech with machine learning. >> The mass–spring account explains where F1 and F2 come from. >> The network learns how those two numbers relate to vowel categories. >> At this stage, the inputs and the task are defined. Whether the network recognizes vowels well is a question for training and testing.
22|A **purely linear network** takes **2 inputs** through **hidden layers of 3, 2 and 4 neurons** to **5 outputs**, with **no activation functions**. >> The two inputs are **F1** and **F2**, and their order keeps their meanings straight. || Every layer is **fully connected**. >> Each receiving neuron connects to every neuron in the layer before it. >> Each connection carries a **weight**. >> A receiving neuron multiplies each incoming value by its weight and adds up the products. || The hidden layers hold intermediate combinations. >> The first hidden layer has **three** neurons, the second has **two** and the third has **four**. >> Hidden values are new combinations of the inputs. They are not new measured formants, and they are not finished categories. >> Different weights let neurons combine the same inputs in different ways. || The five outputs stand for five words. >> From top to bottom, they are **/done/, /dune/, /dean/, /Don/** and **/Dan/**. >> Keeping that order fixed keeps the meaning of each output value. || **Purely linear** means each layer passes on its weighted sums with no activation function in between. >> The computation is a chain of weighted sums. >> Passing sums forward describes the computation, but it does not by itself make the final five values into probabilities.
