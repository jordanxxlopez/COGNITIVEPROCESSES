2|An **oscillation** is a repeated movement back and forth around **equilibrium**, the position where the system would rest. When the spring is stretched or squeezed, it pulls or pushes the mass back toward that resting position. >> The mass has **inertia**, so it does not stop when it reaches equilibrium. It keeps going and displaces the spring on the other side. >> The spring now acts in the opposite direction and brings the mass back again, and the cycle repeats. || The same mass can move in two directions. >> In **longitudinal motion**, the mass travels along the line of the spring, so the spring itself gets longer and shorter. >> In **transverse motion**, the mass travels across the spring line. The two spring segments are already pulled tight, and moving the mass above or below their resting line tilts their pull so that it points back toward the line. || The return comes from a force that keeps changing direction. >> While the mass is on one side, the spring acts toward equilibrium from that side. >> Once the mass crosses over, the displacement flips, and so does the restoring direction. The back-and-forth motion grows out of this repeated exchange between inertia and the restoring force. || Both pictures repeat at the same **frequency**, the number of cycles per second, which here is **0.5 Hz**. >> They match because the mass and the total restoring stiffness are the same in both directions. Direction of motion alone does not set the rate. >> The model has no damping, so the motion keeps going at full size instead of dying away.
3|Two properties set how fast a mass on a spring oscillates, its **mass** and its **stiffness**. The demonstration runs three systems side by side and changes one property at a time. >> The reference system has mass **m** and stiffness **K** and oscillates at **0.5 Hz**. >> The heavier system has mass **4m** with the same stiffness and oscillates at **0.25 Hz**. >> The stiffer system has the original mass with stiffness **4K** and oscillates at **1 Hz**. || **Greater mass lowers the frequency** when stiffness stays the same. >> A heavier mass is harder to speed up, slow down and turn around. >> The same restoring force therefore needs more time to finish each cycle, and four times the mass halves the rate. || **Greater stiffness raises the frequency** when mass stays the same. >> A stiffer spring produces a stronger pull back toward equilibrium for the same displacement. >> The mass turns around sooner and fits more cycles into the same time, and four times the stiffness doubles the rate. >> Mass sets how strongly the system resists a change in motion, while stiffness sets how strongly it is pulled back. || Frequency is read by counting complete repetitions over the same stretch of time. >> How far the mass travels is its **amplitude**, which is a separate property from its rate. >> The longitudinal and transverse versions match in each row because their **effective restoring stiffness**, the restoring action in the actual direction of motion, is the same. Across the spring line, that stiffness comes from tension and geometry, so making a spring harder to stretch along its length does not automatically make it stiffer across it.
4|Two equal masses joined by springs between two fixed ends have **two normal modes**. A **normal mode** is a coordinated pattern of motion in which every part repeats at one shared frequency and keeps the same relationship to the other parts. >> The chain has three equal coupling springs, one between each fixed end and a mass and one in the middle. || In **Mode 1**, both masses move in the same direction at the same time. >> The distance between them barely changes, so the middle spring is stretched and squeezed very little. >> This is the slower pattern, at **0.4 Hz** in the demonstration. || In **Mode 2**, the masses move toward and away from each other. >> Now the middle spring is compressed and stretched strongly on every cycle. >> That extra restoring force makes the pattern faster, at about **1.73 times** the Mode 1 frequency. It is faster without being exactly twice as fast. || A mode describes the whole system, not one mass. >> Both masses take part in each mode, so one mass does not belong to Mode 1 while the other belongs to Mode 2. >> The masses and springs stay the same in both patterns, and only their relationship changes. || The two modes can happen at the same time. >> Their movements add together at each mass, reinforcing where they point the same way and partly or fully canceling where they point opposite ways. This adding is called **superposition**. >> The bottom row of the demonstration is the exact sum of the two modes, so the combined motion contains both frequencies at once. The longitudinal and transverse chains follow the same relationships in different directions.

6|Three equal masses between fixed ends have **three normal modes**, one for each movable mass. The chain has four equal coupling springs. || In **Mode 1**, all three masses move in the same direction. >> The middle mass travels farthest, and the two outer masses travel less. Moving together does not require moving the same distance. >> This is the slowest pattern, at **0.32 Hz** in the demonstration. || In **Mode 2**, the two outer masses move in opposite directions while the middle mass stays at equilibrium. >> The pulls on the middle mass from its two sides balance, so it does not move. >> A point that stays still throughout a mode is called a **node**. >> This pattern runs at about **1.85 times** the lowest frequency. || In **Mode 3**, the two outer masses move together while the middle mass moves against both of them. >> Every neighbor now opposes its neighbor, so the springs between them change strongly. >> This is the fastest pattern, at about **2.41 times** the lowest frequency. || The ordering follows how strongly neighboring masses oppose each other. >> More opposition between neighbors means more spring stretching, which means a stronger restoring force and a faster pattern. >> The same three masses take part in every mode, and no extra mass joins in at a higher mode. The colors track individual masses, and the resting marks give the position each displacement is measured from. || The frequency ratios belong to this particular chain and are not simple whole-number multiples. >> When all three modes run together, their motions add at each mass. >> The middle mass can still move in the mixture, because Modes 1 and 3 move it even though it is a node in Mode 2.
7|Each movable mass adds one more **normal mode** in the direction being modeled. The atlas lines up chains with one through six masses, so they have one through six modes. >> Each row is one chain, and each column is one mode number. >> Smaller chains leave the later columns empty, because they have no such modes, which gives **21** patterns in all. >> Every chain has equal masses, one more coupling spring than masses and two fixed ends. || Within a chain, **Mode 1** is the broadest pattern, with all the masses moving together. >> Each higher mode adds another group moving in the opposite direction. >> More reversals across the chain mean more places where neighbors pull against each other. The springs change more, the restoring force is stronger and the pattern repeats faster. || The fixed endpoints never move in any mode. >> A mass that stays still inside one mode is a node of that pattern, but the rest of the chain is still moving. >> A higher mode does not use smaller masses or stiffer springs. It uses the same parts arranged in a different relationship, and the mode number names the pattern, not any particular mass. || Colors mark the mode number and rows mark the number of masses. >> Every pattern is scaled to the same largest displayed displacement, so the height of a picture says nothing about its frequency. >> **Amplitude** is how far things move, and frequency is how often they repeat. >> Chains of separate masses generally do not have the exact whole-number frequency spacing of an ideal continuous string.
8|A **fixed–fixed string** is held still at both ends, so both endpoints are **nodes**. >> **Mode 1** fits one half-wave into the string’s length. >> Modes 2 through 5 fit two, three, four and five half-waves. || More half-waves in the same length means a shorter wavelength. >> At the same wave speed, a shorter wavelength repeats faster. >> The five modes run at **100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz**, which are consecutive **harmonics**, whole-number multiples of the lowest frequency, the **fundamental**. Both odd and even harmonics are present. || The same string can carry all five modes at once. >> Their movements add at every point along the string, reinforcing in some places and canceling in others as their cycles shift. >> The overall shape keeps changing even though each component stays perfectly regular. >> A spot can look almost flat for a moment through cancellation and then move strongly a moment later. No component has been removed when that happens. || The playback is slowed by a factor of **250**, so 100 Hz is displayed at **0.4 Hz**. >> Every mode is slowed by the same factor, which keeps their relative rates the same. || A **pickup** at a single point, **17%** of the way along the string, records the movement there. >> Its **spectrum** puts frequency on one axis and amplitude on the other, and it has one peak for each of the five components. >> The peaks are equal in height because each mode’s strength was chosen to give the same contribution at this pickup. At another position the heights would differ, so equal peaks do not mean equal motion everywhere.
9|A **membrane** is a vibrating surface with fixed outer edges. Its modes are labeled with two numbers, which count the moving regions along the two directions of the surface. >> **(1,1)** has one broad region moving up and down. >> **(2,1)** and **(1,2)** split the surface into two regions, in different directions. >> **(2,2)** makes four regions, and **(3,1)** makes three regions along one direction. >> Neighboring regions move in opposite directions, and the lines between them are **nodes** that stay still. || The five modes add at every point of the surface. >> Their contributions reinforce or cancel, producing one constantly changing shape. >> The combined panel uses a smaller vertical scale than the single-mode panels, so heights cannot be compared directly across them. || A node stays still for the whole cycle of its mode. >> A moving region also passes through the resting surface for an instant on every cycle, but that brief crossing does not make it a node. >> Following one region as it rises, crosses rest and falls, while its neighbor does the opposite, separates the two. >> The two numbers describe how the surface is divided. They are not two frequencies, two membranes or a count of elapsed cycles. || A **pickup** at one marked point records the motion there, and its **spectrum** has one peak per mode. >> The mode strengths were chosen to make the five peaks equal at that point. >> The frequencies **100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz** were **assigned for the illustration**. They are not the natural resonances of this rectangle, so their even spacing teaches how frequencies combine without claiming that every membrane has harmonic spacing.
10|The **scientific method** depends on stating very clear theories about how the world works, usually in the form of **differential equations**. >> A differential equation describes how quantities in a system change. >> Solving the equations gives a **prediction** of what we should find when we actually measure the world. || The theory here is that the **vocal folds are a membrane**. >> When a membrane vibrates, the wave of vibration has **equidistant peaks** in its spectrum. These evenly spaced peaks are called **harmonics**. >> The equations involved are like the spring and membrane equations already met, only somewhat more complicated. || The theory leads to a specific prediction. >> If anyone’s **laryngeal vibration** signal is measured, its spectrum should have **equidistant harmonics**, peaks separated by equal frequency intervals. >> The test is to record real vocal-fold vibration and examine its spectrum. Evenly spaced peaks would agree with the prediction, and a different pattern would challenge the model. || The prediction is more specific than just seeing vibration. >> The vocal folds could vibrate without producing evenly spaced peaks, so the spectrum is what tests the theory. >> The membrane animation used assigned harmonic frequencies, so it pictures what the idea looks like but does not test it. Only a recording of real vocal-fold vibration can do that, and the experiment is proposed here without a reported outcome.
11|A **fixed–free system** has one end held still and the other end free to move. >> The fixed end must be a **node**, a point of no movement. >> The free end must be an **antinode**, the point of greatest movement over a cycle. || The allowed shapes fit odd numbers of **quarter-waves**. >> The simplest mode fits one quarter-wave into the length. >> The next modes fit three, five and seven quarter-waves. >> These are the **first, third, fifth and seventh harmonics**, so the second allowed mode is the third harmonic, not the second. || Only odd counts satisfy both endpoint conditions. >> An even count of quarter-waves would put a node at the free end, which the free end does not allow. >> Higher modes fit shorter wavelengths into the same length, so they repeat faster. || The demonstration uses **100, 300, 500 and 700 Hz**, slowed by a factor of **250** for display. >> A spectrum taken at the free end has four equal peaks, because the modes were given equal strengths. >> An antinode does not stay at its farthest point. It swings through rest to the other side while the fixed end stays still. >> Counting allowed shapes and comparing frequencies answer different questions, even though both use numbers. || The fixed–fixed and fixed–free examples were **tuned separately** to the same fundamental. >> If only the endpoint changed, with the same length and wave speed, the fundamental would drop to half. >> Matching labels across the two examples therefore do not isolate the effect of the free end.
