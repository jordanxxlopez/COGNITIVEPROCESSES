## p2
- The **2026 Nobel Prize in economics** was awarded to **Robert Hall** and **Michael Woodford** for the **microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization**. **Stabilization** means returning a system to its equilibrium and keeping it there. This is the same role that **balancing feedback** plays, and the same role a spring plays when it pulls a mass back to rest.
  - In their model of the economy, **unemployment and inflation rise and fall in turn**, in the same way that the position and the velocity of a mass on a spring take turns.
  - The goal of stabilization policy is to **reduce the size of these rises and falls**, so the economy avoids very high unemployment and runaway inflation.
- Woodford's theory is called **Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium**, or **DSGE**. Each part of the name describes one feature of the model.
  - Dynamic: The economy changes over time according to rules.
  - Stochastic: The model can handle **random shocks**, such as the start of a war or a large company's CEO being caught embezzling. This is similar to stochastic gradient descent, where one unusual data point can push the estimate far off, but later data pull it back toward the average.
  - Equilibrium: The goal is a steady state without large, sudden jumps.
- **Macroeconomic** and **microeconomic** describe two levels of the same economy.
  - Macroeconomic: These quantities describe a whole economy, such as the inflation rate or the number of people who are unemployed.
  - Microeconomic: These events are single choices, such as one person buying something.
  - Explaining large patterns from many small events is the same kind of problem as speech. In speech, many small vibrations in the vocal tract add up to words that a listener understands without noticing any single vibration.

## p3
- A mass-spring system vibrates because **the spring tends to return to equilibrium**, while **a moving mass tends to keep moving**. The vibration can occur in two directions, and both behave in the same way.
  - Longitudinal motion: The mass moves back and forth along the line of the spring.
  - Transverse motion: The mass moves up and down between two walls, across the line of the springs.
  - Both directions are useful, because transverse motion is the easier way to understand the vocal folds, while longitudinal motion is the easier way to understand the air in the vocal tract.
- **A part changes the frequency only while it is doing its job.** A mass changes the frequency only when it moves, and a spring changes the frequency only when it is stretched or compressed. This one principle explains every result about modes.

## p4
- **Mass and stiffness change the frequency in opposite directions.**
  - Mass: Increasing the mass lowers the frequency, so the system completes fewer cycles per second. A heavier mass is harder to turn around.
  - Stiffness: Increasing the stiffness raises the frequency, so the system completes more cycles per second. A stiffer spring pulls back with more force.

## p5
- A system with **two masses** has **two modes** (ways of vibrating), and it can also vibrate in both at once.
  - Mode 1: The masses move together. The middle spring is carried along without being stretched, so in practice the system uses **only two springs**, and the frequency is **lower**.
  - Mode 2: The masses move against each other. All **three springs** are stretched and compressed, which adds **stiffness** and gives a **higher frequency**.
- Making the **middle spring stiffer** affects the two modes differently.
  - Mode 1: The frequency **does not change**, because the middle spring is never stretched in this mode.
  - Mode 2: The frequency **rises**, because the middle spring is stretched and compressed throughout this mode.
  - A spring therefore matters only in a mode where it is being stretched or compressed.
- In real systems, the masses usually rise and fall together while also moving against each other, so **both modes occur at once**.

## p6
- A system with **three masses** has **three modes**.
  - Mode 1: All three masses move together.
  - Mode 2: The middle mass stays still while the two outer masses move against each other.
  - Mode 3: The middle mass moves against both outer masses.
- Making **Mass 2**, the middle mass, heavier affects only the modes in which that mass moves.
  - Mode 1 and Mode 3: The frequency **goes down**, because the middle mass moves in both modes.
  - Mode 2: The frequency **does not change**, because the middle mass does not move in this mode.
  - **A mass affects the frequency only through its movement.** A mass that does not move has no effect, a mass that moves a little has a small effect, and a mass that moves a lot has a large effect.

## p7
- **Each added mass adds one more mode**, and the same patterns repeat as the chain gets longer.
  - Mode 1: Everything moves together.
  - Mode 2: The system splits into **two parts** that move against each other. With an odd number of masses, the middle mass stays still.
  - Mode 3: The system splits into **three parts**, with the middle moving one way and the two sides moving the other way.
- Adding more and more masses and springs produces a **string**. **Every small piece of a string has weight, which is its mass, and every small piece resists being stretched or compressed, which is its springiness.**
  - The same three mode patterns appear in a string.
  - Higher modes have more parts moving against each other, so the string is stretched and compressed more, and those modes vibrate faster.
  - In musical instruments, these frequencies are heard as **notes**. The ear can pick out many instruments at once because the ear itself is built from masses and springs.

## p8
- A string fixed at both ends with its first mode at **100 hertz** has its next modes at **200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz**. Every mode is an **exact multiple** of the first, and these multiples are called **harmonics**.
  - The second mode contains **two** copies of the first mode's bump, the third contains **three**, and the fifth contains **five**. Each mode's frequency is therefore the first frequency multiplied by the number of copies.
- A single mode on its own produces a **pure tone**, which sounds so harsh that it is used for sirens. Everyday sounds contain many modes at once, which is why a real string's motion looks irregular, much like the waveform of speech.
- A **spectrum** shows this mixture. **Frequency** is on the horizontal axis, and **amplitude** (strength) is on the vertical axis. For this string, the spectrum has spikes at 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz.

## p9
- All of the results so far follow from a short list of **principles of mass-spring systems**, and the principles are consistent with each other.
  - Mass and stiffness: **Increasing the mass lowers the frequency, and increasing the stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - Number of modes: **A system with n masses has n modes of vibration**, and each higher mode has a higher frequency.
  - Added mass: **Adding mass at a location lowers the frequency more when that location moves more.** How much a location moves depends on where it is.
  - Motion and stretching: **Where movement is greatest, there is no stretching, and where stretching is greatest, there is no movement.**
  - Added stiffness: **Adding stiffness at a location raises the frequency more when that location is stretched and compressed more.** This also depends on where the location is.
  - Fixed-fixed and fixed-free: In a **fixed-fixed system** (fixed at both ends), the harmonics are **whole-number multiples of the lowest frequency**, because each mode contains a whole number of copies of the lowest mode. In a **fixed-free system** (fixed at one end and free at the other), they are **odd multiples**, because each mode contains an odd number of copies.

## p10
- Making one piece of a string **heavier**, such as thickening its exact middle, changes some modes and not others.
  - Mode 1: The middle moves a lot, so the added mass **lowers** the frequency.
  - Mode 2: The middle does not move, so the added mass has **no effect**.
  - Mode 3: The middle moves again, so the frequency goes down.
  - Mode 4: The middle is still again, so the frequency does not change. Moving up through the modes, the effect **alternates**.
- **Along a vibrating string, locations of motion and locations of stretching alternate.** Where a piece moves the most, it is carried up and down and is hardly stretched. Where the string is stretched the most, such as right next to a fixed end, there is **no movement**.
  - **A useful shortcut is that the springiest locations are the locations that do not move.** In **Mode 5** of a string fixed at both ends, the string does not move at the **two fixed ends** or at the **four points where it crosses its middle line**, so those **six locations** are the springiest.
  - The **most motion**, and therefore the **least springiness**, occurs at the tops of the **five bumps**.

## p11
- Changing one small piece of a string can move two modes in **opposite directions**. Consider a location where **Mode 4 moves the most** and where **Mode 5 crosses its middle line**, so Mode 5 does not move there. Both **mass** and **stiffness** are added to that piece.
  - Mode 4: The piece is moving, so the added mass **lowers** the frequency. The piece is barely stretched, so the added stiffness has no effect.
  - Mode 5: The piece is not moving, so the added mass has no effect. The piece is stretched the most, so the added stiffness **raises** the frequency.
  - The same change therefore **lowers Mode 4's frequency and raises Mode 5's frequency**. Each mode is worked out separately by asking whether the location is moving or stretching in that mode.
- **Perturbation theory** predicts how a small change in one location affects how something vibrates. In the late nineteenth century, researchers tested this by placing small amounts of **wax** on different parts of a **drum**.
  - When wax was placed where the drum vibrated a lot, the extra mass made the drum vibrate more slowly, causing its frequency (pitch) to **decrease**.
  - When wax was placed where the drum did not vibrate, it had **no effect** on the drum's frequency or sound.

## p12
- The **larynx** contains the **vocal folds**, which form a **membrane** (a thin sheet of tissue) similar to a drum. Like a drum, the vocal folds have many modes of vibration.
  - Mode 1: All the masses move up and down together.
  - Mode 2: The two halves move against each other.
  - Higher modes: The surface divides into more and more parts.
  - The vocal folds are fixed around their edges, so they behave like a fixed-fixed system, and their frequencies are harmonics (whole-number multiples of the lowest one).
- Changes to the mass and stiffness of the vocal folds change the voice. Heavy smokers can develop **nodules** (small growths) on the vocal folds. A nodule adds mass at one location and changes the stiffness of the tissue around it, so the folds vibrate differently and the voice sounds different.

## p13
- The **theory** predicts that the vocal folds vibrate in many modes at once, so a measured voice should have a spectrum with **equidistant harmonics** (harmonics that are evenly spaced). A voice was recorded and analyzed in **Praat**. Its lowest harmonic was about 130 hertz, and the next harmonics were close to two and three times that value, so **the measurement matched the prediction.**
  - This is how the **scientific method** works. A clear theory is written as **differential equations**, the equations are solved to produce a prediction, and the prediction is checked against a measurement.
  - Each person's lowest frequency is different, but the harmonics above it are always close to multiples of it.

## p14
- A **building** is **fixed at one end and free at the other**. The base is fixed to the ground, while the top can sway in strong winds or an earthquake. Engineers design a building to sway only in **Mode 1**. That motion can feel unpleasant on a high floor, but it is safe. Swaying in a higher mode could break the building.
- The **air inside the vocal tract** is also a fixed-free system.
  - Glottis end: The **vocal folds are closed**, so the air there **cannot move**, and that end is **fixed**.
  - Lip end: The air can move in and out freely, so that end is **free**.
  - Unlike a building, the air in the vocal tract vibrates in **all of its modes**. Speaking changes the mass and stiffness of the air at different locations along the tract.
- A fixed-free system has **frequencies at 100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz**, which are only the odd multiples of 100. The first mode goes from **no motion** at the fixed end to **maximum motion** at the free end, which is one quarter of a wave. The next possible modes contain **three** and then **five** of these quarter-wave pieces.
  - Two pieces would end with no motion at both ends, which is the pattern of a fixed-fixed system, so even multiples never occur.

## p15
- The air in the vocal tract can be modeled as a **chain of masses and springs** that is fixed at the glottis and free at the lips. The masses represent the air's tendency to keep moving, and the springs represent the air's springiness.
  - First formant: The lowest mode corresponds to the **first formant**, **F1**.
  - Second formant: The next mode corresponds to the **second formant**, **F2**. It vibrates **three times faster** than the first mode, because it contains three of the first mode's quarter-wave pieces.
  - The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called **formants**, while the frequencies of a fixed-fixed system are called harmonics.
- Three locations along the tube are important for vowels.
  - /a/: The constriction is **one-third of the way from the glottis**.
  - /i/: The constriction is **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - /u/: The constriction is at the **free end, at the lips**.

## p16
- A **constriction** (narrowing) is a location where the tongue or lips narrow the vocal tract. Its position is measured from the **glottis**, the space between the vocal folds at the fixed end.
  - Done: There is **no significant constriction** in the vocal tract, so this is the central vowel.
  - Dean: The constriction occurs under the roof of the mouth, about **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**, producing a **high front** vowel.
  - Don: The constriction occurs far back in the throat, about **one-third of the way from the glottis**, producing a **low back** vowel.
  - Dune: The constriction occurs **at the lips** because of **lip rounding**, producing a **high back** vowel.
  - Dan: The tongue is positioned low and toward the front of the mouth, producing a **low front** vowel.

## p18
- **A constriction increases both the stiffness and the mass of the air.**
  - Stiffness: Compressed air becomes **stiffer**, in the same way that the air in a tire becomes stiffer when it is compressed.
  - Mass: Packing air into a smaller space raises its **density**, which makes it more **massive**.
- It might seem that raising mass and stiffness together would **cancel out**. **That is false** for any system with many masses and springs, because in a given mode each location is either mostly **moving** or mostly **stretching**.
  - Location with no motion and a lot of stretching: Only the added stiffness matters, so the frequency **rises**.
  - Location with a lot of motion and no stretching: Only the added mass matters, so the frequency **falls**.
  - Speech itself proves that the effects do not cancel, because constrictions at different locations clearly change the frequencies of the voice.

## p19
- For **/u/**, **lip rounding** creates a constriction at the **free end**, which raises the mass and the stiffness of the air there.
  - Mode 1: The air at the free end moves a lot and is hardly compressed, so **motion matters most**, and the added mass **lowers F1**.
  - Mode 2: The air at the free end also moves a lot, so **motion matters most** again, and the added mass **lowers F2**.
  - Result: **Both F1 and F2 go down** for /u/.
- A speaker started from the central vowel, with formants near 500, 1500 and 2500 hertz, and moved into /u/. **Both formants dropped**, as predicted.
  - The rounded lips added mass where the air moves the most. The added stiffness had no effect there, because the air at the lips is moving, not stretching.

## p20
- For **/i/**, the constriction is about **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**, and that one location behaves differently in the two modes.
  - Mode 1: The air at this location moves a lot and is hardly compressed, so **motion matters most**, and the added mass **lowers F1**.
  - Mode 2: The air at this location is strongly compressed, so **springiness matters most**, and the added stiffness **raises F2**.
  - Result: One formant falls while the other rises, because the same constriction is at a location of motion for F1 and at a location of compression for F2.
- A speaker went from the central vowel into /i/. The **first formant dropped sharply**, and the **second formant rose**.

## p21
- For **/a/**, the constriction is about **one-third of the way from the glottis**, and the pattern is reversed.
  - Mode 1: This location is near the fixed end, where the air is strongly compressed, so **springiness matters most**, and the added stiffness **raises F1**.
  - Mode 2: The air at this location moves a lot, so **motion matters most**, and the added mass **lowers F2**.
  - Result: **F1 goes up and F2 goes down**, so the two formants move closer together.
- The reasoning also works in reverse.
  - Large rise: **If a constriction raises a formant a lot, the air at that location must be springy and hardly moving in that mode.**
  - Large fall: **If a constriction lowers a formant a lot, the air at that location must be moving a lot.**

## p22
- The same principles explain all the vowels. **Added mass matters where the air moves, and added stiffness matters where the air is compressed and expanded.** The vocal tract is fixed at the glottis and free at the lips, so its formants are **odd multiples** of the lowest one.

## p23
- The air in the tube can be modeled as **lines of air particles**, where each particle represents billions of real air molecules. The closed vocal folds are at the **fixed end**, and the open lips are at the **free end**.
  - Compression and expansion: Where the lines crowd together and then spread apart, the air is **compressing and expanding**, which is **springiness**.
  - Motion: Where the lines move back and forth without changing their spacing, the air is acting as a **mass**. This is easiest to see at the **free end**.
- Each formant has a fixed number of points with no motion.
  - F1: There is one point of no motion, at the fixed end.
  - F2: There are two points of no motion.
  - F3: There are three points of no motion.
  - Between each pair of no-motion points, locations of maximum motion and locations of maximum compression alternate, as they do along a vibrating string.
- For the **schwa**, which has no constriction, a measured voice had formants near **500, 1500 and 2500 hertz**. These are the odd multiples that the theory predicts.

## p24
- The question **Where were you a year ago?** uses all of the constriction rules at once. Each sound places a constriction at a different location, so the formants move up and down continuously instead of staying at fixed values.
  - /i/-like sound: The tongue forms a constriction near the front of the mouth, so **F1 goes down and F2 goes up**. This location is a location of motion in Mode 1 and a location of compression in Mode 2.
  - you: **Lip rounding** forms a constriction at the lips, so **F1 and F2 both go down**. The air at the lips is moving in every mode.
  - /a/-like sound: The tongue moves back and down, so **F1 rises and F2 falls**. This location is a location of compression in Mode 1 and a location of motion in Mode 2.
- **Speech is a continuous series of constrictions, not a list of separate vowels.** The frequencies of the air in the vocal tract are always changing, and the same mass and spring principles predict which way each formant moves at every moment.
