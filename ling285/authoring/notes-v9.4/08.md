## p3
- **A spring stops at a place, but a mass stops after a process.** This one idea separates two very different kinds of motion. A spring with no mass attached returns to one location and stays there. A spring with a mass attached keeps moving past that location, back and forth. Only then does it finally come to rest.
  - Everything is measured with one number, the **displacement x**, how far the end of the spring is from its **natural position**, where the spring rests when nothing is pulling or pushing on it. The natural position is x at 0, a stretch to the right is plus 1, and a compression to the left is minus 1.
- **The spring alone, pulled out to plus 1 and let go, pulls itself back and stops exactly at zero without overshooting.** Plotted against time, the curve starts at plus 1 and bends downward, dropping quickly at first and then more and more slowly. It gets closer and closer to 0 but never crosses below it. The label is **"x approaches 0 without crossing it."**
  - The motion slows down because the pull of the spring depends on how far it is stretched. A large stretch produces a large pull back, and a small stretch produces a small pull. As the end gets close to zero, the pull becomes almost zero. So the motion becomes almost zero too.
  - Pushed in to minus 1 and released, the spring pushes itself back out and settles at zero. The curve starts at minus 1 and rises toward 0, flattening out right at 0 and never going above it. Whether it starts from plus 1 or minus 1, a massless spring always ends at the same place and never passes through it. That is what it means to say a spring **stops at a place**.
- **Adding a mass changes the behavior completely.** A mass, drawn as a small cart on wheels, is attached to the free end. Pulled to plus 1 and released, the mass does not stop at zero. It shoots right through the natural position and keeps going to the other side.
  - A moving mass carries its motion with it. When the mass reaches zero, the spring is no longer pulling on it. But the mass is already moving fast. So it continues past zero and compresses the spring on the other side. The compressed spring pushes the mass back, it stops briefly near minus 1, and it is pushed back through zero toward the positive side again. **The mass carries motion through every crossing.**
  - Plotted against time, x now makes a wave. It starts at plus 1, falls through 0 to nearly minus 1, rises back through 0 to nearly plus 1, and repeats. This repeating back-and-forth motion is called **oscillation**.
- **Each swing of the mass is slightly smaller than the one before, so the peaks of the wave shrink a little over time.** This is a **lightly damped oscillation**. **Damping** is anything that slowly dissipates (uses up) the motion, such as friction. With light damping, the mass swings many times before it finally settles at zero.
  - This is why a mass **stops after a process**. It does eventually come to rest at the natural position, but only after a long sequence of crossings, each a little smaller than the last. A mass-spring system therefore has a **frequency**, the number of back-and-forth cycles it completes per second. A massless spring has no frequency at all, because it never swings.

## p4
- **Perturbation theory asks what happens to the behavior of a system when one of its properties is changed.** To **perturb** a system means to give one of its parameters (settings) a small nudge. For a mass-spring system, the two settings that matter are the **mass m** and the **stiffness k** of the spring, and the behavior of interest is the frequency f of the oscillation.
  - Mass up, frequency down: A heavier mass is harder to speed up and harder to slow down. The spring pulls with the same strength as before. But that pull changes the heavier mass's speed less. Each swing takes longer. So fewer swings fit into one second. When m goes up, f goes down.
  - Stiffness up, frequency up: **Stiffness k** is how strongly a spring resists being stretched or compressed. A stiffer spring pulls back harder for the same stretch. That speeds the mass up faster. So each swing finishes sooner, and more swings fit into one second. When k goes up, f goes up.
  - These two rules are the core of perturbation theory for oscillators. There is no need to calculate an exact frequency to use them. Only the direction the frequency moves matters.
- **Speaking changes the effective mass and stiffness of air in the vocal tract.** The **vocal tract** is the air-filled tube from the vocal folds in the throat up through the mouth to the lips. Moving the tongue, jaw and lips compressions some portions of the air in that tube and opens up others.
  - Compressing a portion of air changes how much that air acts like a mass and how much it acts like a spring. The **effective mass** and **effective stiffness**, how much each portion acts like a mass or a spring, are different for every vowel and every consonant, because each sound uses a different shape of the tube.
  - The air is set into vibration by the vocal folds. The tube has different mass and stiffness in different places. So the air vibrates at **many frequencies at once**. The particular set depends on **where** the air is compressed and **how much**. So each vowel and consonant has its own set.
- **MRI images of five vowels make these shapes visible.** The five words are **dean, dune, done, Dan** and **Don**, which differ only in their vowel. In dean the tongue is high and forward, compressing the air at the front and leaving it open at the back. In dune the tongue is high but bunched toward the back, with the lips pushed forward. In done the tongue sits in a middle position. In Dan the jaw and tongue are lower and toward the front. In Don the jaw is open widest and the tongue is low and pulled back, compressing the air in the throat.
  - Each tongue position compressions the air in a different place and by a different amount, giving a different combination of mass and stiffness and therefore a different set of vibration frequencies. That set is what makes dean sound different from Don.
- **The listener has to recover these frequencies from the sound.** The ear has to figure out which low, middle and high frequencies are present in the voice and how strong each one is, and the brain then decides which sound was spoken. Hearing a high frequency of one strength with a low frequency of another might say the word was **"scene,"** while two other frequencies might say **"son."** How the ear separates a single incoming sound into its frequencies is answered by the structure of the inner ear.

## p5
- **Sound is a wave of vibration in the air.** When someone speaks, waves of vibration travel through the air and arrive at the listener's **external ear**, the part on the outside of the head. From there the vibration travels inward through the ear canal toward the **inner ear**.
- **The cochlea is the part of the inner ear that reports frequencies to the brain, and it is the one name to know.** It is a small, coiled tube shaped like a snail shell, buried deep inside the temporal bone of the skull. The cochlea sends the brain signals saying which frequencies are present in a sound.
- **Enlarged, the inner ear has two main regions.** The upper region holds three looping tubes, the **semicircular ducts**, named anterior, lateral and posterior, with two small sacs, the **utricle** and **saccule**, and small sensory structures called **cristae** inside swellings called **ampullae**. These are part of the inner ear but are not the part that separates sound frequencies.
  - The lower region is the coiled **cochlea**. Three fluid-filled channels run through it side by side, the **vestibular duct**, the **cochlear duct** and the **tympanic duct**, spiraling together around the coil.
  - The inner ear is a set of soft, membrane-walled tubes, the **membranous labyrinth**, sitting inside matching hollow spaces in hard bone, the **bony labyrinth**. The soft inner tubes are where the sensing happens, and the bone keeps them safe. The **vestibulocochlear nerve** leaves the inner ear and carries the cochlea's signals about frequencies to the brain.

## p6
- **Sound reaches the cochlea through a chain of structures.** Sound waves first strike the **tympanic membrane**, the eardrum, a thin sheet stretched across the end of the ear canal that vibrates (shakes back and forth) as air pressure pushes on it.
  - The eardrum is attached to the first of three tiny bones, the **malleus**, which moves the second bone, the **incus**, which moves the third bone, the **stapes**. The stapes sits against an opening in the cochlea. So when it vibrates, it pushes in and out on the fluid inside. This chain carries the vibration from the air outside, through the eardrum and three small bones, into the fluid of the inner ear.
- **The basilar membrane runs the length of the cochlea.** Inside the coiled cochlea is a long, thin strip of tissue, the **basilar membrane**. It begins near the stapes, where vibration enters, and follows the spiral all the way to the tip of the coil, where the opening is called the **helicotrema**.
  - When the stapes pushes on the cochlear fluid, the fluid moves. That movement makes the basilar membrane vibrate. The basilar membrane turns the vibration of the fluid into information about frequency. Uncoiling the pathway in the mind makes it easier to see how different locations along one membrane can respond differently.

## p7
- **The basilar membrane is not the same all along its length.** Uncoiled and laid flat, one end of the strip is wide and the other is narrow. The wide end is near the helicotrema, and the narrow end is near the stapes.
  - Wide end: The membrane has a **large mass** and a **small stiffness**. It is heavy and compliant (loose).
  - Narrow end: The membrane has a **small mass** and a **large stiffness**. It is light and stiff. Between the two ends, mass gradually decreases and stiffness gradually increases.
- **Each spot on the membrane acts like its own mass-spring system, with its own natural frequency.** At the heavy, floppy end, large mass lowers the frequency, and small stiffness also lowers it. So this end vibrates at **low frequencies**. At the light, stiff end, small mass raises the frequency, and large stiffness also raises it. So this end vibrates at **high frequencies**. **Frequency increases steadily along the membrane.**
- **A sound with many frequencies is sorted out by place.** Each frequency in the sound makes the part of the membrane tuned to it vibrate the most. A low-frequency component shakes the heavy, floppy region, and a high-frequency component shakes the light, stiff region. A vowel is thus spread out along the membrane, and how strongly each location vibrates reflects how strong that frequency is.
- **Rows of neurons attached along the membrane report the pattern to the brain.** When a region vibrates, the neurons attached there send signals **to the brain**. The brain tells which frequencies are present by **which neurons are active**, and how strong each is by how strongly they signal. A pattern at one set of locations is "scene," and a pattern at a different set is "son."
  - **Georg von Békésy won the Nobel Prize in 1961** for showing that different frequencies cause the strongest vibration at different places along the basilar membrane, the mechanism that lets the ear separate a sound into its frequencies.

## p8
- **The vocal folds are two small muscles in the throat, each about 1 cm long and about 1 mm high.** They sit across the airway, and air from the lungs has to pass between them. In a camera view down the throat, the two folds meet along a line, and the gap between them appears as a narrow dark slit that repeatedly opens and closes when the folds vibrate.
- **Muscle has mass and stiffness, so the vocal folds behave like a mass-spring system, and perturbation theory applies directly.** About **10 other muscles** attached to the bones and cartilages housing the folds can change them in two ways. They can **stretch** the folds, making them **stiffer**. This raises the frequency. They can **bunch** the folds, making them **effectively heavier**. This lowers the frequency. Speakers control how fast their folds vibrate by adjusting their effective mass and stiffness.
- **Controlling vocal fold frequency serves several purposes.** In **singing** it produces different musical notes. In **English** it conveys **questions, mistrust, degree of belief, surprise, emotion** and more, so the same words can mean different things depending on how the frequency rises and falls. In a **tone language** it distinguishes the **meanings of words**, so two words with identical consonants and vowels can be different words.
  - The vibration frequency of the vocal folds is called **pitch**, also F0, the **fundamental frequency**. A higher F0 means the folds open and close more times per second, heard as a higher pitch.
- **Air pressure from the lungs blows the vocal folds apart, and the flowing air sucks them back together.** With air in the lungs and the folds closed, pressure builds beneath them. When it is high enough, it forces them apart, and air flows through the gap. Air rushing through the narrow gap pulls the folds back inward, just like a shower curtain pulled toward the running water. This inward pull from fast-moving air is the **Bernoulli force**, the same force that lifts airplanes.
  - Once the folds are back together, the gap closes and the airflow stops, pressure builds again, and the cycle repeats **hundreds of times per second**. Each full opening and closing is one vibration, so the number of cycles per second is F0, set by the mass and stiffness of the folds.

## p9
- **A harmonic oscillator is a mass on a spring with no damping, described by two rules, one for position and one for velocity.** The first is that ẋ equals v. The second is that v̇ equals minus k over m times x. A dot above a variable means how fast that variable is changing as time passes.
  - The first rule says that the rate at which position changes is the velocity. If the mass is moving to the right with some speed, its position increases at exactly that speed.
  - The second rule says that the rate at which velocity changes depends on the position. The minus sign means the change in velocity always points back toward zero. When the mass is stretched to positive x, its velocity is pushed in the negative direction, and when it is compressed to negative x, its velocity is pushed in the positive direction.
  - The ratio k over m sets how strongly the velocity is pushed. A stiffer spring, larger k, pushes harder, and a heavier mass, larger m, is pushed less. This ratio holds both parts of perturbation theory in one quantity.
- **To follow the motion step by step, specific numbers are picked.** The ratio k over m is 1, and the **time step Δt** is 0.5, the small increment of time moved forward with each round of arithmetic. The motion starts at x equal to 1 and v equal to 0, meaning the mass is pulled out to plus 1 and held still at the moment it is released.
- **Each step updates x first and then updates v using the new x.** The new x is the old x plus Δt times the old v. The new v is the old v minus Δt times the new x. The first rule moves the position forward by the current velocity times the time step. The second changes the velocity by the position times the time step, with a minus sign.
  - The key detail is that the velocity update uses the **new x**, the one just calculated in the same step, rather than the old one. This method is called **position-first symplectic Euler**, and the order of the updates matters.
- **Step 1 starts from x at 1 and v at 0.** The new x is 1 plus 0.5 times 0, which is 1.000. Then v uses the new x, so v is 0 minus 0.5 times 1.000, which is minus 0.500. The position has not moved yet, because the mass started at rest. But the spring has begun pushing the velocity in the negative direction.
  - In step 2, x uses the velocity from step 1, so x is 1.000 plus 0.5 times minus 0.500, which is 0.750. Then v is minus 0.500 minus 0.5 times 0.750, which is minus 0.875. The mass has started moving back toward zero and is speeding up in the negative direction.
  - In step 3, x is 0.750 plus 0.5 times minus 0.875, which is 0.3125, shown as 0.313, and v is minus 0.875 minus 0.5 times 0.3125, which is about minus 1.031. Values are displayed rounded to three decimals, but each later step uses the full-precision numbers.
- **After 12 steps the position runs 1, 1.000, 0.750, 0.313, minus 0.203, minus 0.668, minus 0.966, minus 1.022, minus 0.823, minus 0.418, 0.091, 0.578 and 0.920, and the velocity runs 0, minus 0.500, minus 0.875, minus 1.031, minus 0.930, minus 0.596, minus 0.113, 0.398, 0.810, 1.019, 0.973, 0.684 and 0.224.**
  - Plotted against time, x starts at 1, falls through zero between the third and fourth steps, reaches its lowest value of minus 1.022 at the seventh step, and climbs back through zero to 0.920 by the twelfth step, one nearly complete swing. The velocity drops to its most negative value, minus 1.031, at the third step, right around when the mass passes through zero, rises through zero between the sixth and seventh steps, right around when the mass reaches its farthest point, and peaks at 1.019 at the ninth step.
  - The mass moves fastest through the natural position and slowest at its farthest point. This matches the mass carrying its motion through every crossing. The size of the swing stays close to 1 throughout, so the oscillation does not grow or shrink noticeably.
- **These values are an approximation.** The values are not exact samples of a perfect oscillation but an approximate (close but not exact) oscillator produced by stepping forward in chunks of 0.5. The method is also different from updating both x and v at the same time from the old values, which gives different numbers.
  - In Python the 12 steps are a short loop. The lists x and v start with 1.0 and 0.0. Each time through the loop, append adds a new value to the end of a list. In the first line inside the loop, x[-1] is the old position and v[-1] the old velocity, so the new position is the old position plus dt times the old velocity. By the time the second line runs, the new position has been appended, so x[-1] now refers to the **new x**, which is exactly how the velocity update uses the new position.

## p10
- **Explicit Euler, also called forward Euler, updates both x and v from the previous pair.** Both new values are calculated from the previous position and the previous velocity, and the newly calculated x is not used when updating v. The settings are k of 1, m of 1 and dt of 0.5, with starting values x[0] of 1 and v[0] of 0.
  - The new position x[n] equals the previous velocity v[n minus 1] times dt plus the previous position x[n minus 1]. The new velocity v[n] equals minus k over m times the **previous** position times dt plus the previous velocity. Both calculations use the previous pair.
- **The difference from the position-first method shows up right away in step 2.** Step 1 gives x[1] of 1 and v[1] of minus 0.500, the same as before. In step 2, x[2] is minus 0.500 times 0.5 plus 1, which is 0.750, but v[2] uses x[1] of 1, the previous position, and comes out to minus 1. In the position-first method, v2 used the new position 0.750 and came out to minus 0.875.
  - By step 3 the positions have separated too. Explicit Euler gives x[3] of minus 1 times 0.5 plus 0.750, which is 0.250, while the position-first method gave 0.313. Then v[3] is minus 0.750 times 0.5 plus minus 1, which is minus 1.375.
- **Steps 4 through 12 continue the same way, each using the previous velocity and previous position.** The positions run minus 0.438, minus 1.188, minus 1.828, minus 2.172, minus 2.059, minus 1.402, minus 0.231, 1.290 and 2.869. The velocities run minus 1.500, minus 1.281, minus 0.688, 0.227, 1.313, 2.342, 3.043, 3.159 and 2.514. Displayed values are rounded to three decimals, while every update is carried out with full precision.
- **With this time step, explicit Euler makes the oscillation grow.** The position still swings down and back up. So the method still produces an oscillation. But the swings keep getting bigger. The position reaches minus 2.172 at step 7 and 2.869 at step 12, far beyond the starting value of 1, and the velocity reaches 3.159 at step 11.
  - A real mass on a spring cannot swing farther than where it started unless something adds energy to it. So this growth is **numerical error** (error introduced by the stepping arithmetic), not a real physical increase in energy. It comes from the method itself when dt is 0.5.
  - The position-first method, with the same time step, kept the swing near a size of 1. The only difference between the two methods is whether the velocity update uses the new position or the previous one. That is why the order of the updates matters so much.
- **In Python, explicit Euler is a loop with a counter n from 1 to 12.** Inside the loop, x[n-1] and v[n-1] always refer to the previous pair, so even after the new position is appended, the velocity update still uses the old position. Each line is a direct copy of one of the two explicit Euler rules.

## p11
- **Euler's explicit method solves differential equations using arithmetic.** A **differential equation** is an equation about rates of change, like ẋ equals v and v̇ equals minus k over m times x. Euler's method turns each into an update where the new value equals the previous value plus a rate multiplied by a small time step.
  - Every update is just multiplication and addition. Repeating these simple arithmetic steps many times follows how a system like a mass on a spring moves through time, without any other kind of mathematics. The output is close but not exact, so the chosen time step and update procedure affect the result. The method is named for **Leonhard Euler**.
- **The same stepping procedure is done in Python.** Computers are built to repeat arithmetic quickly and exactly, so a loop can carry out the Euler updates for as many steps as wanted. Each pass computes one new position and one new velocity and appends them to lists.
- **Software 1.0 is software written as explicit instructions**, programs where people write out every instruction the computer must follow, step by step. The Euler loops are an example, since every arithmetic operation is spelled out by the programmer.
