## p2
- Every vibrating system in speech follows a small set of principles, and the principles are consistent with each other.
  - Mass and stiffness: **Increasing the mass lowers the frequency, and increasing the stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - Number of modes: **A system with n masses has n modes**, and each higher mode has a higher frequency. In higher modes, more parts move against each other, so more springs are stretched and compressed.
  - Location of a change: **Adding mass at a location lowers the frequency more when that location moves more.** **Adding stiffness at a location raises the frequency more when that location is stretched and compressed more.**
  - Motion and stretching: **Where movement is greatest, there is no stretching, and where stretching is greatest, there is no movement.**
  - Harmonics and formants: In a **fixed-fixed system** (fixed at both ends), the frequencies are called **harmonics**, and they are **whole-number multiples** of the lowest frequency. In a **fixed-free system** (fixed at one end and free at the other), the frequencies are called **formants**, and they are **odd multiples**.
- It might seem that raising both the mass and the stiffness of a system would make the two effects **cancel out**. That is true only for a **single mass on a single spring**. In a system with many masses and springs, a location that moves a lot is hardly stretched, and a location that is stretched a lot hardly moves, so only one of the two changes matters at any one location.
  - Picture a spot on a string that never moves but is strongly stretched. Added mass there has no effect, but added stiffness makes the string pull back harder, so the frequency goes up.
  - Picture a spot that moves up and down the most. Added stiffness there has no effect, but added mass makes that spot harder to move, so the frequency goes down.

## p3
- Adding masses adds modes, and the same mode shapes keep repeating.
  - Mode 1: Everything moves together.
  - Mode 2: The system splits into two halves that move against each other.
  - Mode 3: The middle moves against the sides.
  - The more the parts move against each other, the more the springs are stretched and compressed, so **higher modes are stiffer and vibrate faster**. A higher mode does not use stiffer springs. It makes the same springs stretch more.

## p4
- In a string fixed at both ends, the first mode is **one half-wave**, a single bump that goes from no motion at one end, to maximum motion in the middle, to no motion at the other end. Each higher mode fits **more of these half-wave pieces** into the same length.
  - Mode 2: The mode **contains two pieces**, with one half moving up while the other half moves down. Each piece behaves like a copy of Mode 1, so Mode 2 has **twice the stiffness** and **twice the frequency**.
  - Mode 3 and Mode 5: Mode 3 **contains three pieces**, and Mode 5 **contains five**, so the fifth mode has five times the frequency of the first.
  - Exam answer: **The fifth mode contains five copies of the first mode**, so it has five times the stiffness and five times the frequency.
- The numbers do not have to start at 100. A voice whose lowest frequency is **250 hertz** has its next harmonics at 500, 750 and 1000 hertz, because each harmonic is still a whole number of copies of the first.

## p5
- A string fixed at both ends vibrates in all of these modes at once. Its **spectrum** has **evenly spaced spikes**, one for each harmonic. **Frequency** is on the horizontal axis, and **amplitude** (strength) is on the vertical axis. If the first harmonic is 100 hertz, the next ones are 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz.
  - The **vocal folds** are fixed on all sides, just behind the Adam's apple, so they behave like a fixed-fixed system and produce harmonics.

## p6
- The vocal folds form a **membrane** (a thin sheet of tissue), and the same counting of pieces works on a surface.
  - First mode: A single bump spans the whole membrane.
  - Second mode: Two bumps fit across the membrane, so the frequency is twice as high.
  - Fourth mode: Four ups and downs fit across the membrane, so the frequency is four times as high.

## p7
- A **building** is **fixed at the base** and **free at the top**. In strong winds or an earthquake, engineers want it to sway only in **Mode 1**, with every floor moving together. That motion can feel unpleasant on a high floor, but it is safe. In a higher mode, some floors would move one way while other floors moved the other way, and the building could break apart.
- A string can be set up the same way, fixed at the bottom and free at the top. Its first mode is no longer a full half-wave. It goes from **no motion at the fixed end** to **maximum motion at the free end**, which is only **one quarter of a wave**.

## p8
- In a fixed-free system, each mode is built from **quarter-wave packets** (pieces that are each a quarter of a wave long). Each packet runs from no motion to maximum motion.
  - Mode 1: The mode contains one packet.
  - Mode 2: The mode contains three packets.
  - Mode 3: The mode contains five packets.
  - Mode 4: The mode contains seven packets. With a lowest mode of 100 hertz, the modes are therefore at 100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz.
- **Two packets can never fit.** Two quarter-wave packets would go from no motion, to maximum motion, to no motion again. That would place a point of no motion at the top as well as the bottom, which would make the system **fixed at both ends**. Adding one more packet, for **three** in total, ends with maximum motion at the top again, so three is the next possible mode.
  - In short, the free end must always be moving, and only an odd number of quarter-wave packets ends with motion at the free end.
- The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called **formants**. **A formant is a frequency at which a system vibrates when it is fixed at one end and completely free at the other.** Formants are **odd multiples** of the lowest one, and they are written **F1**, **F2** and **F3**.

## p9
- Counting quarter-wave packets gives every formant from the first one.
  - F1: The first formant is **500 hertz**.
  - F2: The second formant is **three times F1, which is 1500 hertz**.
  - F3: The third formant is **five times F1, which is 2500 hertz**.
  - Each step from one formant to the next is twice the first formant, because each new mode adds two more quarter-wave packets.

## p10
- The air inside the mouth and throat forms a tube with two different ends.
  - Glottis end: The **vocal folds** are closed, so the air there **cannot move back and forth**, and that end is **fixed**.
  - Lip end: The **lips are open**, so the air can move back and forth freely, and that end is **free**.
  - Comparison: **The air in the vocal tract vibrates the same way a building sways.** The mathematics for both is almost identical, and the frequencies of both are **formants**.
  - Speaking changes the stiffness and mass of the air at different locations in the tract. That moves the formants, which is how listeners hear different vowels and consonants.

## p11
- The air in the tube can be modeled as lines of particles, where each particle represents billions of air molecules.
  - Spring behavior: Where the lines **crowd together and then spread far apart**, the air is compressing and expanding, so it is acting like a **spring**.
  - Mass behavior: Where the lines **move back and forth** without changing their spacing, the air is acting like a **mass**. This is easiest to see at the **free end, at the lips**.
  - Crowding and spreading indicate springiness, and sliding without crowding indicates mass.
- The modes of the air match the modes of a building.
  - Mode 1: The only point of no motion is the fixed end.
  - Mode 2: There are **two** points of no motion.
  - Mode 3: There are **three** points of no motion. Around each of these points, the air is strongly compressed and expanded.
- In **Mode 4** of a building, **the springiest locations are the fixed base and the three points where the building crosses its resting position**, four locations in all. These locations are the springiest because they do not move.
  - Adding stiffness where the building moves the most **changes nothing**, because the building is not acting like a spring there. Adding stiffness where the building does not move makes the frequency **rise sharply**.

## p12
- A **constriction** (narrowing) is a location where the tongue or lips narrow the vocal tract. Its position is measured from the **glottis**, the space between the vocal folds at the fixed end.
  - Done: There is **no significant constriction**, so this is the central vowel.
  - Dean: The constriction occurs under the roof of the mouth, about **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**, producing a **front high vowel**.
  - Don: The constriction occurs far back in the throat, about **one-third of the way from the glottis**, producing a **low back vowel**.
  - Dune: The constriction occurs **at the lips** because of **lip rounding**, producing a **back high vowel**.
  - Dan: The tongue is positioned low and toward the front of the mouth, producing a **front low vowel**.

## p13
- A constriction changes the air in two ways at once.
  - Stiffness: **Compressing air raises its stiffness**, just as compressing the air in a tire makes it stiffer. A constriction where the air is strongly compressed therefore **raises the frequency**.
  - Mass: **Packing air together raises its mass**, because it raises the air's **density**. A constriction where the air moves a lot therefore **lowers the frequency**.
- Every constriction **adds both mass and stiffness**, but the effects do not cancel out. **Along a vibrating string or a vibrating column of air, the locations that move the most alternate with the locations that are compressed and expanded the most.**
  - Any constriction question can be answered in two steps. First, determine whether the air at that location is mostly moving or mostly being compressed in the mode in question. Second, decide whether the added mass or the added stiffness matters there.
- The same two steps answer a question about a string fixed at both ends. Consider a small spot where **Mode 3 crosses its middle line** and **Mode 4 is at the top of a bump**, and add both mass and stiffness to that spot.
  - Mode 3: The string does not move at that spot but is stretched the most, so only the added stiffness matters, and **Mode 3's frequency goes up**.
  - Mode 4: The string moves the most at that spot and is hardly stretched, so only the added mass matters, and **Mode 4's frequency goes down**.

## p14
- Before reasoning about any vowel, it helps to know where the air moves and where it is compressed in the first two modes.
  - Mode 1: The air near the closed glottis end is strongly compressed and expanded, while the air near the open lip end mostly moves back and forth.
  - Mode 2: The air is strongly compressed at the closed end and again about two-thirds of the way along. In between, about one-third of the way along, the air mostly moves.
  - Rule for the ends: **In every mode, the air at the closed end is always springy, and the air at the open end is always moving.** Air can be compressed against a closed end, but at an open end there is nothing to compress it against.

## p15
- The mass-spring chain marks the three vowel locations along the tube.
  - /a/: The constriction is **one-third of the way from the glottis**.
  - /i/: The constriction is **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - /u/: The constriction is at the **lip end**.
  - Each vowel adds mass and stiffness at its own location, and its effect on F1 and F2 depends on whether the air there is moving or being compressed in each mode.
- Adding mass and stiffness at the **very top of a building** lowers the frequency of **every mode**, including Mode 4. The top is the free end, so in every mode it moves the most and is hardly stretched, and only the added mass matters there.
  - **The top of the building corresponds to the lip end of the vocal tract.** This is why lip rounding lowers every formant.

## p16
- For **/u/**, **lip rounding** creates a constriction at the **free end**. In both Mode 1 and Mode 2, the air at the free end moves a lot and is hardly compressed. The added **mass** therefore matters in both modes, and **F1 and F2 both go down**.
  - A speaker moved from the central vowel into /u/, and the recording showed **both formants dropping**, as predicted.

## p17
- For **/i/**, the constriction is about **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - Mode 1: The air at that location moves a lot, so the added mass matters and **F1 goes down**.
  - Mode 2: The air at that location is strongly compressed, so the added stiffness matters and **F2 goes up**.
  - Measurement: A speaker moved from the central vowel into /i/. The **first formant dropped sharply**, and the **second formant rose**.

## p18
- For **/a/**, the constriction is about **one-third of the way from the glottis**, which reverses the /i/ pattern.
  - Mode 1: That location is near the fixed end, where the air is strongly compressed, so the added stiffness matters and **F1 goes up**.
  - Mode 2: The air at that location moves a lot, so the added mass matters and **F2 goes down**.
- The reasoning also works in reverse.
  - Large rise: **If a constriction raises a formant a lot, the air at that location must be very springy and hardly moving in that mode.**
  - Large fall: **If a constriction lowers a formant a lot, the air at that location must be moving a lot.**

## p19
- A **schwa** is produced with no constriction. A measured schwa had formants near **500, 1500 and 2500 hertz**, which are the odd multiples that a uniform fixed-free tube predicts. Every other vowel moves the formants away from these values by changing where the air moves and where it is compressed.

## p20
- Speech uses **two vibrating systems at the same time**.
  - Source: The **vocal folds** are a fixed-fixed system, so they produce **harmonics**. The lowest harmonic is called the **base frequency**, or **F0**. In this example, the harmonics are **100 hertz apart**.
  - Filter: The **air in the vocal tract** is a fixed-free system, so its frequencies are the **formants**. For the schwa, the formants are near **500, 1500, 2500 and 3500 hertz**.
- **The formants determine how tall the harmonics are.** All the harmonics are still present, but **harmonics near a formant are tall, and harmonics far from any formant are short**. The outline of their heights is called the **envelope**, and it rises to a peak at each formant.
  - **The harmonics stay the same from vowel to vowel. Only their heights change, and the envelope is what makes each vowel sound different.**
  - In other words, the vocal folds determine which frequencies are present, and the shape of the vocal tract determines which of those frequencies are loud.

## p21
- For **/i/**, the filter keeps **F1 low and F2 high**. The tall harmonics are grouped at the very bottom of the spectrum, a long stretch of short harmonics follows, and the next tall group appears much higher up. The two lowest formants are therefore **far apart**.
  - The constriction two-thirds of the way from the glottis produces this pattern, because it lowers F1 and raises F2.

## p22
- For **/a/**, the filter **raises F1 and lowers F2**, so the first two groups of tall harmonics are **close together** in the lower part of the spectrum.
  - The same harmonics from the vocal folds are present under both /i/ and /a/, and only the filter differs. Moving the constriction moves the formants, and that changes which harmonics are loud.

## p23
- **A spectrum describes one instant**, but speech is **time-varying** (it changes over time), because the vocal tract keeps moving and constrictions keep forming and releasing. **A spectrogram places many spectra side by side over time.**
  - Time: Time is on the **horizontal axis**.
  - Frequency: Frequency, which was on the horizontal axis of each spectrum, is now on the **vertical axis**.
  - Amplitude: Amplitude, which was the height of each spike, is now shown as **darkness**. The louder a frequency is, the darker it appears.
- The formants appear as **dark bands** that move as the speaker moves. In **/ia/**, the two lowest bands start **far apart** during /i/. As the speaker moves into /a/, **F1 rises and F2 falls**, so the bands **come close together**.
  - Reading a spectrogram means following the dark bands, because those bands are the formants changing over time.

## p24
- **Where were you a year ago?** is a useful sentence for seeing the source and the filter working together over time.
  - Source: The vocal folds keep producing harmonics throughout the sentence.
  - Filter: The vocal tract keeps changing shape, so the envelope over the harmonics changes from moment to moment.
  - Spectrogram: These changes appear as **dark bands that rise and fall**, because the dark bands are the formants and darkness shows amplitude.
  - /i/-like vowel: The two lowest bands spread far apart.
  - you: All the bands drop, because lip rounding forms a constriction at the lips.
- **A spectrum shows one frozen instant, but a spectrogram shows how the formants move**, and real speech is always moving. Reading a sentence like this one means following each band and asking which constriction moved it.

## p25
- Formants can only appear through the harmonics, so the spacing of the harmonics matters.
  - Close harmonics: With harmonics every **100 hertz** and formants at **300 and 2100 hertz**, **many harmonics** sit under each formant peak, so the formants are easy to see.
  - Wide harmonics: With a higher base frequency, such as **250 hertz**, fewer harmonics land on each formant peak, and a formant can fall **between two harmonics**. It then becomes **very hard to tell the harmonics apart from the formants**.
  - Examples: A **toddler's** harmonics can be at 400, 800 and 1200 hertz, and a **soprano's** can start at 800 hertz. That is why their vowels can be very hard to identify.
- **The formants form a smooth outline, and the harmonics are single points along that outline.** A higher F0 means fewer points, so the spectrogram no longer shows clean formant bands.
  - The harmonics work like dots used to trace a curve. With many dots, the curve is obvious, and with only a few dots, the curve is hard to see.
