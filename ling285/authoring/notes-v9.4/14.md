## p2
- Speech uses **two main vibrating systems**. The first is the **vocal folds**, a **membrane** (a thin sheet of tissue) that is **fixed on all sides** and made of many masses and springs. The vocal folds vibrate in many modes at once, and all of the modes add together into one combined motion.
  - Mode 1: All the masses move up and down together.
  - Mode 2: Half of the masses move up while the other half move down.
  - Higher modes: The patterns become more and more complicated.
  - The vocal folds are a fixed-fixed system, so their frequencies are **harmonics** (whole-number multiples of the lowest frequency). The **lowest harmonic** is called **F0**. Each person has a different F0, but the other harmonics are always multiples of it.

## p3
- The second system is the **air in the vocal tract**.
  - Glottis end: The air is **fixed** there, because the closed vocal folds prevent it from moving back and forth.
  - Lip end: The air is **free** there, because it can move in and out of the open lips.
  - Because one end is fixed and the other is free, only the **odd multiples** occur, so the frequencies are one, three, five and seven times the lowest one.

## p4
- Each fixed-free mode is built from **quarter-wavelength packets** (pieces that are each a quarter of a wave long). Each packet runs from no motion at one point to maximum motion at another.
  - Mode 1: The mode contains one packet.
  - Mode 2: The mode contains three packets.
  - Mode 3: The mode contains five packets.
  - Mode 4: The mode contains seven packets.
  - Two packets would end with a point of no motion at the free end, which cannot happen. Only odd numbers of packets fit, which is why the formants are odd multiples of the first.

## p5
- In the vocal tract, the air moves **longitudinally**, which means back and forth along the tube rather than up and down. Each formant is one mode of this back-and-forth motion. F1 is the lowest mode, and F2 is the next one.
  - These modes look like a building's modes turned on their side, with the fixed glottis end on the left and the free lip end on the right.

## p6
- **In every mode, the air at the closed end is always springy, and the air at the open end is always moving.** Air can be compressed against a closed end, but at an open end there is nothing to compress it against.
  - Closed end: The thin layers of air crowd together and then spread far apart, so the air **compresses and expands** and acts like a **spring**.
  - Open end: The layers stay the same distance apart and move back and forth together, so the air acts like a **mass**.
  - Comparison: The lip end swings like a heavy door, and the throat end compresses like a pillow.
- In higher modes, springy locations and moving locations **alternate** more and more times along the tube.

## p7
- The effect of a constriction can be worked out at any location, in any mode, with the same steps. Consider a location inside the tube where, in **Mode 3**, the layers of air crowd together and spread apart but hardly move back and forth. A constriction at that location raises **F3**.
  - Step 1: A constriction always **adds both mass and stiffness** to the air, just as compressing the air in a tire makes it stiffer.
  - Step 2: At this location in Mode 3, the air is acting like a **pure spring**. It is compressed and expanded but does not move back and forth, so the added mass does not matter.
  - Step 3: Only the added stiffness matters, and **increased stiffness raises the frequency**, so **F3 goes up**.
- The answer should not be memorized. It should be **built one step at a time**, with each step based on the one before it. Good arguments in science and in art are built the same way.

## p8
- Images taken while people speak show where constrictions (narrowings) occur in the vocal tract, which extends from the closed glottis to the open lips.
  - Done: There is no significant constriction in the vocal tract.
  - Dean: The constriction occurs near the front of the vocal tract, about two-thirds of the way from the glottis.
  - Don: The constriction occurs toward the back of the throat, about one-third of the way from the glottis.
  - Dune: The constriction occurs at the lips because of lip rounding.
  - Dan: The tongue is positioned low and toward the front of the mouth, producing a low front vowel.

## p9
- A constriction changes two properties of the air.
  - Mass: A constriction increases the air's **mass**, which is how hard the air is to start or stop moving.
  - Stiffness: A constriction increases the air's **stiffness**, which is how strongly the air pushes back when it is compressed.
  - In the mass-spring model, a constriction is drawn as **heavier masses and stiffer springs** at that location.
- A constriction always raises both, but moving locations and springy locations alternate along the tube, so **its effect depends on what the air is doing at that location in each mode**.
  - **When stiffness is what matters at a location, the frequency goes up. When mass is what matters, the frequency goes down.**

## p10
- The same location in the tube can behave differently in the two modes, so each mode has to be checked separately. A location can be a location of motion in Mode 1 and a location of compression in Mode 2. One constriction at that location then lowers F1 and raises F2 at the same time.
  - That is why a vowel is defined by its pair of formants, not by one formant alone.

## p11
- For **/u/**, **lip rounding** creates a constriction at the lips. At the open end, the air moves back and forth and is hardly compressed in **every** mode, so it acts like a pure mass. The added stiffness has no effect, but the added mass does, so **F1 and F2 both go down**.
  - The schwa's formants are about **500 and 1500 hertz**. A measured /u/ had an F1 in the low two hundreds, well below 500.

## p12
- For **/i/**, the constriction is about two-thirds of the way from the glottis.
  - Mode 1: The air at that location moves back and forth, so it acts like a mass. The added mass matters, and **F1 goes down**.
  - Mode 2: The air at that location is compressed and expanded, so it acts like a spring. The added stiffness matters, and **F2 goes up**.
  - Measurement: A speaker moving from the schwa into /i/ produced exactly this pattern, with **F1 going down and F2 going up**.

## p13
- For **/a/**, the constriction is about one-third of the way from the glottis.
  - Mode 1: That location is close to the closed end, so the air there is mostly springy. The added stiffness matters, and **F1 goes up**.
  - Mode 2: That location lies between two springy regions, so the air there is mostly moving. The added mass matters, and **F2 goes down**.
  - Measurement: A speaker producing /a/ showed **F1 going up and F2 going down**. In a typical /a/, F1 and F2 are fairly close together.

## p14
- The same reasoning applies to all three vowels. **First, determine whether the air at the constriction is mostly moving or mostly being compressed in each mode. Then decide whether the added mass or the added stiffness matters.**
  - /u/: The constriction is at the lips, where the air is moving in both modes, so both formants go down.
  - /i/: The constriction is two-thirds of the way along, where the air is moving in Mode 1 and springy in Mode 2, so F1 goes down and F2 goes up.
  - /a/: The constriction is one-third of the way along, where the air is springy in Mode 1 and moving in Mode 2, so F1 goes up and F2 goes down.

## p15
- The vocal folds are the **source**. Their mass and stiffness set **F0**, and F0 sets the spacing of the harmonics.
  - Large, heavy, loose vocal folds: They have high mass and low stiffness, so **F0 is low**, around 100 hertz, with harmonics at 200, 300 and so on.
  - Tighter, lighter vocal folds: F0 is higher, for example 250 hertz.
  - Very tight, light vocal folds: F0 is higher still, for example 350 hertz.
- **F0 does not stay constant while people talk.** Speakers keep changing F0 by tightening and loosening their vocal folds, and listeners use those changes to understand meaning.
  - Statement: When **The dog slept here** is said as a statement, F0 falls at the very end.
  - Question: When the same words are said as a question, F0 rises at the end.
  - The way F0 moves also tells listeners whether a speaker is being sarcastic, pretending, or even lying.

## p16
- The air in the vocal tract is the **filter**, and its formants depend on **where the constriction is**.
  - Schwa: F1 is near 500 hertz and F2 is near 1500 hertz.
  - /u/: Both formants are lower.
  - /i/: F1 and F2 are very far apart.
  - /a/: F1 and F2 are very close together.
- **The core rule is that a formant makes the harmonics closest to it louder, and harmonics far from any formant are weaker.**
  - **The vowel shape is the filter. The vocal folds supply the harmonics, and the filter determines which harmonics come through strongly.**

## p17
- **The lower F0 is, the more harmonics the formants can shape.** When the harmonics are 100 hertz apart, many harmonics sit under each formant, so the spectrum clearly shows the formant peaks.
- With very light, tight vocal folds, a formant at 500 hertz may have **no harmonic near it to strengthen**. With so few harmonics, a formant can fall between them and be missed, or its position can be hard to identify.
  - Opera singer: This is an extreme case, which is why sung vowels are hard to identify. Singers are even trained to shape the vocal tract so that the formants line up with the harmonics.
  - Small child: A small child has an F0 near 500 hertz, so very few harmonics fall near the formants. That is why only the people who care for the child understand the child's speech easily.
  - The formants are the same in every case, but a high voice gives the listener only a few harmonics through which to hear them.

## p18
- A **spectrogram** places many spectra side by side over time.
  - Time: Time is on the horizontal axis.
  - Frequency: Frequency is on the vertical axis.
  - Amplitude: **Darkness** shows how much energy, or amplitude, is present.
- F0 can be measured in **Praat** by selecting a short stretch of speech, viewing its spectrum, and identifying the lowest harmonic. One measured voice had an F0 of about 170 hertz, and the next harmonic was at about twice that value.

## p19
- The sentence **Where were you a year ago?** brings every idea about speech together. The vocal folds supply harmonics the whole time, and each new sound changes where the tongue and lips form a constriction, so the formants keep moving from one moment to the next.
  - Spectrogram: **Time** is on the horizontal axis, **frequency** is on the vertical axis, and the **dark bands are the formants**.
  - /i/-like vowel: **F2 rises high and F1 stays low**, because the constriction is at a location that is springy in Mode 2 and moving in Mode 1.
  - you: The formants **drop**, because lip rounding adds mass where the air moves the most, and that lowers all of them.
  - a year ago: F2 goes up and then down again as the tongue moves from one vowel to the next.
- **The formants are always changing during speech, so speech is not a sequence of frozen vowels.** Every rise and fall of a formant can be predicted with the same mass and spring reasoning, by identifying the location of the constriction and whether the air there is moving or being compressed in each mode.
  - This constant change is why a network that sees two formants from one frozen moment cannot understand real speech. Speech recognition requires systems that take in whole stretches of sound over time.
  - A spectrogram of a real sentence is a continuous record of the tongue and lips, and reading it means following the dark bands as they rise and fall.

## p20
- A recording that was cut and joined back together shows how complex speech perception is. The **m** portion of a recorded **ma** was joined to the **a** portion of a recorded **na**, and listeners clearly heard **na**, not ma.
  - **Whether listeners hear m or n depends more on the vowel than on the nasal sound itself.**
  - After m: One formant of the vowel stays fairly **flat**.
  - After n: That formant shows a **big drop**.
  - Listeners therefore decide which consonant they heard **during the vowel**, after the consonant has ended. This was one of the first striking results in speech perception, published in the **1950s** by the linguist **André Malécot**.
- Listeners **take in whole stretches of speech and then decide** about the sounds that came first. For about seventy years, speech recognition systems that treated each slice of sound separately **failed**. Modern speech recognition uses large language models, which use what comes later to decide about what came before.

## p21
- A **neural network** can be trained to recognize vowels from formants. Its **two inputs** are **F1 and F2**, measured from real recordings of five vowels, and its task is to decide which vowel was spoken.
  - **The network's knowledge is stored in its weights**, and the weights are learned from the data.
  - Information enters at the input, somewhat like sound reaching the ear. Each hidden layer turns the numbers it receives into a new set of numbers, somewhat like the stages of processing in the brain.

## p22
- The network has three parts.
  - Output layer: There are **five output neurons**, one for each word, **done, dune, dean, Don and Dan**.
  - Hidden layers: There are **three hidden layers** with **3, 2 and 4 neurons**.
  - Connections: Every neuron in one layer connects to every neuron in the next layer, so the network is **fully connected**.
  - This version is **purely linear**, which means it has **no activation functions**. Each neuron simply passes on the result of its inner product.

## p23
- Before training, **every weight is a random number between minus one and one**.
  - Positive weight: The input pushes the next neuron up.
  - Negative weight: The input pushes the next neuron down.
  - The example input is **F1 of 320 and F2 of 2000 hertz**. A very low F1 with a very high F2 is the pattern of **/i/**, so the correct answer is **dean**.

## p24
- **Forward propagation** carries the input through the network one layer at a time. Each neuron computes an **inner product**, which means it multiplies each incoming number by its weight and adds the results.
  - For the top neuron of the first hidden layer, F1 is multiplied by its weight, F2 is multiplied by its weight, and the two results are added. That sum is the neuron's activation.
  - The other neurons do the same with their own weights. The first hidden layer produces three numbers, which pass through the next weights to produce two numbers, then four, and then five at the output.
  - These calculations never have to be done by hand. What matters is that every step is an inner product.
- The output is a **probability distribution** over the five words, with values of **.5, .1, .1, .1 and .2**, which add up to one. With random weights, the network gives its highest probability to **done**, so it is wrong.
  - The correct answer is given as the target distribution **0, 0, 1, 0, 0**. **Stochastic gradient descent** then changes each weight so that next time the inner products give a high probability to dean.
  - The network starts out guessing, the target shows what the correct guess was, and learning adjusts every weight so the correct guess wins next time.

## p25
- **PyTorch** describes the network as a list of layers.
  - Sequential: The network has **one layer after another, in order**.
  - Linear: The layer **computes inner products** of the weights and the activations from the previous layer.
  - nn.Linear(2, 3): The layer **takes input from 2 neurons and has 3 neurons**. The next lines read 3 in and 2 out, then 2 in and 4 out, then 4 in and 5 out.
  - Bias set to False: Every layer stays purely linear, which matches the diagram of the network.
- **Only layers that perform calculations get a line of code.** The **input layer** has no incoming weights and computes no inner products, so it never gets its own line, and the first line describes the **first hidden layer**.
  - This network has **five layers in total** but only **three hidden layers**. The **last line is always the output layer**, so the question must be read carefully.
- To **add a hidden layer of 3 neurons right before the output**, the old last line, 4 in and 5 out, becomes **4 in and 3 out**, and a new last line takes **3 in and gives 5 out**.
  - Quick check: The first number on each line must match the second number on the line before it.
- Consider a network written as 4 in and 3 out, 3 in and 8 out, 8 in and 4 out, 4 in and 4 out, and 4 in and 5 out.
  - Input: The network takes **4 numbers as input**.
  - Hidden layers: It has **four hidden layers** with 3, 8, 4 and 4 neurons.
  - Output: It chooses among **5 categories**. When the first category is correct, the target distribution has a **1 in the first slot and 0 everywhere else**.
