## p2
- Every vibrating system in speech follows a small set of principles, and they all fit together.
  - **More mass lowers the frequency, and more stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - **A system with n masses has n modes**, and each higher mode has a higher frequency. This happens because more parts move against each other, so more springs stretch and compress.
  - **Adding mass at a spot lowers the frequency more when that spot moves more.** **Adding stiffness at a spot raises the frequency more when that spot stretches and compresses more.** Both depend on where the change is made.
  - **Where there is the most movement, there is no stretching. Where there is the most stretching, there is no movement.**
  - In a **fixed-fixed system**, which is fixed at both ends, the frequencies are called **harmonics**. They are **whole-number multiples** of the lowest frequency. In a **fixed-free system**, which is fixed at one end and free at the other, the frequencies are called **formants**, and they are **odd multiples**.
- If you raise both the mass and the stiffness of a system, you might think the two effects **cancel out**. That is only true for a **single mass on a single spring**. In a system with many masses and springs, a spot that moves a lot is hardly stretching, and a spot that stretches a lot is hardly moving. So at any one spot, only one of the two changes matters.
  - Picture a spot on a string that never moves but is stretched hard. Extra mass there does nothing, but extra stiffness makes the string pull back harder. So the frequency goes up.
  - Picture a spot that swings up and down the most. Extra stiffness there does nothing, but extra mass makes that spot harder to move. So the frequency goes down.

## p3
- Adding masses adds modes, and the same shapes keep repeating. In **Mode 1**, everything moves together. In **Mode 2**, the system splits into two halves that move against each other. In **Mode 3**, the middle moves against the sides.
  - The more the parts move against each other, the more the springs work. So **higher modes are stiffer and vibrate faster**. In plain terms, a higher mode does not use stiffer springs. It makes the same springs do more stretching.

## p4
- In a string fixed at both ends, the first mode is **one half-wave**. This is a single bump that goes from no motion at one end, to the most motion in the middle, to no motion at the other end. Each higher mode fits **more of these half-wave pieces** into the same length.
  - **Mode 2 holds two pieces**, with one half moving up while the other half moves down. Each piece acts like a copy of Mode 1, so Mode 2 has **twice the stiffness** and **twice the frequency**.
  - **Mode 3 holds three pieces**, and **Mode 5 holds five**. So the fifth mode has five times the frequency of the first.
  - The short exam answer is that **the fifth mode fits five copies of the first mode**, so it has five times the stiffness and five times the frequency.
- The numbers do not have to start at 100. If a voice's lowest frequency is **250 hertz**, its next harmonics are at 500, 750 and 1000 hertz, because each one is still a whole number of copies of the first.

## p5
- A string fixed at both ends vibrates in all of these modes at once. Its **spectrum** has **evenly spaced spikes**, one for each harmonic. **Frequency** goes along the bottom, and **amplitude**, which is strength, goes up. If the first harmonic is 100 hertz, the next ones are 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz.
  - The **vocal folds** are fixed on all sides, just behind the Adam's apple. So they behave like this kind of fixed-fixed system and produce harmonics.

## p6
- The vocal folds form a **membrane**, and the same counting of pieces works on a surface. The first mode is a single bump across the whole membrane. The second mode fits two of those bumps, so it has twice the frequency. The fourth mode fits four ups and downs, so it has four times the frequency.

## p7
- A **building** is **fixed at the bottom** and **free at the top**. In strong winds or an earthquake, engineers want it to sway only in **Mode 1**, with every floor moving together. That can feel a little unpleasant on a high floor, but it is safe. In a higher mode, some floors would move one way while other floors moved the other way, and the whole building could break apart.
- A string can be set up the same way, fixed at the bottom and free at the top. Its first mode is no longer a full half-wave. It goes from **no motion at the fixed end** to **lots of motion at the free end**, which is only **one quarter of a wave**.

## p8
- In a fixed-free system, each mode is built from **quarter-wave packets**, which are pieces that are each a quarter of a wave long. Each piece runs from no motion to lots of motion. **Mode 1 holds one piece, Mode 2 holds three, Mode 3 holds five and Mode 4 holds seven.** So if the lowest mode is 100 hertz, the modes are at 100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz.
- **Two pieces can never fit.** Two quarter-wave pieces would go from no motion, to lots of motion, to no motion again. That would put a still point at the top as well as the bottom, which would make the system **fixed at both ends**. Adding one more piece, for **three** in total, ends with lots of motion at the top again, so three is the next possible mode.
  - The simple version is that the free end must always be moving, and only an odd number of quarter-wave pieces ends with motion at the free end.
- The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called **formants**. **A formant is a frequency at which a system vibrates when it is fixed at one end and completely free at the other.** Formants are **odd multiples** of the lowest one, and they are written **F1**, **F2** and **F3**.

## p9
- If you count quarter-wave pieces, you can get every formant from the first one. If **F1 is 500 hertz**, then **F2 is three times that, 1500 hertz**, and **F3 is five times that, 2500 hertz**.
  - Each step from one formant to the next is twice the first formant, because each new mode adds two more quarter-wave pieces.

## p10
- The air inside the mouth and throat forms a tube. At one end are the **vocal folds**. They are closed, so the air there **cannot move back and forth**, and that end is **fixed**. At the other end the **lips are open**, so the air can move back and forth freely, and that end is **free**.
  - So **the air in the vocal tract moves the same way a building sways**. The math for both is almost exactly the same, and the frequencies of both are **formants**.
  - When we speak, we change the stiffness and mass of the air in different parts of the tract. That moves the formants, and that is how listeners hear different vowels and consonants.

## p11
- The air in the tube can be modeled as lines of particles, where each particle stands for billions of air molecules.
  - Where the lines come **very close together and then spread far apart**, the air is compressing and stretching, so it is acting like a **spring**.
  - Where the lines just **move back and forth** without changing their spacing, the air is acting like a **mass**. This is easiest to see at the **free end, at the lips**.
  - An easy way to read the model is that bunching and spreading means springiness, and sliding without bunching means mass.
- The air's modes match a building's modes. In **Mode 1**, the only point of no motion is the fixed end. **Mode 2** has **two** points of no motion, and **Mode 3** has **three**. Around each of these points, the air compresses and expands a lot.
- In **Mode 4** of a building, **the springiest places are the fixed bottom and the three points where the building crosses its resting position**, which makes four places in all. These are the springiest places because they do not move.
  - If you add stiffness where the building moves the most, **nothing changes**, because the building is not acting like a spring there. If you add stiffness where the building does not move, the frequency **goes way up**.

## p12
- A **constriction** is a place where the tongue or lips squeeze the air. Its location is measured from the **glottis**, which is the tiny space between the vocal folds at the fixed end.
  - In **done**, the central vowel, there is **no constriction**.
  - In **dean**, a **front high vowel**, the air is squeezed under the roof of the mouth, about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **Don**, a **low back vowel**, the air is squeezed far back, about **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **dune**, a **back high vowel**, the lips are rounded close together, which squeezes the air **at the lips**. **Dan** is a **front low vowel**.

## p13
- **Compressing air raises its stiffness**, just as squeezing a tire makes it stiffer. So a constriction where the air is compressed a lot **raises the frequency**. **Packing air together raises its mass**, because it raises the air's **density**. So a constriction where the air moves a lot **lowers the frequency**.
- So every constriction **adds both mass and stiffness**, but that does not mean the effects cancel out. **Along a vibrating string or a vibrating column of air, the spots that move the most take turns with the spots that compress and expand the most.**
  - To answer any constriction question, ask two things in order. First, is the air at this spot mostly moving or mostly compressing in this mode? Second, does the extra mass or the extra stiffness count there?
- The same two steps answer a question about a string fixed at both ends. Take a small spot where **Mode 3 crosses its middle line** and **Mode 4 is at the top of a bump**. Now add both mass and stiffness to that spot.
  - In **Mode 3**, the string does not move at that spot but is stretched the most. So only the extra stiffness counts, and **Mode 3's frequency goes up**.
  - In **Mode 4**, the string moves the most at that spot and hardly stretches. So only the extra mass counts, and **Mode 4's frequency goes down**.

## p14
- Before reasoning about any vowel, it helps to know where the air moves and where it compresses in the first two modes.
  - In **Mode 1**, the air near the closed glottis end compresses and expands a lot. The air near the open lip end just moves back and forth.
  - In **Mode 2**, the air compresses a lot at the closed end and again about two thirds of the way along. In between, about one third of the way along, the air mostly moves.
  - **In every mode, the air at the closed end is always springy, and the air at the open end is always moving.** Air can be squeezed against a closed end, but at an open end there is nothing to squeeze it against.

## p15
- The mass-spring chain marks the three vowel locations along the tube. **/a/** is **one third of the way from the glottis**, **/i/** is **two thirds of the way**, and **/u/** is at the **lip end**. Each vowel adds mass and stiffness at its own spot. Its effect on F1 and F2 depends on whether the air there is moving or compressing in each mode.
- If you add mass and stiffness at the **very top of a building**, the frequency of **every mode goes down**, including Mode 4. The top is the free end, so in every mode it moves the most and is almost never stretched. So only the extra mass counts there.
  - **The top of the building corresponds to the lip end of the vocal tract.** That is why rounding the lips lowers every formant.

## p16
- **/u/** rounds the lips, which squeezes the air at the **free end**. In both Mode 1 and Mode 2, the air at the free end moves a lot and is hardly compressed. So the extra **mass** counts in both modes, and **F1 and F2 both go down**.
  - A speaker moved from the central vowel into /u/, and the recording showed **both formants dropping**, just as predicted.

## p17
- **/i/** squeezes the air about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **Mode 1**, the air at that spot moves a lot, so the extra mass counts and **F1 goes down**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the air at that spot is compressed a lot, so the extra stiffness counts and **F2 goes up**.
  - A speaker moved from the central vowel into /i/. The **first formant dropped a lot**, and the **second formant went up**.

## p18
- **/a/** squeezes the air about **one third of the way from the glottis**, which reverses the /i/ pattern.
  - In **Mode 1**, that spot is near the fixed end, and the air there is compressed a lot. So the extra stiffness counts and **F1 goes up**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the air at that spot moves a lot. So the extra mass counts and **F2 goes down**.
- You can also reason backward. **If a squeeze raises a formant a lot, the air at that spot must be very springy and hardly moving in that mode.** **If a squeeze lowers a formant a lot, the air at that spot must be moving a lot.**

## p19
- A **schwa** has no constriction. A measured schwa had formants near **500, 1500 and 2500 hertz**, which are the odd multiples that a plain fixed-free tube predicts. Every other vowel moves the formants away from these schwa values, by changing where the air moves and where it compresses.

## p20
- Speech uses **two vibrating systems at the same time**.
  - The **vocal folds** are a fixed-fixed system. They are the **source**, and they produce **harmonics**. The lowest harmonic is called the **base frequency**, or **F0**. In this example, the harmonics are **100 hertz apart**.
  - The **air in the vocal tract** is a fixed-free system. It acts as a **filter**, and its frequencies are the **formants**. For the schwa, the formants are near **500, 1500, 2500 and 3500 hertz**.
- **The formants control how tall the harmonics are.** All the harmonics are still there. But **harmonics near a formant are tall, and harmonics far from any formant are short**. The outline of their heights is called the **envelope**, and it rises into a peak at each formant.
  - **The harmonics stay the same from vowel to vowel. Only their heights change, and the envelope is what makes each vowel sound different.**
  - In plain terms, the vocal folds decide which frequencies are present, and the shape of the mouth decides which of those frequencies are loud.

## p21
- For **/i/**, the filter keeps **F1 low and F2 high**. The tall harmonics are bunched at the very bottom of the spectrum. Then comes a long stretch of short harmonics, and the next tall group is much higher up. So the two lowest formants are **far apart**.
  - The squeeze two thirds of the way from the glottis puts the formants there, because it lowers F1 and raises F2.

## p22
- For **/a/**, the filter **raises F1 and lowers F2**. So the first two groups of tall harmonics are **close together** in the lower part of the spectrum.
  - The same harmonics from the vocal folds are under both /i/ and /a/. Only the filter is different. Moving the squeeze moves the formants, and that changes which harmonics are loud.

## p23
- **A spectrum shows one instant**, but speech is **time-varying**, which means it changes over time, because the vocal tract keeps moving and squeezes keep forming and opening up. **A spectrogram lines up many spectra side by side over time.**
  - **Time** goes along the **horizontal axis**.
  - **Frequency**, which was on the horizontal axis of each spectrum, moves to the **vertical axis**.
  - **Amplitude**, which was the height of each spike, becomes **darkness**. The louder a frequency is, the darker it looks.
- The formants show up as **dark bands** that move as the speaker moves. In **/ia/**, the two lowest bands start **far apart** during /i/. As the speaker moves into /a/, **F1 rises and F2 falls**, so the bands **come close together**.
  - Reading a spectrogram means following the dark bands, because those bands are the formants changing over time.

## p24
- **Where were you a year ago?** is a good sentence for seeing the source and the filter working together over time. The vocal folds, which are the source, keep producing harmonics. Meanwhile the vocal tract, which is the filter, keeps changing shape. So the envelope over the harmonics changes from moment to moment.
  - On a spectrogram, these changes show up as **dark bands that rise and fall**, because the dark bands are the formants and darkness shows amplitude.
  - Where the vowel sounds like **/i/**, the two lowest bands spread far apart. During **you**, all the bands drop, because the lips are rounded.
- **A spectrum shows one frozen instant, but a spectrogram shows how the formants move**, and real speech is always moving. To read a sentence like this one, follow each band and ask which squeeze moved it.

## p25
- Formants can only show up through the harmonics, so the spacing of the harmonics matters. Suppose the harmonics are every **100 hertz** and the formants are at **300 and 2100 hertz**. Then **many harmonics** sit under each formant peak, so the formants are easy to see.
- With a higher base frequency, such as **250 hertz**, fewer harmonics land on each formant peak. A formant can even fall **between two harmonics**. Then **it becomes very hard to tell the harmonics apart from the formants**.
  - A **toddler's** harmonics can be at 400, 800 and 1200 hertz, and a **soprano's** can start at 800 hertz. That is why their vowels can be very hard to identify.
- **The formants make a smooth outline, and the harmonics are single points along that outline.** A higher F0 means fewer points, so the spectrogram stops looking like clean formant bands.
  - A simple way to see it is that the harmonics are like dots used to trace a curve. With many dots, the curve is obvious. With only a few dots, the curve is hard to see.
