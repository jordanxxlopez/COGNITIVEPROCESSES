## p2
- The **2026 Nobel Prize in economics** went to **Robert Hall** and **Michael Woodford** for the **microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization**. **Stabilization** means bringing a system back to its equilibrium and keeping it there. This is the same job that **balancing feedback** does, and the same thing a spring does when it pulls a mass back to rest.
  - In their model of the economy, **unemployment and inflation take turns rising and falling**, like the position and the velocity of a mass on a spring.
  - The goal of stabilization policy is to **make these ups and downs smaller**, so the economy avoids very high unemployment and runaway inflation.
- Woodford's theory is called **Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium**, or **DSGE**.
  - **Dynamic** means the economy changes over time according to rules.
  - **Stochastic** means the model can handle **random shocks**, such as a war starting or a big company's CEO being caught stealing money. This is similar to stochastic gradient descent. One unusual data point can push the estimate far off, but later data pull it back toward the average.
  - **Equilibrium** means the goal is a steady state without wild jumps.
- **Macroeconomic** quantities describe a whole economy, such as inflation or the number of people without jobs. **Microeconomic** events are single choices, such as one person buying something. Explaining big patterns from many tiny events is the same kind of problem as speech. In speech, many tiny vibrations in the mouth add up to words that a listener understands without noticing any single vibration.

## p3
- A mass-spring system vibrates because **the spring wants to return to equilibrium** while **a moving mass wants to keep moving**. The vibration can happen **longitudinally**, which is left and right along the spring, or **transversely**, which is up and down between two walls. Both kinds of motion behave the same way.
  - It helps to understand both. The vocal folds are easiest to understand with up-and-down motion, and the air in the vocal tract is easiest to understand with back-and-forth motion.
- **A part only changes the frequency while it is doing its job.** A mass changes the frequency only when it moves. A spring changes the frequency only when it stretches or compresses. This one idea explains every result about modes.

## p4
- **More mass lowers the frequency**, so the system goes back and forth fewer times per second. **More stiffness raises the frequency**, so the system goes back and forth more times per second.
  - A heavier mass is harder to turn around, and a stiffer spring pulls back harder. That is why the two changes push the frequency in opposite directions.

## p5
- A system with **two masses** has **two modes**. In **Mode 1**, the masses move together. In **Mode 2**, they move against each other. The system can also do both at once.
  - **Mode 1 is slower**, because the middle spring just rides along without stretching. So in practice, Mode 1 uses **only two springs**.
  - **Mode 2 is faster**, because all **three springs** stretch and compress. More working springs means **more stiffness**, and more stiffness means a **higher frequency**.
- Suppose you make the **middle spring stiffer** while the system vibrates. **Mode 1 does not change**, because the middle spring never stretches in Mode 1. **Mode 2 goes up**, because in Mode 2 the middle spring is always stretching and compressing.
  - In plain terms, a spring only matters in a mode where it is being stretched.
- The system can also vibrate in **both modes at once**. The masses rise and fall together while also moving against each other, which is how real systems usually move.

## p6
- A system with **three masses** has **three modes**. In Mode 1, all three move together. In Mode 2, the middle mass stays still while the outer two move against each other. In Mode 3, the middle mass moves against both outer masses.
- If you make **Mass 2**, the middle mass, heavier, the frequency of **Mode 1 and Mode 3 goes down**, because the middle mass moves in both of those modes. The same change has **no effect on Mode 2**, because in Mode 2 the middle mass does not move at all.
  - **A mass affects the frequency only through its movement.** If it does not move, it has no effect. If it moves a little, it has a small effect. If it moves a lot, it has a large effect.

## p7
- **Every mass you add creates one more mode**, and the same patterns repeat as the chain gets longer.
  - **Mode 1** is always everything moving up and down together.
  - **Mode 2** always splits the system into **two parts** that move against each other. With an odd number of masses, the middle mass stays still.
  - **Mode 3** always splits the system into **three parts**, with the middle moving one way and the sides moving the other way.
- If you keep adding masses and springs, you get a **string**. **Every tiny piece of a string has weight, which is its mass. Every tiny piece also pulls back when it is stretched or compressed, which is its springiness.**
  - In musical instruments, we hear these frequencies as **notes**. The ear can pick out many instruments at once because the ear itself is built from masses and springs.
  - Higher modes have more parts moving against each other. So the springs stretch and compress much more, and those modes vibrate faster.
  - The string has the same mode patterns. Everything moves together in Mode 1, the two halves move against each other in Mode 2, and the middle moves against the sides in Mode 3.

## p8
- Take a string fixed at both ends whose first mode vibrates at **100 hertz**. Its next modes vibrate at **200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz**, so every mode is an **exact multiple** of the first. These multiples are called **harmonics**.
  - The second mode holds **two** copies of the first mode's bump, the third holds **three**, and the fifth holds **five**. So each mode's frequency is the first frequency times the number of copies.
- A single mode by itself makes a **pure tone**, which sounds so annoying that it is used for sirens. The sounds we normally hear have many modes at the same time. That is why a real string's motion looks messy and irregular, much like the waveform of speech.
- The **spectrum** shows this mixture. **Frequency** goes along the horizontal axis, and **amplitude**, which is strength, goes up the vertical axis. For this string, the spectrum has spikes at 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz.

## p9
- All of the results so far come from a short list of **principles of mass-spring systems**, and they all fit together.
  - **More mass lowers the frequency, and more stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - **A system with n masses has n modes of vibration**, and each higher mode has a higher frequency.
  - **Adding mass at a spot lowers the frequency more when that spot moves more.** How much a spot moves depends on where it is.
  - **Where there is the most movement, there is no stretching. Where there is the most stretching, there is no movement.**
  - **Adding stiffness at a spot raises the frequency more when that spot stretches and compresses more.** This also depends on where the spot is.
  - In a **fixed-fixed system**, which is fixed at both ends, the harmonics are **whole-number multiples of the lowest frequency**, because each mode holds a whole number of copies of the lowest mode. In a **fixed-free system**, which is fixed at one end and free at the other, they are **odd multiples**, because each mode holds an odd number of copies.

## p10
- If you make one piece of a string **heavier**, such as thickening its very middle, some modes change and others do not.
  - In **Mode 1**, the middle moves up and down a lot, so the extra mass **lowers** the frequency.
  - In **Mode 2**, the middle does not move at all, so the extra mass changes **nothing**.
  - In **Mode 3**, the middle moves again, so the frequency drops. In **Mode 4**, the middle is still again, so nothing changes. As you go up through the modes, the effect **switches back and forth**.
- **Along a vibrating string, places of motion and places of stretching take turns.** Where a piece moves the most, it is just carried up and down and is hardly stretched. Where the string is stretched the most, such as right next to a fixed end, there is **no movement** at all.
  - **The shortcut is that the springiest spots are the spots that do not move.** In **Mode 5** of a string fixed at both ends, the string does not move at the **two fixed ends** or at the **four points where it crosses its middle line**. So those **six places** are the springiest.
  - The **most motion**, and so the **least springiness**, is at the tops of the **five bumps**.

## p11
- Changing one small piece of a string can push two modes in **opposite directions**. Take a spot where **Mode 4 moves the most** and where **Mode 5 crosses its middle line**, so Mode 5 does not move there at all. Now add both **mass** and **stiffness** to that piece.
  - **For Mode 4**, the piece is moving, so the extra mass **lowers** the frequency. The piece is barely stretched, so the extra stiffness does nothing.
  - **For Mode 5**, the piece does not move, so the extra mass does nothing. The piece is stretched the most, so the extra stiffness **raises** the frequency.
  - So the same change **lowers Mode 4's frequency and raises Mode 5's frequency**. Work out each mode separately by asking whether the spot is moving or stretching in that mode.
- **Perturbation theory** predicts how a small change in one location affects how something vibrates. In the late nineteenth century, researchers tested this by placing small amounts of **wax** on different parts of a **drum**.
  - When wax was placed where the drum vibrated a lot, the extra mass made the drum vibrate more slowly, causing its frequency (pitch) to **decrease**.
  - When wax was placed where the drum did not vibrate, it had **no effect** on the drum's frequency or sound.

## p12
- The **larynx** holds the **vocal folds**. The vocal folds form a **membrane**, which is a bit like a drum, so they have many modes of vibration. In Mode 1, all the masses move up and down together. In Mode 2, the two halves move against each other. Higher modes divide the surface into more and more parts.
  - The vocal folds are fixed around their edges, so they behave like a system fixed at both ends. That means their frequencies are harmonics, which are whole-number multiples of the lowest one.
- Changing the mass and stiffness of the vocal folds changes the voice. People who smoke heavily can develop **nodules**, which are small growths on the vocal folds. A nodule adds mass at one spot and changes the stiffness of the parts near it. So the folds vibrate differently, and the voice sounds different.

## p13
- The **theory** is that the vocal folds vibrate in many modes at once, so a measured voice should have a spectrum with **equidistant harmonics**, which means harmonics that are evenly spaced. A voice was recorded and examined in **Praat**. Its lowest harmonic was about 130 hertz, and the next harmonics were close to two and three times that value. So **the measurement matched the prediction.**
  - This is how the **scientific method** works. Scientists write a clear theory as **differential equations**, solve them to get a prediction, and then measure to check it.
  - Each person's lowest frequency is different, but the harmonics above it are always close to multiples of it.

## p14
- A **building** is **fixed at one end and free at the other**. The bottom is fixed to the ground, while the top can sway in very strong winds or an earthquake. Engineers design a building to sway only in **Mode 1**. That can feel unpleasant on a high floor, but it is safe. Swaying in a higher mode could break the building.
- The **air inside the vocal tract** is also a fixed-free system. At the bottom, the **vocal folds are closed**, so the air there **cannot move**, and that end is **fixed**. At the lips, the air can move in and out easily, so that end is **free**.
  - Unlike a building, the air in the vocal tract vibrates in **all of its modes**. When we speak, we change the mass and stiffness of the air in different places.
- A fixed-free system has **frequencies at 100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz**, which are only the odd multiples of 100. The first mode goes from **no motion** at the fixed end to **lots of motion** at the free end, which is only a quarter of a wave. The next possible modes hold **three** and then **five** of these quarter-wave pieces.
  - Two pieces would end with no motion at both ends, which is the pattern of a system fixed at both ends. So even multiples never occur.

## p15
- The air in the vocal tract can be modeled as a **chain of masses and springs**. The chain is fixed at the glottis end and free at the lip end. The masses stand for the air's tendency to keep moving, and the springs stand for the air's springiness.
  - The lowest mode corresponds to the **first formant**, **F1**, and the next mode corresponds to the **second formant**, **F2**. The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called **formants**, while the frequencies of a fixed-fixed system are called harmonics.
  - The second mode vibrates **three times faster** than the first, because it holds three of the first mode's quarter-wave pieces.
- Three places along the tube matter for vowels. For **/a/**, the air is squeezed **one third of the way from the glottis**. For **/i/**, it is squeezed **two thirds of the way from the glottis**. For **/u/**, it is squeezed at the **free end, at the lips**.

## p16
- A **constriction** is a place where the tongue or lips narrow the vocal tract and squeeze the air. The **glottis** is the tiny space between the vocal folds at the fixed end, so distances are measured from there.
  - In **done**, the central vowel, there is **no constriction**.
  - In **dean**, a **high front** vowel, the air is squeezed under the roof of the mouth, about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **Don**, a **low back** vowel, the air is squeezed far back in the throat, about **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **dune**, a **high back** vowel made with **lip rounding**, the air is squeezed **at the lips**. **Dan** is a **low front** vowel.

## p18
- **Squeezing air raises both its stiffness and its mass.** Squeezed air is like the air in a tire, which gets **stiffer** when it is squeezed. Packing air into a smaller space also raises its **density**, which makes it more **massive**.
- You might think that raising mass and stiffness together would **cancel out**. **That is false** for any system with many masses and springs, because in a given mode each spot is either mostly **moving** or mostly **stretching**.
  - At a spot with no motion and lots of stretching, only the extra stiffness counts, so the frequency **rises**.
  - At a spot with lots of motion and no stretching, only the extra mass counts, so the frequency **falls**.
  - Speech itself proves the effects do not cancel, because constrictions in different places clearly change the frequencies of the voice.

## p19
- For **/u/**, rounding the lips squeezes the air at the **free end**. This raises the mass and the stiffness of the air there.
  - In **Mode 1**, the air at the free end moves a lot and is hardly compressed. So **motion matters most**, and the extra mass lowers **F1**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the air at the free end also moves a lot. So **motion matters most** again, and the extra mass lowers **F2**.
  - So for /u/, **both F1 and F2 go down**.
- A speaker started from the central vowel, with formants near 500, 1500 and 2500 hertz, and moved into /u/. **Both formants dropped**, just as predicted.
  - The rounded lips added mass to the air right where it moves the most. The extra stiffness did nothing there, because the air at the lips is moving, not stretching.

## p20
- For **/i/**, the air is squeezed about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**. That one location acts differently in the two modes.
  - In **Mode 1**, the air at this spot moves a lot and is hardly compressed. So **motion matters most**, and the extra mass **lowers F1**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the air at this spot is compressed a lot. So **springiness matters most**, and the extra stiffness **raises F2**.
  - In plain terms, the same squeeze lands on a moving spot for F1 and on a springy spot for F2. So one formant falls while the other rises.
- A speaker went from the central vowel into /i/. The **first formant dropped a lot**, and the **second formant went up**.
  - The two formants move apart because the same squeeze is at a moving spot for one mode and at a springy spot for the other.

## p21
- For **/a/**, the air is squeezed about **one third of the way from the glottis**, and the pattern is reversed.
  - In **Mode 1**, this spot is near the fixed end, and the air there is compressed a lot. So **springiness matters most**, and the extra stiffness **raises F1**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the air at this spot moves a lot. So **motion matters most**, and the extra mass **lowers F2**.
  - So for /a/, **F1 goes up and F2 goes down**, and the two formants get closer together.
- You can also reason backward. **If a squeeze raises a formant a lot, the air at that spot must be springy and hardly moving in that mode.** **If a squeeze lowers a formant a lot, the air at that spot must be moving a lot.**

## p22
- The same principles explain all the vowels. **Extra mass matters where the air moves, and extra stiffness matters where the air compresses and expands.** The vocal tract is fixed at the glottis and free at the lips, so its formants are **odd multiples** of the lowest one.

## p23
- The air in the tube can be modeled as **lines of air particles**. Each particle stands for billions of real air molecules. The closed vocal folds are at the **fixed end**, and the open lips are at the **free end**.
  - Where the lines bunch close together and then spread far apart, the air is **compressing and expanding**, which is **springiness**.
  - Where the lines just move back and forth without changing their spacing, the air is acting as a **mass**. This is easiest to see at the **free end**.
- **F1** has one point of no motion, at the fixed end. **F2** has two, and **F3** has three. For the **schwa**, which has no constriction, a measured voice had formants near **500, 1500 and 2500 hertz**. These are the odd multiples that the theory predicts.
  - Between each pair of no-motion points, places of most motion and places of most compression take turns, just like on a vibrating string.

## p24
- A short question like **Where were you a year ago?** uses all of the constriction rules at once. Each sound squeezes the air in a different place, so the formants keep moving up and down instead of staying at fixed values.
  - When the tongue makes an **/i/**-like squeeze near the front of the mouth, **F1 goes down and F2 goes up**. This happens because that spot is moving in Mode 1 and springy in Mode 2.
  - When the lips round for **you**, **F1 and F2 both go down**. This happens because the air at the lips is moving in every mode.
  - When the tongue moves back and down, as in an **/a/**-like sound, **F1 rises and F2 falls**. This happens because that spot is springy in Mode 1 and moving in Mode 2.
- **Speech is a continuous series of squeezes, not a list of separate vowels.** So the frequencies of the air in the vocal tract are always changing. The same mass and spring principles predict which way each formant moves at every moment.
