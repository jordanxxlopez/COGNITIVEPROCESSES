## p2
- A **neural network** takes in information about one moment, uses its **weights** to process it, and makes a decision. It learns those weights by making mistakes and correcting them through **backpropagation**. The idea began in **1943** with a paper by **McCulloch and Pitts**, which proposed that simple math could model how the brain works.
  - The paper starts from the idea that nerve activity is **all-or-none**, where all means active and none means inactive. This is the same idea as the **sigmoid**. So the very first paper on artificial neural networks starts from biology, from how real neurons behave.
  - Warren McCulloch was a **psychiatrist**. The earliest work on neural networks tried to model how the mind works, including mental illness. It was not about making money.
- By **1986**, these ideas had grown into the two **Parallel Distributed Processing** books, written by a group at UC San Diego. **Parallel** means many neurons calculate their activations at the same time. **Distributed** means the information from the inputs is spread across all the neurons.
  - By then, forward propagation and backpropagation were fully worked out. One of the contributors, **Geoffrey Hinton**, later won the **2024 Nobel Prize in Physics** for this work.
- **Given one static vector of formants, these networks could choose between a few possible categories.** A **vector** is just a list of numbers, and static means one fixed list from one moment. But **human thinking is much more complex** than taking in one list of numbers and giving one decision at the end.
  - **Speech changes constantly over time**, because every tiny moment gives a new set of formants.
  - **Language changes over time too**, because a sentence is many words, one after another.
  - So a network that only gets one list of numbers can never model thinking that happens over time.

## p3
- In **1985**, **Michael Jordan** developed **recurrent neural networks** to understand **speech production**, which is how the tongue, the velum and the lips move over time. Features such as nasality, backness and lip rounding each turn on and off at different moments across a sequence of sounds. Jordan realized that a regular network could never handle something that complex and that changes so much over time.
  - His network is **not just sequential**, meaning it is not just one layer after another, because it has **two layers working side by side**.
  - It is called **recurrent** because it **takes its output and sends it back into part of its input**. It produces something at one moment, uses that to predict the next moment, and then uses that to predict the moment after.
- **GPT works the same way, one word at a time.** It takes the words of the prompt and predicts the next word, which becomes the first word of its answer. Then it feeds the prompt plus that new word back in and predicts the next word again. It keeps going like this. So the idea behind GPT started in 1985, with thinking about how speech happens over time.
  - People kept improving the recurrent network from 1985 until **2017**, when the **large language model** was invented.
- **Science and technology grow from other people's ideas over long periods of time.** An idea from 1943 was improved into the networks of 1986. Jordan's idea from 1985 was improved into the large language models of 2017. And the people who came up with these ideas were mostly not the people who made a lot of money from them.
  - These years help you understand the history, but they will not be on the exam.

## p4
- AI systems are built up in steps, and **each step is made from the one before it**.
  - **A single neuron takes in a vector and gives back one number.** It multiplies each input by its weight and adds them all up, which is the **inner product**. The symbol **sigma** just means add them up. This uses only second-grade multiplication and addition to model the electrical and chemical activity of a real neuron.
  - **A neural network takes in one vector and gives back a whole decision vector.** Its layers are connected by sets of weights. Each set of weights turns one vector into the next one through many inner products.
- **A large language model takes in many vectors, one for each moment in a sequence, and gives back one decision vector about what comes next.** Picture 15 vectors, where each one stands for something at one moment, such as one word of a sentence. The model's job is to **predict the sixteenth**.
  - In real systems, there are hundreds of thousands to a million input vectors, and that set is called the **context window**. Each vector is also thousands of numbers long. That is why huge data centers are needed.
  - The sixteenth vector is a **probability distribution over the next word**. English has about 30,000 words, so each entry gives the chance that one particular word comes next.
- **A large language model is made of neural networks.** Each set of weights is a **layer**, and real systems have about a hundred or more layers. **The knowledge is still in the weights.** So an LLM's knowledge of how to write an essay is stored in its weights.
- **An LLM is a dynamical system**, and that is why the Nobel Prize for AI was given in physics. A mass-spring system starts from **initial conditions**, which are its starting position and speed, and its rules decide what happens next. An LLM's initial condition is its set of input vectors. Each layer turns them into **better vectors**, step after step, until the last layer can predict the next word.
  - The calculation at every step is the **inner product**, multiplying and adding billions or trillions of times.
  - In plain terms, an LLM is like the oscillator. But instead of two numbers changing over time, thousands of numbers are improved layer by layer.
- **An LLM learns by predicting the next word and correcting its mistakes.** Take "the cat sat on the," where the real next word is **mat**. A new LLM with random weights might predict something impossible like "in." Backpropagation then adjusts its trillions of weights so its prediction moves toward mat.
  - After this is repeated with about a trillion sentences, the system surprisingly ends up knowing how to speak English.
  - The first problem this design solved, in 2017, was **machine translation**. The same design then worked for speech and many other problems.
- **An agentic system is a graph of LLMs**, which means several LLMs connected to each other. An **agent** in AI today is just an LLM.
  - An **outline LLM** receives the prompt and is told only to outline the answer. A **writing LLM** turns the outline into smooth sentences.
  - A **critic LLM** points out problems in the draft. A **revision LLM** decides whether the draft is good enough or sends it back to the critic, usually for only a few rounds. Then a **final LLM** produces the output.
  - The critic is what greatly reduced **hallucination**, which is when the system makes things up. The conversation between the LLMs inside the system is why many systems take a while to respond.
- **Neurons make neural networks, neural networks make LLMs, and LLMs make agentic systems.** This is like how molecules make cells and cells make organs.
  - Designing a graph of LLMs to solve a problem is a real job today. It takes problem solving more than knowledge of programming languages.
  - The connections between LLMs can also form circuits that nobody planned. These circuits can let a system do things that were never part of the design, and sometimes those things are harmful.
- **The main idea behind all of this is Gestalt completion.** People see a triangle in the Gestalt figure even though no triangle is drawn. A big part of human knowledge is **filling in** what is missing. Training a system only to **predict the missing piece** turns out to copy a big part of human thinking.
  - The same idea is behind the **cloze procedure**, a test introduced by **Wilson Taylor** in **1953**. In this test, words are removed from a text and people fill them in. The name comes from **closure**, the Gestalt term for completing a familiar pattern that is not quite finished, such as seeing a broken circle as a whole circle.
  - **Knowledge can be tested by asking someone to complete a sequence, and for AI, completing sequences is all that knowledge is.** An LLM predicting the next word is playing a cloze game over and over.
  - Underneath everything, **inference is inner products**, and **learning is stochastic gradient descent plus dynamic programming**.

## p5
- **An LLM takes 15 vectors and predicts the sixteenth.** A vector is just a list of numbers, so those numbers can stand for almost anything. Whatever they stand for, the machine plays the same game of predicting the next one.
- The vectors can be **words**. In "The cat that the dog chased ran up the old tree and sat on the," the sixteenth vector is the missing next word. A speaker of English knows a likely ending, such as branch or mat.
  - To get it right, the model has to use the whole sentence, because it was the cat, not the dog, that sat.
- The vectors can be **slices of sound**. A spectrogram is cut into small frames, and each frame becomes one vector of frequencies and amplitudes. The system learns to predict the next small piece of speech from all the pieces before it.
  - In 2019, this approach gave the **best speech recognition in 75 years**. It is also the basis for synthetic voices that are now very hard to tell apart from real people.
  - The newest systems can hold a spoken conversation, and the only clue left is a slight delay before each response. As that delay gets shorter, phone scams become a real danger.
- The vectors can be **pieces of an image**. An image is cut into **16 patches**, read row by row, and the system predicts the missing patch. After training on huge numbers of images, it learns what a dog, a dolphin or a boat is. This became the basis of modern **computer vision**.
- The vectors can even be **amino acids**. A protein is a chain of amino acids, which come in **20 kinds**, and the order of the chain decides how the protein folds into its shape. Researchers treated a protein as just a sequence and played the same predict-the-next game. This pushed the **protein folding problem** many years ahead, which matters for designing drugs and vaccines.
  - That work won the **2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry**.
- **The LLM only ever sees vectors.** Words, sounds, pixels and molecules all reach it the same way. So the same design always asks the same question, which is what comes sixteenth.
  - These systems predict from past data. So when something new comes up that the past data does not cover, they can **hallucinate**.

## p6
- English allows only **certain sequences of sounds** in its words. Knowing which sequences are allowed is part of every speaker's knowledge of the language. **A simple English syllable can have a consonant, then a vowel, then a consonant**, as in Don, mat, sit, cup, dog and fish.
  - The pattern is about **sounds, not letters**. The silent e in shave, kite and rope does not count. Two letters can also spell one consonant sound, as in ship, chin and bath.
- Syllables can also have **clusters**, which are groups of consonants together. A cluster before the vowel is called an **onset**, as in split, flat and black. A cluster after the vowel is called a **coda**, as in desk, help and lamp. **Only certain sequences are allowed before the vowel and after the vowel.**
  - Each real word has an impossible twin marked with a star, such as **flat** and **lfat**, **help** and **hepl**, and **lamp** and **lapm**. Many twins use the same letters in a different order, so the order of the sounds matters.
  - **The order on one side of the vowel is the mirror image of the order on the other side.** In flat, the l is right next to the vowel and the f is farther out. In help, the l is again right next to the vowel, now on the other side, and the p is farther out. Each impossible twin puts the wrong consonant next to the vowel.
- **A long word is a chain of syllables**, such as ba-na-na, At-lan-tic and ath-lete. The sounds t and l are fine when a syllable break falls between them. But **tl cannot start a syllable**, which is why Atlantic is a word but tlantic is impossible.
  - The sound **ng** can end a syllable, as in ring, but it can never start one. So whether a sequence of sounds is allowed depends on where the syllable breaks fall.
- **Every language has its own rules.** Mandarin allows only nasal sounds at the end of a syllable. Cantonese also allows a final p. Russian and Polish allow clusters like zb or dl at the start of a word. Spanish speakers turn a word like stadium into one that starts with e.

## p7
- **Knowing which sound sequences are allowed is called phonotactic knowledge, and it shows up in the same next-item game that GPT plays.** Each item is the beginning of an English word, and the task is to guess the next letter. People agree on their guesses quickly, and how much they agree changes from item to item.
- **After a vowel, almost any letter can come next.** After "ra," likely letters include t, n, r and l, and many others are possible too.
- **At the start of a word, the choices narrow sharply** to exactly what an English onset allows.
  - After **sp**, the next letter is l, r or a vowel, never n. After **k**, it is a vowel or n, as in knee, never t.
  - After **b** and **f**, it is r, l or a vowel, never n. After **sh**, it is a vowel or r, as in shrimp.
  - After **skr**, or after **m** at the start of a word, only a vowel can come.
  - After **bram**, the letter b is almost certain, as in bramble, though a new syllable could also start there.
- **Guessing the next letter is predicting the sixteenth item**, which is the same game an LLM plays. A GPT trained only on this game learns English sounds and spelling, because the only way its predictions can get good is by matching the patterns of English syllables.
  - Its prediction is a **probability distribution** over the next letter, and the shape of that distribution shows what it knows.
  - After "ra," the distribution is **flat**, spread over many letters. After "skr," it is **sharply peaked** on a few vowels, because English allows nothing else there.
  - In plain terms, the fewer letters English allows next, the more the probability piles onto those few letters.

## p8
- English syllables are a good test case for understanding LLMs. English has about 30,000 words, so training on words needs enormous amounts of data. But English has only about 26 letters, or a few dozen sounds. So a small LLM, with thousands of weights instead of trillions, can be trained to learn what every speaker already knows about English syllables.
- Writing that program by hand would take months. So instead, the plan is to **design an agentic system using instructions written in English**. The agentic system builds the LLM, trains it to predict each next letter, and shows what it has learned along the way. Then its knowledge can be compared with what English speakers know about syllables.
