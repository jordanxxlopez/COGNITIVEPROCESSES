## p2
- A **mass-spring system** is a mass attached to a spring. It vibrates because each part "wants" something different. **The spring wants to be at its equilibrium position**, which is its normal resting length. If the spring is stretched or squished, it pulls or pushes to get back to that length. **A moving mass wants to keep moving**, so once it starts moving, it does not stop by itself.
  - When you pull the mass out and let go, the spring pulls it back toward equilibrium. The mass is still moving when it reaches equilibrium, so it **keeps going** past that point and squishes the spring.
  - The squished spring then pushes the mass back the other way. The mass overshoots again, and the cycle repeats. This repeating back-and-forth movement is called **vibration**.
  - On a real table, **friction** slowly stops the motion. The model leaves friction out, so in the model the system vibrates forever.
- The same system can move in **two directions**. It is important to understand both, because some systems are easier to think about in one direction than the other.
  - In **longitudinal** motion, the mass moves **left and right**, back and forth along the line of the spring.
  - In **transverse** motion, the mass sits between **two walls** with a spring on each side, and it moves **up and down**, across the line of the springs.
- **Both kinds of motion work the same way, for the same reason.** When you pull the transverse mass up, the springs **get stretched**. Springs pull back when they are stretched, so they pull the mass back down. When the mass passes the middle, the springs are not stretched at all. But the mass is still moving, so it keeps going and stretches the springs on the other side.
  - Both versions vibrate at the **same rate**, because they have the same mass and the same springs pulling them back.

## p3
- **Frequency** is the number of back-and-forth cycles a system completes each second. Two things control it, the **mass** and the **stiffness**. Each one has the same effect in longitudinal and in transverse motion.
- **A heavier mass vibrates more slowly.** A heavy mass is hard to get moving and hard to turn around, so each back-and-forth trip takes longer. For example, **heavy branches sway slowly, while small branches sway quickly.**
  - Making the mass **four times heavier cuts the frequency in half**.
- **A stiffer spring makes the system vibrate faster.** A stiffer spring pulls back harder toward where it started, so it turns the mass around sooner.
  - Making the spring **four times stiffer doubles the frequency**.
- **More mass lowers the frequency, and more stiffness raises the frequency.** But each part only affects the frequency while it is actually doing its job.
  - **A mass affects the frequency only when it moves, and a spring affects the frequency only when it stretches or compresses.** If a mass never moves, you could make it a thousand pounds heavier and the frequency would not change. If a spring never stretches, it adds no stiffness.

## p4
- A system with **two masses** has two walls, one spring from each wall to a mass, and **one spring between the two masses**. All the masses are equal, and all the springs are equally stiff. This system can vibrate in **two different ways**, which are called **modes**. **A mode is simply one way that a system can vibrate.**
  - In **Mode 1**, the masses move **together**. If you pull both masses up and let go, they rise and fall side by side. In longitudinal motion, they move left and right as a pair.
  - In **Mode 2**, the masses move **against each other**. If you pull one mass down and the other up, then whenever one rises, the other falls.
  - In longitudinal motion, Mode 2 means one mass is pulled left and the other right, so they move toward each other and then apart.
  - A system can also vibrate in **both modes at the same time**. If you pull both masses up, but one higher than the other, they move together and against each other at once. Real movements, including speech, do this all the time.
- **When the masses move together, they go back and forth slowly. When they move against each other, they go back and forth quickly.** So **Mode 2 has the higher frequency**. Both modes use the same masses and the same springs, so to explain the difference, look at **what each spring is doing**.
  - In **Mode 1**, the middle spring never stretches or compresses. It just rides along like a solid bar, so **Mode 1 really only uses two springs**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the middle spring is stretched and squished over and over. So **all three springs are working**, and Mode 2 has three springs' worth of stiffness instead of two.
  - **More stiffness means a stronger pull back toward equilibrium, and a stronger pull means a higher frequency.** In plain terms, a higher mode does not use stiffer springs. It makes the same springs do more stretching.
- This leads to a general rule. **The more the masses move against each other, the more the springs stretch and compress. That gives the mode more stiffness, so it has a higher frequency.** This is why a system with several masses has several frequencies. It has several modes, and each higher mode is stiffer than the one before it.

## p5
- Adding a third mass adds a third way to vibrate, so **a system with three masses has three modes**. You can tell the modes apart the same way as before, by asking which masses move together and which move against each other.
  - All three masses can move back and forth together.
  - The two outer masses can move against each other while the middle mass stays still.
  - The middle mass can move against the two outer masses.
  - The more the masses move against each other, the more the springs stretch. So these three modes go from the lowest frequency to the highest.

## p6
- With **three masses** and four springs, the system has **three modes**, and each mode has its own frequency.
  - In **Mode 1**, all three masses move back and forth **together**. This mode has the **lowest frequency**, because the two middle springs just ride along and add no stiffness.
  - In **Mode 2**, the **middle mass stays still** while the two outer masses move against each other. This mode has the **middle frequency**. To start it, you pull the outer masses apart carefully without touching the middle one.
  - In **Mode 3**, the **middle mass moves opposite to the two outer masses**, so **every mass moves against its neighbors**. This mode has the most stretching and compressing, the most stiffness, and the **highest frequency**.
- A mass only affects the frequency when it moves. So **changing one mass only changes the modes where that mass moves**. If you make the **middle mass heavier**, the frequency of **Mode 1 and Mode 3 goes down**, because the middle mass moves in both of those modes. **Mode 2 does not change**, because the middle mass does not move at all in Mode 2.
- All three modes can also happen **at the same time**. No matter how you start the system, its motion is a mix of these three **pure modes**.
  - **Learning a musical instrument means learning how to make its masses and springs vibrate in certain modes and not others.** How you pluck a string or bow a violin decides which modes start vibrating, and the strongest modes are what we hear as **notes**.
  - A **trained singer** does the same thing with the vocal folds. The singer controls the mass and stiffness of the folds well enough to sing in tune.

## p7
- The same pattern works for any number of masses. **N masses give N modes**, so one mass has one way to vibrate, two masses have two, and six masses have six. Higher modes have **more reversals**, which are places where neighboring masses move in opposite directions. More reversals mean more stretching, so **higher modes have higher frequencies**.
  - In a chain of separate masses like this, the mode frequencies are usually **not whole-number multiples** of the lowest frequency.
- Each mode has the same basic shape no matter how many masses are in the chain.
  - **Every Mode 1 looks alike**, because all the masses move up and down together.
  - **Every Mode 2 looks alike**, because the **left side moves against the right side**. With an odd number of masses, the middle mass stays still.
  - **Every Mode 3 looks alike**, because the **middle moves against the sides**.
  - With **four masses**, the four modes are all four together, the left pair against the right pair, the middle pair against the outer pair, and every mass against its neighbors.
- If you keep adding more and more masses and springs, all the way to an **infinite number**, you get a **string**, like a guitar or piano string. **Every tiny piece of a string has mass, and every tiny piece stretches like a spring**, so a string has an infinite number of modes.
  - In the **lowest mode**, the whole string moves up and down together with almost no stretching between its pieces, so this mode gives the **lowest note**.
  - In each higher mode, more parts of the string move against each other. A string that is plucked carelessly vibrates in many modes at once.
  - A **building** is like a string that is fixed to the ground at the bottom and free to sway at the top. Engineers design buildings to sway only in **Mode 1**, because in higher modes some floors would move one way while other floors moved the other way.

## p8
- Take a **string fixed at both ends** whose lowest mode vibrates at **100 hertz**, which means 100 times per second. Its next modes vibrate at **200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz**. These frequencies are **whole-number multiples** of the lowest one, and modes that line up this way are called **harmonics**.
  - Each higher mode completes more cycles in the same amount of time. These are **consecutive harmonics**, which means both the odd and the even multiples are included.
- The even spacing comes from the way each mode is built from copies of the first mode. **Mode 1 is a single bump.** **Mode 2 is two copies of that bump**, with one half moving up while the other half moves down.
  - Because Mode 2 has two copies, it has **twice the stiffness** of Mode 1, so it has twice the frequency. Mode 3 has three copies, and the fifth mode has **five copies of the first mode**.
  - So if the lowest mode is 200 hertz, the fifth mode is five times 200, which is 1000 hertz.
- A real string vibrates in **all of its modes at the same time**. When the separate motions are added together, the string makes one complicated, changing shape. A **spectrum** shows **which frequencies are in a sound and how strong each one is**. **Frequency** goes along the horizontal axis, and **amplitude**, which is strength, goes up the vertical axis.
  - For a string moving in all five modes, the spectrum has **five evenly spaced spikes**. In this example, the spikes have **equal height**, because nothing has filtered the sound.
  - If the string vibrated **only in Mode 3**, the spectrum would have **one spike at the Mode 3 frequency** and nothing else. This is like a **one-hot encoding**, where only one slot is on.

## p9
- **The vocal folds are more complicated than strings.** They are a sheet of muscle, which is called a **membrane**. So their masses and springs are spread across a surface instead of along a line. The same ideas about modes still apply.
  - In **Mode 1**, the whole surface moves up and down together.
  - In **Mode 2**, the **left half moves against the right half**.
  - In **Mode 3**, the surface is split in the other direction, so the two halves on either side of a still line move against each other.
  - In **Mode 4**, four sections move against each other, and higher modes get more and more complicated.
  - Each mode is labeled with a pair of numbers. The first counts how many bumps fit along the length, and the second counts how many fit across the width.
- In real vocal folds, **all the modes happen at the same time**, so voicing produces a spectrum full of spikes. These evenly spaced frequencies are simplified numbers for teaching. Because the vocal folds are held in place along their edges, they **behave like a string**, so their harmonics are evenly spaced.

## p10
- **The scientific method** depends on **very clear statements about how the world works**, usually written as **differential equations**. **Solving those equations gives a prediction** about what we will find when we actually measure something.
  - The spring equations are one example. Solving them predicts that a spring that is pulled goes back, and a spring that is pushed also goes back.
  - The equations for the vocal folds are the same type as the spring equations. There are just many more of them, and they are a little more complicated. So what you learned from masses and springs is enough to reason about the vocal folds.
  - The **theory** is that the **vocal folds are a membrane**. So when they vibrate, their spectrum should have **evenly spaced peaks**, which are called **harmonics**.
  - The **prediction** is that **when anyone's voice is measured at the larynx, its spectrum will have equidistant harmonics**, which means harmonics that are evenly spaced.
- This prediction was tested by recording two voices in **Praat**, a program for analyzing speech. Each speaker held the **mouth wide open**, so no particular vowel was being made.
  - In the first voice, the lowest spike was near **100 hertz**. The next spikes were close to two, three, four and five times that value, so they were **evenly spaced, just as predicted**.
  - In the second voice, the lowest spike was near **220 hertz**. The next spikes were near two and three times that value, so they were also evenly spaced, but farther apart.
  - **The prediction was correct for both voices.** A theory built from simple masses and springs made a precise, correct prediction about real speech.
- The second voice has a **higher pitch**, so that speaker's vocal folds are probably **smaller and thinner**. **Thinner folds have less mass, and less mass means a higher frequency**, so all the harmonics are spaced farther apart.
  - This is part of how we **recognize people by their voices**. Each person's vocal folds give their voice its own spectrum, and getting to know someone's voice includes getting used to that spectrum.
  - Twins have the same genes and similar vocal folds, so they can be hard to tell apart on the phone.
  - How a person says particular vowels also matters. But vowels depend on the vibration of the **air** above the vocal folds, not on the folds themselves.

## p11
- A string can also be **fixed at one end and free at the other**, just like a **building**, which is fixed to the ground and free to sway at the top. The **fixed bottom** cannot move, so every mode has a point of **no motion** there, which is called a **node**. The **free top** moves the most, and that point is called an **antinode**.
- **In this kind of system, only the odd harmonics occur.** So the modes come at **100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz**, which are one, three, five and seven times the lowest frequency.
  - **Mode 1** goes from no motion at the bottom to the most motion at the top, like a building swaying as a whole.
  - Two copies of Mode 1 would put a still point at both ends. That would make the system **fixed at both ends**, so this pattern cannot happen here.
  - **Three copies** do end with motion at the top. So the next mode has three times the frequency of Mode 1, and the mode after that has five copies.
  - The simple version is that the top must always be moving, and only an odd number of copies ends with motion at the top.
- When **both ends are fixed**, a string has **every whole-number multiple** of the lowest frequency. When **one end is fixed and the other is free**, it has **only the odd multiples**.
- **Civil engineers design buildings to sway only in Mode 1**, so every floor moves together. A building is not designed to be completely stiff, because buildings that cannot bend or move slightly are more likely to be damaged during an earthquake. Engineers also avoid the higher modes, because in those modes some floors would move one way while other floors moved the other way.
- Compare two strings with the **same length and the same wave speed**. If one is fixed at both ends and the other is fixed at one end and free at the other, the fixed-free string has **half the lowest frequency**.
  - When all four fixed-free modes are equally strong, the spectrum measured at the free end has **four equal spikes** at the odd multiples.
