## p2
- A **neural network** can work as a very simple **speech recognizer**. It takes the **two formants, F1 and F2**, of a vowel and decides which of **five words** it heard, **done, dune, dean, Don or Dan**. Networks like this were used fifteen or twenty years ago. Modern **large language models** are built from many networks like this one, the way molecules are built from **atoms**.
- The network has **2 input neurons**, **three hidden layers** with **3, 2 and 4 neurons**, and **5 output neurons**. Every neuron connects to every neuron in the next layer, so the network is **fully connected**.
  - The **input neurons** just take in numbers from the outside world. For example, one speaker's vowel might have an F1 of 400 and an F2 of 1900.
  - **The network's knowledge is stored in its weights.** Each connection has a weight, which is a positive or negative number. The whole network is just a machine that processes numbers using those weights.
  - Each neuron's activation is calculated from the neurons that feed into it. The activations of the last layer become the network's decision.

## p3
- **Forward propagation** carries one input through the network until it reaches a decision. The example input is **F1 of 320 and F2 of 2000**, which is the pattern of **dean**.
  - Each neuron computes an **inner product**. For the top neuron of the first hidden layer, the weight on F1 is multiplied by 320, the weight on F2 is multiplied by 2000, and the two results are added.
  - The result of that inner product, before anything else is done to it, is called the neuron's **linear activation**. Here it comes out slightly below zero.
  - The other neurons do the same thing with their own weights. Their activations then become the inputs to the next layer, which does more inner products. This continues until the output layer.
- At the end, the output numbers are turned into a **probability distribution**. This is a set of numbers between zero and one that **add up to one**, and it is the network's **decision**. The step that does this is called **softmax**. All you need to know about softmax is that it turns numbers into probabilities, and a bigger number gets a bigger probability.
  - Probabilities and percentages are the same idea. A probability of 0.55 is a 55 percent chance.
  - Here the network gives **done about 56 percent** and much less to each of the other words. So it is basically saying **"I heard done,"** even though the input was dean.
- **All of the network's knowledge is just positive and negative numbers, and all of its decision making is just multiplying and adding**, which are things learned in second grade. **Inference** means working out a decision. The AI systems people chat with every day make their decisions using only these simple operations.
  - This is less surprising than it sounds, because brains are chemical machines. Inside neurons, the chemistry of ions such as sodium, potassium and calcium does something close to adding and multiplying.

## p4
- Every step is an inner product, so you can figure out the effect of raising one number without doing any math. Take the top neuron of the last hidden layer. Its activation is about 1.57, and its weight into the **done** output is positive 0.1.
  - If that **activation goes up**, its contribution to done goes up, because the contribution is the activation times a positive weight. So the **probability of done goes up**.
  - If that **weight goes up**, from 0.1 to 0.2, the same contribution gets bigger, because the activation is positive. So the **probability of done goes up** again.
  - **A positive number times a positive number is positive.** So raising either one makes that neuron push the done output up more.
- These questions test whether you understand what happens, not whether you can multiply. A good answer names the direction, up or down, and then gives the reason. For example, "the probability of done goes up because the weight and the activation are both positive, so their product adds more to the done output."
  - In plain terms, each neuron votes for each output through its weight. A positive vote from an active neuron always helps that output.
  - If the weight were negative instead, raising the activation would push the output down. So the sign of the weight decides the direction.

## p6
- **Every network starts out knowing nothing**, because its weights start out **random**. It only gets better when it is told the right answer.
  - The right answer is given as a **desired output**. For a recording of dean, the desired output is **0, 0, 1, 0, 0**. This says there is a 100 percent chance it was dean and zero chance for every other word. A list like this, with one 1 and zeros everywhere else, is called a **one-hot encoding**.
  - The output the network actually produces is the **computed output**. The difference between the computed output and the desired output is the network's **error**.
  - The desired outputs come from real data, such as a table of each student's formants together with the vowel that student actually said.
- **Backpropagation** is the method that changes the weights so the network makes better decisions. After learning, if you give the network 320 and 2000 again, the probability of done should go down and the probability of dean should go up.
  - **Learning happens gradually, one small step at a time.** Just like stochastic gradient descent, the weights do not jump to the right values all at once. Each piece of data nudges the weights a little, then the next piece nudges them again, and so on.
  - Each weight moves a small step in whichever direction makes the error smaller. The **learning rate** sets how big each step is.
  - The weights are updated **starting from the end**. First the weights into the output change, then the weights into the third hidden layer, then the second, then the first. During this backward pass, the activations from the forward pass stay the same.
- This is why AI needs huge **data centers**. A modern chatbot can have **trillions of weights**, and backpropagation has to nudge every one of them, over and over, for months.

## p7
- For the **last weight**, it is easy to tell which way to change it. This is the 0.1 that connects the top neuron of the last hidden layer to **done**.
  - That weight multiplies an activation of about 1.57, so raising the weight would raise the probability of done.
  - The desired probability of done is **zero**, so raising the weight would make the error **worse**.
  - So that weight should be **lowered**. Lowering it shrinks its contribution and pulls the probability of done down toward zero. Only one inner product is needed to figure this out.
- For the **first weight**, it is very hard to tell which way to change it. This is the 0.005 near the input.
  - Changing it changes one activation in the first hidden layer. That changes the second hidden layer, which changes the third, which finally changes the output probabilities.
  - **Its effect on the decision has to pass through all the layers in between**, and it also depends on all the later weights. So you cannot tell just by looking at it whether it should go up or down. Backpropagation still works it out.

## p8
- **The last weights directly control the output probabilities.** So they have a direct effect on the error, and the difference between the computed output and the desired output can be used right away to improve them.
- **The first weights affect their own activations, and those activations then affect two more layers.** It is very hard to figure out how much an early weight is to blame for a mistake at the very end. Its effect on the error also **includes the effect of the last weights**, because the last weights come after it.
- So **improving the last weights is the easy problem**, and improving the first weights is a much bigger problem that already contains the easy one. **Dynamic programming** is a method that solves the small problems first and then uses their answers to solve the bigger problems that depend on them. It was developed by **Bellman** and **Viterbi**.
  - **Backpropagation is stochastic gradient descent plus dynamic programming.** Stochastic gradient descent changes the numbers gradually. Dynamic programming decides the order, which is the **back** in backpropagation.
  - Backpropagation was worked out in the **mid-1980s**. In 2024, a Nobel Prize recognized it, because there would be no AI without it.
  - Research from the last ten years suggests that single neurons in the brain also work forward and then backward, using their chemistry. So this forward-then-backward process may not be so different from what brains do.
  - The short exam answer is this. The last weight affects the decision in one simple step. The first weight's effect passes through many layers and depends on the later weights. So the easy problem is solved first, and its answer is reused for the harder problems.

## p9
- Training needs two tables of numbers, which are called **matrices**. Each row pairs **one recording** with **the vowel it really was**.
  - The **input matrix** has one row per recording and **two columns**, F1 and F2, because the network takes in two numbers. With about 110 recordings, it is 110 by 2.
  - The **output matrix** has one row per recording and **five columns**, one for each word. A **1 marks the true vowel**, and the other columns have 0. It is 110 by 5.
  - So row by row, the network is told the right answer. For example, formants like 800 and 1700 go with one vowel, and formants like 700 and 1100 go with another.

## p10
- A short **Python** program that uses the **PyTorch** library builds and trains this network.
  - The **import** lines tell the program to use the **torch library**. The library does all the background work, so the network can be described in just a few lines.
  - The two **load** lines read in the two matrices. The formants become X, and the true vowels become Y.
- **Sequential** means the network is a **sequence of layers**, one after another. **Linear** means the layer **does inner products**, because a linear layer only multiplies and adds.
  - **nn.Linear(2, 3)** describes the **first hidden layer**, which takes **2 inputs and gives 3 outputs**. The next lines take 3 and give 2, take 2 and give 4, and take 4 and give 5.
  - The network has **five layers**, but there are only **four lines**, because the **input layer does no processing**. It only receives numbers, so the only sign of it in the code is the 2 in the first line.
- The last lines run **one training step** using all 110 rows at once. **SGD** is stochastic gradient descent, and **lr** is the **learning rate**, which sets the size of each step.
  - The **loss** measures the network's mistakes by comparing its outputs with the true vowels. **backward** runs backpropagation to work out how each weight should change, and **step** actually changes the weights.
  - In plain terms, the program describes the network, shows it the data, measures how wrong it is, works backward to find which weights caused the mistake, and then adjusts the weights a little.

## p11
- **Lord Edgar Adrian** won the **Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1932** for discovering that **neurons are nearly digital**. A real neuron is roughly **on or off**. It is either firing or not firing, instead of taking any value at all. The neurons in the network so far are not like that, because their activations can be any number, such as slightly below zero, about 6.6, or about 2.7.
- Because of this discovery, people added an **activation function** called the **sigmoid** to neural networks. The sigmoid takes the inner product and squashes it into a number between zero and one.
  - If the inner product is a **large negative number**, the sigmoid gives about **0**, so the neuron is **off**. An inner product of minus 4 gives almost exactly zero, and minus 10, minus 100 and minus 1000 also give about zero.
  - If the inner product is a **large positive number**, the sigmoid gives about **1**, so the neuron is **on**. An inner product of 4 gives almost exactly one, and 6 or 100 also give about one.
  - Only inner products **near zero** give values in between. So the sigmoid makes the neuron **nearly digital**, either on or off. This is like the chemistry that keeps real neurons from firing without limit.
- The sigmoid also matches something important about **perception** in animals and humans. **Some things that are very different are perceived as the same, and some things that are very similar are perceived as different.**
  - You recognize a friend as the same person whether they wear a hat or not, or a different shirt on a different day. Perception ignores many real differences, just as the sigmoid gives minus 1000, minus 100 and minus 20 all the same value.
  - USC and UCLA are close to each other, both large, and alike in their labs and their football. Yet people see them as completely different. Small differences can matter a lot, just as two inputs slightly below and slightly above zero land on opposite sides of the sigmoid's jump.
  - In plain terms, the flat parts of the curve treat very different inputs as the same, and the steep middle separates inputs that are almost the same.

## p12
- Adding the sigmoid to each hidden neuron changes every number that comes after it. The inputs are still the formants, but each hidden neuron now passes on its sigmoid value instead of its raw inner product.
  - The first hidden neuron's inner product is slightly below zero, and the sigmoid turns it into about **0.4**.
  - The second neuron's inner product is about 6.6, and the sigmoid turns it into about **1**, so that neuron is fully on.
  - Each time a neuron changes, the activations in the later layers are recalculated, and the output probabilities shift.
- With the same random weights and the sigmoid on all nine hidden neurons, the decision moves away from done. The network now spreads its probability more evenly and gives its highest probability, about one third, to **Dan**. So it is **still wrong** about dean.
  - The sigmoid changes how the network calculates, but only training with backpropagation can give it good weights.

## p13
- The program changes in only one way. **nn.Sigmoid** is added after each **hidden** linear layer. It means "apply the sigmoid," and it makes that layer nearly digital.
  - The **last layer has no sigmoid**, because its outputs go straight into the loss calculation, which turns them into probabilities with softmax.
  - The rest of the training program, with SGD and a learning rate of 0.1, stays the same.
- To find out whether the sigmoid makes the vowel recognizer better or worse, you train both versions and compare them. You can also swap in other activation functions that look somewhat like the sigmoid, such as one called **ReLU**, to see whether they improve recognition.
  - **Many of the most basic ideas in AI come from how real brains work.** These include neurons, inner products that collect input from many neurons, and the nearly digital firing that the sigmoid copies.
