## p2
- **Real-time MRI films the inside of the head while a person talks.** The movements of speech are normally hidden, so this is the way to watch them as they happen. The view is a side view cut straight down the middle of the head. The face points left, and the back of the neck is on the right. Air appears black, and soft tissue such as the tongue, lips and throat walls appears gray and white.
  - The **lips** sit at the far left, and they open, close, round and spread during speech.
  - The **tongue** is the large rounded mass filling most of the mouth. It moves more than any other part during speech, bunching up toward the roof of the mouth, pulling back toward the throat, or lying low.
  - The roof of the mouth has a bony front part, the **hard palate**, and a soft movable back part, the **soft palate** or **velum**, which lifts to seal off the nose, or lowers to let air flow out through the nose.
  - Behind the tongue, a dark channel of air runs down the throat. This channel is called the **pharynx**. Below it sits the **larynx**, which holds the **vocal folds**.
- **During speech all of these structures move at the same time and at high speed.** The jaw rises and falls, the lips open and close, the tongue reshapes itself many times per second, and the soft palate lifts and lowers.
  - Each sound depends on a precise shape of the whole passage. The shapes flow smoothly into one another rather than one at a time.
  - The two movies capture the same speaker's vocal tract in two different shapes, and the difference between them comes almost entirely from where the tongue and lips are placed.

## p3
- **We learn to move our speech organs in extremely complex ways with no instructions and almost no chance to see what we are doing.** Nobody tells a young child where to put the tongue tip for a sound. A child also cannot see their own tongue, soft palate or vocal folds. This is because those organs are hidden inside the mouth and throat.
  - **By ages 2 to 4**, children speak with a wide vocabulary and near-adult language, with hardly any explicit instruction. They pick up thousands of words and the rules for combining them just from being around people who talk.
  - **Reading and writing** follow a different path. A child may memorize the visual shapes of the letters early, but reading and writing whole words usually comes at **ages 6 to 7** and requires a great deal of instruction.
  - The contrast between the two is the point. Speech uses hidden organs the learner cannot see, yet it develops almost on its own. Reading and writing use the eyes and hands, which are fully visible, yet they need years of teaching on purpose. So the hard-to-see skill is the easy one to learn. Speech is a deep human cognitive skill that the brain is prepared to acquire.
- **Articulatory phonetics** is the field that studies the movements of the different structures of the vocal tract. The **vocal tract** is the full air passage used in speech, from the larynx up through the throat and mouth to the lips, with a side branch into the nose.
  - Articulatory phonetics asks which structures move, where they move to, and how those movements create the different sounds of a language. Real-time MRI is one of its main tools.
- **In the last two years, voice assistants and other speech technology have come to emulate human speech ability, which means they copy what people can do,** in ways almost no one thought possible. To **emulate** an ability means to copy its behavior closely enough that the result looks the same. These systems now produce natural-sounding speech and recognize spoken language with high accuracy.
  - **LING 285 is about both human speech and AI speech.** One side studies how people produce and perceive speech. The other side studies how modern AI systems model that same ability, so the human skill and the machine version can be compared directly.

## p4
- **The next step is to focus on the air between the tongue and the rest of the vocal tract, not on the tongue and lips themselves.** In the real vocal tract that air space bends. It runs down the throat and then forward through the mouth. If we ignore the curve and straighten the passage, the vocal tract becomes a simple **tube of air**.
  - One end of the tube is the **lips**, where the passage is open to the outside air. The other end is the **larynx** deep in the throat, where the tube is treated as closed. The walls of the tube stand for the tongue and the other surfaces around the air space.
  - The tube is labeled **schwa**, the neutral relaxed vowel, the "uh" sound at the start of "about." For schwa the tongue sits in a central resting position and does not narrow the passage much anywhere. So the air space is about the same width from end to end. This is why schwa fits the straight even tube best.
- **The air inside the tube vibrates by shifting back and forth, not by flowing out the end.** Each small bit of air is drawn as a blue circle, and each column of circles shifts back and forth around its own resting position.
  - Where neighboring columns move toward each other, the air is squeezed together into a **compression**, a region where the air is packed more tightly.
  - Where neighboring columns move apart, the air spreads out into a **rarefaction**, a region where the air is packed more loosely.
  - These regions of packing and spreading travel along the tube and reflect back from its ends. This repeating pattern is the **vibration of air** that makes up the sound of the vowel.
- **Acoustic phonetics** is the field that studies how air vibrates in the vocal tract and what sound those vibrations produce. Articulatory phonetics describes the shape of the tube, and acoustic phonetics describes the physics of the air inside it. The two fields connect directly. Moving the tongue and lips changes the shape of the tube. Changing the shape of the tube changes how the air inside it vibrates.

## p5
- **AI brings real psychological, economic, social and environmental harms, and many scientists also warn about existential harm**, meaning the possibility that advanced AI could threaten humanity's survival or basic way of life. It is tempting to ignore AI and act as if it did not exist. Pretending it does not exist is not a workable option, though.
  - AI exists, with many harms and some benefits. Two examples of benefits are **immunotherapy**, which uses the body's own immune system to fight diseases such as cancer, and **environmental engineering**, the design of solutions to environmental problems.
  - The environmental case cuts both ways, as in the headline asking whether AI will ruin the planet or save it. Running large AI systems uses enormous amounts of energy. At the same time, AI tools are being applied to engineering problems that could help the environment.
- **One of the biggest dangers is that we have no idea how AI works.** The phrase is "we know everything, and understand nothing."
  - "We know everything" means every number and every calculation inside an AI system can be inspected, because people built the system and can look at all of its parts.
  - "We understand nothing" means that no one can explain why the system produces the particular answers it does. This is true even with all of those details in hand.
  - The number of people who know how these systems operate is very, very small. Having the knowledge held by so few people is a danger in itself. This is because decisions about how AI is built and used end up in very few hands.
- **AI literacy is necessary for many groups of people.** AI literacy means a working understanding of what AI systems are and how they behave.
  - Scientists who use AI need it to judge whether their results are trustworthy. Politicians who need to control AI need it to write sensible rules. Lawyers who litigate cases involving AI need it to argue about what a system did and why.
  - The course is built on the belief that a creative thinker in a class like this one can help us understand these systems well enough to benefit from them while avoiding their negative consequences. Understanding is how we get the benefits and keep the harms small.

## p7
- **Diotima's ladder describes the climb from surface knowledge of particular things toward deep understanding of the general ideas behind them.** Diotima, dated here to around 440 BCE, is the teacher who in Plato's Symposium teaches Socrates about this ladder. A chain of teachers runs from her through Greek philosophy, from Diotima to **Socrates**, to **Plato**, to **Aristotle**.
- **The bottom of the ladder holds knowledge that comes from routine and from copying what already exists.** This includes **daily experience, habit and memorization**, along with **specialized skills, repetition and replication**, and learning from **textbooks**.
  - Thinking at this level follows the rule that **similar is same, and slight difference is different**. Things that look alike on the surface are treated as identical. Things that look even a little different are treated as unrelated.
- **The top of the ladder holds creativity, insight, thought, focus, attention, imagination and vision**, along with **problem-posing, problem-solving, critical reading and creative writing**.
  - Thinking at this level follows the reversed rule that **different can be same, and similar can be different**. Things that look different on the surface can share the same deeper structure. Things that look alike can turn out to be fundamentally different.
  - Speech gives a direct example. The sounds f and v sound different, yet they are made in the same place in the mouth, so in that respect they are the same. The th in "thing" and the th in "that" are spelled identically, yet one uses vocal fold vibration and the other does not, so they are different sounds.
  - **Problem-posing** means asking good questions in the first place. **Problem-solving** means answering questions once they have been asked.
- **A person climbs the ladder through repeated cycles of question and answer.** Each time a question is asked and an answer is worked out, understanding goes up one step. The cycles repeat again and again from the bottom to the top. This is the method Socrates became famous for, and it traces back to Diotima's teaching of him.
- **For about 2500 years, people could fake it.** They could do well in school and at work without climbing the ladder. All they had to do was memorize, repeat and replicate. In 2026 that is no longer true. In interviews for jobs and for graduate school, what matters now is whether a person shows the properties at the top of the ladder, such as creativity, insight, problem-posing, and the ability to see when different things are the same and similar things are different.

## p8
- **The quickest way to understand speech sounds is to produce them and pay attention to what the body does.** The exercise uses four pairs of sounds. Each pair is held as one long continuous sound. Partway through, it switches from the first sound to the second.
  - Say the **f** in "fat" and hold it, then switch to the **v** in "vat." Say the **th** in "thing" and hold it, then switch to the **th** in "that." Say the **s** in "sing," then switch to the **z** in "zing." Say the **sh** in "mesh," then switch to the sound written **j** here, the soft buzzing sound in the middle of "beige," not the j in "jump."
- **Within each pair, the difference is voice, which is whether the vocal folds vibrate.** Resting the fingers lightly on the front of the throat while switching makes the change easy to feel.
  - During f, the first th, s and sh, the throat stays still and the sound is a pure hiss of air. During v, the second th, z and the sound in "beige," a buzzing vibration appears in the throat and the sound gets a humming feel to it.
  - The **vocal folds** are two small folds of tissue inside the larynx. When they are held apart, air passes through silently, and the sound is **voiceless**. When they are brought close together, the air flowing between them makes them vibrate rapidly. The sound is then **voiced**.
  - Within each pair, the tongue and lips stay in the same position the whole time. Only the vocal folds change. So voicing is controlled separately from the mouth.
- **From one line to the next, the difference is place of articulation, which is where the narrowing that makes the sound is formed.**
  - For f and v, the lower lip presses lightly against the upper front teeth. For both th sounds, the tip of the tongue touches the upper front teeth. For s and z, the tongue tip comes close to the ridge just behind the upper front teeth. For sh and the sound in "beige," the tongue pulls a bit further back, behind that ridge.
  - Moving from one line to the next moves the narrowing steadily further back in the mouth.
- **Voice and place of articulation are two separate, independent properties of a speech sound.** Every sound in the exercise can be described by stating both, and changing one while keeping the other fixed produces a different sound.

## p9
- **All of these sounds are fricatives.** A fricative is made by bringing two parts of the vocal tract very close together without closing the passage completely. Air pushed from the lungs is forced through the narrow gap. Passing through such a small opening makes the flow rough and chaotic. That rough flow produces the hissing or buzzing noise typical of fricatives. The sound can be held for as long as there is breath.
- **The fricative table is organized by voice in the rows and place of articulation in the columns.** The places are **labial, dental, alveolar and postalveolar**. The voiceless row holds f, th, s and sh. The voiced row holds v, th, z and j.
  - **Labial** means made with the lips. For f and v, the lower lip rises to touch the upper front teeth, and air hisses through the gaps between them. In the MRI, the lower lip is tucked up under the upper teeth while the tongue lies low and relaxed.
  - **Dental** means made at the teeth. For the two th sounds, the tip of the tongue touches or rests just between the upper front teeth. In the MRI, the front of the tongue is pushed forward right up to the teeth.
  - **Alveolar** means made at the **alveolar ridge**, the small bony ridge on the roof of the mouth just behind the upper front teeth. For s and z, the tongue tip rises to form a narrow groove close to that ridge.
  - **Postalveolar** means made just behind the alveolar ridge. For sh and the sound in "beige," a wider section of the front of the tongue rises toward the roof of the mouth a little further back than for s, and the lips often push slightly forward.
- **Two fricatives in the same column differ only in voicing, and two fricatives in the same row differ only in place of articulation.** The two th sounds make the point sharply, since English spells them the same way even though one is voiceless and the other is voiced.

## p10
- **Stops are made by closing the passage completely for a brief moment**, so that air cannot escape through the mouth at all. This is different from a fricative, which leaves a narrow gap open.
  - While the closure is held, air from the lungs keeps pushing into the mouth. Pressure builds up behind the closure. When the closure is released, the trapped air bursts out suddenly. That burst is part of what makes a stop sound short and sharp.
  - A stop cannot be held as a continuous sound, because the sound depends on the closing and the release.
- **The English stops form a grid of three places of articulation and three types.** Reading across a row keeps the place fixed and changes only voicing and whether the nose is open. Reading down a column keeps the type fixed and changes only the place of closure.
  - The first row is **p, b and m**, all made by closing the **lips**. For p, the lips press together and the vocal folds stay still. For b, the lips close the same way but the vocal folds vibrate. For m, the lips close, the vocal folds vibrate, and the **soft palate lowers**, so air flows out through the nose the whole time the lips are closed.
  - The second row is **t, d and n**, all made by pressing the tongue tip against the **alveolar ridge**, the same place used for s and z. For t the vocal folds stay still, for d they vibrate, and for n they vibrate while the soft palate lowers so air escapes through the nose.
  - The third row is **k, g and ng**, all made by raising the **back of the tongue** against the **soft palate**. For k the vocal folds stay still, for g they vibrate, and for ng, the sound at the end of "sing," they vibrate while the passage to the nose opens.
- **The columns follow the same logic as the fricative table.** The first column, **p, t and k**, holds the **voiceless** stops. The second column, **b, d and g**, holds the **voiced** stops. The third column, **m, n and ng**, holds the **nasal** stops, which are voiced and send air through the nose.
  - A sound made with the soft palate lowered is called a **nasal**, and the MRI of m shows the gap opened between the soft palate and the back of the throat.
  - Nine different sounds come from combining just three places of closure with three settings of the vocal folds and soft palate. So a small number of independent movements builds a large set of speech sounds.
