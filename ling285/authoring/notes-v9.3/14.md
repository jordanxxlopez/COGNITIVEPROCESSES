## p2
- Speech uses **two main vibrating systems**. The first is the **vocal folds**. They form a **membrane** that is **fixed on all sides** and made of many masses and springs. The vocal folds vibrate in many modes at once. In Mode 1, all the masses move up and down together. In Mode 2, half move up while the other half move down. Higher modes have more complicated patterns. All five modes add together into one combined motion.
  - The vocal folds are a fixed-fixed system, so their frequencies are **harmonics**. Harmonics are whole-number multiples of the lowest frequency.
  - The **lowest harmonic** is called **F0**. Each person has a different F0, but the other harmonics are always multiples of it.

## p3
- The second system is the **air in the vocal tract**. It is **fixed at the glottis**, because the closed vocal folds stop the air there from moving back and forth. It is **free at the lips**, where the air can move in and out. Because one end is fixed and the other is free, only the **odd multiples** occur. So its frequencies are one, three, five and seven times the lowest one.

## p4
- Each fixed-free mode is built from **quarter-wavelength packets**, which are pieces that are each a quarter of a wave long. Each piece runs from no motion at a still point to the most motion at a moving point. **Mode 1 holds one piece, Mode 2 holds three, Mode 3 holds five and Mode 4 holds seven.**
  - Two pieces would end with a still point at the free end, which cannot happen. So only odd numbers of pieces fit, and that is why the formants are odd multiples of the first.

## p5
- In the vocal tract, the air moves **longitudinally**, which means back and forth along the tube, not up and down. Each formant is one mode of this back-and-forth motion. F1 is the lowest mode, and F2 is the next one.
  - These modes look like a building's modes turned on their side. The fixed glottis end is on the left, and the free lip end is on the right.

## p6
- **In every mode, the air at the closed end is always springy, and the air at the open end is always moving.** Air can be squeezed against a closed end, but at an open end there is nothing to squeeze it against.
  - Near the **closed end**, the thin slices of air come very close together and then spread far apart. So the air **compresses and expands**, and it acts like a **spring**.
  - Near the **open end**, the slices stay the same distance apart and simply move back and forth. So the air acts like a **mass**.
  - In plain terms, the lip end swings like a heavy door, and the throat end squeezes like a pillow.
- In higher modes, springy places and moving places **take turns** more and more times along the tube.

## p7
- You can work out what a constriction does at any spot, in any mode, using the same steps. Take a spot inside the tube where, in **Mode 3**, the lines of air bunch up and spread apart but hardly move back and forth. Squeezing the tract at that spot raises **F3**.
  - Step one is that squeezing air always **adds both mass and stiffness**, just as squeezing a tire makes it stiffer.
  - Step two is that at this spot in Mode 3, the air is acting like a **pure spring**. It compresses and expands but does not move back and forth, so the extra mass does not matter.
  - Step three is that only the extra stiffness counts, and **more stiffness raises the frequency**, so **F3 goes up**.
- Do not memorize the answer. **Build it one step at a time**, with each step based on the one before it. That is how any good argument is built, in science or in art.

## p8
- Images taken while people speak show where each vowel squeezes the air. The tube runs from the closed glottis to the open lips.
  - The vowel in **done** has **no constriction** at all.
  - **dean** squeezes the air near the front, about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - **Don** squeezes the air far back in the throat, about **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - **dune** squeezes the air **at the lips** by rounding them. **Dan** is a low front vowel.

## p9
- A constriction changes two things about the air. It changes the air's **mass**, which is how hard the air is to start or stop moving. It also changes the air's **stiffness**, which is how hard the air pushes back when it is squeezed. In the mass-spring model, a constriction is drawn as **heavier masses and stiffer springs** at that spot.
  - A constriction always raises both. But moving places and springy places take turns along the tube, so **its effect depends on what the air is doing at that spot in each mode**.
  - **When stiffness is what matters at a spot, the frequency goes up. When mass is what matters, the frequency goes down.**

## p10
- The same location in the tube can act differently in the two modes, so you have to check each mode separately. A spot can be moving in Mode 1 and springy in Mode 2. Then one squeeze lowers F1 and raises F2 at the same time.
  - That is why a vowel is defined by its pair of formants, not by one formant alone.

## p11
- **/u/** squeezes the air at the lips. At the open end, the air moves back and forth and is hardly compressed in **every** mode, so the air there acts like a pure mass. So the extra stiffness does not matter, but the extra mass does, and **F1 and F2 both go down**.
  - Schwa's formants are about **500 and 1500 hertz**. A measured /u/ had an F1 in the low two hundreds, well below 500.

## p12
- **/i/** squeezes the air about two thirds of the way from the glottis.
  - In **Mode 1**, the air at that spot moves back and forth, so it acts like a mass. The extra mass counts, and **F1 goes down**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the air at that spot compresses and expands, so it acts like a spring. The extra stiffness counts, and **F2 goes up**.
  - A speaker moving from schwa into /i/ showed exactly this, with **F1 going down and F2 going up**.

## p13
- **/a/** squeezes the air about one third of the way from the glottis.
  - In **Mode 1**, that spot is close to the closed end, so the air there is mostly springy. The extra stiffness counts, and **F1 goes up**.
  - In **Mode 2**, that spot sits between two springy regions, so the air there is mostly moving. The extra mass counts, and **F2 goes down**.
  - A speaker saying /a/ showed **F1 going up and F2 going down**. In a typical /a/, F1 and F2 are fairly close together.

## p14
- The logic is the same for all three vowels. **First, find out whether the air at the squeeze is mostly moving or mostly compressing in each mode. Then decide whether the extra mass or the extra stiffness counts.**
  - At the lips, the air is moving in both modes, so /u/ lowers both formants.
  - Two thirds of the way along, the air is moving in Mode 1 and springy in Mode 2, so /i/ lowers F1 and raises F2.
  - One third of the way along, the air is springy in Mode 1 and moving in Mode 2, so /a/ raises F1 and lowers F2.

## p15
- The vocal folds are the **source**. Their mass and stiffness set **F0**, and F0 sets the spacing of the harmonics.
  - **Big, heavy, loose vocal folds** have a lot of mass and little stiffness, so their **F0 is low**, around 100 hertz, with harmonics at 200, 300 and so on.
  - **Tighter, lighter vocal folds** have a higher F0, such as 250 hertz. **Very tight, light vocal folds** have an even higher F0, such as 350 hertz.
- **F0 does not stay the same while people talk.** Speakers keep changing F0 by tightening and loosening their vocal folds, and listeners use those changes to understand meaning.
  - When **The dog slept here** is said as a statement, F0 falls at the very end. When the same words are said as a question, F0 rises at the end.
  - The way F0 moves also tells listeners whether a person is being sarcastic, pretending, or even lying.

## p16
- The air in the vocal tract is the **filter**. Its formants depend on **where the squeeze is**. Schwa has F1 near 500 and F2 near 1500. /u/ has lower formants. /i/ has F1 and F2 very far apart, and /a/ has them very close together.
- **The core rule is that a formant makes the harmonics closest to it louder, and harmonics far from any formant come out quieter.**
  - **The shape of the vowel is the filter. The vocal folds supply the harmonics, and the filter decides which harmonics come through loudly.**

## p17
- **The lower F0 is, the more harmonics the formants can shape.** When harmonics are every 100 hertz, many harmonics sit under each formant. So the spectrum clearly shows the formant peaks.
- With very light, tight vocal folds, a formant at 500 hertz may have **no harmonic near it to make louder**. With so few harmonics, a formant can fall between them and get missed, or it can be hard to tell where the formant is.
  - An **opera singer** is an extreme example, which is why sung vowels are hard to identify. Singers are even trained to shape the mouth so the formants line up with the harmonics.
  - A **small child** has an F0 near 500 hertz, so very few harmonics fall near the formants. That is why only the people who take care of the child understand the child's speech easily.
  - A simple way to see it is that the formants are the same in every case, but a high voice gives the listener only a few harmonics to hear them through.

## p18
- A **spectrogram** lines up many spectra side by side over time. **Time** goes along the horizontal axis, **frequency** goes up the vertical axis, and **darkness** shows how much energy, or amplitude, there is.
- You can measure F0 in **Praat**. Take a small piece of speech, look at its spectrum, and pick the lowest harmonic. One measured voice had an F0 of about 170 hertz, and the next harmonic was at about twice that value.

## p19
- Saying **Where were you a year ago?** brings every idea about speech together in one short sentence. The vocal folds supply harmonics the whole time. Every new sound changes where the tongue and lips squeeze the air, so the formants keep moving from one moment to the next.
  - On a spectrogram of the sentence, **time** goes along the bottom, **frequency** goes up, and the **dark bands are the formants**.
  - Where the vowel sounds like **/i/**, **F2 rises high and F1 stays low**. This happens because the squeeze lands where Mode 2 is springy and Mode 1 is moving.
  - During **you**, the formants **drop**. Rounding the lips adds mass right where the air moves the most, and that lowers all of them.
  - Through **a year ago**, F2 goes up and then down again as the tongue moves from one vowel to the next.
- **The formants are always changing during speech, so speech is not a string of frozen vowels.** You can predict every rise and fall of a formant with the same mass and spring reasoning. Ask where the squeeze is, and whether the air there is moving or compressing in each mode.
  - This constant change is why a network that sees two formants from one frozen moment cannot understand real speech. Speech recognition needs systems that take in whole stretches of sound over time.
  - In plain terms, a spectrogram of a real sentence is a moving record of the tongue and lips. To read it, you follow the dark bands as they rise and fall.

## p20
- A recording that was cut and joined back together shows how complicated speech perception is. The **m** part of a recorded **ma** was joined to the **a** part of a recorded **na**. Listeners clearly heard **na**, not ma.
  - **Whether listeners hear m or n depends more on the vowel than on the nasal sound itself.** After m, one formant of the vowel stays fairly **flat**. After n, that formant shows a **big drop**.
  - So listeners decide which consonant they heard **during the vowel**, after the consonant is already over. This was one of the first surprising results in speech perception, published in the **1950s** by the linguist **André Malécot**.
- Listeners **take in whole stretches of speech and then decide** about the sounds that came first. For about seventy years, speech recognition machines that treated each slice of sound separately **failed**. Modern speech recognition uses large language models, which use what comes later to decide about what came before.

## p21
- A **neural network** can be trained to recognize vowels from formants. Its **two inputs** are **F1 and F2**, measured from real recordings of five vowels. Its job is to decide which vowel was spoken.
  - **The network's knowledge is stored in its weights**, and the weights are learned from the data.
  - Information enters at the input, a little like sound reaching the ear. Each hidden layer turns the numbers it receives into a new set of numbers, a little like the stages of processing in the brain.

## p22
- The network has **five output neurons**, one for each word, **done, dune, dean, Don and Dan**. It has **three hidden layers** with **3, 2 and 4 neurons**. Every neuron in one layer connects to every neuron in the next layer, so the network is **fully connected**.
  - This version is **purely linear**, which means it has **no activation functions**. Each neuron just passes on the result of its inner product.

## p23
- Before training, **every weight is a random number between minus one and one**. A **positive** weight makes its input push the next neuron up. A **negative** weight makes its input push the next neuron down.
  - The example input is **F1 of 320 and F2 of 2000 hertz**. That is a very low F1 with a very high F2, which is the pattern of **/i/**, so the correct answer is **dean**.

## p24
- **Forward propagation** carries the input through the network one layer at a time. Each neuron computes an **inner product**, which means it multiplies each incoming number by its weight and adds the results.
  - For the top neuron of the first hidden layer, F1 is multiplied by its weight, F2 is multiplied by its weight, and the two results are added. That sum is the neuron's activation.
  - The other neurons do the same with their own weights. So the first hidden layer produces three numbers. These go through the next weights to make two numbers, then four, and then five at the output.
  - You never need to do these calculations by hand. What matters is that every step is an inner product.
- The output is a **probability distribution** over the five words, with values of **.5, .1, .1, .1 and .2**, which add up to one. With random weights, the network gives its highest probability to **done**, so it is wrong.
  - The correct answer is given as the target distribution **0, 0, 1, 0, 0**. **Stochastic gradient descent** then changes each weight, so that next time the inner products give a high probability to dean.
  - In plain terms, the network starts out guessing. The target tells it what the right guess was, and learning nudges every weight so the right guess wins next time.

## p25
- **PyTorch** describes the network as a list of layers. **Sequential** means the network has **one layer after another, in order**. **Linear** means the layer **does inner products** of the weights and the activations from the layer before.
  - **nn.Linear(2, 3)** means **take input from 2 neurons, and have 3 neurons**. The next lines read 3 in and 2 out, then 2 in and 4 out, then 4 in and 5 out.
  - Setting the **bias** to False keeps every layer purely linear, which matches the diagram of the network.
- **Only layers that do calculations get a line of code.** The **input layer** has no weights coming into it and does no inner products. So it never gets its own line, and the first line describes the **first hidden layer**.
  - This network has **five layers in total** but only **three hidden layers**. The **last line is always the output layer**, so read the question carefully.
- To **add a hidden layer of 3 neurons right before the output**, change the old last line from 4 in and 5 out to **4 in and 3 out**. Then add a new last line that takes **3 in and gives 5 out**.
  - A quick check is that the first number on each line must match the second number on the line before it.
- Suppose a network is written as 4 in and 3 out, 3 in and 8 out, 8 in and 4 out, 4 in and 4 out, and 4 in and 5 out. This network takes **4 numbers as input**, has **four hidden layers** with 3, 8, 4 and 4 neurons, and chooses among **5 categories**.
  - When the first category is correct, the target distribution has a **1 in the first slot and 0 everywhere else**.
