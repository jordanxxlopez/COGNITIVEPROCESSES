## p3
- **Real-time MRI** films the inside of the head during speech in a **midsagittal** view, a slice straight down the middle of the head with the face to the left. Air shows black and soft tissue gray or white, so the dark spaces are the open airways, the mouth, the nasal passages and the **pharynx** behind the tongue.
  - The **tongue** fills most of the mouth. Above it is the bony **hard palate**, and hanging from its back is the **velum**, or soft palate, which closes or opens the route from the throat into the nose.
- The tongue, lips, jaw and velum move at once and very quickly. Every consonant and vowel comes from shaping this tube of air in a particular way, and **phonetics** is the study of these sounds.

## p4
- A **fricative** is made by bringing two parts of the mouth close together without closing them. Air forced through the gap becomes turbulent, which gives the hiss or buzz, and the sound can be held as long as there is breath.
- Fricatives are arranged by **place of articulation**, where the gap is, and **voicing**, whether the **vocal folds** vibrate. **Voiceless** f, th (as in "think"), s and sh have only the hiss. **Voiced** v, th (as in "this"), z and j (the sound in "measure") add a buzz that a hand on the throat can feel.
  - **Labial** f and v raise the lower lip to the upper teeth. **Dental** th sounds put the tongue tip at the teeth. **Alveolar** s and z raise the tongue tip toward the **alveolar ridge** behind the upper teeth. **Postalveolar** sh and j form the gap a little farther back.
  - Each pair shares its place and manner. Only vocal fold vibration separates f from v, or s from z. The four MRI images show the constriction stepping backward from the lips to behind the ridge.

## p5
- A **stop** closes the vocal tract completely. Pressure builds behind the closure and bursts out at the release. English has **oral stops** and **nasal stops**.
  - In an **oral stop**, or **plosive**, the velum is raised to seal the nose, so pressure builds. In a **nasal stop**, the mouth closes the same way but the velum is lowered, so air flows out through the nose and the sound hums.
- Each of three places has a voiceless oral stop, a voiced oral stop and a nasal stop. At the **lips**, p, b and m. At the **alveolar ridge**, t, d and n. At the **velum**, k, g and ng, the **velar** sounds. In the MRI images, the nasals m, n and ng have the velum hanging open, while p, t and k have it raised. A stop is an event in time, closure, then pressure or nasal airflow, then release.

## p6
- The fricatives and stops form one table built on **manner of articulation**, **place of articulation** and **voice**. Manner is how the air is shaped, a gap, a sealed closure or a closure with the nose open. The places run from front to back, **labial, dental, alveolar, postalveolar** and **velar**.
- Every consonant is a combination of three values, so t is a **voiceless alveolar oral stop** and z is a **voiced alveolar fricative**. Changing one value changes the sound, from t to d by adding voice and from d to n by opening the nose.
  - The empty cells matter too. English has no dental or postalveolar stops and no velar fricative. The sounds form a **highly structured system** that every speaker uses without ever being taught the table.

## p7
- **Artificial intelligence** is a model of five psychological skills animals have, **knowing, recognizing, attending, acting** and **learning**. Knowing is stored usable information, recognizing is identifying what arrives, attending is selecting what to focus on, acting is producing an output, and learning is changing with experience.
- How AI models each skill is at least slightly based on how humans do it. AI is not a technical innovation that came out of nowhere. It grew out of ideas about human and animal psychology.

## p8
- How AI knows connects to **Wilson Taylor's** 1953 paper "Cloze Procedure," subtitled A New Tool for Measuring Readability, in the Fall 1953 issue of **Journalism Quarterly**. It reported three pilot studies and two experiments comparing cloze results with two **readability formulas**, which estimate how easy a text is to read.
- Taylor asked whether people's ability to restore deleted parts of a message could measure how readable that message was. A tool built to measure human understanding of language ended up describing what it means for a system to know.

## p9
- Taylor called cloze procedure a new psychological tool for measuring the effectiveness of communication. The method is straightforward, the data are easily counted, and the results agreed with the **Flesch** and **Dale-Chall** readability formulas. He credited the experimental psychologists **Charles Osgood** and **Melvin Marks** for starting the work, with advice from Lee Cronbach, Wilbur Schramm, Charles Swanson, George Klare, Kalmer Stordahl and Clifford Christensen.
- The unit of measurement is a **cloze**, pronounced like "close," named after **closure**, the Gestalt psychology term for completing a familiar pattern that is not quite finished, such as seeing a broken circle as whole.
  - The three black circles with wedges cut out make a bright white triangle appear even though no triangle is drawn. The visual system completes it from partial information.

## p10
- The same principle applies to language. Given "Chickens cackle and ___ quack," almost anyone supplies "ducks," and supplying the removed word scores **one cloze unit**. Filling the blank takes the **meanings** and **forms** of all five words, the meanings of their **combinations**, and the parallel structure that pairs an animal with its sound.
- Taylor defined a **cloze unit** as a successful attempt to reproduce a deleted part of a message by deciding from the remaining context what is missing. **Cloze procedure** intercepts a message from a transmitter, deletes parts, and gives the damaged message to receivers, whose repairs produce countable cloze units.
- AI shows its knowledge by completing, or clozing, sequences, as a teacher tests a student by asking for the rest of a sequence. From the AI point of view, **knowledge is nothing more than completion**.

## p11
- **GPT** takes a sequence of words and predicts the next one. To a computer, a word is **a bunch of random numbers**, with the one rule that no two words share the same numbers. Such a list is a **vector**, and one word's vector could hold 1024 numbers, drawn as a tall bar for each of "chickens," "cackle," "and" and "ducks."
- For "chickens cackle and ducks ___," a system that knows English outputs a vector of 10,000 numbers, each the probability that one English word comes next. "Quack" gets a high probability while "cat" and "Zulu" get very low ones. The system's knowledge shows up entirely in how well it fills the blank.

## p12
- The list of probabilities is a **pdf**, a probability distribution function, and the input vectors are the **x's**. GPT turns the x's into the pdf by **recognizing by computing**, mostly **addition, subtraction, multiplication and division**, grade school arithmetic repeated an enormous number of times.
- The knowledge that does this transformation sits in numbers called **w's**, or **weights**. The x's change with every sentence, the w's stay fixed while the system is used, and changing the weights changes the knowledge. Today's systems have **about a couple of trillion weights**.

## p13
- Trillions of weights are not too many to resemble human knowledge. Knowing that chickens cackle but ducks quack is stored in the **synapses**, the connections between the brain's **neurons**, and the brain has about **150 trillion** of them, roughly 75 times the 2 trillion weights of today's AI systems. In both cases knowledge is stored in connection strengths, not in a list of facts.

## p16
- The ordinary way to average 10, 12 and 14 is to add them and divide by three, giving 12. That direct method fails for speech, language, biological molecules and scenes, where no one-step formula exists.
- The AI way starts with a **wild guess** x and improves it through exposure to the data. Take one number, compare it with x, move x a bit closer, and repeat. This is **observation-based error learning**.

## p17
- SGD finds the average, the essence, of [78, 67, 98, 85, 76, 68, 98, 72], whose true value is 80.25, without ever doing that calculation. Pick a random estimate, so x[0] is 130. Pick a random data point, so d[0] is 98. Measure the **Loss**, 130 minus 98, which is 32. Subtract a small part of the Loss, 10 percent, so x[1] is 126.8. Repeat with another data point.
  - If the estimate starts too low, the Loss is negative and subtracting it raises the estimate. From 10 with a draw of 78, the estimate rises by 6.8 to 16.8. The estimate is always pushed toward the data.
- **Stochastic** means random, **gradient** refers to the Loss, and **descent** means reducing. The algorithm makes an initial guess and uses data to improve it.

## p18
- The fraction of the Loss subtracted each step is the **learning rate**. Four graphs plot the estimate against 300 **epochs** for rates of 10, 50, 1 and 80 percent, with runs from **initial conditions** of 130, 80 and 10.
  - At 10 percent the runs reach about 80 within 25 to 50 epochs and then wobble slightly. At 1 percent they are smooth but still short of 80 after 300 epochs. At 50 percent they arrive within a few epochs and then bounce between about 70 and 95. At 80 percent the bouncing spreads from about 65 to 97.
  - The bouncing comes from the random single data points. A large rate moves the estimate most of the way toward whichever number was drawn, so a draw of 98 from 80 at 80 percent jumps the estimate to 94.4.
