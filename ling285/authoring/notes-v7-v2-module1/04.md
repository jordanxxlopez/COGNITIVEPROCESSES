## p3
- The **shortest path problem** asks for the cheapest route through a network from a start to an end. Each circle is a **state**, each arrow is a possible move, and each arrow carries a **cost**. The total cost of a route is the sum of its arrow costs.
- The network runs from **A** to **J** through **stages**, so every route makes exactly four decisions. From A, B costs 12 and C costs 7. From B, D, E and F cost 5, 6 and 9, and from C they cost 14, 10 and 11. From D, G, H and I cost 8, 7 and 10, from E 9, 7 and 9, and from F 10, 7 and 8. G, H and I reach J for 5, 9 and 8.
  - The network models **decision-making over time**. Every choice has an immediate cost and also controls which future costs are available, so a choice that looks good now can lock in expensive moves later.

## p4
- A **greedy algorithm** takes the cheapest arrow out of the current state and never looks ahead. From A it takes C for 7, from C it takes E for 10, from E it takes H for 7, and from H it must pay 9 to reach J. The greedy route A, C, E, H, J costs 33, while A, B, D, G, J costs 30 despite its first move of 12.
- **Greed is not good** here. The cheap move to C led into a costly part of the network and ended at H with the large final 9. Greed cannot know about later costs.
  - A tiny network makes this plain. The top route costs 10 then 10, the bottom route 2 then 100. Greed picks 2 and pays 102 instead of 20. Writing inside each middle state the cost of the rest of the journey, 10 or 100, removes the blindness of greed.

## p6
- An **exhaustive algorithm** lists every route, totals each one, and picks the smallest, so its answer is **guaranteed correct**. Here there are 2 times 3 times 3 times 1, or 18 routes, needing 54 additions and 18 comparisons.
- Its problem is growth. Every added stage multiplies the number of routes, so with many stages and states, checking every route takes a very, very long time. Exhaustive search is accurate but impractical, greedy search is fast but can be wrong, and a method is needed that is both.

## p7
- **Bellman** began with an imaginary step. Suppose the best costs from B to J and from C to J were known, and call them VBJ and VCJ. V stands for **Value**, the lowest total cost from a state to the goal. Then VAJ is the **minimum** of 12 plus VBJ and 7 plus VCJ. Each number in the set is one arrow cost plus the Value of the state the arrow leads to.

## p8
- The difficulty is that VBJ and VCJ are not known. Bellman's strategy is to **decompose a problem into subproblems you can solve**. Finding VAJ becomes finding VBJ and VCJ, and if those cannot be solved directly, they are broken down again.

## p9
- The same reasoning applies at B. If the best costs from D, E and F to J were known, VBJ would be the minimum of 5 plus VDJ, 6 plus VEJ and 9 plus VFJ, and VCJ the minimum of 14 plus VDJ, 10 plus VEJ and 11 plus VFJ. Those Values depend in turn on G, H and I, whose Values are trivial, since each has one arrow to J. VGJ is 5, VHJ is 9, and VIJ is 8.
  - The rules form a chain from the end of the network back to the start, so information about the cost of reaching J travels **backward** one stage at a time.

## p10
- With G, H and I known, the Values are calculated stage by stage. VDJ is the minimum of 13, 16 and 18, so 13. VEJ is 14 and VFJ is 15, all through G. VBJ is the minimum of 18, 20 and 24, so 18, and VCJ is the minimum of 27, 24 and 26, so 24. VAJ is the minimum of 30 and 31, so 30.
- Now **go greedy on the Values, not the costs**. At A, B gives 30 and C gives 31, so go to B. At B, D gives 18, so go to D. At D, G gives 13, so go to G, then J. The best path is **A, B, D, G, J**, total 30. **Dynamic programming** is this two-part method, Values backward from the goal, then greedy choices on the Values.

## p11
- **Dynamic programming is foresight.** From D, E or F the cheapest arrow is always the 7 into H, but H leads to something terrible, the final 9, so H has the largest Value in its stage. At E, H gives 16 while G gives 14. The Value of H warns about the 9 before it is reached, however many stages away it lies.
- **The Values contain the future.** A late obstacle made the early greedy choice of C a bad path. Because each Value already includes every obstacle ahead, going greedy on the Values makes present decisions optimal. **Søren Kierkegaard** wrote that life can only be understood backwards but must be lived forwards, and dynamic programming has that structure.

## p12
- Dynamic programming poses a problem, its subproblems and its sub-subproblems, down to problems that are **trivial**. VAJ needs VBJ and VCJ, which need VDJ, VEJ and VFJ, which need VGJ, VHJ and VIJ, which are single arrow costs. Then the bigger problems are solved one level at a time back up to the original.

## p13
- The Values are **propagated** from each stage to the stage before it by **Bellman's rule**. The Value at one state is the minimum of a set of numbers, each the cost to one of the next states plus the Value at that state. Start at the stage before the goal, apply the rule stage by stage, and stop when A has its Value. Each Value is calculated once and reused, such as VDJ of 13 feeding both the B rule and the C rule, which keeps the method efficient.

## p14
- Go through the bad 12 first to get onto a route that does not get stuck later at the 9. It may seem to take a big brain to weigh the whole future against the present.
- A **lifeguard** faces the same trade-off. On sand alone, running at a **constant speed**, the fastest route is the **shortest path**, a straight line. With the person in the water, running is fast and swimming is slow, so the **path of least time** runs farther along the sand and swims a shorter distance, bending at the water's edge. A longer run now cuts the costly swim later, like paying 12 early to avoid the 9.

## p15
- **Decision-making is like physics.** In **refraction**, light moves faster in air and slower in water, so its path bends at the surface just as the lifeguard's route bends, which is why a pencil in water looks broken at the water line. Light has no brain, yet it takes the least-time route, so accepting an early cost to avoid a larger later one does not require a big brain. Physical systems find these optimal paths on their own.

- A learning process plotted against **epochs** with a 10 percent **learning rate** settles near 80 from any **initial condition**. Runs starting at 130, 80 and 10 meet within about 25 to 50 epochs and then fluctuate together in a narrow band. The change is fastest when x is far from 80 and slows as it gets closer.

In both systems many starting values move toward the same final value, and the speed of change depends on the distance from it.
