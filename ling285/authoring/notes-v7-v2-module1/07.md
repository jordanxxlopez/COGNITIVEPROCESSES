## p2
- A **harmonic oscillator** is a dynamical system with two variables that feed into each other, **position** x and **velocity** v. A dot means change over time, so ẋ is the rate at which position changes and v̇ the rate at which velocity changes. The first rule is that ẋ equals v, so velocity drives position, the red arrow from v to x. The second rule is that v̇ equals minus x, so position drives velocity with a minus sign, the blue arrow from x to v. Together they form a **closed loop of mutual influence**.
- Stepping through from x at 1 and v at 0, velocity turns negative, so position falls, slowly and then faster. When x crosses zero, v̇ is zero and velocity is at its most negative, so position falls fastest there. Below zero, v̇ turns positive, velocity climbs back, position bottoms out at negative 1 when velocity reaches zero, and the sequence runs in reverse. Plotted against time, position and velocity are two waves offset by a quarter of a cycle. **Velocity is zero whenever position is at a peak or a trough, and largest whenever position crosses zero.**

## p3
- An **oscillation**, or **vibration**, is a repeating back-and-forth motion around a central point, and the x-v loop is the basic system that produces it. The influence is **circular**, with no single cause at the start and no single effect at the end. The system **never comes to rest on its own**. Every time one variable reaches its target, the other is at its largest value and pushes it past the target again. Position overshoots zero because velocity is at its maximum there, and velocity overshoots zero because position is at its maximum there.

## p4
- Vibration requires two physical effects working together. The first is a **spring**, strictly a **viscous spring**, the springiness that returns things to a natural shape after a push or pull, with a **stiffness** that is how strongly it pulls back. The second is **mass**, the amount of matter, which determines how an object carries motion once it is moving.
- Vibration is the jolt from a car hitting a bump, it is music, it is boom and bust in economics, it is **oscillation in SGD**, where a trained value swings around its target, and it is the motion of the air in the vocal tract and of the vocal folds during speech.

## p5
- A slice of air in the vocal tract is many particles whose number and size stay the same, and what changes is the distance between them. At **equilibrium spacing** a chosen pair sits at 100 percent of its equilibrium distance. **Compression** pushes the particles closer, to 56 percent for the chosen pair, and is a high-pressure state. **Rarefaction** spreads them apart, to 144 percent, and is a low-pressure state. During speech the slice alternates between the two around its equilibrium size, a vibration of the air, with the sizes exaggerated so the change is visible.

## p6
- The spring effect alone is the rule that ẋ equals minus one third of x. The rate of change is one third of the current distance with the opposite sign, so positive x falls and negative x rises, both toward zero. From an **initial condition** of 1, x drops by one third to two thirds, then by two ninths to four ninths, then by four twenty-sevenths to eight twenty-sevenths, each drop smaller than the last. When x is smaller its speed of falling is smaller too, so the curve starts steep, flattens, and **never crosses zero**.
- The system has a natural resting condition at zero, and a **discrepancy** from it feeds back on itself to cancel that discrepancy. This is **negative feedback**, also called **balancing feedback**, from Meadows. The change always opposes the discrepancy, and such systems are in motion only to reach their **equilibrium**. The number minus one third is the **stiffness**. A higher stiffness closes a bigger share of the remaining distance each step and returns faster, and a lower stiffness returns more slowly.

## p7
- If a mass is moving, it just keeps on moving. A mass needs no discrepancy or target and has no natural resting position. **Galileo, Descartes and Newton** worked out that a mass in motion stays in motion, at **constant velocity**, covering the same distance in every equal interval of time, like a car rolling past the same amount of scenery each moment. This is exactly what the spring lacks.

## p8
- **A spring stops at a place, while a mass stops after a process.** A spring alone, with displacement x measured from its natural position at zero, returns from plus 1 and stops at zero **without crossing it**, and from negative 1 rises to zero without crossing it, flattening as it arrives. With a mass attached, the mass is moving fastest when it reaches zero and carries that motion through the crossing, is pulled back from the other side, stops near negative 1, and comes back through zero, through **every crossing**.
- Graphed over time this is a **lightly damped oscillation**. **Damping** is the gradual loss of motion from the viscous part of the spring, so each swing is a little smaller and the mass comes to rest only after a long sequence of swings. Together the two effects recover the harmonic oscillator. The spring supplies the pull back toward zero, v̇ equals minus x, and the mass supplies persistent motion, ẋ equals v. **A spring and a mass together give vibration.**

## p9
- The vibration of the air slice is described by a cosine, so x equals the cosine of the phase, where x is the **normalized expansion**, plus 1 at **maximum rarefaction**, 0 at **equilibrium** and minus 1 at **maximum compression**. The **phase** tracks the position in the cycle, each 2π is one cycle, and 0 to 6π is three cycles. At 0 the slice is at maximum rarefaction, at π at maximum compression, and at 2π back at maximum rarefaction, with in-between values such as plus 0.84 and minus 0.84 on the way. This is the same wave position traced in the harmonic oscillator, with the springiness of the air pushing it back and the mass of the air carrying it past equilibrium.

## p10
- Different vowels are made by placing the tongue in different positions, seen in side-view MRI images during five words. Vowels are organized by **height** and by **front or back**. **"Dean"** is high and front, with a wide open space behind the tongue. **"Dune"** is high and back. **"Dan"** is low and front. **"Don"** is low and back, narrowing the space behind the tongue. **"Done"** is central. Each tongue position gives the air a different shape, which changes how it vibrates, and that is what makes the vowels sound different.

## p11
- **Frequency** is how many complete cycles a vibration goes through in a given time. Three vibrations over the same 12 seconds all start at maximum rarefaction and cover the same range. The low-frequency one completes 3 cycles, 0.25 cycles per second. The mid-frequency one completes 6, 0.5 cycles per second. The high-frequency one completes 9, 0.75 cycles per second. All three reach the same extremes, and the only difference is how quickly the cycle repeats. One cycle per second is **one hertz**, written **Hz**.

## p12
- A vowel is a sum of vibrations at different frequencies happening at once in the same slice of air. A cosine at f hertz is the cosine of 2π times f times t. The vowel /i/ in "dean" is modeled as 1 Hz plus 4 Hz, the cosine of 2πt plus the cosine of 8πt. It starts at plus 2, maximum rarefaction, ranges from plus 2 to minus 2, is 0 at 0.5 seconds, and shows fast wiggles riding on a slow wave, repeating every second. The vowel /a/, close to "Don," is modeled as 2 Hz plus 3 Hz, the cosine of 4πt plus the cosine of 6πt, with deep troughs near minus 1.6 and a pair of small humps between its peaks.
  - The model frequencies are example values, not the frequencies of real speech. Positive x is rarefaction and negative x compression in both, and **the combination of frequencies is what distinguishes one vowel from another**.

## p13
- **Perturbation theory** studies how a system's behavior changes when a **parameter**, a fixed setting such as mass or stiffness, is nudged. For a mass-spring system the behavior of interest is its **frequency**. Increasing the mass lowers the frequency, because a heavier mass carries its motion more strongly and the spring takes longer to turn it around. Increasing the stiffness raises the frequency, because a stiffer spring pulls back harder and turns the mass around sooner.

## p14
- The mass-spring relationships explain how the ear separates sounds by frequency, the work of **Georg von Békésy**, **Nobel Prize in 1961**. The **basilar membrane** in the inner ear is wide at one end, with large mass and small stiffness, and narrow at the other, with small mass and large stiffness. So the wide end vibrates most for low-frequency sounds and the narrow end for high-frequency sounds, with every point between tuned to an intermediate frequency, a long row of mass-spring systems.
- **Nerve fibers** at each spot become active when that spot vibrates, telling the brain which frequencies are present. A vowel built from two frequencies sets two different places vibrating, so the membrane separates the combination back into its components, which is how the ear tells /i/ from /a/.

## p15
- The **learning rate** γ sets how large each training update is, and an **epoch** is one full round through the training data. Runs starting near 130, 80 and 10 head toward about 80 over 300 epochs. At 1 percent they move smoothly but are still short of 80 at the end. At 10 percent they arrive within 30 to 50 epochs and wiggle only a little. At 50 percent they arrive in a few epochs but bounce between about 70 and 95. At 80 percent they bounce between about 68 and 97.
- This is the **fundamental learning tradeoff**. Raising γ raises speed and lowers accuracy, because large updates keep throwing the value past the target so it oscillates around it, the **oscillation in SGD**. Lowering γ lowers speed and raises accuracy. Thousands of people, over generations, have made their living finding better ways to optimize, so that speed and accuracy increase together.
