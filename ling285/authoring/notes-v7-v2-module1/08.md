## p3
- **A spring stops at a place, but a mass stops after a process.** The **displacement x** is how far the end of the spring is from its **natural position**, x at 0, with a stretch at plus 1 and a squeeze at minus 1. A spring alone, pulled to plus 1 and released, pulls itself back and stops exactly at zero, dropping quickly and then slowly and never crossing, because the pull shrinks as the stretch shrinks. Pushed to minus 1 it rises to zero and never goes above it.
- With a mass attached, the mass shoots right through zero, because it is moving fast when it gets there. It compresses the spring on the other side, stops near minus 1, and is pushed back through zero again, carrying motion through every crossing, so x makes a wave, an **oscillation**. Each swing is slightly smaller than the last, a **lightly damped oscillation**, where **damping** is anything that drains motion, such as friction. So the mass stops after a process of many crossings, and a mass-spring system has a **frequency**, cycles per second, while a massless spring has none.

## p4
- **Perturbation theory** asks what happens when one setting of a system is nudged. For a mass-spring system the settings are the **mass m** and the **stiffness k**, and the behavior is the frequency f. Increasing the mass decreases the frequency, because a heavier mass is harder to speed up and slow down. Increasing the stiffness increases the frequency, because a stiffer spring pulls back harder and each swing finishes sooner.
- Speaking changes the **effective mass** and **effective stiffness** of air in the vocal tract. Moving the tongue, jaw and lips squeezes some portions of the air and opens others, so, set vibrating by the vocal folds, the air vibrates at **many frequencies at once**, and the set depends on where and how much the air is squeezed. MRI images of **dean, dune, done, Dan** and **Don** show the different squeezes. The listener's ear must then find which low, middle and high frequencies are present and how strong each is, so the brain can tell **"scene"** from **"son."**

## p5
- Sound is a wave of vibration in the air that arrives at the **external ear** and travels inward to the **inner ear**. The **cochlea**, the one name to know, is a small coiled tube like a snail shell deep in the skull, and it sends the brain signals saying which frequencies are present. The upper region of the inner ear holds the **semicircular ducts**, the **utricle** and **saccule**, and the **cristae** in their **ampullae**, which do not separate frequencies. The cochlea holds the **vestibular, cochlear** and **tympanic ducts**. Soft tubes, the **membranous labyrinth**, sit inside the hollows of the **bony labyrinth**, and the **vestibulocochlear nerve** carries the signals to the brain.

## p6
- Sound reaches the cochlea through a chain. It strikes the **tympanic membrane**, the eardrum, which moves the **malleus**, then the **incus**, then the **stapes**, which pushes in and out on the fluid inside the cochlea. The **basilar membrane** runs the length of the cochlea, from the stapes end to the **helicotrema** at the tip of the coil. The moving fluid makes the membrane vibrate, and the membrane turns that vibration into information about frequency.

## p7
- The basilar membrane is wide at the helicotrema end, with **large mass** and **small stiffness**, heavy and floppy, and narrow at the stapes end, with **small mass** and **large stiffness**, light and stiff. Each spot acts like its own mass-spring system, so by perturbation theory the floppy end vibrates at **low frequencies** and the stiff end at **high frequencies**, with frequency increasing steadily along the membrane.
- A sound with many frequencies is sorted out by place. Each frequency shakes the part of the membrane tuned to it. **Neurons** attached along the membrane signal **to the brain**, which tells the frequencies by **which neurons are active** and their strength by how strongly they signal. **Georg von Békésy won the Nobel Prize in 1961** for this mechanism.

## p8
- The **vocal folds** are two small muscles, about 1 cm long and 1 mm high, sitting across the airway. Muscle has mass and stiffness, so about **10 other muscles** can **stretch** the folds, making them stiffer and raising the frequency, or **bunch** them, making them effectively heavier and lowering it. That frequency is **pitch**, F0, the **fundamental frequency**. It gives notes in singing, conveys questions, mistrust, belief, surprise and emotion in English, and distinguishes word meanings in tone languages.
- Pressure from the lungs blows the closed folds apart, and the air rushing through sucks them back together, like a shower curtain pulled toward the water. The suction is the **Bernoulli force**, which also lifts airplanes. Pressure builds again and the cycle repeats **hundreds of times per second**.

## p9
- A harmonic oscillator with no damping is described by ẋ equals v and v̇ equals minus k over m times x. The ratio k over m sets how strongly velocity is pushed back toward zero, harder for a stiffer spring and less for a heavier mass. With k over m at 1, a **time step Δt** of 0.5, and a start at x of 1 and v of 0, each step updates x first, as the old x plus Δt times the old v, and then v using the **new x**, as the old v minus Δt times the new x. This is **position-first symplectic Euler**.
- Step 1 gives 1.000 and minus 0.500, step 2 gives 0.750 and minus 0.875, and step 3 gives 0.3125, shown as 0.313, and about minus 1.031. After 12 steps x falls through zero, bottoms out at minus 1.022 at step 7 and climbs back to 0.920, while v drops to minus 1.031 at step 3 and peaks at 1.019 at step 9. The swing stays near a size of 1. These values are an approximation, and in Python the velocity update reads x[-1] after the new position has been appended.

## p10
- **Explicit Euler**, or forward Euler, updates both x and v from the previous pair, so the new v uses the **previous** x. The difference shows at step 2, where v[2] comes out to minus 1 instead of minus 0.875, and by step 3 x[3] is 0.250 instead of 0.313. Steps 4 to 12 give positions of minus 0.438, minus 1.188, minus 1.828, minus 2.172, minus 2.059, minus 1.402, minus 0.231, 1.290 and 2.869.
- With this time step, explicit Euler makes the oscillation grow, far beyond the starting value of 1. A real mass cannot swing farther unless energy is added, so the growth is **numerical error** from the method at dt of 0.5. The only difference from the position-first method is whether the velocity update uses the new position or the previous one. In Python, x[n-1] and v[n-1] always refer to the previous pair.

## p11
- Euler's explicit method solves **differential equations**, equations about rates of change, using arithmetic. Each update is the previous value plus a rate times a small time step, repeated many times, and the result is approximate. A Python loop carries out the updates.
