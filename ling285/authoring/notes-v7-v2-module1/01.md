## p2
- **Real-time MRI** records a moving image of the inside of the head and neck while a person talks. The side view is cut down the middle of the head, with the face to the left. Air shows black, and soft tissue such as the tongue, lips and throat walls shows gray and white.
  - The **lips** sit at the far left. The **tongue** fills most of the mouth and is the most active structure, bunching toward the roof of the mouth, pulling back toward the throat, or lying low.
  - The roof of the mouth has a bony front, the **hard palate**, and a soft movable back, the **soft palate** or **velum**, which lifts to seal off the nose or lowers to open it.
  - The dark channel behind the tongue is the **pharynx**. Below it, the **larynx** holds the **vocal folds**.
- All of these structures move at the same time and at high speed during speech. Each sound depends on a precise shape of the whole passage, and the shapes flow smoothly into one another. The two movies show the same speaker's vocal tract in two shapes that differ mainly in where the tongue and lips sit.

## p3
- We learn to move the speech organs in extremely complex ways with no instructions and almost no visual feedback. A child cannot see the tongue, soft palate or vocal folds, yet **by ages 2 to 4** children speak with a wide vocabulary and near-adult language with hardly any explicit instruction.
  - **Reading and writing** whole words usually comes at **ages 6 to 7** and requires a great deal of instruction, even though the eyes and hands are fully visible. Speech is a deep cognitive skill the brain is prepared to acquire, while literacy is a learned tool built on top of it.
- **Articulatory phonetics** studies the movements of the structures of the **vocal tract**, the air passage from the larynx through the throat and mouth to the lips, with a branch into the nose. It asks which structures move, where they move to, and how those movements create the sounds of a language.
- In the last two years, **voice assistants** and other **speech technology** have come to **emulate** human speech, meaning they reproduce its behavior. **LING 285 is about both human speech and AI speech**, so the human skill and the machine version can be compared directly.

## p4
- The focus moves from the tongue and lips to the **air** between the tongue and the rest of the vocal tract. Ignoring the curve of the real passage turns the vocal tract into a straight **tube of air**, open at the **lips** and treated as closed at the **larynx**, with the walls standing for the tongue and surrounding surfaces.
  - The tube is labeled **schwa**, the neutral relaxed vowel. For schwa the tongue does not narrow the passage much anywhere, so the air space has about the same width from end to end, and the straight even tube fits it best.
- The air inside the tube vibrates without flowing out the end. Each column of air shifts back and forth around its own resting position. Where neighboring columns move together, the air is packed tightly into a **compression**. Where they move apart, it spreads into a **rarefaction**. These regions travel along the tube and reflect from its ends, and this repeating pattern is the **vibration of air** that makes the vowel sound.
- **Acoustic phonetics** studies how air vibrates in the vocal tract and what sound results. Articulatory phonetics describes the shape of the tube, and acoustic phonetics describes the air inside it. Changing the tongue and lip positions changes the tube, and changing the tube changes how the air vibrates.

## p5
- AI brings **psychological, economic, social and environmental harms**, and many scientists warn about **existential harm**, meaning a threat to humanity's survival or basic way of life. Pretending it does not exist is not a workable option. It exists, with many harms and some benefits, such as **immunotherapy**, which uses the body's own immune system against diseases such as cancer, and **environmental engineering**.
  - The environmental case cuts both ways. Running large AI systems uses enormous amounts of energy, while AI tools are also applied to engineering problems that could help the environment.
- One of the biggest dangers is that **we have no idea how it works**. We know everything, because every number and calculation inside a system can be inspected, and understand nothing, because no one can explain why it gives the answers it does. The people who know how these systems operate are very few, which puts the decisions in very few hands.
- **AI literacy** is necessary for scientists who use AI, politicians who need to control it, and lawyers who litigate cases involving it. The course rests on the belief that a creative thinker in a class like this one can help us understand these systems well enough to benefit from them while avoiding their negative consequences.

## p7
- **Diotima**, dated here to around 440 BCE, teaches **Socrates** in Plato's Symposium about a ladder of ascent from surface knowledge of particular things toward deep understanding of the general ideas behind them. The chain of teachers runs from Diotima to Socrates to **Plato** to **Aristotle**.
- The **bottom of the ladder** holds **daily experience, habit and memorization**, along with **specialized skills, repetition and replication**, and learning from **textbooks**. Its rule is that **similar is same, and slight difference is different**.
- The **top of the ladder** holds **creativity, insight, thought, focus, attention, imagination and vision**, along with **problem-posing, problem-solving, critical reading and creative writing**. Its rule is reversed, so **different can be same, and similar can be different**.
  - The sounds f and v sound different yet are made in the same place in the mouth. The two th sounds are spelled the same yet one is voiced and the other is not. **Problem-posing** means asking good questions, and **problem-solving** means answering them.
- A person climbs the ladder through repeated cycles of **question and answer**, the method Socrates became famous for. For about 2500 years people could fake it by memorizing, repeating and replicating. In 2026, interviews for jobs and graduate school look for the properties at the top of the ladder.

## p8
- Produce the sounds and attend to what the body does. Hold the **f** in "fat" and switch to the **v** in "vat." Hold the **th** in "thing" and switch to the **th** in "that." Hold the **s** in "sing" and switch to the **z** in "zing." Hold the **sh** in "mesh" and switch to the buzzing sound in the middle of "beige," written **j** here.
- Within each pair the change is **voice**, whether the **vocal folds** vibrate. With fingers on the throat, f, the first th, s and sh feel still and hiss, while v, the second th, z and the sound in "beige" buzz. The vocal folds are two folds of tissue in the larynx. Held apart they let air pass silently, giving a **voiceless** sound. Brought close together they vibrate in the airflow, giving a **voiced** sound. The tongue and lips do not change within a pair.
- From one line to the next the change is **place of articulation**, where the narrowing is formed. The lower lip meets the upper teeth for f and v, the tongue tip meets the upper teeth for th, the tongue tip nears the ridge behind the teeth for s and z, and the tongue sits a bit further back for sh and the sound in "beige." Voice and place are two separate, independent properties of a speech sound.

## p9
- **Fricatives** are made by bringing two parts of the vocal tract close together without closing the passage. Air forced through the narrow gap becomes turbulent, which makes the hissing or buzzing noise, and the sound lasts as long as there is breath.
- The table has **voice** in the rows and **place of articulation** in the columns. **Labial** sounds f and v bring the lower lip to the upper teeth. **Dental** th sounds put the tongue tip at the teeth. **Alveolar** s and z bring the tongue tip near the **alveolar ridge** just behind the upper teeth. **Postalveolar** sh and the sound in "beige" raise the front of the tongue a little further back.
  - Two fricatives in the same column differ only in voicing, and two in the same row differ only in place. English spells the two th sounds the same way even though one is voiceless and the other is voiced.

## p10
- **Stops** close the passage completely for a brief moment. Pressure builds behind the closure, and the release lets the trapped air burst out, which makes a stop short and sharp. Unlike a fricative, a stop cannot be held.
- The stops form a grid of three places and three types. **p, b and m** close the **lips**. **t, d and n** press the tongue tip against the **alveolar ridge**. **k, g and ng** raise the back of the tongue against the **soft palate**. Across each row the first sound is **voiceless**, the second is **voiced**, and the third is a **nasal**, voiced with the **soft palate lowered** so air flows out through the nose.
  - The columns follow the fricative table. **p, t and k** are the voiceless stops, **b, d and g** the voiced stops, and **m, n and ng** the nasal stops. Three places of closure combined with three settings of the vocal folds and soft palate give nine sounds.
