**Section 1: Principles of Mass-Spring Systems**

- Every vibrating system in speech follows a small set of principles, and each one connects to the others.
  - **Raising the mass lowers the frequency, and raising the stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - **A system with n masses has n modes**, and each higher mode has a higher frequency, because more parts go against each other and more springs stretch and compress.
  - **An increase in mass lowers frequency in proportion to how much that mass moves.** A mass that does not move has zero effect, a mass that moves a little has a small effect, and a mass that moves a lot has a large effect.
  - **An increase in stiffness raises frequency in proportion to how much that spring stretches and compresses.** Both of these effects depend on where the change is made.
  - **Where there is maximal movement there is no stretching, and where there is maximal stretching there is no movement.**
  - In a **fixed-fixed system**, the frequencies are called **harmonics**, and they are **whole-number multiples** of the base frequency. In a **fixed-free system**, the frequencies are called **formants**, and they are **odd multiples** of the base frequency.

- These principles warn against a tempting mistake. If the mass and the stiffness of a system are both raised, it might seem the two effects simply **cancel out**. That is only true for a **single mass on a single spring**. In a system with many masses and springs, a spot that is moving a lot is hardly stretching, and a spot that is stretching a lot is hardly moving, so only one of the two changes matters at that spot.
  - Picture a spot on a string that never moves but is stretched hard. Extra mass there does nothing, while extra stiffness there raises the frequency, so the frequency goes up.
  - Picture a spot that swings up and down the most. Extra stiffness there does nothing, while extra mass slows it down, so the frequency goes down.

**Section 2: More Masses, More Modes**

- Adding masses adds modes, and the same shapes keep repeating. **Mode 1** always has everything moving together, **Mode 2** always splits the system into two halves going against each other, and **Mode 3** always has the middle going against the sides. The more the parts go against each other, the more the springs work, so **higher modes are stiffer and faster**.
  - In plain terms, a higher mode is not made of stiffer springs. It just makes the same springs do more stretching.

**Section 3: Why Fixed-Fixed Frequencies Are Whole-Number Multiples**

- In a string fixed at both ends, the first mode is **one half-wave**, a single bump that goes from no motion at one end, to the most motion in the middle, to no motion at the other end. Each higher mode is built by packing **more of these half-wave packets** into the same length.
  - **Mode 2 holds two packets**, with one half going up while the other half goes down. Each packet behaves like a copy of Mode 1, so Mode 2 has **twice the stiffness** and **twice the frequency**.
  - **Mode 3 holds three packets**, and **Mode 5 holds five**, so the fifth mode has five times the frequency of the first.
  - So the frequencies climb in equal steps. If the first harmonic is 100 hertz, the next ones are 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz.

- The numbers do not have to start at 100. A voice whose base frequency is **250 hertz** has its next harmonics at 500, 750 and 1000 hertz, because each one is still a whole number of copies of the first.
  - The short version for an exam answer is that **the fifth mode has the first mode fitted into it five times**, so it has five times the stiffness and five times the frequency.

**Section 4: Harmonics of Strings and Membranes**

- A string fixed at both ends vibrates in all of these modes at once, and its **spectrum**, with **frequency** along the bottom and **amplitude** going up, has **evenly spaced spikes**, one for each harmonic. The **vocal folds** are fixed on all sides, just behind the Adam's apple, so they behave like this kind of fixed-fixed system and produce harmonics.

- The vocal folds form a **membrane**, and the same packet counting works on a surface. The first mode is a single bump across the whole membrane. The second mode squeezes two of those bumps in, so it has twice the frequency, and the fourth mode squeezes in four ups and downs, so it has four times the frequency.

**Section 5: One Fixed End and One Free End**

- A **building** is **fixed at the bottom** and **free at the top**. In high winds or an earthquake, engineers want it to sway only in **Mode 1**, all together. That is mildly unpleasant on a high floor but safe. A higher mode would send some floors one way and other floors the other way, and the whole structure could shatter.

- A string can be set up the same way, fixed at the bottom and free at the top. Its first mode is not a full half-wave anymore. It goes from **no motion at the fixed end** to **lots of motion at the free end**, which is only **one quarter of a wave**.

**Section 6: Why Fixed-Free Frequencies Are Odd Multiples**

- In a fixed-free system, each mode is built from **quarter-wave packets**, and each packet runs from no motion to lots of motion. **Mode 1 holds one packet**, **Mode 2 holds three**, **Mode 3 holds five** and **Mode 4 holds seven**.
  - So with a base of 100 hertz, the modes come at **1, 3, 5 and 7 times 100 hertz**, which are 100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz.

- **Two packets can never fit.** Stacking two quarter-wave packets gives no motion, then lots of motion, then no motion again. That puts a still point at the top as well as the bottom, which makes the system **fixed at both ends**, not fixed-free. Adding one more packet, for **three** in total, ends with lots of motion at the top again, so that is the next mode that is possible.
  - The simple version is that the free end must always be moving, and only an odd number of quarter-waves lands on a moving end.

- The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called **formants**. **A formant is a frequency of vibration of a system that is fixed on one side and completely free on the other**, and formants are **odd multiples** of the lowest one. **F1** is the first formant, **F2** the second and **F3** the third.

**Section 7: Counting Quarter-Waves**

- Counting quarter-wave packets gives every formant from the first. If **F1 is 500 hertz**, then **F2 is three times that, 1500 hertz**, and **F3 is five times that, 2500 hertz**.
  - The steps between formants are twice the first formant, because each new mode adds two more quarter-wave packets.

**Section 8: The Air in the Vocal Tract Is a Fixed-Free System**

- The air inside the mouth and throat forms a tube. At one end are the **vocal folds**, and because they are closed, the air there **cannot go back and forth**, so that end is **fixed**. At the other end the **lips are open**, so the air can go back and forth freely, and that end is **free**.
  - So the air in the vocal tract is a fixed-free system, just like a building. **The motion of air in the vocal tract is like a building**, and the math describing them is almost exactly the same.
  - Its frequencies are therefore **formants**, which are odd multiples of the lowest one.
  - Speaking changes the stiffness and mass of the air in different parts of the tract, which moves the formants. That is how listeners hear different vowels and consonants.

**Section 9: Compression and Motion Alternate in an Air Column**

- The air in the tube can be modeled as lines of particles, where each particle stands for billions of air molecules.
  - Where the lines come **really close together and then really far apart**, the air is compressing and stretching, which means it is acting like a **spring**.
  - Where the lines just **move back and forth** without changing their distance from each other, the air is acting like a **mass**. The easiest place to see this is the **free end at the lips**, where the air simply moves in and out.
  - An easy way to read it is that bunching and spreading means springiness, and sliding without bunching means mass.

- The air's modes match a building's modes.
  - In **Mode 1**, the only point of no motion is the fixed end. The air near the fixed end compresses a lot, and the air near the lips moves a lot without compressing. A building in Mode 1 is the same, with lots of compression and expansion in the structure near the ground and lots of motion with no stretching near the top.
  - In **Mode 2**, there are **two points of no motion**, one at the fixed end and one inside the tube.
  - In **Mode 3**, there are **three points of no motion**. Around each of them there is lots of compression and expansion, so those are the springiest places.

- The same reasoning answers questions about a building's higher modes. In **Mode 4** of a building, **the most springiness is at the fixed bottom and at the three points where the building's line crosses its resting position**, four places in all, because those points do not move. The places between them move the most and have the least springiness.
  - Raising the stiffness at a place of maximal movement changes **nothing**, because the structure is not acting like a spring there.
  - Raising the stiffness at a place of no movement makes the frequency **shoot up**, because that spot is very springy.

- The same two-step reasoning answers a question about a string fixed at both ends. Take a small spot where **Mode 3 crosses its middle line** while **Mode 4 is at the top of a bump**, and raise both the mass and the stiffness of that spot.
  - In **Mode 3**, the string does not move at that spot but is stretched the most, so only the extra stiffness counts, and **Mode 3's frequency goes up**.
  - In **Mode 4**, the string moves the most at that spot and hardly stretches, so only the extra mass counts, and **Mode 4's frequency goes down**.

- Adding mass and stiffness at the **very top of a building** lowers the frequency of **every mode**, including Mode 4. The top is the free end, so it has the most motion and almost no springiness in every mode. Only the extra mass counts there, and extra moving mass lowers frequency. **The top of the building corresponds to the lip end of the vocal tract.**

**Section 10: Vowel Shapes Locate Constrictions**

- A **constriction** is a place where the tongue or lips squeeze the air. Locations are measured from the **glottis**, the tiny space between the vocal folds at the fixed end.
  - In **done**, the central vowel, there is **no constriction**, and the air is pretty uniform along the whole tract.
  - In **dean**, a **front high vowel**, the air is squeezed under the roof of the mouth, about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **Don**, a **low back vowel**, the air is squeezed far back, about **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **dune**, a **back high vowel**, the lips are rounded close together, which squeezes the air **at the lips**.
  - **Dan** is a **front low vowel**.

**Section 11: Why a Constriction Can Raise One Formant and Lower Another**

- **Compressing air raises its stiffness.** Squeezing air in a tire makes the tire stiffer, so a constriction at a place that experiences lots of compression **raises frequency**.

- **Bunching up air raises its mass**, because squeezing it into a smaller volume raises its **density**. So a constriction at a place where the air moves a lot **lowers frequency**.

- So every constriction **increases both mass and stiffness**, but that does not mean constrictions have no effect on frequency. **The parts of a vibrating medium that move the most alternate with the parts that compress and expand the most.** At any one spot, in any one mode, either the mass or the stiffness is the one that counts.
  - A useful way to answer any constriction question is to ask two things in order. First, is the air at this spot mostly moving or mostly compressing in this mode? Second, which change counts there, the extra mass or the extra stiffness?

**Section 12: /u/, /i/ and /a/**

- **/u/** rounds the lips, which squeezes the air at the **free end**. The free end moves a lot and hardly compresses in both Mode 1 and Mode 2, so the extra **mass** counts in both. **F1 and F2 both go down.**
  - A speaker who moved from the central vowel into /u/ produced exactly this, with **both formants dropping** in the recording.

- **/i/** squeezes the air about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **Mode 1**, that spot has lots of motion, so the extra mass counts and **F1 goes down**.
  - In **Mode 2**, that spot has lots of compression, so the extra stiffness counts and **F2 goes up**.
  - A speaker who moved from the central vowel into /i/ showed the **first formant dropping a lot** and the **second formant going up**.
  - Put simply, the same squeeze lands on a moving spot for F1 and a springy spot for F2, so one formant falls while the other rises.

- **/a/** squeezes the air about **one third of the way from the glottis**, which reverses the /i/ pattern.
  - In **Mode 1**, that spot near the fixed end compresses a lot, so the extra stiffness counts and **F1 goes up**.
  - In **Mode 2**, that spot moves a lot, so the extra mass counts and **F2 goes down**.

- The reasoning also runs backward. **If a constriction raises a formant a great deal, the air at that spot must be very springy and hardly moving in that mode.** **If a constriction lowers a formant a great deal, the air at that spot must be moving a lot.**

- A measured **schwa**, made with no constriction, had formants near **500, 1500 and 2500 hertz**, which are the odd multiples a uniform fixed-free tube predicts.

**Section 13: The Source Supplies Harmonics and the Filter Shapes Their Heights**

- Speech combines **two vibrating systems at the same time**.
  - The **vocal folds** are a fixed-fixed system, so they are the **source**, and they produce **harmonics**. The lowest harmonic is called the **base frequency**, or **F0**, and the others are its multiples. Here the harmonics are **100 hertz apart**.
  - The **air in the vocal tract** is a fixed-free system, so it acts as a **filter**, and its frequencies are the **formants**. For the schwa, the formants sit near **500, 1500, 2500 and 3500 hertz**.

- **The formants shape the heights of the harmonics.** The harmonics are all still there, but **harmonics near a formant are tall, and harmonics far from any formant are short**. The overall outline of the harmonic heights, called the **envelope**, rises into a peak at each formant.
  - **The harmonics stay the same from vowel to vowel, but their heights change, and the envelope is what makes the vowel.**
  - In plain terms, the vocal folds decide which frequencies are present, and the shape of the mouth decides which of them are loud.

**Section 14: The /i/ and /a/ Filters**

- For **/i/**, the filter keeps **F1 low and F2 high**. The tall harmonics are bunched at the very bottom of the spectrum, then a long stretch of short harmonics follows, and the next tall group appears much higher up. The two lowest formants are **far apart**.

- For **/a/**, the filter **raises F1 and lowers F2**. The first two groups of tall harmonics sit **close together** in the lower part of the spectrum, and the higher formants appear farther up.

- The same source harmonics are under both vowels. Only the filter differs, and moving the constriction is what moves the formants and reshapes which harmonics are loud.

**Section 15: A Spectrogram Places Successive Spectra in Time**

- **A spectrum describes one instant.** Speech is not one frozen vowel, though. The vocal tract keeps moving, and constrictions are made and unmade, so speech is **time-varying**.

- **A spectrogram stacks many spectra side by side over time.**
  - **Time** runs along the **horizontal axis**.
  - **Frequency**, which was the horizontal axis of each spectrum, becomes the **vertical axis**.
  - **Amplitude**, which was the height of each spike, becomes **darkness**, so the louder a frequency is, the darker it looks.

- The formants appear as **dark bands** that move as the speaker moves. In the sequence **/ia/**, the two lowest bands start **far apart** during /i/, with F1 low and F2 high. As the speaker moves into /a/, **F1 rises and F2 falls**, so the bands **come close together**.
  - Reading a spectrogram means following the dark bands, because those bands are the formants changing over time.

**Section 16: A Sentence Over Time**

- **Where were you a year ago?** is a good sentence for seeing source and filter working together over time. The vocal folds, as the source, keep producing harmonics, while the vocal tract, as the filter, keeps changing shape, so the envelope over the harmonics changes from moment to moment.
  - On a spectrogram, these changes show up as **dark bands that rise and fall**, since the dark bands are the formants and darkness shows amplitude.
  - Where the vowel sounds like **/i/**, the two lowest bands spread far apart, and during **you**, all the bands drop because the lips are rounded.
- **A spectrum shows one frozen instant, but a spectrogram shows how the formants move**, and real speech is all movement. Reading a sentence like this one means following each band and asking what constriction moved it.

**Section 17: Wide Harmonic Spacing Makes Formants Harder to Identify**

- Formants can only show up through the harmonics, so the spacing of the harmonics matters. With harmonics every **100 hertz** and formants at **300 and 2100 hertz**, there are **many harmonics** under each formant peak, so the formants clearly **structure the amplitudes** of many harmonics and are easy to see.

- With a higher base frequency, the harmonics are spaced farther apart, such as every **250 hertz**. Fewer harmonics land on each formant peak, and a formant can fall **between two harmonics**, so **it becomes very hard to tell the harmonics from the formants**.
  - A **toddler's** harmonics can be at 400, 800 and 1200 hertz, which is why even listeners can find a toddler's vowels hard to identify unless they know the child.
  - A **soprano's** harmonics can start at 800 hertz and continue at 1600 and 2400, so the words of an opera singer are often very hard to follow.

- **Formants are the smooth envelope, and harmonics are samples of it.** A higher F0 means fewer samples, so the spectrogram stops looking like clean formant bands.
  - A simple way to see it is that the harmonics are like the points used to trace a curve. With many points the curve is obvious, and with only a few points it is hard to see.
