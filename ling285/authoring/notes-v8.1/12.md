**Section 1: Dynamic Systems in the Economy**

- The **2026 Nobel Prize in economics** went to **Robert Hall** and **Michael Woodford** for the **microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization**. **Stabilization** means getting a system to its equilibrium and keeping it there, which is the same job that **balancing feedback** does and the same thing a spring does when it pulls a mass back to rest.
  - In their model of the economy, **unemployment and inflation rise and fall in turns**, like the two curves of an oscillator. One curve plays the role of the position, which starts high, goes negative and comes back, and the other plays the role of the velocity, which is the flip of it.
  - The aim of stabilization policy is to **even out these peaks and troughs**, so that the swings get smaller instead of producing high unemployment or runaway inflation.

- Woodford's theory is called **Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium**, or **DSGE**, and each word in the name has a meaning that carries over from dynamical systems.
  - **Dynamic** means the economy is a system that changes over time according to rules.
  - **Stochastic** means the model can handle **random shocks**, such as a war starting, a factory burning down or a large company's CEO being caught embezzling. This is close to stochastic gradient descent, where one odd data point can push the estimate far off, but later data pull it back toward the average.
  - **Equilibrium** means the goal is a steady state that people can live in, without wild jumps.

- **Macroeconomic** quantities describe a whole economy, like inflation or the number of people unemployed, while **microeconomic** events are single choices, like one person buying something. Explaining large patterns from many tiny events is the same kind of problem as speech, where many tiny vibrations in the mouth add up to a meaning a listener understands without tracking any of them.

**Section 2: Masses, Springs and Their Modes**

- A mass-spring system vibrates because **the spring wants to return to equilibrium** while **a moving mass wants to keep moving**. The same vibration can happen **longitudinally**, left and right along the spring, or **transversely**, up and down between two walls, and both kinds of motion behave in exactly the same way. Understanding both is useful, because the vocal folds and the air in the vocal tract each vibrate most clearly in a different direction.
  - **Raising the mass lowers the frequency**, so the system goes back and forth fewer times per second.
  - **Raising the stiffness raises the frequency**, so the system goes back and forth more times per second.

- With **two masses** there are **two modes**. In **Mode 1** the masses move together, and in **Mode 2** they move against each other. The system can also do both at once, with the masses rising and falling together while also moving against each other.
  - **Mode 1 is slower** because the middle spring just rides along without stretching or compressing, so effectively it has **only two springs**.
  - **Mode 2 is faster** because all **three springs** stretch and compress, and more working springs means **more stiffness** and a **higher frequency**.
  - Making the **middle spring stiffer** while the system vibrates leaves **Mode 1 unchanged**, since that spring never stretches in Mode 1. The same change makes **Mode 2 rise**, since in Mode 2 the middle spring stretches and compresses all the time.

- With **three masses** there are **three modes**. In Mode 1 all three move together, in Mode 2 the middle mass is frozen while the outer two move against each other, and in Mode 3 the middle mass moves against both outer masses. The more the springs stretch and compress, the higher the mode's frequency.
  - Making **Mass 2**, the middle mass, heavier **lowers the frequency of Mode 1 and Mode 3**, because the middle mass moves in both.
  - The same change has **no effect on Mode 2**, because in Mode 2 the middle mass does not move at all.
  - **A mass affects frequency only through its movement.** If it does not move, it has no effect, if it moves a little, it has a small effect, and if it moves a lot, it has a large effect.

**Section 3: More Masses and the String**

- **Every added mass adds one more mode**, and the same patterns repeat as the chain grows.
  - **Mode 1** is always everything going up and down together.
  - **Mode 2** always splits the system into **two parts** that go against each other. With an odd number of masses, such as three, five or seven, the middle mass is frozen in Mode 2.
  - **Mode 3** always splits the system into **three parts**, with the middle going one way and the sides going the other.
  - Higher modes have more parts going against each other, so the springs stretch and compress far more, and those modes vibrate faster.

- Packing in more and more masses and springs gives a **string**, like the string of a guitar or the strings inside a piano. **Every little piece of a string has weight, which is its mass, and every little piece hates being stretched or compressed, which is its springiness.** The same mode patterns appear in the string, with everything moving together in Mode 1, the two halves going against each other in Mode 2, and the middle going against the sides in Mode 3.
  - In musical instruments, these frequencies are heard as **notes**.
  - The ear can pick out the notes of many instruments at the same time, because it is itself built of masses and springs that respond to the masses and springs vibrating in the world.

**Section 4: Harmonics of a String Fixed at Both Ends**

- A string fixed at both ends with its first mode at **100 hertz** has its next modes at **200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz**, so every mode is an **exact multiple** of the first. These multiples are called **harmonics**.

- The multiples come from **how many copies of the first mode fit into each higher mode**. The first mode is a single bump that swings from a maximum to a minimum and back.
  - The second mode holds **two** of those bumps, one side going up while the other goes down, so it has twice the frequency.
  - The third mode holds **three** bumps, and the fifth holds **five**, so the frequency keeps multiplying by the number of copies that fit.
  - So if the first mode is 100 hertz, the fifth mode is 500 hertz.

- A real string vibrates in **many modes at once**, and their sum gives an erratic-looking motion, much like the waveform of speech. A single mode by itself is a **pure tone**, which sounds so annoying that it is used for sirens. What we normally hear has many modes at the same time.

- The **spectrum** shows this mixture. **Frequency** runs along the horizontal axis, and **amplitude**, meaning how much vibration there is, runs up the vertical axis. For this string, the spectrum has spikes at 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz, which says that all five frequencies are present in the sound.

- The same reasoning predicts what happens when one piece of the string is made **heavier**, for example by thickening the very middle of the string.
  - In **Mode 1**, the middle moves up and down a lot, so the added mass **lowers** its frequency.
  - In **Mode 2**, the middle does not move at all, so the added mass changes **nothing**.
  - In **Mode 3** the middle moves again, so its frequency drops, and in **Mode 4** the middle is still again, so nothing changes. Going up the modes, the effect **alternates** between lowering the frequency and having no effect.

- **Motion and stretching alternate along a vibrating string.** Where a piece of the string moves the most, it is just carried up and down and is hardly stretched. Where the string is stretched the most, such as right next to a fixed end, there is **no movement** at all. Along the string, these places keep alternating, with the most movement and the least stretching at one spot, then the least movement and the most stretching at the next.
  - **The shortcut is that maximal springiness is at the points of no movement.** In **Mode 5** of a string fixed at both ends, the string does not move at the **two fixed ends** or at the **four points where it crosses its middle line**, so those **six places** have the most springiness.
  - The opposite question has the opposite answer. The **most motion**, and so the **least springiness**, is at the tops of the **five bumps**.

**Section 5: Principles of Mass-Spring Systems**

- All of these results follow from a short set of principles, and they are not separate facts to memorize, because each one follows from the others.
  - **Raising the mass lowers the frequency, and raising the stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - **A system with n masses has n modes of vibration**, and each higher mode has a higher frequency.
  - **An increase in mass lowers frequency in proportion to how much that mass moves**, and how much it moves depends on where it is.
  - **Where there is maximal movement there is no stretching, and where there is maximal stretching there is no movement.**
  - **An increase in stiffness raises frequency in proportion to how much that spring stretches and compresses**, which also depends on where it is.
  - In a **fixed-fixed system**, the harmonics are **multiples of the base frequency**, because the base mode fits a whole number of times into each successive mode.
  - In a **fixed-free system**, the frequencies are **odd multiples of the base frequency**, because the base mode fits an odd number of times into each successive mode.

**Section 6: A Local Change Affects Two Modes Differently**

- Changing one small piece of a string can push two modes in **opposite directions**. Consider a spot where **Mode 4 is moving the most**, and where **Mode 5 crosses its middle line**, so that Mode 5 is not moving there at all. Both the **mass** and the **stiffness** of that small piece are raised.
  - **For Mode 4**, the piece is moving, so the extra mass **lowers** the frequency. The piece is barely stretched, so the extra stiffness does nothing.
  - **For Mode 5**, the piece does not move, so the extra mass does nothing. The piece is stretched the most there, so the extra stiffness **raises** the frequency.
  - So the same change **lowers Mode 4's frequency and raises Mode 5's frequency**. Each mode has to be reasoned out on its own by asking whether the spot is moving or stretching in that mode.

- This way of reasoning about small local changes, called **perturbation theory**, was first worked out with **drums**, which are also made of countless masses and springs. In the late nineteenth century, before cameras could capture such fast motion, small amounts of **wax** were added to different spots on a drum.
  - Wax on a spot predicted to vibrate a lot added moving mass, and the drum's frequency was heard to **drop**.
  - Wax on a spot predicted not to move at all made **no difference** to the sound.

**Section 7: The Vocal Folds as a Membrane**

- The **larynx** holds the **vocal folds**, which form a **membrane** that is a bit like a drum, so they have many modes of vibration. In Mode 1 all the masses go up and down together, in Mode 2 the two halves go against each other, and the higher modes divide the surface into more and more parts.

- The **scientific method** depends on clear theories, usually written as **differential equations**, whose solutions give predictions that can be measured. The theory here is that the vocal folds vibrate in many modes at once, so a measured voice should have a spectrum with **equidistant harmonics**.
  - A voice recorded and examined with **Praat** had its lowest harmonic at roughly 130 hertz, and the next harmonics fell close to twice and three times that value. **The measurement matched the prediction.**
  - Each person's lowest frequency is different, but the harmonics above it are always close to multiples of it.

- Changes to the mass and stiffness of the vocal folds change the voice. People who smoke heavily can develop **nodules** on their vocal folds, which add mass at one spot and change the stiffness of nearby parts. That changes how the folds vibrate, which is part of why a heavy smoker's voice sounds different.

**Section 8: One Fixed End and One Free End**

- A **building** is a system that is **fixed on one side and free on the other**, because it is fixed to the earth at the bottom while the top can sway in very high winds or an earthquake.
  - Structural engineers design buildings to absorb that motion in **Mode 1**, with the whole building swaying together. On a high floor this can be unpleasant, but it is safe.
  - They design buildings to never vibrate in a higher mode, where some floors would go one way and other floors the other way, because that would break the structure.

- The **air inside the vocal tract** is also a fixed-free system. At the bottom, the **vocal folds are closed**, so the air cannot rush past them and **cannot move**, which makes that end **fixed**. At the lips, the air can go in and out easily, so that end is **free**. Unlike a building, the air in the vocal tract vibrates in **all of its modes**, and speaking changes the masses and springs of different parts of the air, which is how listeners know which vowel, consonant and word is being said.

- A fixed-free system has a different spectrum from a fixed-fixed one. **The frequencies jump by odd steps, at 100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz**, instead of climbing through every multiple.
  - The first mode goes from **no motion** at the fixed end to **lots of motion** at the free end, which is only a quarter of a full wave.
  - The next possible mode holds **three** of these no-motion-to-lots-of-motion pieces, so its frequency is three times the first. The mode after that holds **five**, so its frequency is five times the first.
  - Two pieces would end with no motion at both ends, which is a fixed-fixed pattern, so even multiples never occur.

**Section 9: Air in a Fixed-Free Tube**

- The air in the vocal tract can be modeled as a **chain of masses and springs** that is fixed at the glottis end and free at the lip end. The masses stand for the air's inertia and the springs stand for its springiness.
  - The lowest mode of this chain is like the **first formant**, **F1**, and the next mode is like the **second formant**, **F2**. The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called **formants**, while the frequencies of a fixed-fixed system are called harmonics.
  - The second mode oscillates **three times faster** than the first, because it holds three of the first mode's quarter-wave pieces.
  - The masses near the free end move very freely in both modes.

- Three places along the tube matter for vowels. A spot **one third of the way from the glottis** is where **/a/** is constricted, a spot **two thirds of the way from the glottis** is where **/i/** is constricted, and the **free end at the lips** is where **/u/** is constricted.

**Section 10: Vowel Shapes and Constrictions**

- Images of the vocal tract during five vowels show where the air is squeezed. A **constriction** is a place where the tongue or lips narrow the tract and squeeze the air. The **glottis** is the tiny space between the vocal folds at the bottom of the tract, so distances are measured from that fixed end.
  - In **done**, the central vowel, there is **no constriction**, and the airflow is basically unimpeded along the whole tract.
  - In **dean**, a **high front** vowel, the air is squeezed under the roof of the mouth, about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **Don**, a **low back** vowel, the air is squeezed far back in the throat, about **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **dune**, a **high back** vowel made with **lip rounding**, the air is squeezed **at the lips**.
  - **Dan** is a **low front** vowel.

**Section 11: What a Constriction Does to the Air**

- **Constricting air raises both its stiffness and its mass.** Squeezing air is like squeezing the air in a tire, which makes the tire **stiffer**. Squeezing air also packs it into a smaller volume and raises its **density**, which makes the air more **massive** and harder to move.

- It might seem that raising mass and stiffness together would **cancel out**, since more mass lowers frequency and more stiffness raises it. **That is false** for any system with many masses and springs, and it is only true for a single mass on a single spring.
  - In a system with many masses and springs, each spot is either mostly **moving** or mostly **stretching** in a given mode.
  - At a spot with no motion and lots of stretching, the extra mass has no effect, so only the extra stiffness counts and the frequency **rises**.
  - At a spot with lots of motion and no stretching, the extra stiffness has no effect, so only the extra mass counts and the frequency **falls**.
  - Speech itself proves the effects do not cancel, because constrictions in different places clearly change the frequencies of the voice.

**Section 12: /u/ Loads the Moving Lip End**

- For **/u/**, rounding the lips squeezes the air at the **free end**, which raises the mass and the stiffness of the air there.
  - In **Mode 1**, the free end has lots of motion and almost no compression, so **motion dominates** and the extra mass lowers **F1**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the free end again has lots of motion, so **motion dominates** and the extra mass lowers **F2**.
  - The extra stiffness does not matter at either mode, because the air at the lips is moving, not stretching. So **both F1 and F2 go down**.

- This was tested by having a speaker start from the central vowel, whose formants were near 500, 1500 and 2500 hertz, and then move into **/u/**. **Both formants dropped**, as predicted, because the rounded lips added mass to the air right where it moves the most.

**Section 13: /i/ Has Opposite Effects on F1 and F2**

- For **/i/**, the air is squeezed about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**, and that one location acts differently in the two modes.
  - In **Mode 1**, this spot has lots of motion and hardly any compression, so **mass motion dominates**. The extra mass **lowers F1**.
  - In **Mode 2**, this spot has lots of compression, so **springiness dominates**. The extra stiffness **raises F2**.
  - So the same constriction pushes the two formants apart, with **F1 going down and F2 going up**.

- This was tested by having a speaker go from the central vowel into **/i/**. In the recording, the **first formant dropped a lot** and the **second formant went up**, just as the masses and springs predicted.

**Section 14: /a/ Reverses the Two Effects**

- For **/a/**, the air is squeezed about **one third of the way from the glottis**, and the pattern reverses.
  - In **Mode 1**, this spot near the fixed end has lots of compression, so **springiness dominates**. The extra stiffness **raises F1**.
  - In **Mode 2**, this spot has lots of motion, so **mass motion dominates**. The extra mass **lowers F2**.
  - So for **/a/**, **F1 goes up and F2 goes down**, the opposite of /i/, and the two formants come closer together.

- The general rule works in both directions. **If a constriction raises a formant a lot, the air at that spot must be springy and hardly moving in that mode**, so the added stiffness is what counted. **If a constriction lowers a formant a lot, the air at that spot must be moving a lot**, so the added mass is what counted.

**Section 15: The Principles Applied to Air**

- The same principles of mass-spring systems explain the vowels. **Mass up means frequency down and stiffness up means frequency up**, but each change matters only where it can act. **Extra mass matters where the air moves, and extra stiffness matters where the air compresses and expands.** Because the vocal tract is fixed at the glottis and free at the lips, its formants are **odd multiples** of the lowest one, since the quarter-wave base mode fits an odd number of times into each higher mode.

**Section 16: Compression and Motion Alternate in an Air Column**

- The air column can be modeled as **lines of air particles**, where each particle stands for billions of real air molecules. The closed vocal folds sit at the **fixed end**, and the open lips sit at the **free end**.
  - Where the lines of particles bunch close together and spread far apart, the air is **compressing and expanding**, which is a sign of **springiness**.
  - Where the lines just move back and forth without changing their spacing, the air is acting as a **mass**. This is easiest to see at the **free end**, where the air simply moves in and out.
  - At the fixed end the air cannot move, but it compresses and expands a lot.

- The three lowest modes of the air match the modes of a building. **F1** has a single point of no motion, at the fixed end. **F2** has two, one at the fixed end and one inside the tube. **F3** has three, and between every pair of no-motion points the air alternates between places of most motion and places of most compression.

- For the **schwa**, the central vowel with no constriction, the tube behaves like a uniform fixed-free system, so F2 should be three times F1 and F3 five times F1. A measured schwa had formants near **500, 1500 and 2500 hertz**, which are the odd multiples the theory predicts.

**Section 17: Where Were You a Year Ago?**

- A short question like **Where were you a year ago?** puts all of the constriction reasoning to work at once. Each sound squeezes the air at a different place, so the formants move up and down continuously instead of sitting at fixed values.
  - When the tongue makes an **/i/**-like squeeze near the front of the mouth, **F1 goes down and F2 goes up**, because that spot is massy in Mode 1 and springy in Mode 2.
  - When the lips round for **you**, **F1 and F2 both go down**, because the free end is massy in every mode.
  - When the tongue moves back and down, as in an **/a/**-like sound, **F1 rises and F2 falls**, because that spot is springy in Mode 1 and massy in Mode 2.
- **Speech is a continuous series of constrictions, not a list of separate vowels**, so the frequencies of the air in the vocal tract are always changing. The same principles of masses and springs predict which way each formant moves at every moment.
