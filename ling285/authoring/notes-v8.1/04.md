**Section 1: The Shortest Path Problem**

- **The shortest path problem asks for the cheapest route through a network of connected points, from a start to an end.** Each point is a **state**, a place we can be at a given moment. Each arrow is a possible move from one state to another, and each arrow carries a number called its **cost**. The cost can stand for distance, time, effort or anything to be kept as small as possible. The total cost of a route is the sum of the costs on every arrow along it.

- **The network starts at A and ends at J, with the states arranged in columns called stages.** Every arrow moves forward exactly one stage, never skipping a stage or moving backward. Stage one holds A, stage two holds B and C, stage three holds D, E and F, stage four holds G, H and I, and stage five holds J. Any route from A to J crosses exactly four arrows and makes exactly four decisions.
  - From A, the arrow to B costs 12 and the arrow to C costs 7. From B, the arrows to D, E and F cost 5, 6 and 9. From C, the arrows to D, E and F cost 14, 10 and 11.
  - From D, the arrows to G, H and I cost 8, 7 and 10. From E they cost 9, 7 and 9. From F they cost 10, 7 and 8. The last stage has one arrow out of each state. From G to J costs 5, from H to J costs 9, and from I to J costs 8.

- **This network is a model of decision-making over time.** Every choice has an immediate cost, and every choice also controls which states, and therefore which future costs, are available at the next stage. A choice that looks good now can lock us into expensive moves later.


**Section 2: The Greedy Algorithm**

- **A greedy algorithm makes each decision by looking only at the arrows leaving the current state and taking the cheapest one.** It never looks further ahead than one move.
  - At A, the choices are 12 to B and 7 to C, so greed takes us to **C**. At C, the choices are 14, 10 and 11, so greed takes us to **E**. At E, the choices are 9, 7 and 9, so greed takes us to **H**. At H there is only one arrow, to J, costing 9.
  - The greedy route A, C, E, H, J costs 7 plus 10 plus 7 plus 9, which is 33. The route A, B, D, G, J starts with the more expensive move of 12, but its total is 12 plus 5 plus 8 plus 5, which is 30.

- **Greed is not good for this problem.** The greedy rule failed at the very first decision, since the cheap move to C led into a part of the network where every continuation was more costly. Greed also walked straight into H, which ends with the large final cost of 9. The greedy algorithm has no way of knowing about costs that come later, so a late obstacle can make an early cheap-looking choice a bad one.

- **A very small network makes the failure of greed easy to see.** From a start there are two routes to an end, each through one middle state. On the top route the first arrow costs 10 and the second costs 10. On the bottom route the first arrow costs only 2, but the second costs 100.
  - A greedy decision at the start compares 10 with 2 and picks the bottom route, then gets stuck paying 100. The totals are 20 for the top route and 102 for the bottom route, so the top route is five times cheaper even though its first step was five times more expensive.
  - Writing inside each middle state the cost of getting from that state to the end, 10 on top and 100 on the bottom, makes the decision at the start simple. Add the cost of the first arrow to the number stored in the state it leads to, and compare 20 with 102. Storing the cost of the rest of the journey inside each state removes the blindness of greed.


**Section 3: The Exhaustive Algorithm**

- **An exhaustive algorithm solves the shortest path problem by brute force.** It lists every possible route from start to end, adds up the cost of each one, and picks the smallest total. Nothing is skipped, so the answer is **guaranteed to be correct**.
  - The number of routes comes from multiplying the number of choices at each stage. From A there are 2 choices, from the second stage 3, from the third stage 3, and from the fourth stage only 1, so there are 18 complete routes. Each needs 3 additions to total, so checking every route takes 54 additions, followed by comparing 18 totals.

- **The problem with exhaustive search is its growth.** Every added stage multiplies the number of routes by the number of choices at that stage, and every added state per stage makes each multiplier larger. With **many stages and many states** the number of routes explodes, and checking all of them takes a very, very long time.
  - Exhaustive search is accurate but impractical for large problems, while greedy search is fast but can be wrong. A method is needed that is both accurate and efficient.


**Section 4: Bellman's Idea, Imagining the Values**

- **Bellman approached the problem by starting with an imaginary step.** Imagine we already knew the best possible cost of getting from B to J, and from C to J. These numbers are called VBJ and VCJ. The letter V stands for **Value**, and the Value of a state is the lowest total cost of getting from that state to the goal.

- **If those two Values were known, the best cost from A to J, VAJ, would follow from one short calculation.** Any route out of A goes first to either B or C, since those are the only two arrows leaving A.
  - Going to B costs 12 for that arrow, and then the best possible rest of the trip is VBJ, so the best total through B is 12 plus VBJ. Going to C costs 7 and then VCJ, so the best total through C is 7 plus VCJ. The best cost from A is whichever of these two totals is smaller.
  - The word **min** means take the minimum, the smallest number in the set inside the braces. Each number in the set is built the same way, the cost of one arrow plus the Value of the state that arrow leads to.


**Section 5: Decomposing the Problem into Subproblems**

- **The difficulty is that we do not know VBJ or VCJ.** The rule for VAJ is correct, but it depends on two numbers that have not yet been found.

- **Bellman's general strategy handles exactly this situation. To solve a problem, decompose it into subproblems you can solve.** Finding VAJ becomes two smaller problems, finding VBJ and finding VCJ. The phrase "if you can" matters. If a subproblem still cannot be solved directly, it gets broken down again into even smaller subproblems.


**Section 6: Breaking the Problem Down Stage by Stage**

- **Bellman's reasoning at A applies in the same way at B.** We do not know the best cost from B to J, but if we knew the best costs from D, E and F to J, then we could find the best cost from B to J.
  - From B we can go to D for 5, to E for 6 or to F for 9. After each move, the best we can do is the Value of the state we arrived at. So VBJ is the minimum of 5 plus VDJ, 6 plus VEJ and 9 plus VFJ.
  - C works the same way with its arrows costing 14, 10 and 11, so VCJ is the minimum of 14 plus VDJ, 10 plus VEJ and 11 plus VFJ.

- **These rules still depend on unknown Values, so the decomposition continues one more stage.** The Values of D, E and F depend on the Values of G, H and I.
  - VDJ is the minimum of 8 plus VGJ, 7 plus VHJ and 10 plus VIJ. VEJ is the minimum of 9 plus VGJ, 7 plus VHJ and 9 plus VIJ. VFJ is the minimum of 10 plus VGJ, 7 plus VHJ and 8 plus VIJ.

- **At the fourth stage the subproblems finally become ones that can be solved directly.** Each of G, H and I has exactly one arrow, straight to J, so there is no decision to make and each Value is simply the cost of that arrow. VGJ is 5, VHJ is 9, and VIJ is 8.
  - The rules form a chain of dependence that runs from the end of the network back to the start. The known Values of G, H and I feed the rules for D, E and F. Those feed the rules for B and C, and those feed the single rule for A. Information about the cost of reaching J travels **backward** through the network, one stage at a time, starting from the states closest to the goal.


**Section 7: Calculating the Values, Then Going Greedy**

- **With the Values of G, H and I known, the Values of the third stage can be calculated directly.** For D the three totals are 8 plus 5, 7 plus 9 and 10 plus 8, so the minimum of 13, 16 and 18 gives VDJ of 13. For E the totals are 9 plus 5, 7 plus 9 and 9 plus 8, so VEJ is 14. For F the totals are 10 plus 5, 7 plus 9 and 8 plus 8, so VFJ is 15.
  - In all three cases the smallest total comes from going to G. G has the smallest Value of the fourth stage, 5, and that outweighs the differences in the arrow costs.

- **The third-stage Values now go into the rules for B and C.** For B the totals are 5 plus 13, 6 plus 14 and 9 plus 15, so VBJ is 18. For C the totals are 14 plus 13, 10 plus 14 and 11 plus 15, so VCJ is 24. Finally, for A the totals are 12 plus 18 and 7 plus 24, which are 30 and 31, so VAJ is 30.
  - Every state now carries its Value. A is 30, B is 18, C is 24, D is 13, E is 14, F is 15, G is 5, H is 9, and I is 8.

- **The best path comes from going greedy on the Values, not on the costs.** At each state, compare the options by adding the cost of the arrow to the Value of the state it leads to, and take the smallest sum. This is still a one-step-at-a-time choice, but now each option already includes the whole future.
  - At A, going to B gives 12 plus 18, which is 30, and going to C gives 7 plus 24, which is 31, so we go to **B** even though its arrow is the more expensive one. At B, going to D gives 18, to E gives 20, and to F gives 24, so we go to **D**. At D, going to G gives 13, to H gives 16, and to I gives 18, so we go to **G**. From G the only move is to J.
  - The best path is **A, B, D, G, J**, with total 12 plus 5 plus 8 plus 5, which equals 30 and matches VAJ exactly.

- **Dynamic programming is this two-part method.** First, calculate the Values by working backward from the goal. Second, choose moves greedily using those Values. The method needed only one small minimum calculation per state instead of adding up all 18 routes one by one.


**Section 8: Dynamic Programming Is Foresight**

- **Dynamic programming is foresight about the whole route ahead.** Standing at the third stage, at D, E or F, greedy reasoning on arrow costs alone would often be pulled toward H, since the arrow into H costs 7 from every one of D, E and F, the cheapest or tied for cheapest. Dynamic programming reveals that **state H leads to something terrible**. The arrow out of H into J costs 9, the largest final cost in the network, so H has the largest Value in its stage.
  - At E, the arrow cost alone favors H at 7 over G at 9. Adding the Values changes the answer. Going to H gives 7 plus 9, which is 16, while going to G gives 9 plus 5, which is 14. The Value of H warns about the 9 before we ever reach it.
  - This warning still works if there are many more stages between the current state and J. However far away the bad cost lies, it has already been folded into the Values of every state that leads toward it.

- **In the original problem, a late obstacle, the 9 on the arrow from H to J, caused an early greedy decision based on arrow cost to lead to a bad path.** The greedy algorithm picked C at the first step and ended up paying for that 9 at the last step.

- **The Values contain the future.** Each Value already includes information about every obstacle between that state and the goal, so we can safely go greedy on them. The Values make **present decisions optimal**, since the whole future from each state is encapsulated in its Value.
  - **Søren Kierkegaard** wrote that life can only be understood backwards, but it must be lived forwards. Dynamic programming has exactly this structure. The Values are understood backwards, calculated from the goal toward the start. The decisions are lived forwards, made one at a time from A toward J.


**Section 9: Decomposition into Trivial Problems**

- **Dynamic programming starts by posing a problem, then its subproblems, then its sub-subproblems, and so on.** The big problem is solved by solving the subproblems, and the subproblems are solved by solving the sub-subproblems.
  - The big problem is finding VAJ. Its subproblems are finding VBJ and VCJ. Their subproblems are finding VDJ, VEJ and VFJ. Their subproblems are finding VGJ, VHJ and VIJ.

- **The breakdown continues until the problems are trivial, meaning they can be answered with no calculation at all.** For G, H and I, the answer is simply the cost of the one arrow to J. Once the trivial problems are solved, we go back and solve the bigger problems, one level at a time, until we arrive at the original problem.


**Section 10: Propagating Values Backward with Bellman's Rule**

- **The bigger problems are solved by propagating Values from each stage to the stage before it using Bellman's idea.** To propagate means to pass information along, and here the information moves from later stages to the stages before them.

- **Bellman's rule states that the Value at one state is the minimum of a set of numbers, each of which is the sum of the cost to one of the next states and the Value at that next state.** Every rule from VAJ down to VDJ, VEJ and VFJ follows this same pattern.
  - The rule starts with the stage just before the goal, where the Values are known directly. For each state in the stage before that, list every arrow leaving it, add each arrow's cost to the Value of the state where it ends, and keep the smallest sum as that state's Value. Repeat for the stage before, and stop when the starting state has its Value.

- **Each state needs only a handful of additions and one comparison, and each Value is calculated once and reused by every state before it that connects to it.** VDJ of 13 is calculated once and then used in both the B rule and the C rule. This reuse is what keeps dynamic programming efficient when there are many stages and states.


**Section 11: Going Through the Bad 12**

- **Dynamic programming says to go through the bad 12 first to get onto a route that does not get stuck later at the 9.** Paying 12 to reach B instead of 7 to reach C looks like a mistake at the first step, but it puts us on the route with the lowest total. It may seem that making such a choice requires a big brain, since the decision-maker has to weigh the whole future against the present.

- **The same trade-off appears when a lifeguard must reach a person.** When the lifeguard and the person are both on sand, the lifeguard runs at a **constant speed** the entire way, so the fastest route is the **shortest path**, a straight line. This holds whether the person is directly across the sand or off at an angle along the beach.
  - When the person is in the water, the speeds are **different**. The lifeguard runs fast on sand but swims slowly in water. The **path of least time** is no longer a straight line. The lifeguard runs a longer distance along the sand, reaching the water at a point closer to the swimmer, and then swims a short distance. The route bends at the water's edge.
  - The lifeguard's best route accepts a longer stretch of running, which looks worse by distance, to cut down the slow and costly swimming later. This is the same structure as paying 12 early to avoid the 9 at the end.


**Section 12: Decision-Making Is Like Physics**

- **Decision-making is like physics, and light makes the same choice.** Light behaves like the lifeguard in **refraction**, the bending of light as it passes from one material into another. Light travels **faster in air** and **slower in water**, and when it passes between them its path bends at the surface, just as the lifeguard's route bends at the water's edge.
  - This bending is why a pencil standing in a glass of water appears broken at the water line. The light coming from the underwater part of the pencil changes direction at the surface before reaching the eye.

- **Light takes a path shaped the way the lifeguard's best route is shaped, with a bend at the boundary between a fast region and a slow region.** Light has no brain at all, so choosing a route that accepts an early cost to avoid a larger later one does not require a big brain. Physical systems arrive at these optimal paths on their own, and dynamic programming describes the same kind of optimal route through a series of stages.


**Section 13: Figuring Out the Best Route**

- **The complete solution can be written on the network by labeling each state with its Value and its best next state.** A is labeled 30 and B. B is labeled 18 and D, and C is labeled 24 and E. D is labeled 13 and G, E is labeled 14 and G, and F is labeled 15 and G. G, H and I are labeled 5 and J, 9 and J, and 8 and J.

- **With these labels, the best route is read off by starting at A and following each state's best next state**, A to B, B to D, D to G, and G to J, for a total of 30.
  - The labels also give the best route from **any** state, not just from A. From C, the labels say to go to E, then G, then J, at a cost of 24. A single backward calculation solves the problem for every possible starting point at once.


**Section 14: Pop Quiz, Systems That Settle to the Same Value**

- **A learning process can be tracked by plotting a quantity x against epochs, where an epoch is one round of learning in which x gets updated.** The **learning rate** controls how large each update is, and here it is 10 percent. Three runs start from different **initial conditions**, abbreviated **IC**.
  - The run with IC of 130 starts high and drops quickly toward about 80. The run with IC of 10 starts low and rises quickly toward about 80. The run with IC of 80 starts there and stays. Within roughly the first 25 to 50 epochs, all three runs meet, and from then until epoch 300 they fluctuate together in a narrow band around 80.
  - The final value does not depend on where the process starts. The initial condition only changes the early part of the curve. The changes are fastest at the beginning, when x is farthest from 80, and they slow down as x gets closer.

- **Coffee changing temperature in a room follows the same pattern**, tracked as temperature against time over 8 minutes, with **room temperature at 18 degrees**.
  - Three cups of **hot coffee** start at 100, 80 and 60 degrees. All three cool toward room temperature, and by 8 minutes they are at roughly 29, 26 and 23 degrees. Three cups of **iced coffee** start at 10, 5 and 0 degrees. All three warm toward room temperature, and by 8 minutes they are all close to it, at roughly 16 to 17 degrees.
  - The hot coffee starting at 100 drops much faster at first than the one starting at 60. The larger the gap between the coffee and the room, the faster the temperature changes, and the change slows down as the gap shrinks.

- **In both systems, many different starting values move toward the same final value**, about 80 for the learning process and 18 degrees for the coffee. The speed of change depends on how far the current value is from that final value.
