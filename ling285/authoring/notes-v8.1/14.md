**Section 1: The Vocal Folds as the First Vibrating System**

- Speech uses **two major vibrating systems**. The first is the **vocal folds**, a **membrane** that is **fixed on all sides** and made of a whole bunch of masses and springs. It vibrates in many modes at once, with all the masses going up and down together in Mode 1, half going up while the other half goes down in Mode 2, and more complicated patterns above that. All five modes add together into one combined motion, and this is literally what happens in the throat while a person talks.
  - Because the vocal folds are a fixed-fixed system, their frequencies are **harmonics**, which are whole-number multiples of the lowest one.
  - The **bottom-most harmonic** is called **F0**. Each person has a different F0, but the other harmonics are always its multiples.

**Section 2: The Air in the Vocal Tract as the Second Vibrating System**

- The second system is the **air in the vocal tract**. It is **fixed at the glottis**, because the closed vocal folds keep the air there from moving back and forth, and it is **free at the lips**, where the air can go in and out. Its modes follow the fixed-free pattern, with **one, three, five and seven quarter-wave packets**, so its frequencies are the **odd multiples** of the lowest one.

- **The closed end is always springy and the open end is always massy, in every mode.** Air can be squeezed against a closed end, but there is nothing to squeeze against at an open end.
  - Near the **closed end**, the slivers of air come very close together and then far apart, so the air **compresses and expands** and acts like a **spring**.
  - Near the **open end**, the slivers stay equally far apart and simply go back and forth, so the air acts like a **mass**.
  - In higher modes, springy places and massy places **alternate** more and more times along the tube.
  - In plain terms, the lips end swings like a heavy door, and the throat end squeezes like a pillow.

**Section 3: Reasoning About a Constriction in One Mode**

- A constriction can be judged at any spot, in any mode, with the same chain of reasoning. Take a spot inside the tube where, in **Mode 3**, the lines of air are bunching up and spreading apart while hardly moving back and forth. Constricting the tract at that spot raises **F3**.
  - The first step is that constricting air always **increases both its mass and its stiffness**, just as squeezing a tire makes it stiffer.
  - The second step is that at this spot in Mode 3, the air is acting like a **pure spring**. There is compression and expansion but no movement back and forth, so the extra mass does not matter.
  - The third step is that only the extra stiffness counts, and **raising stiffness raises frequency**, so **F3 goes up**.

- Each new spot and mode needs the same steps. The answer is never memorized. It is **built one claim at a time**, with each claim resting on the one before it, which is the same way any good argument is built in science or in art.

**Section 4: Vowels Squeeze the Air in Different Places**

- Images taken while people speak show where each vowel squeezes the air in the tube that runs from the closed glottis to the open lips.
  - The vowel in **done** has **no constriction** at all.
  - **dean** squeezes the air near the front, about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - **Don** squeezes the air far back in the throat, about **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - **dune** squeezes the air **at the lips** by rounding them.
  - **Dan** is a low front vowel.

- In the mass-spring model of the tube, a constriction is drawn as **heavier masses and stiffer springs** at that spot. It always raises both mass and stiffness, but because moving places and springy places alternate, **its effect depends on what the air is doing there in each mode**.

**Section 5: /u/, /i/ and /a/**

- **/u/** squeezes the air at the lips. At the open end the air goes back and forth with hardly any compression in **every** mode, so the air there is a pure mass. The extra stiffness does not matter, the extra mass does, and **F1 and F2 both go down**.
  - Compared with schwa's reference values of about **500 and 1500 hertz**, a measured **/u/** had an F1 down in the low two hundreds, well below 500.

- **/i/** squeezes the air about two thirds of the way from the glottis.
  - In **Mode 1**, the air there moves back and forth, so it acts like a mass, the extra mass counts, and **F1 goes down**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the air there compresses and expands, so it acts like a spring, the extra stiffness counts, and **F2 goes up**.
  - A speaker moving from schwa into /i/ showed exactly this, with **F1 going down and F2 going up**.

- **/a/** squeezes the air about one third of the way from the glottis.
  - In **Mode 1**, that spot is close enough to the closed end that the air is mostly springy, so the extra stiffness counts and **F1 goes up**.
  - In **Mode 2**, that spot sits between two springy regions, so the air there is mostly moving, the extra mass counts, and **F2 goes down**.
  - A speaker producing /a/ showed **F1 going up and F2 going down**, and a typical /a/ has its F1 and F2 fairly close together.

- **Whenever stiffness is what matters at a spot, the frequency goes up, and whenever mass is what matters, the frequency goes down.**

**Section 6: The Source Sets the Harmonic Spacing**

- The vocal folds are the **source**, and their mass and stiffness set **F0** and therefore the spacing of the harmonics.
  - **Big, heavy, loose vocal folds** have a large mass and small stiffness, so their **F0 is low**, around 100 hertz, with harmonics at 200, 300 and so on.
  - **Tighter, lighter vocal folds** have a higher F0, such as 250 hertz, with harmonics at 500 and 750.
  - **Very tight, light vocal folds** have an even higher F0, such as 350 hertz, with harmonics at 700 and 1050.

- **F0 is not fixed while people talk.** Only a robot keeps F0 constant. Speakers keep changing F0 by tightening and loosening the vocal folds with the muscles in the larynx, and listeners use those changes to get meaning.
  - **The dog slept here** said as a statement has F0 falling at the very end, while the same words as a question have F0 rising at the end.
  - The way F0 moves also tells listeners whether a person is being sarcastic, pretending, or even lying.

- F0 can be measured in **Praat** by taking a little piece of speech, viewing a spectral slice and picking the lowest harmonic. One measured voice had F0 around 170 hertz, and the next harmonic sat at about twice that value, as the theory predicts.

**Section 7: The Filter Selects the Formants**

- The air in the vocal tract is the **filter**, and its formants depend on **where the constriction is**. Typical values show the patterns.
  - **Schwa** has F1 near **500** and F2 near **1500** hertz.
  - **/u/** has F1 near 300, F2 near 1000 and F3 near 2000, so all of them are lower.
  - **/i/** has F1 near 300 and F2 near 2200, which are very far apart.
  - **/a/** has F1 and F2 both near 800 to 900 hertz, which are very close together.

- **The core rule is that a formant encourages the closest harmonics to be audible, and harmonics far from that formant are muted.** If there are harmonics near 500 hertz during a schwa, they are amplified, and the farther a harmonic is from 500, the weaker it comes out. Between two formants the harmonics get weaker, then they grow again near the next formant.
  - **The vowel shape is the filter. The voiced source supplies the harmonics, and the filter chooses which harmonics survive.**

**Section 8: One Filter Can Look Different at Different Pitches**

- **The lower F0 is, the more harmonics the formants can shape.** With heavy, loose vocal folds and harmonics every 100 hertz, many harmonics sit under each formant, so the shaped spectrum traces the formant peaks smoothly and is easy to read.

- With very light, tight vocal folds, the harmonics are far apart, and a formant at 500 hertz may have **no harmonic near it to amplify**. Sparse harmonics can miss a formant or make it look ambiguous.
  - An **opera singer** is an extreme case. Their harmonics are so far apart that the formants do not line up with any harmonic, so listeners struggle to hear which vowel is being sung. Singers are even trained to shape the mouth so the formants line up with the harmonics.
  - A **small child** of two or three has an F0 near 500 hertz, so very few harmonics fall near the formants. Unless a listener is one of the child's caretakers and is used to the child's speech, it is very hard to tell what the child is saying.
  - A simple way to see it is that the formants are the same in every case, but a high voice gives the listener only a few harmonics to hear them through.

**Section 9: Spectrograms of Real Speech**

- A **spectrogram** shows how speech changes over time. **Time** runs along the horizontal axis, **frequency** runs up the vertical axis, and **darkness** shows how much energy, or amplitude, there is.

- In a recording of **Where were you a year ago?**, the formants never sit still.
  - Where the vowel sounds like **/i/**, F2 rises high and F1 stays low, because the air is squeezed where Mode 2 is springy and Mode 1 is massy.
  - During **you**, the formants drop, since rounding the lips lowers all of them.
  - Toward the end of the sentence, F2 goes up and then down again as the vowels change. **All the formants are constantly changing during speech.**

- **All the formants are constantly changing during speech, so speech is not a string of frozen vowels.** Every rise and fall of a formant can be predicted with the same mass and spring reasoning, by asking where the constriction is and whether the air there is moving or compressing in each mode.
  - This constant change is why a network that sees two formants from one frozen moment cannot understand real speech, and why speech recognition needs systems that take in whole stretches of sound over time.
  - In plain terms, a spectrogram of a real sentence is a moving record of the tongue and lips, and reading it means following the dark bands as they rise and fall.

**Section 10: Speech Perception Integrates Over Time**

- A spliced recording reveals how complex speech perception is. A speaker says **ma** and **na**. The **m** part of ma is cut out and joined to the **a** part of na. Since the consonant portion came from ma, the result should sound like ma, but **listeners clearly hear na**.

- **The perception of m or n comes more from the vowel than from the nasal sound itself.** The formants of the vowel differ depending on the consonant before it.
  - After **m**, one formant of the vowel stays fairly **stationary**.
  - After **n**, that formant shows a **big drop** at the start of the vowel.
  - So the vowel portion carries the information about which consonant was heard, and listeners decide what consonant they heard **during the vowel**, after the consonant is already over.

- This was one of the first striking results in speech perception, published in the **1950s** by the linguist **André Malécot**. It means listeners do not decide on one slice of sound, then the next, then the next. They **take in whole stretches of speech and then decide** about sounds that came first by using what follows them.
  - For about seventy years, speech recognition machines that treated each slice as its own sound **failed**, despite billions of dollars of effort.
  - A machine that understands speech has to be complex enough to use future sound to decide about the present, which is why modern speech recognition relies on large language models built from many neural networks.

**Section 11: Formants as Input to a Vowel Recognizer**

- A **neural network** can be trained to recognize vowels from formants. Its **two inputs** are **F1 and F2**, measured from real recordings of five vowels, and its job is to decide which vowel was spoken. **The knowledge of the network is in its weights**, which are learned from the data.

- The network has **five output neurons**, one for each word, **done, dune, dean, Don and Dan**. Between the input and the output it has **three hidden layers**, with **3, 2 and 4 neurons**.
  - Information arrives at the input, a little like sound arriving at the ear.
  - Each hidden layer turns the numbers it receives into a new representation, a little like successive stages of processing in the brain.
  - The output layer gives the network's decision about which vowel it heard.

- Every neuron in one layer connects to every neuron in the next, so the network is **fully connected**. This version is **purely linear**, meaning it has **no activation functions**, so each neuron just passes on the result of its inner product.

**Section 12: Random Weights Give Signed Contributions**

- Before training, **every weight is a random number between minus one and one**. A **positive** weight makes its input push the next neuron up, and a **negative** weight makes its input push the next neuron down.

- The example input is **F1 of 320 and F2 of 2000 hertz**. A very low F1 with a very high F2 is the pattern of **/i/**, so the correct answer is **dean**.

**Section 13: Forward Flow and the Output Distribution**

- **Forward propagation** carries the input through the network one layer at a time. Each neuron computes an **inner product**, which means multiplying each incoming number by its weight and adding the results.
  - For the top neuron of the first hidden layer, F1 is multiplied by its weight, F2 is multiplied by its weight, and the two products are added to give that neuron's activation.
  - The other neurons do the same with their own weights, so the first hidden layer produces three numbers. Those numbers pass through the next weights to make two numbers, then four, then five at the output.
  - None of these calculations ever needs to be done by hand. What matters is that every step is an inner product.

- The output is a **probability distribution** over the five words, with values of **.5, .1, .1, .1 and .2**, which add up to one. The network puts its highest probability on **done**, so with random weights it is a **stupid network** that gets the answer wrong.
  - The correct answer is given as the target distribution **0, 0, 1, 0, 0**, with all of the probability on dean.
  - **Stochastic gradient descent** then changes each weight, so that the next time the network sees 320 and 2000, the inner products lead to a high probability for dean.
  - In plain terms, the network starts out guessing, the target says what the right guess was, and learning nudges every weight so the right guess wins next time.

**Section 14: The Same Network in PyTorch**

- **PyTorch** declares the network as a list of layers.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

net = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Linear(2, 3, bias=False),
    nn.Linear(3, 2, bias=False),
    nn.Linear(2, 4, bias=False),
    nn.Linear(4, 5, bias=False),
)
```

- **Sequential** means the network has **one layer after another, in a sequence**. **Linear** means **do inner products** of weights and previous activations.
  - **nn.Linear(2, 3)** reads as **take input from 2 neurons, and have 3 neurons**. The next line takes input from those 3 and has 2, then 2 in and 4 out, then 4 in and 5 out.
  - Turning off the **bias**, by setting it to False, keeps every layer purely linear, matching the diagram of the network. This code only declares the network and does not train it.

- **Only layers that do calculations get a line.** The **input layer** has no weights coming into it and does no inner products, because it just takes in numbers. So the input layer never gets its own line, and the first line describes the **first hidden layer**.
  - This network has **five layers in total**, which are the input layer, three hidden layers and the output layer.
  - It has **three hidden layers**, and the **last line is always the output layer**.
  - "How many layers" and "how many hidden layers" are different questions, so the question has to be read carefully.

- Changing the network means changing the numbers in the lines. To **add a hidden layer of 3 neurons right before the output**, the first three lines stay the same. The old last line, 4 in and 5 out, becomes **4 in and 3 out**, and a new last line takes **3 in and gives 5 out**. Read aloud, the network becomes 2 in and 3 out, 3 in and 2 out, 2 in and 4 out, 4 in and 3 out, and 3 in and 5 out.
  - A quick check for any rewrite is that each line's first number must match the previous line's second number, because each layer takes its input from the layer before it.

- The same reading works for any network written this way. A network declared as 4 in and 3 out, 3 in and 8 out, 8 in and 4 out, 4 in and 4 out, and 4 in and 5 out takes **4 numbers as input**, has **four hidden layers** with 3, 8, 4 and 4 neurons, and decides among **5 categories**, one for each output neuron.
  - Drawn as a network, it has a column of **4 input neurons**, then hidden columns of **3, 8, 4 and 4 neurons**, then a column of **5 output neurons**, with every neuron connected to every neuron in the next column.
  - Asked what the target distribution looks like when the first category is correct, the answer puts a **1 in the first slot and 0 everywhere else**.
