**Section 1: From a Neuron to Agents That Communicate**

- Everything in modern AI grows from **one very simple idea**. A **neuron** takes information from other neurons, multiplies each input by its weight and adds them up, which is the **inner product**. That arithmetic stands in for neurons communicating electrochemically. People who move science forward usually start from extremely simple ideas like this one and then multiply them many times over.
  - A **neural network** repeats that operation layer after layer, taking a vector in and giving a **decision** out. Even something as complex as a person making a decision can be built from inner products and a function like the sigmoid, combined many times.
  - But a network that makes one **static decision**, such as hearing one vowel from two formants, cannot capture cognition that **unfolds in time**.

- **Language depends on long-distance relationships between words.** In "the dog that my parents, who I love very much, who live in a state far, far away," the next word is **barks**, not bark.
  - **Barks** goes with the singular **dog**, no matter how many other words come in between, even plural ones like parents.
  - No one teaches this rule explicitly. Speakers absorb it from the language around them, and an LLM learns it the same way, from data, by using its weights to do inner products, then more inner products, until it predicts barks.
  - English sentence structure, or **syntax**, is so complicated that many linguists spend their careers on it, and that is why an LLM needs so many layers of computation.

- **LLMs can be multilingual and multimodal.** The first use of LLMs, in 2017, was **translation** between languages, which is now nearly solved for hundreds of languages.
  - A system trained on several languages, such as English, Dutch and Japanese, learns how words go together in general, so it also gets good at very different languages, such as Navajo.
  - **Multimodal** systems connect different modes, such as text, speech and images, so they can caption a photo or create an image from a spoken description.

- **Nobody fully understands what all the weights are doing.** The designs were not planned from scratch. Over about 75 years, people tinkered with something that worked a little, changed it so it worked a little better, and kept going, much as dogs were bred over a few hundred years into today's breeds. Researchers around the world are now trying to figure out what the weights do, partly so that much smaller, less wasteful systems could be built.

- **An agentic system is a team of LLMs that talk to each other in English.** An outline LLM plans the answer, a writing LLM writes it, a critic LLM checks whether each statement follows from the one before, and a revision LLM loops with the critic, sometimes under a **controller** agent that passes work between them.
  - The agents are also **multilingual in computer languages**, such as Python and database languages, because they were trained to predict whole programs too.
  - Once each LLM is trained, its weights are usually **not trained further** while the agents work together. Some new systems are starting to learn their own connections, which makes them partly self-designing.
  - Agents share a **common memory** where they can write and read information. That shared memory is a big source of their power and also of the danger, because it lets them coordinate in ways nobody planned.
  - The same tools can do good work. One agentic system acts as a patient teacher for a nearly lost Native American language, even though there is far too little text in that language to train an ordinary LLM on it.

**Section 2: Parallel Heads and Successive Layers**

- **Predicting the next word is so difficult that an LLM needs many layers, and in each layer it needs many parallel heads.** A head is a separate expert, and in each layer the knowledge from all the heads is brought together to produce **improved vectors**. Then the same thing happens again in the next layer.
  - This is a question for the exam, so the structure matters. **Layers go one after another from the input to the output, and heads sit side by side within each layer.**

- **A classroom comparison makes heads and layers clear.** Imagine trying to improve oneself by visiting a series of classes, one after another.
  - In the first class, each student is asked for an independent opinion on how to improve. Each student is like a **head**, an expert with its own view.
  - All of those opinions are then **combined** to make one improved version. That combining is what a layer does with the opinions of its heads.
  - Then the next class is visited, and the same thing happens again. **The classes are the layers.**
  - In plain terms, layers give an LLM its depth, one round of improvement after another, and heads give it width, many experts consulted at once within each round.

- **Each head is a set of weights**, and an expert's knowledge is in its weights. Every head in every layer learns how to improve the vectors so that, at the very end, the next word can be predicted.
  - Large systems have **a hundred or more layers** and on the order of **a hundred heads** in each layer, which shows how complicated language, vision and the other things these systems handle really are.
  - The heads and layers are trained the same way as everything else. A sentence like "the dog that ... barks" is taken from text, the system predicts the next word, and if it predicts the wrong word, **every head in every layer** adjusts its weights through backpropagation so the next prediction is closer to barks.
  - Doing this at scale uses enormous resources. In some states, a large share of the water goes to the data centers that run this training.

**Section 3: A Dynamical Rule Across Time and Depth**

- **A dynamical system is a rule that is fed back to itself.** Numbers go in, the rule acts on them, numbers come out, and the output is fed back in as the next input, again and again. The overall goal is to understand the general structure, not to memorize formulas.
  - In the **harmonic oscillator**, the two numbers are the position x and the velocity v. Starting with x at one and v at zero, the first step leaves x the same and makes v go down.
  - x and v **take turns**. The new x is computed from the old v, and then the new v is computed from that new x. Step by step, the rule draws a **cosine** for x.
  - Fields as far apart as finance use the same idea with different rules. Stock prices are modeled with equations that are fed back on themselves, though the real market never follows them exactly.

- **Jordan's network was a marriage of neural networks and dynamical systems.** Based on the time variation in speech production, Jordan designed a network whose **output is copied back into its state units**, which also keep a fading copy of their own previous values.
  - **Plan units** and **state units** feed the hidden layer, the hidden layer feeds the output, and the output loops back into the state. The same weights are used at every step.
  - After more than **thirty years of tinkering**, this idea became the **LLM**.

- **In an LLM, the layer is the rule.** Each layer applies its weights to every vector, and the next layer works on the result, so **depth plays the role of time**. Following one number up through the layers traces out a curve, much like the oscillator's curve, though not an exact cosine.
  - So the LLM is an unrolled recurrent network, laid out layer by layer, just as the spring's motion was laid out step by step when it was computed on a computer.
  - The rule in each layer is the weights of its heads, and those weights are learned through backpropagation by predicting the next word.
  - Agentic systems also contain loops, such as the critic and revision loop, so dynamical systems run all the way up through AI.
  - The mathematics of inner products that LLMs depend on was first studied deeply in **quantum mechanics**, about a hundred years ago, so physics and AI are closely tied in more ways than one.

**Section 4: One Prediction Task for Different Sequences**

- An LLM is given **15 vectors** and asked to predict the **16th**, and the vectors can come from anywhere, because the machine only ever sees lists of numbers.
  - They can be **15 words** of a sentence, such as "The cat that the dog chased ran up the old tree and sat on the," with the next word missing.
  - They can be **15 frames of a spectrogram**, with the next slice of speech missing.
  - They can be **15 patches of an image**, read row by row, with the bottom-right patch missing.
  - They can be **15 amino acids** in a protein. In the B chain of insulin, the residue that follows the first fifteen is the amino acid written Y.

- **The same design and the same prediction game work for language, speech, vision and proteins.** Whatever the vectors stand for, the job is always to predict what comes next in the sequence.

**Section 5: Which Sequences Can Be English Words?**

- English has about 30,000 words, so training an LLM on words takes enormous data, money and energy. But speakers also know a much smaller system made of **only a few dozen sounds**, which is how vowels and consonants can follow each other to make words. Because there are so few sounds, an LLM for this system can be small enough to study closely.

- **A simple English syllable is a consonant, then a vowel, then a consonant**, as in Don, sit, shave, cup, dog and fish. The pattern is about sounds, so the silent e of shave does not count.
  - English also allows **clusters**, as in split, flat, black, glad, desk, help and milk. Only certain clusters are allowed, so **sblit**, **lfat**, **desb** and **lapm** are impossible, and a product called "desb" would sound instantly un-English.
  - Long words are chains of syllables. **Atlantic** is fine because t and l sit across a syllable break, but **tlantic** is impossible, because tl cannot start an English syllable.

- **Every language has its own rules for sequences**, and speakers absorb them without being taught.
  - **Mandarin** allows only nasal sounds at the end of a syllable, so words like mat, sit and shave are impossible in it.
  - **Cantonese** also allows a final p, as in cup.
  - **Russian and Polish** allow clusters like zb or dl at the start of a word, which English never allows.
  - **Spanish** does not allow an s and t to start a word, so speakers add an e at the start of a word like stadium, keeping each syllable simple.

**Section 6: Guessing the Next Letter**

- Guessing the next letter of an English word reveals this sound knowledge directly. **After a vowel, almost anything can follow**, but **at the start of a word the choices narrow sharply**.
  - After **sp**, only l, r or a vowel can come, and after **skr** or a word-initial **m**, only a vowel can come.
  - After **k**, a vowel or n can come, as in knee, and after **sh**, a vowel or r, as in shrimp.

- **Guessing the next letter is predicting the 16th vector.** A GPT that only plays this game learns English sounds and spelling. Its predicted probability distribution is **flat** after a vowel and **sharply peaked** after skr, because English allows almost nothing else there.

**Section 7: Building an LLM with an Agentic System**

- An LLM that learns English syllables can be built by **describing it in English** to an agentic system, instead of programming it by hand. The request specified **five layers**, **four heads in each layer**, and **input vectors 64 numbers long**.
  - It asked the system to pick **1000 English words at random** and train the LLM on them, taking each letter and trying to **predict the next letter**, then improving the weights to predict better.
  - It also asked the system to show **ten generated words after each step of training**, to reveal what the LLM currently knew about English.

- The agentic system understood the request in its own technical terms. It built a small **character-level transformer**, another name for an LLM that works letter by letter, with **attention heads**.
  - **Attention** means a head consults the rest of the sequence to work out which preceding items matter, just as predicting barks requires attending to dog.
  - It chose to train on **pronunciations** instead of spellings, so the silent e in shave would not mislead it, and it wrote the program in Python on its own.

- The results show how hard the problem is. After training on 1000 words, the generated words were **still mostly gibberish**, with perhaps a few vowels starting to appear in the right places. Training on **10,000 words** and for many more steps was the suggested next change. A choice that seems easy for any speaker, such as which sounds can follow which, is not easy even for an LLM.

- This way of working has a name.
  - **Software 1.0** is writing programs in a programming language such as Python or C.
  - **Software 2.0** is giving a neural network input data and output data, so the program becomes the **weights** that predict the outputs from the inputs.
  - **Software 3.0** is using an agentic system, a set of LLMs talking to each other and to the user, to build and run a program. Doing this with plain English instructions is called **vibe coding**, and it means someone with a good idea no longer needs to hire a programmer to build it.

- The first attempt at this kind of problem was in **Claude Shannon's 1948 paper, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication."** Working by hand, Shannon generated sequences of letters that came closer and closer to English, from random strings of consonants to strings with vowels and real short words.
  - That paper founded **information theory**, one of the most important fields in engineering, and it remains important for LLMs today.
  - **Viterbi**, one of the developers of dynamic programming, published his most famous work in an information theory journal, so the ideas behind backpropagation and LLMs share the same roots.
