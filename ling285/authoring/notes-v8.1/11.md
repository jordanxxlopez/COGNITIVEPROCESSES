**Section 1: One Mass, Two Directions**

- A **mass-spring system** is a mass hooked up to a spring, and its behavior comes from what each part "wants." **The spring wants to be at its equilibrium position**, which is its resting length, so if it is stretched or squished it pulls or pushes to get back there. **A mass that is moving wants to keep on moving**, so once it gets going it does not stop by itself.
  - When the mass is pulled out and let go, the spring pulls it back toward equilibrium. The mass is moving when it gets there, so it **keeps on moving** past equilibrium and squishes the spring.
  - The squished spring now pushes the mass back the other way, the mass overshoots again, and the cycle repeats. That repeating back and forth is **vibration**.
  - On a real table, **friction** slowly brings the motion to a stop. The model leaves friction out, so in the model the system vibrates forever.

- The same system can move in **two directions**, and it is important to understand both kinds of motion, because some systems are easier to think about one way than the other.
  - In **longitudinal** motion, the mass moves **left and right**, back and forth along the line of the spring.
  - In **transverse** motion, the mass sits between **two walls** with a spring on each side, and it moves **up and down**, across the line of the springs.

- **Both kinds of motion work in exactly the same way, and for the same reason.** Pulling the transverse mass up **stretches the springs**, and since springs hate being stretched, they pull the mass back down.
  - As the mass passes the middle, the springs are not stretched at all. The mass is still moving, though, so it keeps going and stretches them on the other side.
  - Both versions vibrate at the **same rate**, because they have the same mass and the same total stiffness in the direction of motion. Changing the direction of motion does not change the rate.

**Section 2: Mass and Stiffness Change the Rate**

- **Frequency** is the number of back-and-forth cycles a system completes each second, and two things set it, the **mass** and the **stiffness**. Each change has the same effect in longitudinal motion and in transverse motion.

- **A heavier mass vibrates more slowly.** A heavy mass is hard to get moving and hard to turn around, so each trip takes longer. **Heavy branches sway back and forth slowly, while little branches sway quickly.**
  - Making the mass **four times heavier cuts the rate in half**.

- **A stiffer spring makes the system vibrate faster.** The stiffer something is, the harder it pulls back toward where it started, so it turns the mass around sooner.
  - Making the spring **four times stiffer doubles the rate**.

- The two rules point in opposite directions. **Raising the mass lowers the frequency, and raising the stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - Each part has its effect only while it is doing its job. **A mass affects frequency by moving, and a spring affects frequency by stretching and compressing.** A mass that never moves could be made a thousand pounds heavier without changing the frequency at all, and a spring that never stretches adds no stiffness.

**Section 3: Two Masses, Two Modes**

- With **two masses**, the system has two walls, a spring from each wall to a mass, and **one spring between the two masses**. All the masses are equal, and all the springs have the same stiffness.

- This system can vibrate in **two different ways**, which are called **modes**. **A mode is just another word for a way of vibrating**, and the modes are told apart by asking whether the masses go **together** or **against each other**.
  - In **Mode 1**, the masses move **together**. In transverse motion, both are pulled up and let go, and they rise and fall side by side. In longitudinal motion, both are pulled to the left, and they move left and right as a pair.
  - In **Mode 2**, the masses move **against each other**. One is pulled down and the other up, so whenever one rises the other falls. In longitudinal motion, one is pulled left and the other right, so they move toward each other and then apart.

- A system can also vibrate in **both modes at the same time**. Pulling both masses up, but one higher than the other, starts this mixed motion.
  - Both masses start to fall, which is Mode 1. Because they start at different heights, the middle spring also pushes them against each other, which is Mode 2.
  - The result goes **together and against each other at once**, so there are moments when both masses head the same way and moments when they head opposite ways. This seems strange, but real movement and real speech production behave this way all the time.

**Section 4: Why the Second Mode Vibrates Faster**

- The two modes have **different frequencies**. **When the masses go together, they move back and forth slowly, and when they go against each other, they move back and forth quickly**, so **Mode 2 has the higher frequency**.

- This is puzzling at first, because both modes use **the same two masses and the same three springs**. The explanation comes from asking **what each spring is actually doing** in each mode.
  - In both modes, the **two outer springs** stretch and compress as the masses move.
  - In **Mode 1**, the masses move together, so the **middle spring never stretches or compresses**. It just rides along like a rigid bar, so **effectively Mode 1 has only two springs**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the masses move against each other, so the middle spring is stretched and squished over and over. **All three springs are working**, so Mode 2 has three units of stiffness instead of two.

- **More stiffness means more pull back toward equilibrium, and more pull means a higher frequency.** Nothing about the springs themselves changed. What changed is **how each mode uses the springs**, and that difference alone makes Mode 2 faster.

- The general rule follows directly. **The more the masses go against each other, the more the springs stretch and compress, so the more effective stiffness a mode has and the higher its frequency.** This is why a system with several masses has several frequencies. It has several modes, and each higher mode has higher stiffness.

**Section 5: Three Masses, Three Modes**

- With **three masses** and four springs, the system has **three modes**, one more than with two masses.
  - In **Mode 1**, all three masses go back and forth **together**, and this mode has the **lowest frequency**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the **middle mass stays still** while the two outer masses move against each other, and this mode has the **middle frequency**. To start it, the outer masses are pulled apart very carefully without touching the middle one.
  - In **Mode 3**, the **middle mass moves opposite to the two outer masses**, and this mode has the **highest frequency**. It is easiest to see in transverse motion, where the middle mass drops while both outer masses rise.

- The frequencies follow the same stiffness reasoning.
  - **Mode 1 is very slow** because the masses move together, so the **two middle springs do nothing**. Their job is to stretch and compress, and when they just ride along they add no stiffness.
  - In **Mode 2**, the springs do stretch and compress, but less than they could.
  - In **Mode 3**, **everything goes against everything else**, so it has the most stretching and compressing, the most stiffness, and the fastest motion.

- Because a mass acts only by moving, **changing one mass affects only the modes in which that mass moves**. Making the **middle mass heavier lowers the frequency of Mode 1 and Mode 3**, since the middle mass moves in both of them. **Mode 2 stays the same**, because in Mode 2 the middle mass does not move at all.

- All three modes can also happen **at the same time**, and however the system is started, it vibrates as some mix of these **pure modes**.
  - **Learning a musical instrument means learning how to make the masses and springs vibrate in certain modes and not others.** How a string is plucked or a violin is bowed decides which modes get excited, and the favored modes are heard as **notes**.
  - A **trained singer** does the same with the vocal folds, controlling their mass and stiffness well enough to carry a tune.

**Section 6: One More Mass, One More Mode**

- The pattern holds for any number of masses. **N masses give N independent modes**, so one mass has one way to vibrate, two masses have two, and six masses have six.
  - Moving to higher modes, more parts move against each other, so the pattern has **more reversals**, which are places where neighboring masses head in opposite directions.
  - More reversals mean more stretching, so **higher modes have higher frequencies**.
  - In chains of separate masses like these, the mode frequencies are generally **not whole-number multiples** of the lowest frequency.

- The same mode looks alike no matter how many masses the chain has.
  - **Every Mode 1 is alike**, because all the masses go up and down together.
  - **Every Mode 2 is alike**, because the **left side goes against the right side**. With two masses, that simply means one mass against the other, and with an odd number of masses, the middle one stays still.
  - **Every Mode 3 is alike**, because the **middle goes against the sides**.
  - With **four masses**, the four modes are all four together, the left pair against the right pair, the middle pair against the outer pair, and every mass against its neighbors.

- Packing in more and more masses and springs, all the way to an **infinite number**, gives a **string**, like a guitar or piano string. **Every little piece of a string has mass, and every little piece stretches like a spring**, so a string has an infinite number of modes.
  - In the **lowest mode**, the whole string goes up and down together with hardly any stretching between its pieces, so it gives the **lowest note**.
  - In each higher mode, more and more parts of the string move against each other.
  - A string plucked without any skill vibrates in many modes at once.

**Section 7: A String's Modes and Its Spectrum**

- A **string fixed at both ends** with its lowest mode at **100 hertz** has its next modes at **200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz**, and **hertz** means times per second. Each higher mode completes more cycles in the same amount of time.
  - These frequencies are **whole-number multiples** of the lowest one, and modes that line up this way are called **harmonics**. Here they are **consecutive harmonics**, which include both the odd and the even multiples.

- The even spacing comes from how each mode is built out of the first one. **Mode 1 is a single bump**, and **Mode 2 is two copies of that bump**, with one half going up while the other half goes down.
  - With two copies, Mode 2 has **twice the stiffness** of Mode 1, so it has twice the frequency. Mode 3 fits three copies and has three times the stiffness, and the fifth mode has **the first mode fitted into it five times**.
  - So each mode adds one more unit of stiffness, and the frequencies climb in equal steps. With a lowest mode of 200 hertz, the fifth mode is five times 200.

- A real string vibrates in **all of its modes at the same time**. Adding the separate motions together at every point gives one complicated, changing shape, and that combined motion is what an actual plucked string does.

- The **spectrum** of a sound tells **which frequencies are in it and how much of each there is**. **Frequency** runs along the horizontal axis, and **amplitude**, or energy, runs up the vertical axis.
  - For the string moving in all five modes, the spectrum has **five evenly spaced spikes**, one at each mode's frequency. In this example the spikes have **equal height**, since nothing has filtered the sound to strengthen some frequencies and weaken others.
  - If the string vibrated **only in Mode 3**, the other modes would have no motion, so the spectrum would have **one spike at the frequency of Mode 3** and nothing else. That pattern is like a **one-hot encoding**, with one active slot and all the others at zero.

**Section 8: The Vocal Folds as a Membrane**

- **The vocal folds are even more complicated than strings.** They are a sheet of muscle, which is called a **membrane**, so their masses and springs spread across a surface instead of along a line. The same logic of modes still applies.
  - In **Mode 1**, the whole surface goes up and down together.
  - In **Mode 2**, the **left half goes against the right half**.
  - In **Mode 3**, the surface splits the other way, and the two halves on either side of a still middle line go against each other.
  - In **Mode 4**, four sections go against each other, and still higher modes get more and more complicated. Each mode is labeled by a pair of numbers that counts how many bumps fit along the length and how many fit across the width.

- In real vocal folds, **all the modes happen at the same time**, so voicing makes the folds vibrate in many modes at once and produces a spectrum full of spikes.
  - The evenly spaced frequencies attached to these membrane modes are teaching values.
  - The vocal folds are held fixed along their edges, so they **behave like a string**, which has evenly spaced harmonics.

**Section 9: Theory, Prediction and Experiment**

- **The scientific method** depends on **very clear statements of how the world works**, usually written as **differential equations**, which describe how quantities change over time. **Solving those equations gives a prediction** of what the world will be like when it is actually measured.
  - The spring equations are an example. Solving them predicts that a spring that is pulled goes back, and a spring that is pushed also goes back.

- For the voice, the reasoning moves from a theory to a prediction.
  - The **theory** is that the **vocal folds are a membrane**, so when they vibrate, the vibration has **equidistant peaks in its spectrum**. These evenly spaced peaks are called **harmonics**.
  - The equations for the vocal folds are the same type as the spring equations, only many more of them and slightly more complicated, so the intuition from masses and springs is enough to reason with.
  - The **prediction** is that **anyone's laryngeal vibration signal, when measured, will have a spectrum with equidistant harmonics.**

- The prediction was tested by recording two voices in **Praat**, a program for analyzing speech. Each speaker made a sound with the **mouth wide open**, so that no particular vowel was being made.
  - In the first voice, the lowest real spike was near **100 hertz**, and the next spikes fell close to two, three, four and five times that value, **evenly spaced just as predicted**.
  - In the second voice, the lowest spike was near **220 hertz**, and the next ones fell near twice and three times that value, again evenly spaced but farther apart.
  - **The prediction held for both voices**, so a theory built from simple masses and springs made a precise and correct prediction about real speech.

- The difference between the two voices follows from the mass rule. The second voice has a **higher pitch**, so those vocal folds are probably **smaller and thinner**. **Thinner folds have less mass, and less mass means a higher frequency**, so the lowest harmonic is higher and every harmonic after it is spaced farther apart.

- This is part of how people are **recognized by their voices**. Each person's vocal folds give their voice its own spectrum, and becoming familiar with someone includes becoming familiar with that spectrum.
  - Twins share the same genetics and similar vocal folds, so they can be hard to tell apart on the phone.
  - How a person says particular vowels also matters, but that depends on the vibration of the **air** above the vocal folds, not on the folds themselves.

**Section 10: One Fixed End and One Free End**

- A system can also be **fixed at one end and free at the other**. A **building** is like this, because it is fixed to the ground at the bottom and free to sway at the top in high winds or an earthquake. A string can be set up the same way, **fixed at the bottom** and **free at the top**.
  - The **fixed bottom** cannot move, so every mode has a point of **no motion** there, which is called a **node**.
  - The **free top** moves the most, so every mode has its point of **largest motion** there, which is called an **antinode**.

- This end condition changes which modes are possible. **Only the odd harmonics occur**, so the modes come at **100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz**, which are one, three, five and seven times the lowest frequency.
  - **Mode 1** has no still point inside the string. Its motion grows steadily from the fixed bottom to the free top, like a building swaying as a whole.
  - The odd steps come from fitting copies of Mode 1 together. Two copies would put a still point at both ends, which would make the system **fixed at both ends**, so that pattern is impossible here.
  - **Three copies** do end with free motion at the top, so the next mode holds three copies and has three times the stiffness and three times the frequency of Mode 1. The mode after that holds five copies.
  - When all four modes are excited equally, the spectrum measured at the free end has **four equal spikes**.

- **Civil engineers design buildings to sway only in Mode 1**, with every floor moving together. A building is not made so stiff that it cannot move at all, since a building that cannot give at all fares worse in an earthquake. In higher modes, some floors would move one way while others moved the other way, which would be dangerous, so the design keeps the swaying in the lowest mode.

- For a string of the **same length and the same wave speed**, switching from fixed at both ends to fixed at one end and free at the other **halves the lowest frequency**.
  - When **both ends are fixed**, the string has **every whole-number multiple** of the lowest frequency.
  - When **one end is fixed and the other is free**, it has **only the odd multiples**.
