**Section 1: What a Dynamical System Is**

- **A dynamical system is a system whose state changes over time according to rules.** Dynamical systems appear in two places in this course, in **animal behavior**, such as an octopus defending its home or a person speaking, and in **AI learning**, where a program changes its internal numbers as it is trained.

- **Every dynamical system can be described with three parts, elements, relations and a goal.** The **elements** are the pieces that make up the system. The **relations** are the ways those pieces are connected and coordinated with each other. The **goal** is what the whole coordinated system is working to achieve.
  - Using these three parts lets very different systems be described in the same terms, whether the system is an animal's body, a vocal tract or a machine learning program.


**Section 2: The Octopus as a Dynamical System**

- **An octopus defending its den is a dynamical system in behavior.** Its **elements** are its eight **arms** and the **ground** it sits on and pushes against.

- **The relations are the coordinated connections between each arm, the ground and the fish the octopus is interacting with.** These relations are **planned by a neural system** that lives in the octopus's head and also throughout the rest of its body. The octopus does not control its arms from one central point alone, because the planning is spread across the body.

- **The goal is to keep the den.** The octopus coordinates its arms, its grip on the ground and the movement of the fish so that it can protect its home.


**Section 3: The Speech System as a Dynamical System**

- **Human speech is also a dynamical system, with many more elements than the octopus.** The **elements** are the **many parts of the tongue**, the **lips**, the **velum**, the **larynx**, the **palate** and the **pharyngeal wall**.
  - The tongue is not one single element. Its tip, body and root can each move somewhat independently, so it counts as many parts. The **velum** is the soft back part of the roof of the mouth, which raises to close off the nasal passage or lowers to let air flow through the nose. The **larynx** is the voice box in the throat, which holds the vocal folds. The **palate** is the hard roof of the mouth, and the **pharyngeal wall** is the back wall of the throat behind the tongue.

- **The relations between all these parts are planned by the brain.** Producing a single word requires the lips, tongue, velum and larynx to move at precisely coordinated times relative to each other.

- **The goal of the speech system is to convey a thought.** All of this coordinated movement exists so that an idea in one person's mind can be transmitted to another person.
  - The real-time MRI recording shows the system in action. Over about three seconds of speech, the lips, jaw and tongue change shape and position continuously. Speech is a set of many parts moving together over time, not a series of frozen positions.


**Section 4: Dynamical Systems Throughout the Body**

- **All of our daily voluntary actions, such as walking or reaching, are dynamical systems, and so are the involuntary actions of the heart, the pancreas and neurons.** Each involves parts whose activity changes over time in coordinated ways.
  - The elements can be as big as **organs** or parts of organs, and as small as **cells** and the **chemicals** inside cells. **Biology, chemistry and physics** all study dynamical systems at these different scales.
  - The **speech system** is a **very complex dynamical system**, since it combines many body parts, many muscles and brain planning all working toward one goal.

- **There are so many dynamical systems in nature that one dynamical system can be used to study another.** Two systems can be extremely different on the surface, such as an octopus arm and a human tongue, while sharing an **underlying similarity** in how their elements, relations and goals are organized.
  - This is **going up Diotima's ladder**. Studying many different dynamical systems moves understanding from individual cases toward the general principles that all of them share.


**Section 5: The Octopus Arm Slap Study**

- **The octopus example comes from a 2023 paper in Marine Biology by Jean Alupay, Jennifer Mather and Khalil Iskarous, titled "A syntactic analysis of a complex motor action" and subtitled "the octopus arm slap."** The researchers studied an octopus of the genus **Abdopus**, filmed in **tidal pools in Okinawa, Japan**, as it **slapped at fish** in the wild. A slap is a quick arm strike against a fish near the den, and 19 slaps were analyzed.
  - The arm movements were measured in two **reference frames**. An **external reference frame** measures position relative to the surroundings, and an **animal-centered reference frame** measures position relative to the octopus's own body.

- **The octopus built each slap out of two kinematic primitives, rotation and translation of its hydrostatic arms.** Kinematic primitives are basic building-block movements. Rotation means turning the arm around a point, and translation means moving the arm from one place to another without turning it.
  - A **muscular hydrostat** is a body structure with no bones that is supported by muscles pushing against each other around an incompressible core. The octopus arm is one, which is why it can bend at any point along its length.
  - The octopus combined these primitives **serially**, one after another, and **in parallel**, at the same time.

- **By combining a small number of primitives, the octopus keeps its movement flexible while controlling only a few degrees of freedom.** A **degree of freedom** is one independent way a system can move. An octopus arm could bend in almost unlimited ways, so limiting control to a few combined primitives makes the movement manageable. The authors named this **flexible rigidity**.

- **The slap supports Flash and Hochner's embodied organization view of motor behavior, and their idea that motor primitives combine syntactically.** Embodied organization means that much of the organization of movement comes from the structure of the body itself, not only from commands in the brain.
  - **Syntax** in language is the set of rules for combining words into sentences. Calling the slap **syntactic** means small movement units are combined by rules into a larger action, the way words are combined into sentences. This is one dynamical system, the octopus arm, being used to study another, human language.

- **The octopus uses sensory feedback about the moving fish together with feed-forward motor primitives.** **Feedback** is information coming in from the senses during the movement that allows correction. **Feed-forward** control sends out a pre-planned movement without waiting for correction. Combining the two lets the octopus build complex actions that stay in **dynamic equilibrium** with a changing environment.
  - The paper's keywords are **Octopus, Muscular hydrostat, Motor control** and **Abdopus**. The book Octopus, The Ocean's Intelligent Invertebrate, by Jennifer Mather, Roland Anderson and James Wood, is a broader source on octopus behavior.


**Section 6: AI as a Model of Animal Psychological Skills**

- **AI is a model of several animal psychological skills, knowing, recognizing, attending, acting and learning.** It is a system built to imitate abilities that animals, including humans, already have.
  - **Knowing** is holding stored information about the world, such as how words are used. **Recognizing** is identifying what something is when it is encountered. **Attending** is focusing on the parts of the input that matter most. **Acting** is producing an output, such as a movement for an animal or a word for a language model. **Learning** is changing internal knowledge based on experience so that future performance improves.

- **The cloze procedure is an early example of measuring one of these skills in humans.** Wilson Taylor introduced it in "Cloze Procedure," subtitled A New Tool for Measuring Readability, in Journalism Quarterly in Fall 1953.
  - In a **cloze task**, words are removed from a passage and readers fill in the blanks. The more easily readers guess the missing words, the more readable and predictable the text is. The article reported three pilot studies and two experiments comparing cloze results with two readability formulas.
  - Filling in a missing word from its context is the same basic skill that modern language AI is built to perform, predicting a word from the words around it.


**Section 7: How GPT Predicts the Next Word**

- **In GPT, each word is represented as a vector, a list of thousands of numbers.** These vectors are called **x's** or **tokens**. In "chickens cackle and ducks," each of the four words is its own tall column of numbers, one column per token.

- **All of the AI's knowledge about speech, language or anything else is stored in a few trillion numbers called weights, or w's.** The weights are not a dictionary or a list of facts. They are numbers whose values together determine how the system responds to input.

- **The AI combines its knowledge, the W's, with the input vectors, the x's, using mostly addition, subtraction, multiplication and division.** The complexity comes from the enormous number of these simple calculations, not from any complicated single operation.

- **These calculations produce a probability distribution function over possible next words.** A **probability distribution** assigns each possible word a probability, and all the probabilities add up to one. The distribution is the system's way of saying "this is my guess for the next word."
  - Given "chickens cackle and ducks," the curve over the line of possible words has small bumps over many words and a tallest peak over **"quack."** Words like "a," "cat" and "Zulu" get only a little probability.

- **The way the W's combine with the x's is itself a dynamical system.** Learning is a simpler dynamical system than this prediction process, so it is the easier one to work through first.


**Section 8: Where the Weights Come From**

- **The weights come from a training process that starts with no knowledge at all, summed up as "Fake it till you make it!"** Training follows a repeated sequence of steps.
  - The system **starts with random W's**, completely random numbers, and any values at all can be used as the starting point.
  - The system is given a sequence of words, such as "chickens cackle and ducks," and is asked to **predict the next word** by combining its W's with the input vectors.
  - With random weights the prediction is poor. The system's distribution might have its tallest peak over "cat," with only low, uneven probability spread across the other words, including "quack."
  - We already know the correct next word is "quack." The **target distribution** puts all of the probability on "quack" as a single sharp spike and zero on every other word. The system is told to **change its W's** so that its output looks more like this target.
  - The system is exposed to **more sequences** and asked to predict the next word again, **billions of times**, and each time its W's are changed to **improve its guessing**.

- **This repeated cycle of guessing, comparing to the correct answer and adjusting the weights is how LLMs learn and know.** A large language model gets all of its knowledge from gradually correcting random weights over billions of examples.


**Section 9: Two Tricks of AI Learning**

- **AI learning depends on two tricks, each captured by a phrase in an image generated by ChatGPT.**
  - The first trick is **"Fake It Till You Make It."** The system begins with **random weights** and makes predictions right away, even though those first predictions are essentially meaningless. It does not wait until it has knowledge before acting. It acts first and gains knowledge along the way.
  - The second trick is **"Learn by Making Mistakes."** Each wrong prediction produces an **error**, the difference between what the system guessed and what was correct. The system uses that error to adjust its weights, so every mistake becomes information that improves the next prediction.


**Section 10: Averaging as a Learning Problem**

- **Finding an average seems very easy with a short list of numbers.** The average of 10, 12 and 14 is found by adding them and dividing by how many there are, so 36 divided by 3 gives 12.

- **This simple formula does not work for more complex things like speech, language, biological molecules and scenes, which are exactly what AI works with.** For those there is no short list of numbers to add up and divide, so a method that improves an estimate step by step is needed.

- **The averaging method used here learns from the data one piece at a time.** Start with a **list of numbers**. Make a **wild guess**, called x, about their average. Improve x through **exposure to the data**, by taking one number from the list and checking how far it is from the current value of x. Then move x **a bit closer** to the number observed. Repeat with another number, and then another.
  - This is **observation-based error learning**, since each observation reveals an error in the current guess and the guess is corrected in the direction of that observation.
  - This is the same logic as training an LLM, applied to a much simpler problem. The random starting guess for x plays the role of the random starting weights, and moving x toward each observed number plays the role of changing the weights toward the correct next word.


**Section 11: Starting With a Wild Guess**

- **SGD, stochastic gradient descent, estimates an average by learning from the data one observation at a time.** It asks what the average, the **essence**, of the list [78, 67, 98, 85, 76, 68, 98, 72] is. Adding the eight numbers gives 642, and dividing by eight gives the true average of 80.25, the target the learning process should approach.

- **SGD does not start by calculating that average. It starts by picking any random number x as the estimate.** At **time 0** the estimate is set to 130, which is far above every number in the list, so x[0] is 130.
  - The number in brackets is the **time step**. The value x[0] is the estimate at time 0, x[1] is the estimate after one update, and so on.

- **The learning rate, written γ, is set to 25 percent.** The learning rate controls how large each correction is, meaning what fraction of the error the estimate moves on each step.
  - Plotted against the update number, the estimate at time 0 is a single point at 130, while the true average of 80.25 sits far below it as a flat horizontal line.


**Section 12: One Observation Creates Error**

- **The first step of learning is to randomly pick one number d from the data.** The first observation drawn is 98, so d[0] is 98.

- **The discrepancy, also called the loss, is the difference between the current estimate and the observation.** With the estimate at 130 and the observation at 98, the loss is 32. The loss measures how wrong the estimate is compared with this piece of data.
  - The discrepancy is positive, which means the estimate is 32 units too high compared with this observation. The average of a data set should be **close to the data**, so an estimate far from an observed value is a sign that the estimate needs to change.
  - Plotted together at update 0, the estimate is at 130 and the observation is at 98, below it, with the true average of 80.25 lower still.


**Section 13: Moving 25 Percent of the Error**

- **The correction step subtracts 25 percent of the discrepancy from the current estimate.** With a discrepancy of 32 and a learning rate of 0.25, the correction is 8. Subtracting it from 130 gives the new estimate, so x[1] is 122.

- **The estimate moves toward this observation, but not all the way to it.** It moves one quarter of the distance, from 130 to 122. Moving only part of the way keeps any single observation from taking over the estimate.
  - Plotted over updates, the estimate line drops from 130 at update 0 to 122 at update 1, heading down toward the average line at 80.25.


**Section 14: Repeating the Correction, Update 2**

- **The same procedure is repeated with a new random observation each time.** Draw a number, compute the discrepancy, and subtract 25 percent of that discrepancy from the estimate.

- **On the second update, the random observation is 85.** The discrepancy between the current estimate of 122 and this observation is 37. The correction is 25 percent of 37, which is 9.25, and subtracting it gives 112.75 as x[2]. The estimate moves toward this observation, dropping from 122 to 112.75.


**Section 15: Repeating the Correction, Update 3**

- **On the third update, the random observation is 76.** The discrepancy is again positive, since the estimate of 112.75 is still above the observation. The discrepancy here is 36.75. The correction is 25 percent of 36.75, which is 9.1875, and subtracting it gives about 103.563 as x[3]. The estimate moves toward this observation, dropping from 112.75 to about 103.563.


**Section 16: Repeating the Correction, Update 4**

- **On the fourth update, the random observation is 68.** The discrepancy between 103.563 and 68 is 35.563. The correction is 25 percent of that, about 8.891, and subtracting it gives 94.672 as x[4].

- **Across the first four updates, the estimate falls steadily, 130, 122, 112.75, 103.563 and 94.672.** Every observation so far has been below the estimate, so every correction has pushed the estimate down toward the average line at 80.25.


**Section 17: A High Datum Pulls x Upward**

- **On the fifth update, the random observation is 98, which is higher than the current estimate of 94.672.** A **datum** is a single data point. The discrepancy is now **negative**, since the estimate is below the observation. It is 94.672 minus 98, which is negative 3.328.

- **The rule still says to subtract 25 percent of the discrepancy, and the correction is a negative number, about negative 0.832.** Subtracting a negative number is the same as adding, so a negative discrepancy makes the subtraction **move x upward**. The estimate rises slightly from 94.672 to 95.504, toward the high observation.
  - The same single rule handles both cases. When the estimate is above an observation, the discrepancy is positive and the estimate goes down. When the estimate is below an observation, the discrepancy is negative and the estimate goes up. In both cases the estimate moves toward the observation.
  - Plotted over updates, the estimate line bends slightly upward at update 5 after four straight drops, the first sign that the path does not move smoothly in one direction.


**Section 18: Repeating the Correction, Update 6**

- **On the sixth update, the random observation is 72, which is below the estimate again.** The discrepancy between 95.504 and 72 is 23.504. The correction is 25 percent of that, which is 5.876, and subtracting it gives 89.628 as x[6]. The estimate moves toward this observation, dropping from 95.504 to 89.628.


**Section 19: Repeating the Correction, Update 7**

- **On the seventh update, the random observation is 78.** The discrepancy between 89.628 and 78 is 11.628, smaller than before because the estimate is now much closer to the data. The correction is 25 percent of 11.628, which is 2.907, and subtracting it gives 86.721 as x[7]. The estimate drops from 89.628 to 86.721.


**Section 20: Repeating the Correction, Update 8**

- **On the eighth update, the random observation is 67, the lowest number in the list.** The discrepancy between 86.721 and 67 is 19.721. The correction is 25 percent of that, about 4.930, and subtracting it gives 81.791 as x[8].

- **After eight updates, the estimate is only about 1.5 above the true average of 80.25.** The estimate moved toward this observation, dropping from 86.721 to 81.791.


**Section 21: A High Datum Pulls x Upward Again**

- **On the ninth update, the random observation is 98 once more.** The value 98 appears twice in the list, and since each draw is random, the same value can be picked more than once. This is the third time 98 has been drawn.

- **The estimate of 81.791 is well below 98, so the discrepancy is negative and fairly large, negative 16.209.** Subtracting 25 percent of a negative discrepancy, about negative 4.052, moves the estimate upward, from 81.791 to 85.843.
  - A single high observation pushed the estimate away from the true average, even though the estimate had been getting very close. Each step responds only to the one observation drawn, not to the whole data set.


**Section 22: Repeating the Correction, Update 10**

- **On the tenth update, the random observation is 85, which is extremely close to the current estimate.** The discrepancy between 85.843 and 85 is 0.843, less than 1, so the correction is tiny, about 0.211. The estimate barely moves, from 85.843 to 85.632.
  - When an observation agrees closely with the estimate, the estimate hardly changes. The path is almost level between updates 9 and 10.


**Section 23: Repeating the Correction, Update 11**

- **On the eleventh update, the random observation is 76.** The discrepancy between 85.632 and 76 is 9.632. The correction is 25 percent of that, which is 2.408, and subtracting it gives 83.224 as x[11]. The estimate moves toward this observation, dropping from 85.632 to 83.224.


**Section 24: The Final Correction and the Whole Path**

- **On the twelfth update, the random observation is 68.** The discrepancy between 83.224 and 68 is 15.224. The correction is 25 percent of that, which is 3.806, and subtracting it gives 79.418 as x[12]. This is less than 1 below the true average of 80.25, even though the process started at 130.

- **Over all twelve updates, the estimate went 130, 122, 112.75, 103.563, 94.672, 95.504, 89.628, 86.721, 81.791, 85.843, 85.632, 83.224 and 79.418.** The path falls steeply at first, bumps upward twice when 98 is drawn, and ends up hovering near the average line.


**Section 25: What SGD Is**

- **SGD consists of two steps.** First, **make an initial estimate or guess**, which can be completely random. Second, **use data to improve the estimate**, one observation at a time, by moving the estimate part of the way toward each observation.

- **Each word in the name describes part of the method.**
  - **Stochastic** means a **random observation** is used. At each step the data point used for the correction is picked at random, which is why the estimate is sometimes pushed down and sometimes pulled up.
  - **Gradient** means a **loss-related direction**. The discrepancy tells both how wrong the estimate is and which direction it needs to move. A positive discrepancy means move down, and a negative discrepancy means move up.
  - **Descent** means **reducing the mistake**. Every correction is chosen so that the error for the observed data point gets smaller.

- **The path jitters, but its tendency is toward the average.** Individual steps can move away from the true average, as happened when 98 was drawn, but over many updates the random pushes up and down balance out and the estimate settles near 80.
  - This is observation-based error learning in its simplest form. The same method, applied to trillions of weights instead of a single number x, is how LLMs change their W's to predict the next word.


**Section 26: As the Estimate Decreases, the Loss Decreases**

- **A second data set makes the relationship between the estimate and the loss easier to track.** The data are [62, 70, 78, 82, 90, 98], and their average is 80, since they add up to 480 and 480 divided by 6 is 80.

- **The mean motion of SGD describes how the estimate moves on average.** Each random observation is sometimes above and sometimes below the average, so on average the estimate is pulled toward the mean of the data, 80. Replacing the random observation with the average gives the rule for the mean motion. The new estimate x[t plus 1] equals the old estimate x[t] minus 25 percent of the distance between the old estimate and 80.
  - Here t stands for any time step, so this one rule describes every update.

- **The loss at each time step is one half of the discrepancy squared.** Squaring the discrepancy makes the loss positive whether the estimate is too high or too low, so both kinds of mistake count as error. The loss is zero only when the estimate equals 80 exactly.
  - The rate at which this loss changes as x changes equals the discrepancy, x[t] minus 80. This is why the discrepancy is the **gradient**, the loss-related direction, and why subtracting a fraction of it moves the estimate in the direction that lowers the loss.

- **The starting point is set far above the average, at x[0] equal to 140.** At time 0 the discrepancy is 60, so the loss is one half of 3600, which is 1800. On the first update the estimate moves 25 percent of the way toward 80, from 140 to 125, and the loss falls to one half of 2025, which is 1012.5. On the second update the estimate falls to 113.75 and the loss to about 569.5.
  - Each update keeps 75 percent of the previous discrepancy, so the discrepancy shrinks from 60 to 45 to 33.75 and keeps shrinking. The estimate curve falls quickly at first and then flattens as it approaches the target line at 80, reaching about 82 by update 12. The loss curve falls from 1800 at update 0 to nearly 0 by update 12.
  - Unlike the stochastic path, the mean motion is **smooth** with no jitter, since it uses the average of the data instead of a random observation at each step. In this case the **estimate goes down and the loss goes down** together.


**Section 27: The Estimate Increases While the Loss Decreases**

- **The same data set and the same learning rate of 25 percent can be used with only the initial condition changed.** The **initial condition** is the starting value of the estimate. This time the estimate starts far below the average, at x[0] equal to negative 100.
  - At time 0 the discrepancy is negative 180, but squaring makes the loss positive and very large, one half of 32400, which is 16200.

- **On the first update, subtracting 25 percent of a negative discrepancy moves the estimate upward.** The estimate goes from negative 100 to negative 55, and the loss falls to 9112.5. On the second update the estimate rises again to negative 21.25, and the loss falls to about 5125.8.

- **The estimate rises toward 80, climbing from negative 100 to about 74 by update 12.** The discrepancy shrinks in size at every step, and so one half of the discrepancy squared falls toward 0, from 16200 at update 0 to nearly 0 by update 12.

- **The direction changes, but the learning does not.** Starting above the average, the estimate moves down. Starting below the average, the estimate moves up. In both cases the loss decreases, so learning is defined by the loss going down, not by the estimate moving in any particular direction.


**Section 28: Learning Rate and Initial Conditions**

- **SGD can be run for 300 epochs, meaning 300 update steps, with three initial conditions and four learning rates.** The three initial conditions are IC of 130, well above the average, IC of 80, right at the average, and IC of 10, well below it. Every run settles around 80, the average of the data.
  - With a **learning rate of 10 percent**, the run starting at 130 falls and the run starting at 10 rises, and both reach the neighborhood of 80 within about the first 25 to 50 epochs. After that all three runs wiggle gently within a few units of 80, roughly between 75 and 85.
  - With a **learning rate of 50 percent**, the runs starting at 130 and 10 reach the neighborhood of 80 within the first few epochs. After that all three runs jump up and down widely, roughly between 70 and 95, and never settle.
  - With a **learning rate of 1 percent**, the runs move very slowly and very smoothly. After 300 epochs the run starting at 130 has only come down to about 82, and the run starting at 10 has only climbed to about 77, so neither has fully reached the average.
  - With a **learning rate of 80 percent**, the runs reach the average region almost immediately. After that all three runs jump violently, roughly between 68 and 98, nearly the full range of the data. Each update moves the estimate most of the way to whatever observation was just drawn, so the estimate mostly tracks individual data points instead of their average.

- **The initial condition affects only the first part of each run.** After enough epochs all three runs behave the same way, and the learning rate determines how fast they arrive and how much they jitter once they get there.


**Section 29: The Fundamental Learning Tradeoff**

- **The learning rate creates a fundamental learning tradeoff between speed and accuracy.**
  - When γ **increases**, learning **speed increases**, since each correction is larger and the estimate reaches the average region in fewer steps. At the same time **accuracy decreases**, since the large corrections make the estimate jump around the average instead of settling on it.
  - When γ **decreases**, learning **speed decreases**, since each correction is small and the estimate needs many steps to reach the average. At the same time **accuracy increases**, since the small corrections keep the estimate steady and close to the true average once it arrives.

- **With a single learning rate, the fastest learning and the most accurate result cannot be had at the same time.** The 1 percent run is accurate but slow, and the 50 and 80 percent runs are fast but noisy.

- **Thousands of people, for generations, have made their living finding better ways to optimize**, meaning to adjust a learning process so that it works as well as possible. The goal is to find methods where **speed increases and accuracy increases** together, so that systems like LLMs learn quickly while ending up with accurate weights.
