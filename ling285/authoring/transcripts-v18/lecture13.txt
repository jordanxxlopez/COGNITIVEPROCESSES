4|A **fixed–fixed string** is held at both ends, so neither end can move. Both ends must be **nodes**. This rule means the string can only make shapes that fit whole **half-waves** between its ends. || The simplest shape fits **one half-wave**. >> The whole string bends up and down as one piece. >> This is the lowest mode, and its frequency is the **fundamental**. || The next shape fits **two half-waves**. >> A still point appears in the middle. >> The two halves move in opposite directions. || Higher modes fit three, four and five half-waves. >> More half-waves in the same length means each wave is shorter. >> Shorter waves repeat faster, so each higher mode has a higher frequency. || Every mode is built from a whole number of the same basic half-wave. >> So every frequency is a **whole-number multiple** of the fundamental. >> Both odd and even multiples are allowed, because any whole number of half-waves keeps both ends still. || The half-wave pieces mark places along the string. >> They stay in place while the string moves up and down. >> Higher harmonics simply hold more half-wave pieces in the same length.

8|A **fixed–free string** is held at the bottom and free at the top. Its shapes are built from **quarter-waves**. A quarter-wave runs from a **node**, where nothing moves, to an **antinode**, where movement is greatest. >> The fixed bottom must be a node. >> The free top must be an antinode. || **One quarter-wave** fits both rules. >> It starts still at the bottom and moves the most at the top. >> This is the **fundamental**. || The next shapes add quarter-waves **two at a time**. >> Adding just one would end on a still point at the top, which a free end cannot have. >> Adding two keeps both ends correct. >> So the allowed shapes hold **one, three, five and seven** quarter-waves. || More pieces in the same length means shorter waves and faster vibration. >> These modes are the **first, third, fifth and seventh harmonics**. >> So the second mode is the third harmonic, not the second. || Even numbers of quarter-waves never work. >> They would always end on a still point at the free top. >> That is why a fixed–free string has only **odd** harmonics.
9|This **pop quiz** asks how many quarter-waves each **fixed–free pattern** has. The ends of the string give the answer. >> The fixed bottom is always a **node**. >> The free top is always an **antinode**. >> Each step from a node to an antinode, or back, is **one quarter-wave**. || Counting these steps gives **one, three, five and seven** quarter-waves. >> Each higher pattern adds one more node and one more antinode. >> More pieces make the waves shorter, so the higher patterns vibrate faster. || Each pattern has two different numbers. >> The **mode number** counts the patterns in order, first, second, third and fourth. >> The **harmonic number** compares a pattern’s frequency with the fundamental. >> These two numbers are different here, because only odd counts fit both ends. >> So the fourth mode is the **seventh harmonic**.

13|A **constriction** narrows the air passage. Narrowing changes two things about the air at that spot. || First, the air becomes **stiffer**. >> Squeezed air pushes back harder. >> Where a mode squeezes the air a lot, this extra stiffness makes the mode faster. The frequency **goes up**. || Second, the air gains **mass**. >> Bunched-up air is denser. >> Where a mode moves the air a lot, this extra mass makes the mode slower. The frequency **goes down**. || A constriction adds both mass and stiffness, but they do not simply cancel. >> The places where air **moves the most** take turns with the places where air is **squeezed the most**. >> Each mode puts these places in different spots. >> So one spot can be a moving place for one mode and a squeezing place for another. || To see which effect wins, the air must be followed through a full cycle. >> How far one bit of air travels reveals the moving mass. >> How the gaps around it open and close reveals the squeezing. || So one constriction can work mainly through **mass** for one formant and mainly through **stiffness** for another. >> One formant goes down, and the other goes up. >> The rules stay the same. Mass lowers frequency where air moves, and stiffness raises it where air is squeezed.

20|**Source–filter theory** explains a vowel as two parts working together. The **source** is the vibration of the vocal folds. The **filter** is the vocal tract, which shapes that vibration. || The source makes a **harmonic comb**. >> The vocal folds vibrate at a **fundamental frequency**, called **F0**. >> They also produce **harmonics** at every whole-number multiple of F0. >> In this example, the harmonics are **100 Hz** apart. >> Before the vocal tract acts, these harmonics are evenly spaced and all the same strength. || The filter **changes the strength** of each harmonic. >> The vocal tract has its own resonances, called **formants**. >> Harmonics close to a formant become stronger. Harmonics far from a formant become weaker. >> The harmonics stay at the same frequencies. Only their strengths change. || The overall pattern of strengths is called the **spectral envelope**. >> The envelope is what makes a vowel sound like that vowel. || Two terms that are easy to mix up are kept apart here. >> **F0** is how fast the vocal folds repeat. >> **F1** is the lowest resonance of the vocal tract. >> A vowel is made of many harmonics. The formants decide which of them are strong.
21|For **/i/**, the vocal tract keeps **F1 low** and makes **F2 high**. It works on the same 100 Hz comb from the source. >> There is a group of strong harmonics near the low end. >> The next strong group is much higher up. >> Between them, the harmonics are weak. || The harmonics themselves do not move. >> They keep the same even spacing. >> Only their strengths change, and that change is what the filter does. || This pattern comes from where /i/ narrows the tract. >> At the narrowing, the first mode moves the air a lot. The extra mass lowers F1. >> At the same spot, the second mode squeezes the air a lot. The extra stiffness raises F2. >> So F1 and F2 end up far apart, which is what makes /i/ sound like /i/. || Each harmonic has two separate properties. >> Its **frequency** is where it sits. >> Its **amplitude** is how strong it is. >> A strong harmonic at a low frequency is still a low-frequency harmonic.
22|For **/a/**, the vocal tract does the reverse of /i/. **F1 goes up**, and **F2 comes down**. This brings the first two formants close together. >> In this example, **F1** is at **750 Hz**, and **F2** is near **1200 Hz**. >> The source is the same as before. Only the shape of the tract has changed, and that alone makes a different vowel. || A formant does not have to line up with a harmonic. >> The F1 of 750 Hz falls between the harmonics at **700** and **800 Hz**. >> Those two nearby harmonics become strong because they are close to the formant. >> But no new harmonic appears at 750 Hz. The filter can only change the strength of harmonics the source already has. || A formant and a harmonic are different things. >> A **formant** is a resonance of the vocal tract. It belongs to the filter. >> A **harmonic** is a frequency made by the vocal folds. It belongs to the source. >> The filter decides how strong each harmonic is. The harmonics, in turn, reveal where the formants are.
23|A **spectrum** shows the sound at **one moment**. A **spectrogram** lines up **many spectra over time**, so it tracks how the sound changes. || A spectrum gives the strength of each frequency during one short slice of sound. >> Taking several slices of **/i/** and then several of **/a/** gives a series of spectra. || A spectrogram puts those slices side by side. >> Time runs from left to right. >> Frequency runs from bottom to top. >> **Darker** marks mean **stronger** sound, and lighter marks mean weaker sound. || This makes two kinds of change easy to tell apart. >> When a band gets darker, that frequency is getting stronger. >> When a band moves up, the formant is moving to a higher frequency. >> Moving to the right always means later in time, not higher in frequency. || As **/i/** changes into **/a/**, **F1 rises** and **F2 falls**. >> The two lowest bands move toward each other. >> The spectrogram follows the changing shape of the vocal tract over time, which a single spectrum cannot do.

25|Formants can only be seen through the harmonics. The filter can only change the strength of frequencies that the source provides. So how clearly a formant shows up depends on how close together the harmonics are. || When harmonics are **close together**, formants are easy to see. >> With harmonics every **100 Hz**, many harmonics fall near each formant. >> Their strengths rise toward the center of the formant and fall on either side. >> This traces the shape of the formant clearly. || When **F0** goes up, the harmonics spread apart. >> With harmonics every **250 Hz**, far fewer harmonics fall near each formant. >> A formant can fall between two harmonics, so no harmonic sits at its center. >> It becomes very hard to tell harmonics and formants apart. || Some voices make this even harder. >> A **toddler** has a high voice, so the harmonics could be **400 Hz** apart. >> A **soprano** sings even higher, with harmonics that could be **800 Hz** apart. >> With so few harmonics, the formants are very hard to read. || The vocal tract has not changed in these cases. >> Its formants are in the same places. >> Only the source has changed. A higher voice gives fewer harmonics, and fewer harmonics give a less clear picture of the same formants.
