Here it stands for neural networks from **1943 to 1986**. >> Two formants go in, and the network picks one of five words. || The period starts with **McCulloch and Pitts** in **1943**. >> Their paper treats each neuron as **all-or-none**, firing or not firing. >> That let them describe what neurons do with simple logic. || It ends with the **Parallel Distributed Processing** books of **1986**. >> These books made networks of many simple connected units, trained by changing their weights, widely used. || These networks could do one kind of job well. >> They take one **fixed set of numbers**, like two formants, and pick from **a few categories**. || The problem is that **human thinking is far more complex**. >> Speech and language happen over time. >> What comes next depends on what came before, and a network that sees one fixed input cannot keep track of the past.
3|In **1985**, **Michael Jordan** built **recurrent neural networks** to study how people produce speech. >> This line of work led to large language models, and then to agentic AI. || The new idea is a loop. >> The network’s outputs are fed back into it through **state units**. >> So the network always has a memory of what it just said. || Because of this loop, each output depends on the outputs before it. >> That is what **recurrent** means. >> An ordinary network forgets each input as soon as it is done with it. || The plot shows the network saying a word, one moment at a time. >> Each line follows one feature of the sound, such as voicing, lip rounding or the hiss of an s. >> The hissing feature rises wherever an s comes. || Lip **rounding** starts early and stays on for most of the word. >> So features do not switch on and off one sound at a time. They blend into each other, as they do in real speech.
4|This picture builds from the smallest piece to the biggest system. >> **One neuron** takes in a list of numbers, multiplies each by a weight, and adds them up into **one number**. >> That adding-up step is an **inner product**. || A **network** is layers of neurons. >> Each layer turns one list of numbers into another. >> The last layer gives a **decision**, one score for each possible answer. || An **LLM** is a much bigger network that reads a **sequence**. >> Fifteen vectors go in, one for each piece of text, and pass up through many layers. >> Out comes vector **16**, the model’s probabilities for what comes next. || An **agentic system** connects several LLMs together. >> One makes an outline, one writes, one critiques, one revises, and one finishes. >> The critic and the reviser can go back and forth in a **loop**. || All of these rest on the same two operations. >> Using the network means computing **inner products**. >> Training it means **SGD plus dynamic programming**, which is backpropagation.
5|An LLM reads **15 vectors** and has to guess the **16th**. >> The question is what those vectors stand for. || They can be **words in a sentence**. >> “The cat that the dog chased ran up the old tree and sat on the ___.” >> The 16th vector is the **missing word**. || They can be **slices of sound** or **pieces of a picture**. >> For a spectrogram, the 16th vector is the **next slice of sound**. >> For a picture cut into squares, it is the **missing corner square**. || They can even be the parts of a **protein**. >> Here they are the first **15 amino acids** of insulin. >> The 16th vector is the **next amino acid**. || **The LLM only ever sees vectors.** >> Words, sounds, pictures and molecules all look the same to it. >> Each time, it answers one question, **what comes next?**
6|This page asks which strings of sounds could be **English words**. >> The simplest English syllable is a **consonant, a vowel, and a consonant**, as in Don, mat or fish. || Consonants can also come in **clusters**. >> English allows split, flat and stop at the start of a word. >> It allows desk, help and lamp at the end. || But only certain clusters are allowed, and **order matters**. >> *lfat, *hepl and *lapm use the same letters as flat, help and lamp in the wrong order. >> The allowed order flips around the vowel. || Long words are **chains of syllables**. >> In At·lan·tic, t and l sit on two sides of a syllable break, so they are fine. >> A word cannot start with tl, so *tlantic is impossible. >> ng can end a syllable, as in ring, but cannot start one. || This knowledge of allowed sound patterns is called **phonotactics**. >> English speakers know these rules without ever being taught them.
7|This pop quiz gives the start of an English word and asks you to **guess the next letter**. >> The starts include ra_, sp_, skr_ and m_. || After a vowel, almost **any letter** can come next. >> After ra_, the guesses are spread across many letters. || At the start of a word, the choices are **narrow**. >> After sp_, b_ or f_, only l, r or a vowel can come next. >> After skr_ or m_, only a vowel can come next. || Some letters can **never** come next. >> English has no words starting with *spn, *skrl or *mb. || **Guessing the next letter is the same game an LLM plays.** >> A GPT trained only on this game learns English sounds and spelling. >> Its guesses are spread out after a vowel and focused on a few letters after skr_.
