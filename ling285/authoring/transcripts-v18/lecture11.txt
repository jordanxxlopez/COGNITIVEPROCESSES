2|A mass on a spring has a resting place called **equilibrium**. When the mass is moved away from that place, the spring is stretched or squeezed, and it pulls or pushes the mass back. That pull toward rest is the **restoring force**. >> The restoring force gets the mass moving toward rest. >> The mass has **inertia**, so it does not stop at rest. It keeps going and bends the spring the other way. || Now the spring pulls in the opposite direction. The mass slows down, turns around and comes back. >> This happens again and again. >> A movement that repeats around a resting place is called an **oscillation**. || The mass can swing in two directions. >> In **longitudinal motion**, it moves along the spring, so the spring gets longer and shorter. >> In **transverse motion**, it moves across the spring. The spring is already pulled tight, so when the mass moves off the line, the spring tilts and pulls it back. || Both directions repeat at the same **frequency**, which is the number of cycles in each second. >> They match because the mass is the same and the spring is equally stiff in both directions. >> The direction of motion changes how the spring pulls, but it does not change how fast the cycle repeats.
3|Two things set how fast a mass on a spring swings. One is the **mass** itself, and the other is the **stiffness** of the spring. Each comparison changes only one of them, so its effect stands out clearly. || **A heavier mass swings more slowly.** >> A heavy mass is harder to speed up and harder to stop. >> The same spring needs more time to turn it around, so each cycle takes longer. >> Making the mass **four times** heavier cuts the frequency **in half**. || **A stiffer spring makes the mass swing faster.** >> A stiff spring pulls back harder for the same stretch. >> The mass turns around sooner, so more cycles fit into each second. >> Making the spring **four times** stiffer **doubles** the frequency. || Mass and stiffness work against each other. >> Mass resists changes in motion. >> Stiffness drives the mass back toward rest. || Speed of repetition is a separate thing from size of swing. >> How far the mass moves is its **amplitude**. A big swing is not necessarily a fast one. >> Frequency comes from counting full cycles over the same amount of time. || The sideways and lengthwise versions match because each one has the same **effective stiffness** in its own direction of motion. For sideways motion, that stiffness comes from how tightly the spring is stretched.
4|With two equal masses held between fixed walls, the system has **two normal modes**. A **normal mode** is a pattern where every part moves at the same rate and keeps the same relationship to the others. || In **Mode 1**, the two masses move together in the same direction. >> The space between them hardly changes. >> The spring in the middle barely stretches, so the pull back is weaker and this mode is slower. || In **Mode 2**, the masses move toward each other and then apart. >> The middle spring now stretches and squeezes hard. >> That extra pull makes this mode faster, though not exactly twice as fast. || Both masses take part in both modes. >> The parts are the same in each mode. >> What changes is how the masses move relative to each other, together in one mode and opposed in the other. || The two modes can also happen at the same time. >> At each mass, the two movements simply add. Where they push the same way, they build up, and where they push opposite ways, they cancel. >> This adding is called **superposition**. The combined motion looks uneven, but it holds both frequencies at once.

6|Three equal masses between fixed walls have **three normal modes**, one for each mass that can move. || In **Mode 1**, all three masses move the same way. >> The middle mass moves the most, and the outer masses move less. >> Neighbors are not working against each other, so the springs stretch only a little. This is the slowest mode. || In **Mode 2**, the two outer masses move in opposite directions, and the middle mass stays still. >> The pulls on the middle mass from each side are equal and opposite, so they cancel. >> A point that stays still during a mode is called a **node**. || In **Mode 3**, the outer masses move together, and the middle mass moves the other way. >> Every mass is now working against its neighbor, so every spring stretches hard. >> This is the fastest mode. || The rule behind the order is simple. >> The more neighbors move against each other, the more the springs stretch. >> More stretching means a stronger pull back, and a stronger pull back means a faster mode. || The same three masses move in every mode. >> When all three modes happen together, their movements add at each mass. >> The middle mass still moves in the mixture, because the first and third modes move it even though the second does not.
7|A chain gains one more **normal mode** for every mass that can move. A chain of one mass has one mode, and a chain of six masses has six. || In every chain, **Mode 1** is the simplest pattern, with all the masses moving together. >> Each higher mode adds one more group that moves the opposite way. >> More groups moving against each other means more springs being stretched. >> That gives a stronger pull back and a faster rate. || The two ends of the chain are fixed, so they never move. >> Inside a chain, a mass can stay still in one mode. That still point is a **node** of that mode, while the rest of the chain keeps moving. >> A higher mode uses the very same masses and springs. Only the pattern of movement changes. || The size of a movement and the rate of a movement are different things. >> **Amplitude** is how far the parts move. >> Frequency is how often the movement repeats. A tall pattern is not necessarily a fast one. || Chains made of separate masses do not have the perfectly even, whole-number spacing of frequencies that an ideal string has.
8|A **fixed–fixed string** is held still at both ends, so both ends are **nodes**. The string can only vibrate in shapes that fit whole half-waves between its ends. >> The first mode fits one half-wave, a single arch. >> Each higher mode fits one more half-wave. || More half-waves in the same length means shorter waves. >> At the same speed, shorter waves repeat faster. >> The modes run at **100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz**. || The lowest frequency is the **fundamental**. >> The others are exact whole-number multiples of it. >> Frequencies spaced this way are called **harmonics**. || One string can carry all of these modes at the same time. >> At each point along the string, the movements add. Some build up, and some cancel. >> The overall shape keeps changing, but each mode inside it stays regular. A point can look still for a moment and then move strongly again, and every mode is still there. || A **pickup** records the motion at one point on the string. >> Its **spectrum** has one peak for each mode, at that mode’s frequency. >> The modes were tuned to give equal peaks at that one point. At a different point, the peaks would have different heights.
9|A **membrane** is a vibrating surface with fixed edges. A surface spreads in two directions, so each mode is named with two numbers. Each number counts the moving regions in one direction. >> In **(1,1)**, the whole surface moves as one region. >> In **(2,1)** and **(1,2)**, the surface splits into two regions, in one direction or the other. >> In **(2,2)**, it splits into four regions, and in **(3,1)**, into three in a row. || Regions that sit next to each other always move in opposite directions. >> The lines between them stay still. These still lines are **nodes**. >> The outer edges stay still too, because they are fixed. || All the modes can happen at once, and their movements add at every point. >> Some places build up and others cancel, so the whole surface keeps changing shape. || A node stays still the whole time. >> A moving region also passes through the flat resting level, but only for an instant on its way past. That passing moment does not make it a node. >> The two numbers describe how the surface divides. They are not two separate frequencies. || A **pickup** at one point gives a spectrum with one peak for each mode. >> The frequencies here were **picked for teaching** and spaced evenly. A real rectangle would have its own natural frequencies, so the even spacing models how frequencies combine rather than how every membrane behaves.
10|Science depends on stating very clear theories about how the world works. In this field, those theories are usually written as **differential equations**. >> A differential equation describes how the parts of a system change over time. >> Solving it gives a **prediction**, a statement of what a real measurement should show. || The theory here is that the **vocal folds are a membrane**. >> A vibrating membrane makes a wave whose spectrum has **evenly spaced peaks**. >> These evenly spaced peaks are called **harmonics**. || The theory leads to a clear prediction. >> If someone’s **laryngeal vibration** is measured, its spectrum should have **equidistant harmonics**, with equal gaps between the peaks. >> The test is to record real vocal-fold vibration and look at how its peaks are spaced. >> Even spacing would support the theory. Uneven spacing would count against it. || The spacing is what makes the prediction a real test. >> The vocal folds vibrate in any case, so seeing vibration alone settles nothing. >> The membrane animation used frequencies that were chosen to be even, so it pictures the idea but cannot test it. A recording of a real voice is what can test it.
11|A **fixed–free system** is held at one end and free at the other. Each end sets a rule for every shape the system can take. >> The fixed end must be a **node**, a point that does not move. >> The free end must be an **antinode**, the point that moves the most. || The shapes that follow both rules fit odd numbers of **quarter-waves**. >> The simplest shape fits one quarter-wave. It is still at the fixed end and swings most at the free end. >> The next shapes fit three, five and seven quarter-waves. >> These are the **first, third, fifth and seventh harmonics**. So the second allowed mode is the third harmonic, not the second. || Even numbers of quarter-waves are not allowed. >> An even number would put a still point at the free end, which breaks the rule for that end. >> Higher modes fit shorter waves into the same length, so they repeat faster. || The antinode does not stay at its farthest point. >> It swings through rest to the other side on each cycle. >> The fixed end stays still the whole time. || The fixed–fixed and fixed–free examples were tuned separately to share the same lowest frequency. >> If only one end were freed, with nothing else changed, the lowest frequency would drop **by half**.
