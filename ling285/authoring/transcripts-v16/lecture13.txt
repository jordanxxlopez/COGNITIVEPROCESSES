4|Why do the frequencies of a **fixed–fixed string** land on neat whole-number multiples? The answer comes from its ends. Both are held, so both must be **nodes**, and the only shapes that fit are whole numbers of **half-waves**. || The simplest shape fits **one half-wave**. >> The string bows up and down as a single region, moving most in the middle. >> This is **Mode 1**, and its frequency is the **fundamental**. || **Mode 2** squeezes in **two half-waves**. >> A still point appears at the center. >> The two halves move in opposite directions, one rising while the other falls. || **Modes 3, 4 and 5** fit three, four and five half-waves into the same length. >> Each extra half-wave makes the pieces shorter, so the **wavelength** shrinks. >> Waves travel along the string at the same speed, so shorter waves have to repeat more often. >> Every mode is built from a whole number of base half-waves, so every frequency is a **whole-number multiple** of the fundamental. Odd counts and even counts both keep the ends still, so both kinds of multiple show up. || As the demonstration builds up the packets one by one, the first half-wave lights up in every mode, the second in Modes 2 through 5, and so on, until the fifth appears in Mode 5 alone. >> The packets stay put while the string moves up and down. >> They do not count passing cycles, and they do not travel along the string. Higher harmonics simply hold more half-wave packets in the same space.

8|Turn now to a **fixed–free string**, held at the bottom and free at the top. Its shapes are built from **quarter-waves**, each one running from a **node**, where nothing moves, to an **antinode**, where movement is biggest. >> The fixed bottom has to be a node. >> The free top has to be an antinode. || A single **quarter-wave** fits both requirements. >> Movement grows from nothing at the bottom to its largest at the top. >> That is the **fundamental**. || To get the next shape, quarter-waves are added two at a time. >> One extra would end the string on a node, which the free top cannot have. >> A pair keeps both ends right and adds one more reversal inside. >> The four allowed modes hold **one, three, five and seven quarter-waves**. || Fitting more pieces into the same length shortens the waves, and shorter waves vibrate faster. >> The modes come out at the **first, third, fifth and seventh harmonics**. >> That makes the second mode the third harmonic, the third mode the fifth and the fourth mode the seventh. || Even counts are never allowed, because they always finish on a node at the top. >> So even harmonics are simply missing from a fixed–free string. || In the demonstration, the quarter-wave packets appear one at a time across all four modes. >> Later packets show up only in the higher modes, and packets six and seven only in the fourth. >> The counts of 1, 3, 5 and 7 packets line up exactly with the odd harmonics.
9|Here is a quick **pop quiz**. For each **fixed–free pattern**, how many quarter-waves does it contain? The ends hold the answer. >> The fixed bottom is always a **node**. >> The free top is always an **antinode**. >> Each trip from a node to an antinode, or back again, covers **one quarter-wave**. || Count the trips and the four patterns come out at **one, three, five and seven** quarter-waves. >> Each step up adds one more node and one more antinode inside the string. >> More pieces in the same length shorten the waves, so the higher patterns repeat faster. >> The divisions mark fixed places in each standing shape while the material moves through them. || Notice that each pattern carries two different numbers. >> The **mode number** just counts the allowed patterns in order. >> The **harmonic number** compares the pattern’s frequency with the fundamental. >> On a fixed–free string, those two numbers come apart, because only odd counts work. >> The fourth allowed mode is the **seventh harmonic**, not the fourth.

13|When the tongue narrows the airway into a **constriction**, two things happen to the air at that spot, and they push frequency in opposite directions. || The air gets **stiffer**. >> Squeezed air resists being squeezed further. >> Where a mode **compresses** the air a lot, that extra stiffness speeds up the return and **raises the frequency**. || The air also gets more **massive**. >> Bunching air together raises its density. >> Where a mode **moves** the air a lot, that extra moving mass is slower to turn around and **lowers the frequency**. || So a constriction adds mass and stiffness at the same time. Do they just cancel? >> They do not, because the parts of a vibrating medium that **move the most** alternate with the parts that **compress the most**. >> Each mode puts those regions in different places, so the same spot can be a moving region for one mode and a squeezing region for another. || The way to see which effect wins is to watch the air through a whole cycle. >> Follow one bit of air and see how far it travels. That travel carries the mass. >> Watch the gaps on either side close and open. That is compression, the source of the push-back. >> A tongue-height label or a single frozen frame cannot give both pieces. || One narrowing can therefore work mostly through **mass** for one formant and mostly through **stiffness** for another. >> One formant drops while the other climbs. >> Neither rule flips. Mass still lowers frequency where air moves, and stiffness still raises it where air compresses.

20|**Source–filter theory** treats every vowel as two things working together, a **source** at the vocal folds and a **filter** in the vocal tract above them. || Start with the source. The vibrating vocal folds produce a **harmonic comb**. >> The vibration repeats at a **fundamental frequency**, **F0**, and it also contains harmonics at every whole-number multiple of F0. >> In the example, the harmonics are **100 Hz** apart, climbing all the way to **4000 Hz**. >> Before the vocal tract does anything, the spectrum is a row of evenly spaced lines, all the same height to keep the picture simple. || Now add the filter. The vocal tract **sculpts the heights** of those lines. >> Harmonics close to the tract’s resonances, its **formants**, get stronger, and harmonics far from any formant get weaker. >> Not one line moves left or right. Only the heights change. >> Trace the tops of the lines and you get the **spectral envelope**, and that envelope is the vowel. || For schwa, four resonance regions, **F1 through F4**, shape the comb, with the strongest harmonics gathered at low frequency. || Keep the two axes straight. >> Frequency runs across, and amplitude runs up, so a taller line is a stronger one, not a higher one. >> **F0** is how fast the source repeats, and **F1** is the tract’s lowest resonance, two quite different things. >> The vowel is still made of many harmonics. The formants just decide which ones stand out, and these values describe this example rather than every speaker.
21|For **/i/**, the filter keeps **F1 low** and pushes **F2 high**, acting on the very same 100 Hz comb. >> A strong cluster of harmonics sits down near the low end. >> The next strong cluster sits far up the axis, with a quiet gap of weak harmonics in between. >> More clusters follow around **F3** and **F4**. || The harmonics themselves have not budged. >> They keep their even spacing, and only their heights have changed. >> That is what lets the filter’s effect be seen apart from the source. || Where does the low-F1, high-F2 pattern come from? >> The front constriction of /i/ sits at a spot where Mode 1 moves the air a lot, so the added mass pulls F1 down. >> At the same spot, Mode 2 squeezes the air a lot, so the added stiffness pushes F2 up. >> The two formants end up far apart, the mark of /i/. || Reading the picture means making two separate judgments for each line. >> How far across it sits tells its **frequency**. >> How tall it stands tells its **amplitude**. >> A tall line near the low end is still a low-frequency line, even next to a shorter line farther up. || The thin harmonic lines sample the broad formant regions, and the envelope they trace is the vowel. These values describe one schematic /i/, not every speaker.
22|For **/a/**, the filter does the reverse of /i/. **F1 goes up** and **F2 comes down**, so the first two formants crowd close together. >> In the example, **F1 is at 750 Hz** and **F2 is near 1200 Hz**. >> **F3** and **F4** stay higher up. >> The source is the same comb as before. Changing nothing but the shape of the tract has turned one vowel into another. || Two different kinds of change can be spotted in the spectrum. >> When a formant moves, the favored region shifts along the frequency axis. >> When a harmonic’s amplitude changes, that line grows taller or shorter. || Here is a subtle point. A formant does not have to sit right on top of a harmonic. >> The 750 Hz target for F1 falls between the harmonics at **700** and **800 Hz**. >> Those two neighbors get stronger because they are close to the resonance. >> No new line appears at 750 Hz, because the filter can only strengthen or weaken what the source already supplies. || That is why the two terms need to stay separate. >> A **formant** is a resonance of the filter, the vocal tract. >> A **harmonic** is a frequency component of the source, the vocal folds. >> The filter decides how strong each harmonic is, and the harmonics, in turn, reveal where the formants lie.
23|A **spectrum** captures **one instant** of sound. A **spectrogram** **stacks many spectra over time**, so it tracks how the sound changes. || Each spectrum covers one short slice of sound. >> Frequency runs across it, and amplitude runs up. >> The example takes three slices from **/i/** and three from **/a/**. || Turning those spectra into a spectrogram takes two moves. >> First, each spectrum is stood on its side, so its frequency axis now points upward. A peak on the right of a spectrum becomes a mark near the top of the strip. >> The strips are then lined up left to right in time order, so moving right means moving later, not higher. || Second, **height turns into darkness**. >> **Black means high amplitude, gray means medium and white means low**. >> Now height on the page gives frequency, and darkness gives strength. >> When a band darkens, it is getting stronger, and when a band climbs, it is moving to a higher frequency. || Watch what happens as **/i/** turns into **/a/**. >> **F1 rises and F2 falls**, so the two lowest dark bands move toward each other. >> The higher bands stay above them and shift less. >> The spectrogram lets the changing resonances of the tract be followed through time, which no single spectrum can do.

25|Here is a practical puzzle. Formants belong to the filter, but they can only be seen through the source harmonics, since the filter can only reshape frequencies the source supplies. How clearly a formant shows up depends on how tightly the harmonics are packed. || When harmonics are **close together**, formants are easy to read. >> Take harmonics at **100, 200, 300, 400 Hz** and onward, with **F1 at 300** and **F2 at 2100 Hz**. >> Lots of lines fall near each formant, and their heights rise toward its center and fall away on each side, tracing its shape clearly. || When **F0** goes up, the harmonics spread out. >> Try harmonics at **250, 500, 750** and onward instead. >> Now only a few lines fall near each formant, and a formant can slip right between two of them. >> One strong line might sit near a formant without marking its center, and it becomes very hard to tell harmonics from formants. || Some voices make this much harder. >> A **toddler’s** harmonics could sit at **400, 800** and onward. >> A **soprano’s** could sit at **800, 1600, 2400** and onward. >> Across the three spectra and spectrograms, the crowded rows of lines thin out into a few lonely traces. || Notice what has not changed. >> The vocal tract can be exactly the same in every case, with the same resonances in the same places. >> Only the source is different, and a high-pitched source offers fewer frequencies to reveal the same filter.
