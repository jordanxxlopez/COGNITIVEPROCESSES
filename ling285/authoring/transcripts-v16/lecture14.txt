4|Think of a **quarter-wavelength** as the shortest piece of a standing wave that goes from stillness to full swing, from a **node** to an **antinode**. A fixed–free string needs exactly that pair, a node at its fixed bottom and an antinode at its free top. >> A single quarter-wave fits between the two ends. >> That gives the simplest pattern, with nothing still inside the string, and it sets the fundamental. || What comes next? >> Adding one more quarter-wave would carry the shape back to a node at the top, which a free end cannot have. >> Adding **two** brings the top back to an antinode, with one new node and antinode tucked inside. >> So the allowed patterns hold **one, three, five and seven quarter-waves**. || Squeezing more pieces into the same length shortens the waves, and shorter waves at the same speed repeat faster. Each allowed pattern is higher than the last. || As the packets appear one at a time, every mode that contains a packet lights it up together. >> The four modes end up holding **1, 3, 5 and 7** packets, exactly the odd harmonics. >> The numbered pieces mark the sections of each standing shape and stay put while the string swings back and forth. || Nobody chose to leave out the even frequencies. >> Only odd counts can start at a node and finish at an antinode. >> The two ends of the string rule the even patterns out on their own.

6|Every spot in a vibrating air column has two properties that are easy to confuse. >> **Movement** is how far a bit of air travels. >> **Compression** is how much the gaps between neighbors change. >> A whole group of air can travel a long way together with steady gaps, while somewhere else the gaps squeeze and stretch hard around a bit of air that hardly moves. || Each property is read differently. >> For movement, pick one circle and follow it through a whole cycle. >> For compression, watch the gaps between neighboring circles, shrinking where air is squeezed and widening where it spreads out. || Together they are what keep the air vibrating. >> Changing compression acts like a spring, because crowded air pushes back toward normal spacing. >> The air’s **inertia** acts like a mass, carrying it past its resting place so the cycle keeps going. || The ends of the tube set where each region falls. >> The **closed end** holds movement back. >> The **open end** lets the air move freely. >> Each higher mode fits more alternating stretches of movement and compression into the same tube, in its own positions. || The moving **Schwa** reference and the **F1, F2 and F3** rows are resonances of one air system, not separate streams. >> That is why a constriction can act mostly on moving mass in one mode and mostly on compression in another. >> What it does depends on the mode as well as on where it sits.
7|Time for a **pop quiz**. Six spots along the air column are labeled **A** through **F**. What is each one doing? >> Follow each spot through a **complete cycle**. >> How far its air travels gives its movement. >> How the gaps around it change gives its compression. >> A single still frame cannot settle the question, since a fast-moving bit of air might be caught just as it passes through rest. || At a spot with strong **movement**, added mass is what matters. >> More **effective mass** is harder to get moving and harder to stop. >> The cycle drags out, and the resonance tends to **fall**. || At a spot with strong **compression change**, added stiffness is what matters. >> More **stiffness** shoves the air back toward its spacing harder. >> When that wins, the cycle quickens, and the resonance tends to **rise**. || **Modes 1, 2 and 3** lay these regions out in different places. >> The higher modes add extra reversals and so add more alternating regions. >> The very same spot can be a swinging spot in one mode and a squeezing spot in another. || So any prediction about a local change needs two answers. >> Where along the tube is the change? >> Which mode is in question? Only together do they say whether a narrowing mostly adds moving mass or mostly adds stiffness for that resonance.

10|**Mode 1** and **Mode 2** are two ways that one column of air can resonate, held at the glottis and open at the lips. >> Both swing the air strongly near the open lips. >> Mode 2 has an extra reversal inside the tube, so along the rest of the column its movement and compression sit in different places. || Three markers sit at the **back**, the **front** and the **lips**. >> Each marker is the same spot in both modes. >> Narrow the tract at one marker and the change is felt by both modes at once. >> The difference lies in what each mode is doing there. || If a mode **swings the air strongly** at that marker, the narrowing’s added mass matters, and the vibration slows. >> If a mode **squeezes the air strongly** there, the added stiffness matters, and the vibration speeds up. >> Since the two modes can be doing opposite things at the same marker, one narrowing can send **F1** and **F2** in opposite directions. || The two modes keep a tidy ratio only in an ideal tube. >> With a perfectly uniform shape, Mode 2 runs at **three times** the frequency of Mode 1. >> Once the tract changes shape, the formants move and the ratio changes. || Formants and harmonics should never be mixed up. >> **Formants** are resonances of the tract, and they move when the tract’s shape changes. >> **Harmonics** are pieces of the source, spaced by how fast the vocal folds repeat, and they move when the pitch changes.

15|The vocal folds make the voiced **source**, and how fast they repeat, their **F0**, decides how far apart the harmonics sit. || The membrane picture comes back first. >> A **fixed–fixed membrane** with five modes, (1,1) at **100 Hz** up to (3,1) at **500 Hz**, adds up into one motion. >> Read at a pickup point, that motion has evenly spaced peaks. >> The frequencies were assigned for the illustration, so the picture models harmonic spacing rather than describing every real membrane. || Then come three source spectra, each spanning **0 to 4000 Hz**, before the vocal tract has done anything. >> At **F0 = 100 Hz**, the lines crowd together, with dozens in the window. >> At **F0 = 250 Hz**, from **tighter, lighter vocal folds**, the lines thin out to **250, 500, 750** and onward. >> At **F0 = 350 Hz**, from **very tight and light vocal folds**, they thin out further to **350, 700, 1050** and onward. || The pattern is simple. **Lower F0 means denser harmonic sampling, and higher F0 means sparser sampling.** >> It follows the familiar mass–spring logic. >> Heavier, looser folds repeat slowly, and lighter, tighter folds repeat fast. || Any voiced vowel carries **many frequencies** at once. >> The source decides which harmonics are there to begin with. >> The tract then decides which of them come out strong. >> So changing pitch changes the spacing, while changing the vowel changes which regions win, two separate jobs inside one sound.
16|The vocal tract is the **filter**, a **fixed–free** tube with one closed end and one open end, and it picks out **formants**. Its rule fits in two lines. >> **A formant encourages the closest harmonics to be audible.** >> **Harmonics far from that formant are muted.** >> The source hands over a full set of harmonics, and the filter decides which ones survive. The shape of the vowel is the filter. || Each vowel puts its formants somewhere different. >> **Schwa** spreads its first two apart, near **500** and **1500 Hz**. >> **/u/** pulls both down, to about **300** and **1000 Hz**, with a third near **2000 Hz**. >> **/i/ or /ɪ/** keeps F1 low, near **300 Hz**, and lifts F2 far up, near **2200 Hz**. >> **/a/** crowds its first two together, at **800** and **900 Hz**. >> These numbers belong to this illustration. || Shift the tongue or the lips and the tract’s shape changes, which moves these favored regions around. >> Each target lifts the harmonics closest to it. >> Harmonics in between stay weak, carving valleys between the peaks. || Keep targets and lines apart. >> A **formant target** is a resonance of the tract. >> A **harmonic line** is a piece of the source. >> A target can land between two lines and still lift both of them. The filter changes heights without creating new lines.
17|Here is a grid that holds one vowel filter steady while the pitch changes. Source rows cross **vowel filter** columns, **dashed red ticks** mark the formant targets, and **colored stems** mark the harmonics after filtering. || Each row keeps one source. >> The top row uses **heavy, loose** folds with crowded harmonics. >> The middle row uses **lighter, tighter** folds at **F0 = 250 Hz**. >> The bottom row uses the **lightest, tightest** folds at **F0 = 350 Hz**. || Each column keeps one filter. >> Go down a column and only the pitch changes, so the ticks stay put while the stems spread apart. >> Go across a row and only the vowel changes, so the stems stay put while the ticks move. >> The grid pulls apart what the source does from what the tract does. || **Dense harmonics trace the formant shape well.** >> At a low pitch, lots of stems crowd around each resonance. >> Their tops draw a smooth **envelope**, and more harmonics make that envelope easier to read. || **Sparse harmonics can miss a formant or make it look ambiguous.** >> At a high pitch, only a few stems sample each formant. >> A tall stem may sit next to a target rather than on it, though the target has not moved an inch. >> The two close /a/ targets can melt into what looks like one region. || A formant that is hard to read is still there. >> The ticks never move down a column. >> When the targets hold still and the stems spread out, the source has changed and the filter has not.
18|A **spectrum** freezes **one instant**, and a **spectrogram** **stacks many spectra over time**. Two moves turn the first into the second. || Each spectrum covers one short slice of sound, with frequency across and amplitude as height. >> The sequence takes three slices of **/i/** and then three of **/a/**. || **Move one** stands each spectrum on its side. >> Frequency now runs up the page, so a high-frequency peak becomes a mark near the top. >> The slices line up from left to right in time order. || **Move two** turns **height into darkness**. >> **Black** means strong, **gray** means medium and **white** means weak. >> A band that **climbs** is moving to a higher frequency. >> A band that **darkens** is getting stronger right where it is. || Watch out for the horizontal axis. >> In a spectrum, moving right means a higher frequency. >> In a spectrogram, moving right means later in time. || As **/i/** turns into **/a/**, the first strong band **climbs** and the second **drops**, so the two draw together. >> An upper band dips as well, while a fainter one stays up high. >> Joining neighboring slices traces how the tract’s resonances shift, with height on the page giving frequency and darkness giving strength.

21|All of this speech science now heads **back to AI**. The plan is a **neural network** trained on students’ own **formants** that decides which vowel is being spoken. || The network gets two numbers, **F1** and **F2**. >> These are the first two resonance regions that the vocal tract emphasizes. >> Two numbers are enough to work with, since the network does not need every point of the waveform. >> The tongue and lips push these two formants around in characteristic ways, so the pair carries much of what tells vowels apart. || Input and output are two different kinds of information. >> The **input** is a pair of measurements of the sound. >> The **output** picks which vowel category the sound belongs to. >> The measured pair is evidence, and the true label is the answer. Recognition means learning to get from one to the other. || This is where the physics of speech meets machine learning. >> The mass–spring account explains where F1 and F2 come from. >> The network learns how those two numbers line up with vowel categories, using measurements from the class. >> For now the inputs and the task are set, and training and testing will reveal how well the network does.
22|The vowel network is **purely linear**. Two inputs go in, pass through **hidden layers of 3, 2 and 4 neurons**, and come out as **5 outputs**, one for each word, with **no activation functions** anywhere. >> The inputs are **F1** and **F2**, always in that order so each position means one formant. || Every layer is **fully connected**. >> Each receiving neuron is wired to every neuron in the layer before it. >> Every wire carries a **weight**, a number that sets how much that incoming value counts. >> The receiving neuron multiplies each incoming value by its weight and adds everything up, and that sum is its value. || The hidden layers do the in-between work. >> They have **three**, then **two**, then **four** neurons. >> Each hidden value is a fresh weighted blend of the values before it, not a new formant and not a finished answer. >> Because every neuron has its own weights, neurons blend the same inputs in different ways. || The outputs stand for five words, top to bottom **/done/, /dune/, /dean/, /Don/** and **/Dan/**. >> Keeping that order fixed keeps each number tied to its word. || **Purely linear** means each layer just hands its weighted sums to the next. >> From start to finish, the network is one long chain of weighted sums. >> Nothing in that chain turns the five outputs into probabilities by itself.
23|One example goes into the network, **F1 = 320** and **F2 = 2000**, and before any training **every edge gets a random weight**. || The input and the weights do different jobs. >> The input **measures** the sound. >> The weights are the network’s **settings**, deciding how those measurements get combined. >> Change the weights and every result inside the network changes, even with the same input. || Each connection has one weight. >> A neuron multiplies its incoming values by their weights and adds the products. >> A neuron in the first hidden layer hears from both F1 and F2, so it holds two weights. >> The three neurons there each hold their own pair, so they build three different blends of the same input. || **Before training, every weight is a random number between −1 and 1.** >> The edges carry numbers such as **0.95**, **−0.91** and **0.28**. >> The heading says **blue for positive** and **orange for negative**, though the negative edges come out green in the figure. The written signs are the reliable guide, with the sign giving direction and the size giving strength. >> A negative weight flips its input, so some contributions add up and others pull against each other. || These are just **random starting values**, with no knowledge of vowels in them yet. >> A **forward pass** runs an example through whatever weights are there now. >> **Learning** is the separate job of changing those weights so the network gets better.
24|In the **forward flow**, the input travels through the network **layer by layer**, and what comes out the end is **a distribution over the five words**. >> In the animation, dots carry the input along the connections from one layer to the next. >> At each layer, the values are combined using the current weights and passed on. >> That trip is the **forward pass**. Changing the weights is a separate step, the learning. || The illustrated output hands each word a probability. >> **/done/** gets **0.5**, the largest share. >> **/Dan/** gets **0.2**. >> **/dune/**, **/dean/** and **/Don/** get **0.1** each. >> So /done/ is the favored guess, while the others keep a little support. || For a set of numbers to count as probabilities, each must sit between zero and one and all of them must add to one, which these do. || The bars picture the kind of output the network is meant to give. >> A purely linear network has nothing in it that forces a proper distribution. >> So the bars stand for the goal, not a checked result of these exact weights. || One more distinction matters. >> The **predicted category** is the word the network favors. >> The **true category** is the vowel that was actually spoken. >> Only the true label can say whether the guess was right, so the tallest bar and the smiley face cannot settle it alone.
25|In **PyTorch**, the whole vowel network fits in a handful of lines. >> **import torch** brings in the library. >> **import torch.nn as nn** brings in its neural-network tools under the short name **nn**. >> The finished model is stored as **net**. || **nn.Sequential** stacks the layers in the order that information flows through them. >> A comment in the code marks the shape, purely linear, **2 to 3 to 2 to 4 to 5**. >> Each **nn.Linear** layer takes in values and hands out weighted sums. || The four layers match the picture one for one. >> **nn.Linear(2, 3, bias=False)** turns 2 inputs into 3. >> **nn.Linear(3, 2, bias=False)** turns 3 into 2. >> **nn.Linear(2, 4, bias=False)** turns 2 into 4. >> **nn.Linear(4, 5, bias=False)** turns 4 into the 5 outputs. >> Each layer hands out exactly as many values as the next layer takes in, so everything flows through to the five words. || Inside, **nn.Linear(in, out)** keeps an **out × in weight matrix**, one weight for every wire. >> **bias=False** strips out the extra constant a layer could tack onto each sum. >> All the weights stay, learning is still possible, and every layer remains a **pure linear map**. || This code is a **declaration only, with no training**. >> There are no activation layers, so the whole thing stays linear. >> Writing it down neither trains the network nor produces the probability bars. That would take labeled examples and a way to update the weights.
