2|Picture a mass resting on a spring at **equilibrium**, the place where it would stay if left alone. Pull it to one side and the spring stretches, so the spring pulls back toward rest. Push it the other way and the spring compresses, so it pushes back. This pull toward rest is the **restoring force**. >> The restoring force speeds the mass up as it heads home. >> By the time it gets there, the mass is moving fast, and its **inertia** carries it straight through to the other side. || On the far side, the spring is deformed the opposite way, so the force reverses. >> The mass slows, stops and heads back. >> The trip repeats again and again, and that repeated trip is an **oscillation**. || The mass can make this trip in two directions. >> When it moves along the spring, the motion is **longitudinal**, and the spring itself gets longer and shorter. >> When it moves across the spring, the motion is **transverse**. The two spring segments are already stretched tight, and once the mass leaves their line, their tilted pull aims it back toward the line. || In the demonstration, the two directions keep perfect time with each other at **0.5 Hz**, one full cycle every two seconds. >> They match because the moving mass and the total restoring stiffness are the same. >> Nothing in the model drains energy, so there is **no damping**, and the swings never shrink.
3|What makes one oscillator faster than another? Three systems answer the question by changing one thing at a time from a reference that has mass **m**, stiffness **K** and a frequency of **0.5 Hz**. || The first change makes the mass four times larger, to **4m**, and leaves the spring alone. >> The system slows to **0.25 Hz**, half its original rate. >> A heavier mass has more inertia, so the same spring force takes longer to speed it up, slow it down and turn it around. || The second change makes the spring four times stiffer, to **4K**, and leaves the mass alone. >> The system speeds up to **1 Hz**, twice its original rate. >> A stiffer spring pulls back harder at every displacement, so the mass reverses sooner and fits more cycles into each second. || Put together, **mass lowers frequency and stiffness raises it**. >> Mass describes how much the system resists a change in motion. >> Stiffness describes how hard the system is pulled back toward rest. || Rate is judged by counting complete cycles over the same time, and not by how big the swing looks. >> Swing size is **amplitude**, which is a separate property. >> The longitudinal and transverse versions agree in every row because each pair shares the same **effective restoring stiffness**, the restoring action in its own direction of motion. Across the spring, that stiffness comes from tension and geometry, so stiffening a spring along its length does not by itself make it stiffer sideways.
4|Two equal masses sit between two fixed walls, linked by three equal springs. A system like this has **two normal modes**, and in each one every part moves at a single shared frequency in a fixed relationship to the others. || In **Mode 1**, the two masses move together, in the same direction at the same moment. >> Their spacing hardly changes, so the middle spring is barely stretched. >> With the middle spring nearly idle, the restoring force is weaker, and the mode is slower, at **0.4 Hz**. || In **Mode 2**, the masses move against each other, closing in and then spreading apart. >> Now the middle spring is squeezed and stretched hard on every cycle. >> That extra pull makes the mode faster, at about **1.73 times** the Mode 1 rate, which is higher but not double. || Both masses take part in both modes. >> The masses and springs never change, and only the relationship between the masses does, moving together in one mode and opposing each other in the other. || The two patterns can also run at the same time. >> At each mass, the two motions add, reinforcing where they point the same way and canceling where they point opposite ways. This adding is called **superposition**. >> The bottom row of the demonstration is the exact sum of the two modes. It looks irregular, yet it carries both frequencies, and the longitudinal and transverse chains behave the same way.

6|With three equal masses and four equal springs between fixed walls, the chain has **three normal modes**. || In **Mode 1**, all three masses swing the same way. >> The middle one travels farthest, and the outer two travel less, so moving together does not mean moving the same distance. >> No neighbors oppose each other, so the springs work little, and this is the slowest mode, at **0.32 Hz**. || In **Mode 2**, the two outer masses swing in opposite directions, and the middle mass stays perfectly still. >> The pulls on the middle mass from its two sides are always equal and opposite, so they cancel. >> A point that stays still through a whole mode is called a **node**. >> This mode runs at about **1.85 times** the lowest rate. || In **Mode 3**, the outer masses swing together while the middle mass swings against them. >> Every mass now opposes its neighbor, so every spring is stretched hard. >> This is the fastest mode, at about **2.41 times** the lowest rate. || The ordering comes from how strongly neighbors oppose one another. >> More opposition means more stretching, a stronger restoring force and a faster cycle. >> The same three masses take part in every mode, with colors tracking each mass and resting marks showing where each displacement is measured from. || These ratios belong to this chain and are not whole numbers. >> When all three modes run together, they add at each mass. >> The middle mass still moves in that mixture, carried by Modes 1 and 3, even though Mode 2 holds it still.
7|The atlas lays out chains of one to six equal masses in rows, with mode numbers in columns. The rule is that each movable mass brings one more **normal mode**, so a chain of N masses has N modes. >> Shorter chains leave the later columns blank, which gives **21** patterns across the grid. >> Every chain has equal masses, one more spring than masses and two fixed ends. || Within each chain, **Mode 1** is always the broadest pattern, with all the masses moving together. >> Each step up adds another group moving the opposite way. >> More reversals create more places where neighbors pull against each other, which stretches the springs more and drives a faster cycle. || The fixed ends stay still in every mode. >> When a mass stays still within one mode, it is a **node** of that mode, and the rest of the chain keeps moving. >> A higher mode is the same set of masses and springs moving in a new arrangement, and the mode number names that arrangement. || Colors mark mode number and rows mark chain size. >> Every pattern is drawn to the same largest displacement, so a taller picture is not a faster one. >> **Amplitude** is how far things move, and frequency is how often they repeat, so the rate must be read from the timing. >> Chains of separate masses do not land on the exact whole-number spacing of an ideal continuous string.
8|A **fixed–fixed string** is held still at both ends, so both ends are **nodes** in every mode. The string can only take shapes that fit whole half-waves between those ends. >> **Mode 1** fits one half-wave, a single broad arch. >> Modes 2 through 5 fit two, three, four and five half-waves. || Squeezing more half-waves into the same length shortens the wavelength, and shorter waves at the same speed repeat faster. >> The modes run at **100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz**. >> The lowest is the **fundamental**, and the rest are whole-number multiples of it, so they are consecutive **harmonics**, odd and even alike. || All five modes can vibrate on the string at the same time. >> At each point, their displacements add, reinforcing in some places and canceling in others. >> As the faster modes pull ahead, the pattern of agreement shifts, and the overall shape keeps changing. A point can go nearly flat for a moment and then move strongly again, with every mode still present. || The animation is slowed by a factor of **250**, so 100 Hz plays at **0.4 Hz**, and the equal slowdown keeps every relative rate the same. || A **pickup** sits **17%** of the way along the string and records the motion at that spot. >> Its **spectrum** has one peak for each mode. >> The mode strengths were chosen so that the five peaks are equal at this spot. Somewhere else the heights would differ, so equal peaks do not mean equal motion along the whole string.
9|A **membrane** is a vibrating surface with fixed edges. Because a surface spreads in two directions, each mode carries two numbers, one counting the moving regions across each direction. >> **(1,1)** is the whole surface rising and falling as one region. >> **(2,1)** and **(1,2)** split it into two regions, along one direction or the other. >> **(2,2)** makes four regions, and **(3,1)** makes three along one direction. >> Neighboring regions always move opposite to each other, with still **node** lines between them. || All five modes can run at once, and they add at every point. >> Some places reinforce and others cancel, so the combined surface keeps changing shape. >> The combined panel uses a smaller vertical scale to fit, so its height does not compare directly with the single panels. || A node is still for the entire cycle. >> A moving region crosses the flat resting level for a split second on each cycle and keeps going, so that crossing is not a node. >> Following one region as it rises, crosses and falls, while its neighbor does the reverse, makes the difference clear. The two numbers describe how the surface divides, not two frequencies or two membranes. || A **pickup** at one marked point records the motion there, and its **spectrum** has one peak per mode. >> The strengths were set to make the peaks equal at that point. >> The frequencies **100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz** were **assigned for the illustration** and are not this rectangle’s natural resonances. Their even spacing models how frequencies combine, without claiming that every membrane is harmonic.
10|Science moves forward by stating very clear theories about what the world is like, and in this field those theories are usually **differential equations**. >> A differential equation spells out how the quantities in a system change. >> Solving it produces a **prediction**, a concrete statement about what a real measurement should show. || The theory on the table is that the **vocal folds are a membrane**. >> When a membrane vibrates, its wave of vibration has **equidistant peaks** in the spectrum, and those evenly spaced peaks are called **harmonics**. >> The equations are the same kind already used for springs and membranes, only a little more complicated. || From the theory comes a prediction anyone can check. >> If a person’s **laryngeal vibration** signal is measured, its spectrum should show **equidistant harmonics**, with equal gaps between the peaks. >> The check is an experiment that records real vocal-fold vibration and looks at how its peaks are spaced. >> Even spacing would agree with the theory, and uneven spacing would count against it. || What makes the prediction sharp is the spacing. >> The folds vibrate in any case, so simply seeing vibration proves nothing. >> The membrane animation was built with assigned harmonic frequencies, so it pictures the idea without testing it. Only a recording of a real voice can test it, and that experiment is proposed here with its outcome still to come.
11|In a **fixed–free system**, one end is held still and the other is free to move. Each end sets a condition that every shape must meet. >> The fixed end must be a **node**, where nothing moves. >> The free end must be an **antinode**, where movement is greatest. || The shapes that meet both conditions fit odd numbers of **quarter-waves** into the length. >> The simplest fits a single quarter-wave, rising from stillness at the fixed end to full swing at the free end. >> The next fit three, five and seven quarter-waves. >> They are the **first, third, fifth and seventh harmonics**, so the second allowed mode is actually the third harmonic. || An even count of quarter-waves would leave a node at the free end, which the free end forbids. >> Fitting more quarter-waves into the same length shortens the waves, so each higher mode repeats faster. || The demonstration runs the modes at **100, 300, 500 and 700 Hz**, slowed **250** times so they can be seen. >> They are given equal strengths, so the spectrum at the free end shows four equal peaks. >> The antinode does not hold still at its farthest point. It swings through rest to the other side on every cycle, while the fixed end never moves. || The fixed–fixed and fixed–free strings were each **tuned separately** to the same 100 Hz fundamental. >> With the same length and wave speed, freeing one end would cut the fundamental in half. >> The matching labels therefore come from separate tuning and do not isolate what the free end does.
