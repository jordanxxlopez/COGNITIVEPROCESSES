2|The familiar vowel network now stands for a whole era of neural networks, **1943 to 1986**. >> Two formants go in, three weighted hidden layers process them, and five outputs answer for done, dune, dean, Don and Dan. >> The weights are the same illustrative ones used for forward propagation and backpropagation. || The era opens with **McCulloch and Pitts (1943)**. >> Their paper, **“A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity,”** starts from the **“all-or-none” character of nervous activity**. >> If a neuron either fires or doesn’t, neural events can be handled with propositional logic. || It closes with the two **Parallel Distributed Processing** books of **1986**. >> Those volumes made trainable networks of many simple connected units a mainstream tool. || The caption sums up what these networks could do. **Given some static vector of formants, the NN could make a decision about a few possible categories.** >> A static vector is one fixed snapshot, here just F1 and F2. >> From that one snapshot, the network picks one of five words. || Then it names the catch. **Human cognition is far more complex.** >> Speech unfolds over time, and in a sentence each word depends on the ones before it. >> A network that turns one snapshot into a label has nowhere to keep what came earlier, and that is the gap the next kinds of network had to close.
3|In **1985, Michael Jordan developed recurrent neural networks** to understand **speech production**, a line of work that grew into **LLMs** and then **agentic AI**. >> That’s Michael Jordan in the photo. || His network has four kinds of units. >> **Plan units** take in which sequence to say. >> **Hidden units** do the combining, and **output units** give the response. >> The new piece is the **state units**. The outputs curve back into them, and each one also loops back into itself. || Those loops are what make the network **recurrent**. >> The state units keep a running trace of what the network just said. >> That trace feeds back in alongside the plan, so every new output depends on the recent ones. >> The vowel classifier can’t do this, because an input goes through it once and is forgotten. || The plot, **Performance on /istrstry/**, follows the network as it produces a word over 32 time steps. >> Each row tracks one articulatory feature, **Voice, Place, Sonorant, Sibilant, Nasal, Height, Back and Round**. >> Sibilant bumps up wherever an s comes, while Nasal and Height barely move. || Watch the **Round** line. >> It climbs early and stays up for most of the word, instead of flicking on and off for single sounds. >> Because each output leans on the last, features flow smoothly across neighboring sounds, and that habit of feeding past outputs back in lives on in today’s language models.
4|Four columns build from the smallest piece to the biggest system. >> First comes **one neuron**. A vector **x** comes in, each part gets multiplied by a weight **w**, and everything adds up to one number **y**. >> **Vector in, one number out.** That sum is an inner product. || Next comes a **network** with layers of 4, 3, 4 and 5 units, linked by **W₁, W₂ and W₃**. >> Each W turns one vector into the next one. >> The last layer is a **decision vector**, one value per answer, just like the vowel classifier. **Vector in, decision vector out.** || Then comes an **LLM**. >> **Vectors 1 through 15**, one per token, enter at the bottom and climb through **5 layers** of W blocks, where six colors mean six kinds of weights. >> Out of the top comes **vector 16**, a little bar chart labeled **PDF**, the model’s probabilities for the next token. >> It is **a dynamical system with many vectors in and one decision vector out**. || Last comes an **agentic system, a graph of LLMs**. >> Separate LLMs **Outline, Write, Critic, Revise** and produce the **Final** version. >> A **loop** lets the Critic send the draft back to Revise as many times as it needs. || Underneath all four, only two things are going on. >> **Inference is inner products**, weighted sums handed from layer to layer. >> **Learning is SGD plus DP**, gradient descent powered by backpropagation’s dynamic programming.
5|The page opens with **15 vectors, x₁ to x₁₅**, an empty spot for **x₁₆**, and a question, **vectors of what?** >> An LLM reads a row of vectors and guesses the next one, and each reveal fills the row with something different. || First, **a sentence cut into 15 words**. >> “The cat that the dog chased ran up the old tree and sat on the ___.” >> **x₁₆ is the missing word**, and to get it you have to keep track of the cat, since it is the one sitting. || Then the words give way to **a spectrogram cut into 15 frames**, and after that to **an image cut into 16 patches**. >> In the spectrogram, running from 0 to 5 kHz over time, **x₁₆ is the next frame of sound**. >> In the picture of a house, a tree and the sun, read row by row, **x₁₆ is the missing bottom-right patch**. || Last comes **a protein, the insulin B chain**, as **15 amino acids**. >> F V N Q H L C G S H L V E A L curl into a helix, and **x₁₆ is the next residue, Y**. || The bottom line ties the four together. **The LLM only ever sees vectors.** >> Words, sounds, pixels and molecules all arrive as rows of vectors. >> So it is **one machine with one question, what is x₁₆?**
6|Which strings could be English words? The answer builds up in three rows. >> Twenty **one-syllable words**, like Don, mat, cup, fish and ring, each have a single consonant, a single vowel and a single consonant. >> So **a simple syllable in English can have a consonant, then a vowel, then a consonant**. || Next come **clusters**, consonants bunched together. >> Before the vowel they are **onsets**, as in split, flat, black, glad and stop. >> After the vowel they are **codas**, as in desk, box, help, milk and lamp. >> Each real word sits over a starred impossible twin, like *sblit, *lfat, *bnack, *desb and *hepl. || Many twins just swap the order of the letters. >> flat becomes *lfat, help becomes *hepl, lamp becomes *lapm. >> So **order matters**, and the allowed order flips around the vowel. >> **Only certain sequences are allowed before the vowel and after the vowel.** || Longer words string several syllables together. >> ba·na·na, win·dow, pump·kin, nap·kin, At·lan·tic, ath·lete, ad·mit and spa·ghet·ti all break into pieces. >> t·l, th·l and d·m are fine across a break, but no word can start with them, so *tlantic, *thlete and *dmit are out. >> ng can end a syllable, as in ring, but never start one, so *ngapkin is out too. || Put together, **a long word is a chain of syllables** that all follow the same rules. >> **t·l is fine across a break, but tl cannot start a syllable.** >> Knowing which sound sequences a language allows is **phonotactics**, and English speakers know it without ever being taught.
7|The pop quiz asks how **phonotactic knowledge can be revealed through GPT learning**. >> Each item is the beginning of an English word, and you **guess the next letter**. >> The starts are ra_, sp_, k_, skr_, m_, bram_, b_, f_ and sh_. >> People agree fast, and how much they agree shifts from item to item. || Right after a vowel, the field is wide open. >> After **ra_**, t, n, r, l and plenty of others all work, since **after a vowel almost anything** can follow. || At the very start of a word, the choices shrink. >> After **sp_**, **b_** or **f_**, only **l, r or a vowel** will do, never *spn, *bn or *fn. >> After **k_**, a vowel or **n**, as in knee, never *kt. >> After **sh_**, a vowel or **r**, as in shrimp, never *shb. || Some starts leave almost no room. >> After **skr_** or **m_**, only a vowel can come, never *skrl or *mb. >> After **bram_**, b is nearly certain, as in bramble, unless a new syllable begins. || And that is the point. **Guessing the next letter is predicting x₁₆.** >> A **GPT trained only on this game learns English sounds and spelling**. >> Its guesses are spread flat after fa_ and pile up on a few vowels after skr_.
