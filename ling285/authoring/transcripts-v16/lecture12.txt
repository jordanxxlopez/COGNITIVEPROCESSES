2|Springs are not the only things that swing. Any system whose parts keep influencing one another over time is a **dynamic system**, and economists model whole economies this way. >> **Woodford’s theory** is called **Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium**, or **DSGE** for short. >> Its name says the economy changes over time, gets knocked about by random shocks and has many linked markets that settle toward balance together. || Picture two lines running across time, one for each economic quantity. >> The **black curve** follows **unemployment**. >> The **gray curve** follows **inflation**. >> Early on, both lurch through tall, jagged peaks and deep dips. Once the economy is stabilized, the same lines rise and fall far more gently. || That calming is what **stabilization** means. >> The economy keeps moving, and unemployment and inflation remain two different things. >> What changes is the size of the swings, so that harmful extremes like very high unemployment or runaway inflation are avoided. || The spring and the economy raise one shared question, what drives a fluctuation and what can tame it. >> In the spring, inertia and the restoring force keep the mass swinging. >> In the economy, shocks and the responses of its parts keep the numbers swinging. The calmer curves picture the goal, without testing any particular policy.

9|Everything about these oscillators can be boiled down to seven **principles of mass–spring systems**. || The first two describe the system as a whole. >> **More mass means lower frequency, and more stiffness means higher frequency.** A heavier load turns around slowly, and a stiffer spring snaps it back quickly. >> **A system with n masses has n modes**, and each mode is faster than the one before. || The next three bring in **location**. >> Added mass lowers a mode’s frequency in proportion to how much that mass actually moves in the mode, which depends on where it sits. >> Added stiffness raises a mode’s frequency in proportion to how much that spring stretches or compresses, which also depends on where it sits. >> Movement and stretching never peak in the same place. Where the motion is largest, neighbors travel together and nothing stretches, and where the stretching is largest, the point in the middle hardly moves. || That is why one change can matter a lot in one mode and almost nothing in another. >> A spot that swings widely in a mode is sensitive to added mass. >> A spot sitting at a node of that mode barely notices, though it can still move in other modes. || The last two concern the endpoints. >> A **fixed–fixed system** has harmonics at whole-number multiples of its base frequency, because the base shape fits a whole number of times into each higher mode. >> A **fixed–free system** has harmonics at **odd multiples**, because the base shape fits an odd number of times into each higher mode.

11|Here is a **pop quiz**. A spot just left of the middle of a string gets extra mass or extra stiffness, and the question is what happens to **Mode 4** and **Mode 5**. || The answer depends on what each mode is doing at that spot. >> **Mode 4** swings the spot strongly, and its neighbors swing along with it, so the string there barely stretches. >> **Mode 5** hardly moves the spot, but its neighbors move differently, so the string there stretches and compresses a lot. || Start with the **extra mass**. >> In Mode 4, the added load rides through a big swing on every cycle, so the mode slows down and its frequency falls. >> In Mode 5, the spot barely moves, so the added mass goes almost nowhere and the frequency stays put. >> Mass lowers frequency only when the mass is moving. || Now the **extra stiffness**. >> In Mode 5, the string at the spot stretches hard, so a stiffer spring there adds plenty of restoring force and the frequency rises. >> In Mode 4, the string at the spot hardly stretches, so the extra stiffness changes little. || Add **both** at once and the two effects split neatly, **Mode 4 goes down and Mode 5 goes up**. >> The spot is identical in both modes. >> What differs is its job, mostly swinging in one mode and mostly stretching in the other, judged across a whole cycle rather than a single frozen moment.

15|The air in your vocal tract can be thought of as a chain of small masses joined by springs. >> Air in motion has **inertia** and wants to keep moving. >> Squeeze it and its pressure rises, so it pushes back, and that is the spring. >> Each bit of air moves **longitudinally**, back and forth along the tube around its usual place, instead of flowing out of the mouth. || The tube is a **fixed–free** system. >> At the **glottis**, the fixed end, the air has little room to move. >> At the open **lips**, the free end, it moves most easily. || The two slowest patterns of this tube line up with the first two formants. >> **Mode 1 is F1-like**, the lowest resonance, with all the air moving in one broad pattern. >> **Mode 2 is F2-like**, with one more reversal tucked inside the tube. >> In a perfectly uniform tube, Mode 2 runs **three times** as fast as Mode 1, the odd-multiple pattern of any fixed–free system. Change the shape of the tract and that ratio changes too. || Each mode is a coordinated motion of the whole column, not a separate stream of air. >> In some stretches, neighbors travel together and their gaps stay steady. >> In others, the gaps keep opening and closing. || Three markers show where three vowels narrow the tract. >> **/a/** narrows about **one-third** of the way from the glottis. >> **/i/** narrows about **two-thirds** of the way. >> **/u/** narrows at the **lips**. >> Each marker is the same spot in both modes. Because mass matters where a mode moves and springiness matters where it squeezes, one narrowing can nudge F1 and F2 in different directions.
16|Two everyday dimensions describe vowels, how high the **tongue** sits and how far **front or back** it is. On top of those, every vowel narrows the airway at its own spot. The chart places five words, dean, dune, Dan, Don and done. || Look first at the two high vowels. >> **Dean** is **high and front**, narrowing about **two-thirds** of the way from the glottis to the lips. >> **Dune** is **high and back**, narrowing right at the **lips**. >> They share a height, yet they narrow in different places, so their tract shapes differ. || Then the two low vowels. >> **Dan** is **low and front**, with no numbered narrowing. >> **Don** is **low and back**, narrowing about **one-third** of the way from the glottis. || **Done** sits in the **center** with **“No constriction,”** and it serves as the open reference. || Each label tracks something different. >> High or low is about tongue height. >> Front or back is about where the tongue sits along the mouth. >> The fraction is about distance along the air path, counted from the glottis. >> Knowing that a vowel is high still leaves its narrowing place open, as dean and dune make clear. || The narrowing place is what ties the mouth to the sound. >> Narrowing the airway changes how mass-like and spring-like the air is at that spot. >> How each formant responds depends on how its mode moves and squeezes that spot.

19|For **/u/**, the model loads the open lips, which are the **free end** of the air column. The air elements there are drawn larger to stand for more **effective mass**, simply more air that has to be shuttled back and forth at that spot. || What are the two lowest modes doing at the lips? >> In **Mode 1**, **motion dominates** there. >> In **Mode 2**, **motion dominates** there as well. >> The free end is where both modes swing the most. || A heavier moving load is slower to speed up and slower to turn around. >> Wherever motion dominates, that extra load stretches out each cycle. >> Both modes slow, so **F1 goes down and F2 goes down** compared with the uniform tube. || The two rows let the same lip spot be compared in two different resonance patterns. >> Inside the tube the patterns differ, since Mode 2 has an extra reversal. >> At the lips, though, both swing strongly, so the load slows both. >> F1 is still the lower formant and F2 still the higher one. Each shift is measured against its own uniform reference. || The shared direction comes from the role the lips play in both modes. >> Motion wins in both, so loading lowers both. >> Narrowings elsewhere can sit where the modes behave differently, so this is not a rule that every narrowing lowers every formant.
20|For **/i/**, the narrowing sits near the front, about **two-thirds** of the way from the glottis to the lips. One spot is squeezed, but that spot means something different to each mode. || In **Mode 1**, **mass motion dominates** at the narrowing. >> The air there swings strongly in this mode. >> Narrowing it adds moving mass, which is harder to turn around. >> The cycle slows, so **F1 goes down**. || In **Mode 2**, **springiness dominates** at the same spot. >> The air there is squeezed and stretched hard in this mode, while it travels less. >> Narrowing it makes the push-back stronger. >> The cycle speeds up, so **F2 goes up**. || The spot itself never moves to a new place. >> Its job changes with the mode. >> Where neighbors travel together, the moving load is what counts. Where neighbors keep crowding together and pulling apart, the push-back is what counts. || The upshot is **lower F1 and higher F2** than the uniform tube. >> Adding mass and springiness together does not cancel out. >> Each mode answers to whichever effect rules at that spot in its own pattern, so a single narrowing drives the two formants apart.
21|For **/a/**, the narrowing moves back, to about **one-third** of the way from the glottis to the lips. Here the two modes swap the roles they had for /i/, while mass and stiffness behave exactly as always. || In **Mode 1**, **springiness dominates** at this spot. >> The air here is squeezed and stretched hard in this mode. >> Narrowing it strengthens the push-back, so the cycle quickens and **F1 goes up**. || In **Mode 2**, **mass motion dominates** at this spot. >> The air here swings widely in this mode. >> Narrowing it adds moving mass, so the cycle slows and **F2 goes down**. || One spot, two jobs. >> In a squeezing job, the spot itself barely travels while the air on either side crowds in and thins out. >> In a swinging job, the spot travels far while the gaps around it hardly change. >> At the one-third point, Mode 1 hands the spot a squeezing job and Mode 2 hands it a swinging job. || So /a/ comes out with **higher F1 and lower F2**, the mirror of /i/. >> Moving a narrowing changes which job each mode gives it, and nothing about mass or stiffness themselves. >> The arrows give the predicted direction of each change, not how big it is.

23|Every spot in a vibrating air column can be described two ways, by its **movement** and by its **compression**. >> **Movement** is how far a bit of air travels. >> **Compression** is how the gaps between neighbors change. Tighter gaps mean compression, and looser gaps mean **rarefaction**, air that is thinned out. >> Neighbors can travel a long way together with steady gaps, or crowd and spread around a bit of air that barely moves. || The animation pulls one air column apart into its patterns. >> The moving **Schwa** row is the uniform reference. >> The **F1, F2 and F3** rows are the first three resonances of that very same column. >> The closed left end holds the air still, the open right end lets it move, and each higher mode packs in more alternating stretches of movement and compression. || Compression and movement are the spring and the mass. >> Crowded air pushes back toward normal spacing. >> Moving air keeps going because of inertia. >> Between them, the air keeps oscillating around its usual positions. || Strong movement and strong compression live in different places. >> Where movement is large, there is a lot of moving mass. >> Where spacing changes a lot, there is strong push-back, even though the air in the middle hardly travels. || Watching a single bit of air over a cycle reveals its movement, and watching the gaps beside it reveals compression. >> Added mass counts where movement is strong. >> Added stiffness counts where compression is strong. >> Those places shift from mode to mode, and each row is a resonance of the same air, not a separate stream.
