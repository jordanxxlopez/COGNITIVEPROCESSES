## p2
- **Real-time MRI films the inside of the head while a person talks.** This makes the hidden movements of speech visible. The view is a side view cut down the middle of the head. The face points left, and the back of the neck is on the right. Air appears black in the image. Soft tissue such as the tongue, lips and throat walls appears gray and white.
  - The **lips** sit at the far left. They open, close, round and spread during speech.
  - The **tongue** is the large rounded mass that fills most of the mouth. It is the most active articulator (speech organ).
  - Raised: The tongue bunches up toward the roof of the mouth.
  - Retracted: The tongue pulls back toward the throat.
  - Lowered: The tongue lies low in the mouth.
  - The roof of the mouth has two parts. The front part is bony and is called the **hard palate**. The back part is soft and movable and is called the **soft palate** or **velum**. The soft palate lifts to seal off the nose, or lowers to let air flow out through the nose.
  - Behind the tongue, a dark channel of air runs down the throat. This channel is the **pharynx**. Below it sits the **larynx**, which holds the **vocal folds**.
- **During speech, all of these structures move at the same time and at high speed.** The jaw rises and falls. The lips open and close. The tongue reshapes itself many times per second. The soft palate lifts and lowers.
  - Each sound depends on a precise shape of the whole passage. The shapes flow smoothly into one another.
  - The two movies capture the same speaker's vocal tract in two different shapes. The difference comes almost entirely from where the tongue and lips are placed.

## p3
- **We learn to move our speech organs in extremely complex ways with no instructions and almost no visual feedback (seeing what we are doing).** Nobody tells a young child where to put the tongue tip for a sound. A child cannot see their own tongue, soft palate or vocal folds, because those organs are hidden inside the mouth and throat.
  - **By ages 2 to 4**, children speak with a wide vocabulary and near-adult language. This happens with hardly any explicit instruction. Children pick up thousands of words and the rules for combining them just from being around people who talk.
  - **Reading and writing** follow a different path. A child may memorize the shapes of the letters early. Reading and writing whole words usually comes at **ages 6 to 7**, and it requires a great deal of instruction.
  - The contrast between the two is the point. Speech uses hidden organs that the learner cannot see, yet it develops almost on its own. Reading and writing use the eyes and hands, which are fully visible, yet they need years of teaching. So speech is a deep human cognitive skill that the brain is prepared to acquire.
- **Articulatory phonetics is the field that studies the movements of the structures of the vocal tract.** The **vocal tract** is the full air passage used in speech. It runs from the larynx up through the throat and mouth to the lips, with a side branch into the nose.
  - Articulatory phonetics asks which structures move, where they move to, and how those movements create the different sounds of a language. Real-time MRI is one of its main tools.
- **In the last two years, voice assistants and other speech technology have come to emulate human speech ability.** To **emulate** an ability means to reproduce its behavior closely enough that the result looks the same. These systems now produce natural-sounding speech, and they recognize spoken language with high accuracy.
  - **LING 285 is about both human speech and AI speech.** One side studies how people produce and perceive speech. The other side studies how modern AI systems model that same ability. The two can then be compared directly.

## p4
- **The next step is to focus on the air between the tongue and the rest of the vocal tract.** In the real vocal tract, that air space bends. It runs down the throat and then forward through the mouth. Ignoring the curve and straightening the passage turns the vocal tract into a simple **tube of air**.
  - One end of the tube is the **lips**. There the passage is open to the outside air. The other end is the **larynx** deep in the throat. There the tube is treated as closed. The walls of the tube stand for the tongue and the other surfaces around the air space.
  - The tube is labeled **schwa**. Schwa is the neutral relaxed vowel, the "uh" sound at the start of "about." For schwa, the tongue sits in a central resting position and does not narrow the passage much anywhere. So the air space is about the same width from end to end, and the straight even tube fits schwa best.
- **The air inside the tube vibrates by shifting back and forth. It does not flow out the end.** Each small bit of air is drawn as a blue circle. Each column of circles shifts back and forth around its own resting position.
  - Compression: Where neighboring columns move toward each other, the air is compressed (pressed together). In this region the air is packed more tightly.
  - Rarefaction: Where neighboring columns move apart, the air is rarefied (spread out). In this region the air is packed more loosely.
  - These regions of packing and spreading travel along the tube and reflect back from its ends. This repeating pattern is the **vibration of air**. It makes up the sound of the vowel.
- **Acoustic phonetics is the field that studies how air vibrates in the vocal tract and what sound results.** Articulatory phonetics describes the shape of the tube. Acoustic phonetics describes the physics of the air inside it. The two fields connect directly to each other. Moving the tongue and lips changes the shape of the tube, and changing the shape of the tube changes how the air inside it vibrates.

## p5
- **AI brings real harms, and pretending it does not exist is not a workable option.** The harms are psychological, economic, social and environmental. Many scientists also warn about **existential harm**, meaning the possibility that advanced AI could threaten humanity's survival or basic way of life.
  - AI exists, with many harms and some benefits. One benefit is **immunotherapy**, which uses the body's own immune system to fight diseases such as cancer. Another is **environmental engineering**, the design of solutions to environmental problems.
  - The environmental case cuts both ways. Running large AI systems uses enormous amounts of energy. At the same time, AI tools are being applied to engineering problems that could help the environment.
- **One of the biggest dangers is that we have no idea how AI works.** The phrase for this is "we know everything, and understand nothing."
  - "We know everything" means every number and every calculation inside an AI system can be inspected. People built the system, so they can look at all of its parts.
  - "We understand nothing" means that no one can explain why the system produces the particular answers it does, even with all of those details in hand.
  - Very few people know how these systems operate. That is a danger in itself, because decisions about how AI is built and used end up in very few hands.
- **AI literacy is necessary for many groups of people.** AI literacy means a working understanding of what AI systems are and how they behave.
  - Scientists: They need it to judge whether results that use AI are trustworthy.
  - Politicians: They need it to write sensible rules for controlling AI.
  - Lawyers: They need it to argue about what a system did and why.
  - The course is built on one belief. A creative thinker in a class like this one can help us understand these systems well enough to benefit from them while avoiding their negative consequences.

## p7
- **Diotima's ladder describes the climb from surface knowledge toward deep understanding.** Surface knowledge is knowledge of particular things. Deep understanding is understanding of the general ideas behind them. Diotima, dated here to around 440 BCE, taught **Socrates** about this ladder in Plato's Symposium. Socrates taught **Plato**, and Plato taught **Aristotle**.
- **The bottom of the ladder holds knowledge that comes from routine and copying.** This includes **daily experience, habit and memorization**. It also includes **specialized skills, repetition and replication**, and learning from **textbooks**.
  - The rule at this level is that **similar is same, and slight difference is different**. Things that look alike are treated as identical. Things that look a little different are treated as unrelated.
- **The top of the ladder holds creativity, insight, thought, focus, attention, imagination and vision.** It also holds **problem-posing, problem-solving, critical reading and creative writing**.
  - The rule at this level is reversed. **Different can be same, and similar can be different.** Things that look different can share the same deeper structure. Things that look alike can be fundamentally different.
  - Speech gives a direct example. The sounds f and v sound different, yet they are made in the same place in the mouth. The th in "thing" and the th in "that" are spelled the same, yet one uses vocal fold vibration and the other does not.
  - **Problem-posing** means asking good questions in the first place. **Problem-solving** means answering questions once they have been asked.
- **A person climbs the ladder through repeated cycles of question and answer.** Each cycle lifts understanding one rung higher. The cycles repeat again and again from the bottom to the top. This is the method Socrates became famous for.
- **For about 2500 years, people could fake it.** They could do well by memorizing, repeating and replicating, without climbing the ladder. In 2026 that is no longer true. Interviews for jobs and graduate school now look for the properties at the top of the ladder.

## p8
- **The quickest way to understand speech sounds is to produce them and notice what the body does.** The exercise uses four pairs of sounds. Each pair is held as one long sound that switches partway through.
  - Hold the **f** in "fat," then switch to the **v** in "vat." Hold the **th** in "thing," then switch to the **th** in "that." Hold the **s** in "sing," then switch to the **z** in "zing." Hold the **sh** in "mesh," then switch to the buzzing sound in the middle of "beige," written **j** here. It is not the j in "jump."
- **Within each pair, the difference is voice. Voice is whether the vocal folds vibrate.** Rest the fingers lightly on the front of the throat while switching.
  - During f, the first th, s and sh, the throat stays still. The sound is a pure hiss of air.
  - During v, the second th, z and the sound in "beige," a buzzing vibration appears in the throat. The sound gains a humming quality (periodic voicing).
  - The **vocal folds** are two small folds of tissue inside the larynx. When they are held apart, air passes through silently, and the sound is **voiceless**. When they are brought close together, the airflow makes them vibrate rapidly, and the sound is **voiced**.
  - Within each pair, the tongue and lips stay in the same position. Only the vocal folds change. So voicing is controlled separately from the mouth.
- **From one line to the next, the difference is place of articulation. Place of articulation is where the narrowing that makes the sound is formed.**
  - Labiodental (f and v): The lower lip presses against the upper front teeth.
  - Dental (both th sounds): The tongue tip touches the upper front teeth.
  - Alveolar (s and z): The tongue tip comes close to the alveolar ridge just behind the upper front teeth.
  - Postalveolar (sh and the sound in beige): The tongue pulls a bit further back.
  - Each new line moves the narrowing further back in the mouth.
- **Voice and place of articulation are two separate, independent properties of a speech sound.** Every sound in the exercise can be described by stating both. Changing one while keeping the other fixed produces a different sound.

## p9
- **All of these sounds are fricatives.** A fricative is made by bringing two parts of the vocal tract very close together without closing the passage. Air from the lungs is forced through the narrow gap. The small opening makes the airflow turbulent (rough and chaotic). The turbulent flow produces the hissing or buzzing noise. The sound can be held as long as there is breath.
- **The fricative table has voice in the rows and place of articulation in the columns.** The places are **labial, dental, alveolar and postalveolar**. The voiceless row holds f, th, s and sh. The voiced row holds v, th, z and j.
  - **Labial** means made with the lips. For f and v, the lower lip rises to touch the upper front teeth. Air hisses through the gaps between them. In the MRI, the lower lip is tucked under the upper teeth, and the tongue lies low.
  - **Dental** means made at the teeth. For the two th sounds, the tip of the tongue touches the upper front teeth. In the MRI, the front of the tongue is pushed forward to the teeth.
  - **Alveolar** means made at the **alveolar ridge**. The alveolar ridge is the small bony ridge on the roof of the mouth just behind the upper front teeth. For s and z, the tongue tip rises to form a narrow groove close to that ridge.
  - **Postalveolar** means made just behind the alveolar ridge. For sh and the sound in "beige," a wider section of the front of the tongue rises a little further back than for s. The lips often push slightly forward.
- **Two fricatives in the same column differ only in voicing. Two fricatives in the same row differ only in place.** The two th sounds make this clear. English spells them the same way, yet one is voiceless and the other is voiced.

## p10
- **Stops are made by closing the passage completely for a brief moment.** During the closure, no air can escape through the mouth. A fricative leaves a narrow gap open, and a stop does not.
  - While the closure is held, air from the lungs keeps pushing into the mouth. Pressure builds up behind the closure. When the closure is released, the trapped air bursts out. That burst makes a stop sound short and sharp.
  - A stop cannot be held as a continuous sound. Its sound depends on the closing and the release.
- **The English stops form a grid of three places of articulation and three types.** Each row keeps one place and changes the type. Each column keeps one type and changes the place.
  - The first row is **p, b and m**. All three close the **lips**.
  - Voiceless p: The vocal folds stay still.
  - Voiced b: The vocal folds vibrate.
  - Nasal m: The vocal folds vibrate and the **soft palate lowers** (the velum drops), so air flows out through the nose while the lips are closed.
  - The second row is **t, d and n**. All three press the tongue tip against the **alveolar ridge**, the same place used for s and z. For t, the vocal folds stay still. For d, the vocal folds vibrate. For n, the vocal folds vibrate and the soft palate lowers.
  - The third row is **k, g and ng**. All three raise the **back of the tongue** against the **soft palate**. For k, the vocal folds stay still. For g, the vocal folds vibrate. For ng, the sound at the end of "sing," they vibrate and the passage to the nose opens.
- **The columns follow the same logic as the fricative table.** The first column, **p, t and k**, holds the **voiceless** stops. The second column, **b, d and g**, holds the **voiced** stops. The third column, **m, n and ng**, holds the **nasal** stops. Nasal stops are voiced, and they send air through the nose.
  - A sound made with the soft palate lowered is called a **nasal**. The MRI of m shows the gap opened between the soft palate and the back of the throat.
  - Nine sounds come from three places of closure combined with three settings of the vocal folds and soft palate. A small number of independent movements builds a large set of speech sounds.
