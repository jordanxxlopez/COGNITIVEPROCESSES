## p2
- **Every vibrating system in speech follows the same short list of principles.**
  - **Raising the mass lowers the frequency, and raising the stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - **A system with n masses has n modes.** Each higher mode has a higher frequency, because more parts move against each other and more springs stretch.
  - **Adding mass lowers the frequency in proportion to how much that mass moves.** A mass that does not move has no effect. A mass that moves a little has a small effect. A mass that moves a lot has a large effect.
  - **Adding stiffness raises the frequency in proportion to how much that spring stretches and compresses.** Both effects depend on where the change is made.
  - **Where there is the most movement there is no stretching, and where there is the most stretching there is no movement.**
  - In a **fixed-fixed system**, the frequencies are called **harmonics**, and they are **whole-number multiples** of the base frequency.
  - In a **fixed-free system**, the frequencies are called **formants**, and they are **odd multiples** of the base frequency.
- **Raising mass and stiffness at the same time does not cancel out, except for a single mass on a single spring.** In a system with many masses and springs, a spot that moves a lot hardly stretches, and a spot that stretches a lot hardly moves. So at any one spot, only one of the two changes matters.
  - Picture a spot on a string that never moves but is stretched hard. Extra mass at that spot does nothing. Extra stiffness at that spot raises the frequency. So the frequency goes up.
  - Picture a spot on a string that moves up and down the most. Extra stiffness at that spot does nothing. Extra mass at that spot slows it down. So the frequency goes down.

## p3
- **Adding masses adds modes, and the same mode shapes repeat.**
  - **Mode 1** always has everything moving together.
  - **Mode 2** always splits the system into two halves that move against each other.
  - **Mode 3** always has the middle moving against the sides.
- **Higher modes are stiffer and faster because more parts move against each other.** More parts moving against each other means more springs are stretching.
  - A higher mode does not have stiffer springs. A higher mode makes the same springs do more stretching.

## p4
- **In a string fixed at both ends, each higher mode is made of more copies of the first mode.** The first mode is **one half-wave**. A half-wave is one bump that goes from no motion at one end, to the most motion in the middle, to no motion at the other end.
  - **Mode 2 holds two half-waves.** One half goes up while the other half goes down. Each half acts like a copy of Mode 1, so Mode 2 has **twice the stiffness** and **twice the frequency**.
  - **Mode 3 holds three half-waves**, and **Mode 5 holds five**, so the fifth mode has five times the frequency of the first.
  - So the frequencies climb in equal steps. If the first harmonic is 100 hertz, the next harmonics are 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz.
- **The numbers do not have to start at 100.** A voice with a base frequency of **250 hertz** has its next harmonics at 500, 750 and 1000 hertz. Each harmonic is still a whole number of copies of the first.
  - The short exam answer is that **the fifth mode has the first mode fitted into it five times**, so it has five times the stiffness and five times the frequency.

## p5
- **A string fixed at both ends vibrates in all its modes at once, and its spectrum has evenly spaced spikes.** A **spectrum** has **frequency** along the bottom and **amplitude**, or strength, going up. Each spike is one harmonic.
  - The **vocal folds** are fixed on all sides, just behind the Adam's apple. So the vocal folds behave like this kind of fixed-fixed system and produce harmonics.

## p6
- **The same copy-counting works on a membrane.** The vocal folds form a **membrane**, which is a vibrating sheet.
  - The first mode is one bump across the whole membrane.
  - The second mode fits two of those bumps, so it has twice the frequency.
  - The fourth mode fits four ups and downs, so it has four times the frequency.

## p7
- **A building is fixed at the bottom and free at the top.** In strong wind or an earthquake, engineers want a building to sway only in **Mode 1**, with the whole building moving together.
  - Mode 1 feels unpleasant on a high floor, but it is safe.
  - A higher mode would send some floors one way and other floors the other way, and the building could break apart.
- **A string can also be fixed at the bottom and free at the top.** The first mode of this string is not a full half-wave. The first mode goes from **no motion at the fixed end** to **the most motion at the free end**, which is only **one quarter of a wave**.

## p8
- **In a fixed-free system, each mode is built from quarter-wave pieces.** Each piece goes from no motion to the most motion.
  - **Mode 1 holds one piece.**
  - **Mode 2 holds three pieces.**
  - **Mode 3 holds five pieces.**
  - **Mode 4 holds seven pieces.**
  - So with a base of 100 hertz, the modes come at 100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz.
- **Two pieces can never fit, so the steps are always odd.**
  - First, two pieces stacked together give no motion, then the most motion, then no motion again.
  - That puts a still point at the top as well as the bottom, which would make the system **fixed at both ends**.
  - So the next possible mode adds one more piece, for **three** pieces in total, and that ends with motion at the free top again.
  - The simple version is that the free end must always be moving, and only an odd number of quarter-waves ends on a moving point.
- **The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called formants.** A **formant** is a frequency of vibration of a system that is fixed at one end and completely free at the other end. Formants are **odd multiples** of the lowest one, and they are written **F1**, **F2** and **F3**.

## p9
- **Counting quarter-wave pieces gives every formant from the first one.** If **F1 is 500 hertz**, then **F2 is three times that, 1500 hertz**, and **F3 is five times that, 2500 hertz**.
  - Each step between formants is twice F1, because each new mode adds two more quarter-wave pieces.

## p10
- **The air in the vocal tract is a fixed-free system.** The air in the mouth and throat forms a tube.
  - At one end are the **vocal folds**. The vocal folds are closed, so the air there **cannot move back and forth**. That makes this end **fixed**.
  - At the other end, the **lips are open**, so the air can move back and forth freely. That makes this end **free**.
- **The motion of air in the vocal tract is like the motion of a building**, and the math for both is almost exactly the same.
  - So the frequencies of the air are **formants**, which are odd multiples of the lowest one.
  - Speaking changes the stiffness and mass of the air in different parts of the tract. That moves the formants, and listeners hear the movement as different vowels and consonants.

## p11
- **The air in the tube can be modeled as lines of particles.** Each particle stands for billions of air molecules.
  - Where the lines come **very close together and then very far apart**, the air is compressing and expanding. The air is acting like a **spring**.
  - Where the lines **move back and forth** without changing their distance from each other, the air is acting like a **mass**. This is easiest to see at the **free end at the lips**, where the air simply moves in and out.
  - Bunching and spreading means springiness, and sliding without bunching means mass.
- **The modes of the air match the modes of a building.**
  - Mode 1: The only point of no motion is the fixed end. The air near the fixed end compresses a lot, and the air near the lips moves a lot without compressing. A building in Mode 1 is the same, with lots of compression near the ground and lots of motion near the top.
  - Mode 2: There are **two points of no motion**, one at the fixed end and one inside the tube.
  - Mode 3: There are **three points of no motion**. The air around each of those points compresses and expands a lot, so those are the springiest places.
- **In Mode 4 of a building, the most springiness is at the four places that do not move.** These are the fixed bottom and the three points where the building's line crosses its resting position. The places between them move the most and have the least springiness.
  - Raising the stiffness at a place that moves the most changes **nothing**, because the structure is not acting like a spring there.
  - Raising the stiffness at a place that does not move makes the frequency **go up a lot**, because that place is very springy.

## p12
- **Each vowel places its constriction at a different location.** A **constriction** (narrowing) is a location where the tongue or lips narrow the vocal tract. Locations are measured from the **glottis**, the space between the vocal folds at the fixed end.
  - Done: There is **no significant constriction**, and the vocal tract is fairly uniform along its whole length.
  - Dean: The constriction occurs under the roof of the mouth, about **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**, producing a **front high vowel**.
  - Don: The constriction occurs far back in the throat, about **one-third of the way from the glottis**, producing a **low back vowel**.
  - Dune: The constriction occurs **at the lips** because of **lip rounding**, producing a **back high vowel**.
  - Dan: The tongue is positioned low and toward the front of the mouth, producing a **front low vowel**.

## p13
- **A constriction raises both the stiffness and the mass of the air.**
  - Stiffness: **Compressing air raises its stiffness**, the same way compressing the air in a tire makes it stiffer. So a constriction at a location with a lot of compression **raises the frequency**.
  - Mass: **A constriction raises the mass of the air**, because it packs the air into a smaller space and raises its **density**. So a constriction at a location where the air moves a lot **lowers the frequency**.
- **Even though a constriction raises both, it still changes the formants, because moving places and compressing places take turns.** At any one spot, in any one mode, only the extra mass or only the extra stiffness counts.
  - A reliable way to answer any constriction question is to ask two questions in order. First, is the air at this spot mostly moving or mostly compressing in this mode? Second, which change counts there, the extra mass or the extra stiffness?
- **The same two questions answer a question about a string fixed at both ends.** Choose a small spot where **Mode 3 crosses its middle line** while **Mode 4 is at the top of a bump**, and raise both the mass and the stiffness of that spot.
  - Mode 3: The string does not move at that spot but is stretched the most, so only the extra stiffness counts, and **Mode 3's frequency goes up**.
  - Mode 4: The string moves the most at that spot and hardly stretches, so only the extra mass counts, and **Mode 4's frequency goes down**.

## p14
- **Before reasoning about a vowel, it helps to know where the air moves and where it compresses in the first two modes.**
  - Mode 1: The air near the closed glottis end compresses and expands a lot, and the air near the open lip end moves back and forth.
  - Mode 2: The air compresses a lot at the closed end and again at a second place about two thirds of the way along. In between, about one third of the way along, the air mostly moves.
- **The closed end is always springy, and the open end is always moving, in every mode.** Air can be compressed against a closed end, but at an open end there is nothing to compress it against.

## p15
- **The mass-spring chain marks the three vowel locations along the tube.** **/a/** is one third of the way from the glottis, **/i/** is two thirds of the way, and **/u/** is at the lip end. Each vowel adds mass and stiffness at its own spot, and the effect on F1 and F2 depends on whether the air there is moving or compressing in each mode.
- **Adding mass and stiffness at the very top of a building lowers the frequency of every mode.** The top is the free end, so in every mode the top moves the most and is almost not springy. Only the extra mass counts there.
  - **The top of the building matches the lip end of the vocal tract.** That is why rounding the lips lowers every formant.

## p16
- **For /u/, F1 and F2 both go down.** **Lip rounding** creates a constriction at the **free end**. The free end moves a lot and hardly compresses in both Mode 1 and Mode 2, so the extra **mass** counts in both modes.
  - A speaker who went from the central vowel into /u/ showed **both formants dropping** in the recording.

## p17
- **For /i/, F1 goes down and F2 goes up.** For /i/, the constriction is about **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - Mode 1: That spot has lots of motion, so the extra mass counts and **F1 goes down**.
  - Mode 2: That spot has lots of compression, so the extra stiffness counts and **F2 goes up**.
  - A speaker who went from the central vowel into /i/ showed the **first formant dropping a lot** and the **second formant going up**.
  - The same constriction is at a location of motion for F1 and at a location of compression for F2, so one formant falls while the other rises.

## p18
- **For /a/, F1 goes up and F2 goes down, which is the reverse of /i/.** For /a/, the constriction is about **one-third of the way from the glottis**.
  - Mode 1: That spot near the fixed end compresses a lot, so the extra stiffness counts and **F1 goes up**.
  - Mode 2: That spot moves a lot, so the extra mass counts and **F2 goes down**.
- **The reasoning also works backward.**
  - If a constriction raises a formant a lot, the air at that spot must be **very springy and hardly moving** in that mode.
  - If a constriction lowers a formant a lot, the air at that spot must be **moving a lot** in that mode.

## p19
- **A schwa matches the prediction for a uniform tube.** A **schwa** is made with no constriction. A measured schwa had formants near **500, 1500 and 2500 hertz**, which are the odd multiples that a uniform fixed-free tube predicts.
  - Every other vowel moves the formants away from these schwa values by changing where the air moves and where the air compresses.

## p20
- **Speech uses two vibrating systems at the same time, the source and the filter.**
  - The **vocal folds** are a fixed-fixed system. They are the **source**, and they produce **harmonics**. The lowest harmonic is called the **base frequency**, or **F0**. In this example the harmonics are **100 hertz apart**.
  - The **air in the vocal tract** is a fixed-free system. It acts as a **filter**, and its frequencies are the **formants**. For the schwa, the formants are near **500, 1500, 2500 and 3500 hertz**.
- **The formants decide how tall each harmonic is.** All the harmonics are still there. **Harmonics near a formant are tall, and harmonics far from any formant are short.** The outline of the harmonic heights is called the **envelope**, and the envelope has a peak at each formant.
  - **The harmonics stay the same from vowel to vowel. Their heights change, and the envelope is what makes each vowel sound different.**
  - The vocal folds decide which frequencies are present, and the shape of the mouth decides which of those frequencies are loud.

## p21
- **The /i/ filter keeps F1 low and F2 high, so the two formants are far apart.** In the /i/ spectrum, the tall harmonics are bunched at the very bottom. Next comes a long stretch of short harmonics. The next tall group appears much higher up.

## p22
- **The /a/ filter raises F1 and lowers F2, so the two formants are close together.** In the /a/ spectrum, the first two groups of tall harmonics sit close together in the lower part of the spectrum.
  - The same source harmonics are under both /i/ and /a/. Only the filter is different. Moving the constriction moves the formants and changes which harmonics are loud.

## p23
- **A spectrogram shows how the spectrum changes over time.** A **spectrum** shows one instant. Speech changes over time, because the tongue and lips keep moving and constrictions are made and released.
  - **Time** runs along the **horizontal axis**.
  - **Frequency** runs up the **vertical axis**. In a single spectrum, frequency was the horizontal axis.
  - **Amplitude** is shown as **darkness** on the display. The louder a frequency is, the darker it looks.
- **Formants appear as dark bands that move as the speaker moves.** In **/ia/**, the two lowest bands start **far apart** during /i/. As the speaker moves into /a/, **F1 rises and F2 falls**, so the bands **come close together**.
  - Reading a spectrogram means following the dark bands, because the dark bands are the formants changing over time.

## p24
- **"Where were you a year ago?" shows the source and the filter working together over time.** The vocal folds, which are the source, keep producing harmonics. The vocal tract, which is the filter, keeps changing shape. So the envelope over the harmonics changes from moment to moment.
  - On a spectrogram, these changes appear as **dark bands that rise and fall**. The dark bands are the formants, and darkness shows amplitude.
  - Where the vowel sounds like **/i/**, the two lowest bands spread far apart. During "you," all the bands drop because the lips are rounded.
- **A spectrum shows one frozen moment, but a spectrogram shows how the formants move.** Real speech is always moving. Reading a sentence means following each band and asking which constriction moved it.

## p25
- **Formants can only be seen through the harmonics, so the spacing of the harmonics matters.** With harmonics every **100 hertz** and formants at **300 and 2100 hertz**, **many harmonics** sit under each formant peak. The formants clearly shape the heights of many harmonics, so the formants are easy to see.
- **With a higher base frequency, the formants become hard to see.** With harmonics every **250 hertz**, fewer harmonics land on each formant peak. A formant can even fall **between two harmonics**, so **it becomes very hard to tell the harmonics from the formants**.
  - A **toddler's** harmonics can be at 400, 800 and 1200 hertz. That is why listeners can find a toddler's vowels hard to identify unless they know the child.
  - A **soprano's** harmonics can start at 800 hertz and continue at 1600 and 2400 hertz, so the words of an opera singer are often very hard to follow.
- **Formants are the smooth envelope, and harmonics are samples taken from that envelope.** A higher F0 means fewer samples, so the spectrogram stops looking like clean formant bands.
  - A simple way to see it is that the harmonics are like points used to trace a curve. With many points, the curve is clear. With only a few points, the curve is hard to see.
