2|A **dynamic system** changes over time as its parts act on one another, and that idea reaches well beyond springs. Economics has its own dynamic systems. >> **Woodford’s theory**, **Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium** or **DSGE**, models an economy as a dynamic system. >> Its name combines change over time, random shocks and many linked markets that settle toward balance together. || The stabilization picture follows two quantities through time. >> The **black curve** is **unemployment**. >> The **gray curve** is **inflation**. >> Before stabilization, both swing through large, jagged peaks and dips, and afterward the swings are smaller and smoother. || **Stabilization** means making those swings smaller. >> The economy keeps changing, and unemployment and inflation stay two separate quantities. >> The goal is to keep away from harmful extremes such as very high unemployment or runaway inflation. || Physical oscillators and economies share one central question, what drives a fluctuation and what calms it. >> In a spring, inertia and the restoring force drive the swing. >> In an economy, shocks and the responses of its parts drive it. The calmer curves picture the goal of stabilization.

9|The **principles of mass–spring systems** sum up how these systems behave in seven statements. || **Mass and stiffness pull frequency in opposite directions.** >> More mass lowers the frequency, since a heavier load turns around more slowly. >> More stiffness raises the frequency, since a stronger restoring force reverses the motion sooner. || **An n-mass system has n modes**, each with a higher frequency than the last. || **Position decides how much a change matters.** >> Added mass lowers a mode’s frequency in proportion to how much that mass moves in the mode. >> Added stiffness raises a mode’s frequency in proportion to how much that spring stretches or compresses. >> Maximal movement comes with no stretching, and maximal stretching comes with no movement. Neighbors that move together keep their spacing, so motion and compression peak in different places. || One change can therefore matter in one mode and barely register in another. >> It matters where the mode moves or stretches strongly. >> It matters little at a node of that mode, and that same node can move in other modes or in a mixture. || **Endpoints set the harmonic pattern.** >> A **fixed–fixed system** has harmonics at whole-number multiples of the base frequency, because the base shape fits a whole number of times into each higher mode. >> A **fixed–free system** has harmonics at **odd multiples** of the base frequency, because the base shape fits an odd number of times into each higher mode.

11|The **pop quiz** asks what happens to two modes when mass or stiffness is added at one spot on a string, just left of the midpoint. || The two modes use that spot in opposite ways. >> **Mode 4** moves the spot strongly, while its neighbors move along with it, so little stretching happens there. >> **Mode 5** moves the spot very little, while its neighbors move differently, so the string stretches and compresses strongly there. || **Adding mass** acts on movement. >> Mode 4 carries the extra mass through a large swing, so its frequency falls. >> Mode 5 barely moves the spot, so its frequency stays about the same. >> Mass lowers frequency in the modes that move it. || **Adding stiffness** acts on stretching. >> Mode 5 stretches the spot strongly, so the stiffer spring adds restoring force and raises its frequency. >> Mode 4 barely stretches the spot, so its frequency stays about the same. || Adding **both** at that spot **lowers Mode 4 and raises Mode 5**. >> The spot is the same in both modes. >> Its role differs, mainly movement in Mode 4 and mainly stretching in Mode 5, measured over a whole cycle of each mode.

15|The air in the vocal tract behaves like a chain of masses and springs. >> Moving air has **inertia** and keeps moving. >> Squeezed air pushes back, and the spring-like links in the model represent that restoring effect. >> The air elements move **longitudinally**, back and forth along the tube, oscillating around their usual places. || The tube is a **fixed–free** system. >> The **glottis** is the fixed end. >> The open **lips** are the free end. || The lowest two patterns match the lowest two formants. >> **Mode 1 is F1-like**, the tube’s lowest resonance. >> **Mode 2 is F2-like**, with one more reversal along the tube. >> In the uniform tube, Mode 2 runs **three times** as fast as Mode 1, the odd-multiple pattern of a fixed–free system. Changing the tract’s shape changes that ratio. || Each mode is a coordinated pattern of the whole column of air. >> In some places, neighboring elements travel together and keep their spacing. >> In others, their gaps close and open over and over. || Three markers place the vowel constrictions. >> **/a/** is about **one-third** of the way from the glottis. >> **/i/** is about **two-thirds** of the way. >> **/u/** is at the **lips**. >> Each marker is the same physical spot in both modes. Added mass acts where a mode moves strongly, and added springiness acts where it compresses strongly, so one narrowing can push F1 and F2 differently.
16|Vowels are classified by **tongue height** and **front–back position**, and each one narrows the airway at a particular place. Five words sit on a chart of those two dimensions. || The two high vowels narrow the tract at different places. >> **Dean** is **high and front**, with its constriction about **two-thirds** of the way from the glottis to the lips. >> **Dune** is **high and back**, with its constriction at the **lips**. >> They share a height and differ in where they narrow. || The two low vowels sit at the bottom. >> **Dan** is **low and front**. >> **Don** is **low and back**, with its constriction about **one-third** of the way from the glottis. || **Done** is **central** and marked **“No constriction.”** >> It is the open reference shape. || Each label captures its own feature. >> High and low describe tongue height. >> Front and back describe where the tongue sits along the mouth. >> The fraction gives distance along the air path from the glottis. || The constriction’s location ties the articulation to the sound. >> Narrowing the airway changes the local mass-like and spring-like behavior of the air. >> How each formant responds depends on how its mode moves and compresses that spot.

19|The **/u/ model** loads the open lips, the **free end** of the air column. Larger elements at the lips represent more **effective mass**, meaning more air that must be moved back and forth there. || Both lowest modes move strongly at the free end. >> In **Mode 1**, **motion dominates** at the lips. >> In **Mode 2**, **motion dominates** at the lips as well. || A larger moving load takes longer to speed up and reverse. >> Wherever motion dominates, the added load lengthens each cycle. >> Both modes slow, giving **F1 down and F2 down** relative to the uniform tube. || The two rows compare one lip location across two resonance patterns. >> Each pattern is arranged differently inside the tube, yet both move strongly at the lips. >> The extra load slows both while F1 remains the lower formant and F2 the higher one. >> Each change is measured against that mode’s own uniform reference. || The common direction comes from the lip region’s role in both modes. >> Here motion dominates in both, so loading lowers both. >> The air keeps oscillating around its usual positions, with the larger elements marking the added load.
20|The **/i/ constriction** is near the front, about **two-thirds** of the way from the glottis to the lips. One physical region is narrowed, and that region does a different job in each mode. || In **Mode 1**, **mass motion dominates** at the narrowing. >> The air there moves strongly. >> Narrowing adds effective moving mass, which slows the reversal. >> **F1 goes down**. || In **Mode 2**, **springiness dominates** at the narrowing. >> The air there is strongly squeezed and stretched. >> Narrowing strengthens the restoring response, which speeds the reversal. >> **F2 goes up**. || The region stays in one place, and its role changes from mode to mode. >> Where neighbors travel together, moving load is what counts. >> Where neighbors keep pressing together and pulling apart, the restoring response is what counts. || The outcome is **lower F1 and higher F2** than the uniform tube. >> The mass effect governs F1, and the spring effect governs F2. >> One constriction therefore moves the two formants in opposite directions, by two separate causes.
21|The **/a/ constriction** is farther back, about **one-third** of the way from the glottis to the lips. At this spot the two modes trade roles compared with /i/. || In **Mode 1**, **springiness dominates** at the narrowing. >> The air there is strongly compressed. >> Narrowing adds restoring force, so the cycle speeds up and **F1 goes up**. || In **Mode 2**, **mass motion dominates** at the narrowing. >> The air there moves strongly. >> Narrowing adds effective moving mass, so the cycle slows and **F2 goes down**. || Both rows narrow the same back location, and each row is its own coordinated motion. >> In one pattern, the spot moves little while its neighbors squeeze and spread around it. >> In the other, the spot moves a lot while its spacing barely changes. >> That is why springiness governs Mode 1 and moving mass governs Mode 2 here. || The outcome is **higher F1 and lower F2**, the mirror image of /i/. >> Moving the constriction changes which role matters most, while mass and stiffness keep their usual effects. >> The arrows give the predicted direction of each change.

23|Each spot in an air column can be described by its **movement** and its **compression**. >> **Movement** is how far an air element travels. >> **Compression** is how the gap between neighbors changes. Narrower gaps are compression, and wider gaps are **rarefaction**, or thinned-out air. >> Neighbors can travel together with steady spacing, or squeeze and spread around an element that hardly moves. || The animation splits one air column into its patterns. >> The moving **Schwa** row is the uniform reference. >> The **F1, F2 and F3** rows are the first three resonances of that column. >> The closed left end holds the air still, and the open right end lets it move. Higher modes pack more alternating regions of movement and compression into the tube. || Compression and movement play the roles of spring and mass. >> Crowded air pushes back toward normal spacing, which supplies the restoring force. >> Moving air keeps going because of inertia. >> Together they keep the air oscillating around its usual positions. || Strong movement and strong compression occupy different places. >> Where movement is large, the region carries a lot of moving mass. >> Where spacing changes a lot, the region supplies strong restoring force while its central element stays nearly put. || Tracking one element over a cycle measures its movement, and tracking its gaps measures compression. >> Added mass acts where movement is strong. >> Added stiffness acts where compression is strong. >> Those places shift from mode to mode, and each row is its own resonance of the same air.
