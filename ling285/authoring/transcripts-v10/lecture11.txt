2|A mass on a spring **oscillates**, which means it moves back and forth around **equilibrium**, its resting position. Displacing the mass stretches or squeezes the spring, and the spring answers with a force aimed back at equilibrium. >> Because of its **inertia**, the moving mass carries on past equilibrium and displaces the spring on the far side. >> The spring then pushes or pulls the other way, sends the mass back and starts the next cycle. || The mass can travel in two directions. >> **Longitudinal motion** runs along the spring, so the spring lengthens and shortens. >> **Transverse motion** runs across the spring. The two segments are already under tension, and lifting or lowering the mass angles their pull back toward the resting line. || Each side of the cycle has its own restoring direction. >> On one side of equilibrium, the spring acts toward the center from that side. >> After the mass crosses, the displacement reverses and the force reverses with it. Inertia carries the mass across, and the restoring force brings it back, over and over. || The two directions repeat at the same **frequency**, **0.5 Hz**, or one cycle every two seconds. >> They match because both have the same mass and the same total restoring stiffness. >> The model has no damping, so each cycle is as large as the one before.
3|An oscillator’s rate depends on its **mass** and its **stiffness**. Three systems run together, and each comparison changes one property while holding the other fixed. >> The reference has mass **m** and stiffness **K** and runs at **0.5 Hz**. >> Quadrupling the mass to **4m** slows it to **0.25 Hz**. >> Quadrupling the stiffness to **4K** speeds it to **1 Hz**. || **More mass means a lower frequency.** >> A heavier mass takes more force to speed up, slow down and reverse. >> With the same spring, each cycle takes longer, and four times the mass gives half the rate. || **More stiffness means a higher frequency.** >> A stiffer spring pulls back harder for the same displacement. >> The mass reverses sooner and completes more cycles per second, and four times the stiffness gives twice the rate. >> Mass is the system’s resistance to changes in motion, and stiffness is the strength of its return. || The rate is found by counting complete cycles over equal stretches of time. >> **Amplitude**, the distance the mass travels, is a separate property. >> Each longitudinal and transverse pair matches because both share the same **effective restoring stiffness**, meaning the restoring action in the direction of motion. For transverse motion, that stiffness comes from the tension and layout of the spring segments.
4|Two equal masses linked by three equal springs between fixed ends have **two normal modes**. In a **normal mode**, every part of the system moves at one frequency and keeps a fixed relationship to the other parts. || In **Mode 1**, the two masses move in step, in the same direction. >> Their spacing stays nearly constant, so the middle spring barely changes length. >> This mode runs at **0.4 Hz**. || In **Mode 2**, the masses move in opposite directions. >> The middle spring is squeezed and stretched hard on every cycle. >> The added restoring force makes this the faster mode, at about **1.73 times** the frequency of Mode 1. || Each mode is a pattern of the whole chain. >> Both masses move in both modes. >> The masses and springs are identical in the two patterns, and the difference lies in how the masses move relative to each other. || Both modes can run together on the same masses. >> At each mass the two motions add, strengthening where they agree and canceling where they disagree. This is **superposition**. >> The combined motion in the bottom row is the exact sum of the two modes and carries both frequencies. The longitudinal and transverse chains follow the same pattern.

6|Three equal masses with four equal springs between fixed ends have **three normal modes**. || **Mode 1** moves all three masses in the same direction. >> The middle mass swings the farthest, and the outer two swing less. >> This is the lowest mode, at **0.32 Hz**. || **Mode 2** moves the two outer masses in opposite directions. >> The pulls on the middle mass cancel, so it stays at equilibrium. >> A point that stays still throughout a mode is a **node**. >> This mode runs at about **1.85 times** the lowest frequency. || **Mode 3** moves the outer masses together and the middle mass against them. >> Each mass now moves opposite to its neighbor, so every spring stretches and squeezes strongly. >> This is the highest mode, at about **2.41 times** the lowest frequency. || The more neighbors oppose each other, the faster the mode. >> Opposing neighbors stretch the springs more, which strengthens the restoring force and speeds up the cycle. >> All three masses take part in every mode. The colors follow each mass, and the resting marks give the position each displacement starts from. || The frequency ratios are specific to this chain. >> When the modes combine, their motions add at each mass. >> The middle mass moves in the mixture because Modes 1 and 3 move it, even though Mode 2 holds it still.
7|Every movable mass adds one **normal mode** in the modeled direction. The atlas sets chains of one through six masses in rows, with mode numbers in columns, for **21** patterns in all. >> Each chain has equal masses, one more spring than masses and two fixed ends. >> A chain of three masses fills three columns, and a chain of six fills six. || In every chain, **Mode 1** is the broadest pattern, with all masses moving together. >> Each higher mode adds one more group moving the opposite way. >> More reversals put more neighbors in opposition, which stretches the springs more, strengthens the restoring force and raises the frequency. || The fixed ends stay still in every mode. >> A mass that stays still within one mode is a node of that pattern while the others keep moving. >> Higher modes use the very same masses and springs in a different arrangement, and the mode number names the arrangement. || Colors mark the mode number, and rows mark the number of masses. >> Every pattern is drawn with the same largest displacement, so frequency is read from the repetition rate. >> **Amplitude** is how far the parts move, and frequency is how often they repeat. >> Chains of separate masses have frequency spacings that differ from the whole-number spacing of an ideal continuous string.
8|A **fixed–fixed string** is clamped at both ends, which makes both ends **nodes**. >> **Mode 1** fits one half-wave between the ends. >> Modes 2, 3, 4 and 5 fit two, three, four and five half-waves. || Packing more half-waves into the same length shortens the wavelength and raises the frequency. >> The five modes run at **100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz**. >> These are consecutive **harmonics**, whole-number multiples of the **fundamental**, and they include both odd and even multiples. || All five modes can share the string at once. >> Their motions add at every point, so the overall outline keeps changing while each component stays regular. >> A point can go nearly still through cancellation and then move strongly as the modes drift back into step. Every component is present the whole time. || The display is slowed **250** times, so 100 Hz appears at **0.4 Hz**. >> Slowing every mode by the same factor keeps their relative rates intact. || A **pickup** placed **17%** of the way along the string records the motion at that one point. >> Its **spectrum** has one peak for each component, with frequency along one axis and amplitude along the other. >> The mode strengths were set so the five peaks come out equal at this point. A pickup elsewhere would record different heights.
9|A **membrane** is a vibrating surface with fixed edges, and its modes carry two-number labels. The two numbers count the moving regions along the two directions of the surface. >> **(1,1)** is a single region moving as one. >> **(2,1)** and **(1,2)** split the surface in two, one along each direction. >> **(2,2)** makes four regions, and **(3,1)** makes three along one direction. >> Neighboring regions move opposite to each other, separated by **node** lines that stay still. || The five modes add at every point into one changing surface. >> Where they agree they reinforce, and where they disagree they cancel. >> The combined panel uses a smaller vertical scale than the single-mode panels. || A node is still for the whole cycle. >> A moving region passes through the resting level for an instant each cycle and then keeps moving. >> Following one region as it rises, crosses and falls, while its neighbor moves the other way, separates moving regions from nodes. >> The two numbers describe how the surface divides into regions. || A **pickup** at one marked point records its motion, and the **spectrum** there has one peak per mode. >> The strengths were chosen to make the five peaks equal at that point. >> The frequencies **100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 Hz** were **assigned for the illustration** instead of calculated from this rectangle’s own resonances. They model how evenly spaced frequencies combine on a surface.
10|The **scientific method** rests on stating very clear theories about the world, usually as **differential equations**. >> A differential equation describes how the quantities in a system change. >> Solving it yields a **prediction** of what a real measurement should find. || The theory here treats the **vocal folds as a membrane**. >> A vibrating membrane produces a wave whose spectrum has **equidistant peaks**, called **harmonics**. >> The equations are of the same kind as those for springs and membranes, with somewhat more detail. || The prediction follows directly. >> Any person’s **laryngeal vibration** signal should have a spectrum with **equidistant harmonics**, peaks separated by equal intervals. >> Testing it means recording real vocal-fold vibration and examining the spacing of the peaks. Even spacing supports the theory, and uneven spacing challenges it. || The spectrum is the part of the signal that carries the prediction. >> Vibration alone is expected either way, so the evenly spaced peaks are what the experiment checks. >> The membrane animation uses assigned harmonic frequencies, so it pictures the idea, while a recording of real vocal folds tests it. The experiment is proposed here, ready to be carried out.
11|A **fixed–free system** is held at one end and free at the other. >> The fixed end must be a **node**, with no movement. >> The free end must be an **antinode**, the place of largest movement. || Shapes that meet both conditions fit odd numbers of **quarter-waves**. >> The first mode fits one quarter-wave. >> The next fit three, five and seven quarter-waves. >> These are the **first, third, fifth and seventh harmonics**, so the second allowed mode is the third harmonic. || Odd counts are the only ones that leave the fixed end still and the free end moving. >> An even count would put a node at the free end. >> Higher modes fit shorter waves into the same length, so they repeat faster. || The four modes run at **100, 300, 500 and 700 Hz**, slowed **250** times for display. >> Given equal strengths, they produce four equal peaks in the spectrum measured at the free end. >> An antinode swings through the resting line to the opposite side on every cycle, while the fixed end stays still. || The fixed–fixed and fixed–free strings were **tuned separately** to share a fundamental. >> With the same length and wave speed, freeing one end would cut the fundamental in half. >> The matching labels therefore come from separate tuning, and the effect of the free end is to halve the fundamental.
