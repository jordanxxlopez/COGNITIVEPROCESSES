4|On a **fixed–fixed string**, both ends are held, so both ends are **nodes** in every mode, and every allowed shape fits a whole number of **half-waves** between them. || **Mode 1** fits **one half-wave**. >> The string rises and falls as one broad region. >> This mode sets the **fundamental**, the lowest frequency. || **Mode 2** fits **two half-waves**. >> A still point forms at the center. >> The two halves move in opposite directions. || **Modes 3, 4 and 5** fit **three, four and five half-waves**. >> Each added half-wave shortens the **wavelength**. >> Shorter waves at the same speed repeat faster. >> Every mode holds a whole number of base half-waves, so every frequency is a **whole-number multiple** of the fundamental, odd and even alike. || Numbered sections label the half-wave packets of each shape. >> Section one appears in all five modes, section two in Modes 2 through 5, and so on up to section five in Mode 5 alone. >> The sections stay fixed in place while the string moves up and down. >> Higher harmonics hold more half-wave packets in the same length, which is exactly why they are higher.

8|A **quarter-wave** runs from a **node** to an **antinode**. On the **fixed–free string**, the bottom is fixed and the top is free. >> The fixed bottom must be a node. >> The free top must be an antinode. || **One quarter-wave** meets both conditions and gives the **fundamental**. >> Movement starts at zero at the bottom and grows to its largest at the top. || Adding quarter-waves in pairs keeps both conditions in place. >> Each pair adds one more reversal inside the string. >> The four allowed modes hold **one, three, five and seven quarter-waves**. || More sections in the same length shorten the wavelength and raise the frequency. >> The four modes are the **first, third, fifth and seventh harmonics**. >> The second mode is the third harmonic, the third mode the fifth and the fourth mode the seventh. || Odd counts are the only ones that end on an antinode at the free top. >> An even count would end on a node there, which a free end rules out. || Numbered pieces label the quarter-wave sections. >> Higher modes contain the extra pieces, and pieces six and seven belong to the fourth mode alone. >> The pieces mark fixed positions in the standing pattern while the string moves.
9|The **pop quiz** asks for the quarter-wave count of each **fixed–free pattern**, read straight from its endpoints. >> The fixed bottom is a **node**. >> The free top is an **antinode**. >> Each step from node to antinode, or back, covers **one quarter-wave**. || The four patterns hold **one, three, five and seven** quarter-waves. >> Each holds two more than the one before. >> Packing more sections into the same length shortens the wavelength, so the higher patterns repeat faster. >> The numbered divisions stay in fixed positions while the material moves. || Each pattern carries two different numbers. >> **Mode number** counts the allowed patterns in order. >> **Harmonic number** compares a pattern’s frequency with the fundamental. >> Only odd counts fit both ends, so the two numbers separate. >> The fourth allowed mode is the **seventh harmonic**.

13|A **constriction** narrows the airway and raises two properties of the air at once. || It raises **stiffness**. >> Compressed air resists further compression more strongly. >> At a place where the air is **heavily compressed**, a constriction **raises the frequency**. || It raises **mass**. >> Bunching air up increases its density. >> At a place where the air **moves a lot**, a constriction adds moving mass and **lowers the frequency**. || Both increases happen together, and each one acts in a different region. >> The parts of a vibrating medium that **move the most** alternate with the parts that **compress and expand the most**. >> Each mode places those regions at its own positions. || The way to tell the effects apart is to follow the air through a cycle. >> One element’s travel measures how much mass is being carried there. >> The opening and closing of the gaps beside it measures compression, the source of the restoring force. >> Watching both through a cycle reveals which effect governs that spot in that mode. || One narrowing can therefore act mainly through **mass** for one formant and mainly through **stiffness** for another. >> One formant falls and the other rises. >> Both rules hold exactly as before, mass lowering frequency where air moves and stiffness raising it where air compresses.

20|**Source–filter theory** splits speech into a **source**, the vibration of the vocal folds, and a **filter**, the vocal tract that shapes that vibration. || The glottal source produces a **harmonic comb**. >> It repeats at a **fundamental frequency**, **F0**, with harmonics at whole-number multiples of F0. >> In the example, the harmonics sit **100 Hz** apart and run up to **4000 Hz**. >> Before the tract acts, they are evenly spaced vertical lines of equal height. || The tract filter **sculpts the heights** of the harmonics. >> Harmonics near the tract’s resonances, the **formants**, grow stronger, and the rest grow weaker. >> Every line keeps its position, and only its height changes. >> The outline of those heights is the **spectral envelope**, and that envelope is the vowel. || In the schwa example, four resonance regions, **F1 through F4**, shape the comb, with the strongest group at low frequency. || The horizontal axis gives frequency, and the vertical axis gives amplitude. >> A taller line is a stronger component. >> **F0** is the source’s repetition rate, and **F1** is the tract’s lowest resonance. >> The vowel is made of many harmonics, with the formants deciding which of them are strong.
21|The **/i/ filter** combines a **low F1** with a **high F2**, and it acts on the same 100 Hz comb. >> One strong group of harmonics sits near the low end. >> The next strong group sits far higher, past a weak stretch. >> More strong groups follow around **F3** and **F4**, near **3000** and **3600 Hz**. || The source lines keep their even spacing and positions. >> The tract changes only their heights. >> The filter’s effect therefore stands apart from the source’s repetition. || This low-F1, high-F2 pattern comes from the front constriction of /i/. >> The narrowing adds mass where Mode 1 moves and stiffness where Mode 2 compresses. >> F1 drops and F2 climbs, which pushes the two formants far apart. || Each line is read in two ways. >> Its horizontal position is its **frequency**. >> Its height is its **amplitude**. >> A tall line near the low end is a strong low-frequency component. || The harmonic lines sample the broad formant regions. >> The envelope traced by their heights is the vowel /i/ in this schematic.
22|The **/a/ filter** has a **higher F1** and a **lower F2** than /i/. Here **F1 is at 750 Hz** and **F2 near 1200 Hz**, so the first two formants sit close together. >> **F3** and **F4** stay higher, near **2600** and **3500 Hz**. >> The source is unchanged, and the new tract shape makes a new vowel. || The filter reshapes the harmonic **strengths**. >> Moving a formant shifts the favored region along the frequency axis. >> Changing a harmonic’s amplitude changes its height. >> The spectrum records these as two separate kinds of change. || A formant can sit **between two source harmonics**. >> The 750 Hz F1 target falls between the **700** and **800 Hz** harmonics. >> Those two nearby harmonics become strong, and the source supplies no line exactly at 750 Hz. || A **formant** is a resonance region of the filter. >> A **harmonic** is a frequency component of the source. >> The filter decides how strong each harmonic is, and the harmonics reveal where the formants are.
23|A **spectrum** shows **one instant**, and a **spectrogram** **stacks many spectra over time**. || A spectrum describes the frequency components of one short slice of sound. >> Frequency runs across, and amplitude runs up. >> The example uses three successive spectra of **/i/** and three of **/a/**. || A spectrogram arranges those slices from left to right in time. >> Its frequency axis runs upward, so a peak at the right side of a spectrum becomes a mark high in the spectrogram. >> Moving right in a spectrogram means moving later in time. || **Height in the spectrum becomes darkness in the spectrogram.** >> In grayscale, **black is high amplitude, gray is medium and white is low**. >> Vertical position gives frequency, and darkness gives strength. >> A darkening band is growing stronger, and a rising band is moving to higher frequency. || From **/i/** to **/a/**, **F1 rises and F2 falls**. >> The two lowest bands draw toward each other. >> The higher bands stay above them with smaller shifts. >> The spectrogram tracks the tract’s resonances as they change over time.

25|Formants are easiest to identify when the source harmonics are **close together**. Each harmonic samples the tract’s response, and many closely spaced samples trace each formant clearly. || With harmonics at **100, 200, 300, 400 Hz** and onward, a vowel with **F1 at 300** and **F2 at 2100 Hz** shapes the amplitudes of many harmonics. >> Several lines cluster around each formant. >> Their heights rise and fall with the filter, so each formant region stands out. || A higher **F0** spaces the harmonics farther apart. >> With harmonics at **250, 500, 750** and onward, fewer lines fall near each formant. >> A formant can sit between two harmonics, and one strong line can sit near a formant without marking its center. >> Telling harmonics from formants becomes very hard. || Higher voices space the harmonics wider still. >> A **toddler’s** harmonics could be **400, 800** and onward. >> A **soprano’s** could be **800, 1600, 2400** and onward. >> Across the three spectra and spectrograms, closely spaced components give way to a few isolated traces. || The filter itself is unchanged. >> The tract keeps the same resonances. >> The source now offers fewer frequencies to reveal that same filter.
