**Section 1: The Vocal Folds as the First Vibrating System**

- Speech uses **two main vibrating systems**. The first is the **vocal folds**, a **membrane** (a thin sheet of tissue) that is **fixed on all sides** and made of many masses and springs. The vocal folds vibrate in many modes at once. In Mode 1, all the masses move up and down together. In Mode 2, half move up while the other half move down. Higher modes have more complicated patterns. All five modes add together into one combined motion, and this is what actually happens in the throat while a person talks.
  - Harmonics: Because the vocal folds are a fixed-fixed system, their frequencies are **harmonics**, which are whole-number multiples of the lowest one.
  - F0: The **lowest harmonic** is called **F0**. Each person has a different F0, but the other harmonics are always its multiples.

**Section 2: The Air in the Vocal Tract as the Second Vibrating System**

- The second system is the **air in the vocal tract**. It is **fixed at the glottis**, because the closed vocal folds prevent the air there from moving back and forth, and it is **free at the lips**, where the air can move in and out. Its modes follow the fixed-free pattern, with **one, three, five and seven quarter-wave packets**, so its frequencies are the **odd multiples** of the lowest one.

- **In every mode, the air at the closed end is always springy, and the air at the open end is always moving.** Air can be compressed against a closed end, but at an open end there is nothing to compress it against.
  - Closed end: The thin layers of air crowd very close together and then spread far apart, so the air **compresses and expands** and acts like a **spring**.
  - Open end: The layers stay equally far apart and move back and forth together, so the air acts like a **mass**.
  - Higher modes: Springy locations and moving locations **alternate** more and more times along the tube.
  - Comparison: The lip end swings like a heavy door, and the throat end compresses like a pillow.

**Section 3: Reasoning About a Constriction in One Mode**

- The effect of a constriction can be worked out at any location, in any mode, with the same chain of reasoning. Consider a location inside the tube where, in **Mode 3**, the layers of air crowd together and spread apart but hardly move back and forth. A constriction at that location raises **F3**.
  - Step 1: A constriction always **increases both the mass and the stiffness of the air**, just as compressing the air in a tire makes it stiffer.
  - Step 2: At this location in Mode 3, the air is acting like a **pure spring**. It is compressed and expanded but does not move back and forth, so the added mass does not matter.
  - Step 3: Only the added stiffness matters, and **increased stiffness raises the frequency**, so **F3 goes up**.

- Each new location and mode requires the same steps. The answer should not be memorized. It should be **built one step at a time**, with each step based on the one before it. Good arguments in science and in art are built the same way.

**Section 4: Vowels Place Constrictions in Different Locations**

- Images taken while people speak show where constrictions (narrowings) occur in the vocal tract, which extends from the closed glottis to the open lips.
  - Done: There is no significant constriction in the vocal tract.
  - Dean: The constriction occurs near the front of the vocal tract, about two-thirds of the way from the glottis.
  - Don: The constriction occurs toward the back of the throat, about one-third of the way from the glottis.
  - Dune: The constriction occurs at the lips because of lip rounding.
  - Dan: The tongue is positioned low and toward the front of the mouth, producing a low front vowel.

- In the mass-spring model of the tube, a constriction is drawn as **heavier masses and stiffer springs** at that location. A constriction always raises both mass and stiffness, but moving locations and springy locations alternate, so **its effect depends on what the air is doing at that location in each mode**.

**Section 5: /u/, /i/ and /a/**

- For **/u/**, **lip rounding** creates a constriction at the lips. At the open end, the air moves back and forth with hardly any compression in **every** mode, so the air there acts like a pure mass. The added stiffness has no effect, but the added mass does, so **F1 and F2 both go down**.
  - Measurement: Compared with the schwa's reference values of about **500 and 1500 hertz**, a measured **/u/** had an F1 in the low two hundreds, well below 500.

- For **/i/**, the constriction is about two-thirds of the way from the glottis.
  - Mode 1: The air at that location moves back and forth, so it acts like a mass. The added mass matters, and **F1 goes down**.
  - Mode 2: The air at that location is compressed and expanded, so it acts like a spring. The added stiffness matters, and **F2 goes up**.
  - Measurement: A speaker moving from the schwa into /i/ produced exactly this pattern, with **F1 going down and F2 going up**.

- For **/a/**, the constriction is about one-third of the way from the glottis.
  - Mode 1: That location is close enough to the closed end that the air there is mostly springy. The added stiffness matters, and **F1 goes up**.
  - Mode 2: That location lies between two springy regions, so the air there is mostly moving. The added mass matters, and **F2 goes down**.
  - Measurement: A speaker producing /a/ showed **F1 going up and F2 going down**. In a typical /a/, F1 and F2 are fairly close together.

- **When stiffness is what matters at a location, the frequency goes up, and when mass is what matters, the frequency goes down.**

**Section 6: The Source Sets the Harmonic Spacing**

- The vocal folds are the **source**, and their mass and stiffness set **F0** and therefore the spacing of the harmonics.
  - Large, heavy, loose vocal folds: They have high mass and low stiffness, so **F0 is low**, around 100 hertz, with harmonics at 200, 300 and so on.
  - Tighter, lighter vocal folds: F0 is higher, for example 250 hertz, with harmonics at 500 and 750.
  - Very tight, light vocal folds: F0 is higher still, for example 350 hertz, with harmonics at 700 and 1050.

- **F0 does not stay constant while people talk.** Only a robot keeps F0 constant. Speakers keep changing F0 by tightening and loosening the vocal folds with the muscles of the larynx, and listeners use those changes to understand meaning.
  - Statement: When **The dog slept here** is said as a statement, F0 falls at the very end.
  - Question: When the same words are said as a question, F0 rises at the end. The way F0 moves also tells listeners whether a speaker is being sarcastic, pretending, or even lying.

- F0 can be measured in **Praat** by selecting a short stretch of speech, viewing its spectrum, and identifying the lowest harmonic. One measured voice had an F0 of about 170 hertz, and the next harmonic was at about twice that value, as the theory predicts.

**Section 7: The Filter Selects the Formants**

- The air in the vocal tract is the **filter**, and its formants depend on **where the constriction is**. Typical values show the patterns.
  - Schwa: F1 is near **500** hertz and F2 is near **1500** hertz.
  - /u/: F1 is near 300, F2 near 1000 and F3 near 2000, so all of them are lower.
  - /i/: F1 is near 300 and F2 is near 2200, so they are very far apart.
  - /a/: F1 and F2 are both near 800 to 900 hertz, so they are very close together.

- **The core rule is that a formant strengthens the harmonics closest to it, while harmonics far from that formant are weakened.** During a schwa, harmonics near 500 hertz are amplified, and the farther a harmonic is from 500, the weaker it becomes. Between two formants the harmonics become weaker, and then they grow stronger again near the next formant.
  - **The vowel shape is the filter. The vocal folds supply the harmonics, and the filter determines which harmonics come through strongly.**

**Section 8: One Filter Can Look Different at Different Pitches**

- **The lower F0 is, the more harmonics the formants can shape.** With heavy, loose vocal folds and harmonics every 100 hertz, many harmonics sit under each formant, so the shaped spectrum follows the formant peaks smoothly and is easy to read.

- With very light, tight vocal folds, the harmonics are far apart, and a formant at 500 hertz may have **no harmonic near it to amplify**. With so few harmonics, a formant can fall between them and be missed, or its position can be hard to identify.
  - Opera singer: This is an extreme case. The harmonics are so far apart that the formants do not line up with any harmonic, so listeners struggle to hear which vowel is being sung. Singers are even trained to shape the vocal tract so that the formants line up with the harmonics.
  - Small child: A child of two or three has an F0 near 500 hertz, so very few harmonics fall near the formants. Unless a listener cares for the child and is used to the child's speech, it is very hard to tell what the child is saying.
  - The formants are the same in every case, but a high voice gives the listener only a few harmonics through which to hear them.

**Section 9: Spectrograms of Real Speech**

- A **spectrogram** shows how speech changes over time. **Time** is on the horizontal axis, **frequency** is on the vertical axis, and **darkness** shows how much energy, or amplitude, is present.

- In a recording of **Where were you a year ago?**, the formants are never still.
  - /i/-like vowel: F2 rises high and F1 stays low, because the constriction is at a location that is springy in Mode 2 and moving in Mode 1.
  - you: The formants drop, because lip rounding forms a constriction at the lips, which lowers all of them.
  - End of the sentence: F2 goes up and then down again as the vowels change. **All the formants are constantly changing during speech.**

- **The formants are always changing during speech, so speech is not a sequence of frozen vowels.** Every rise and fall of a formant can be predicted with the same mass and spring reasoning, by identifying the location of the constriction and whether the air there is moving or being compressed in each mode.
  - This constant change is why a network that sees two formants from one frozen moment cannot understand real speech, and why speech recognition requires systems that take in whole stretches of sound over time.
  - A spectrogram of a real sentence is a continuous record of the tongue and lips, and reading it means following the dark bands as they rise and fall.

**Section 10: Speech Perception Integrates Over Time**

- A recording that was cut and joined back together shows how complex speech perception is. A speaker says **ma** and **na**. The **m** portion of ma is cut out and joined to the **a** portion of na. Because the consonant portion came from ma, the result might be expected to sound like ma, but **listeners clearly hear na**.

- **Whether listeners hear m or n depends more on the vowel than on the nasal sound itself.** The formants of the vowel differ depending on the consonant before it.
  - After m: One formant of the vowel stays fairly **flat**.
  - After n: That formant shows a **big drop** at the start of the vowel.
  - Result: The vowel portion carries the information about which consonant was produced, so listeners decide which consonant they heard **during the vowel**, after the consonant has ended.

- This was one of the first striking results in speech perception, published in the **1950s** by the linguist **André Malécot**. Listeners do not decide on one slice of sound, then the next, then the next. They **take in whole stretches of speech and then decide** about the sounds that came first by using the sounds that follow them.
  - For about seventy years, speech recognition systems that treated each slice of sound separately **failed**, despite billions of dollars of effort.
  - A system that understands speech must be complex enough to use future sound to decide about the present, which is why modern speech recognition relies on large language models built from many neural networks.

**Section 11: Formants as Input to a Vowel Recognizer**

- A **neural network** can be trained to recognize vowels from formants. Its **two inputs** are **F1 and F2**, measured from real recordings of five vowels, and its task is to decide which vowel was spoken. **The network's knowledge is stored in its weights**, which are learned from the data.

- The network has **five output neurons**, one for each word, **done, dune, dean, Don and Dan**. Between the input and the output it has **three hidden layers**, with **3, 2 and 4 neurons**.
  - Input layer: Information arrives at the input, somewhat like sound arriving at the ear.
  - Hidden layers: Each hidden layer turns the numbers it receives into a new set of numbers, somewhat like the stages of processing in the brain.
  - Output layer: The output layer gives the network's decision about which vowel it heard.

- Every neuron in one layer connects to every neuron in the next, so the network is **fully connected**. This version is **purely linear**, which means it has **no activation functions**, so each neuron simply passes on the result of its inner product.

**Section 12: Random Weights Give Signed Contributions**

- Before training, **every weight is a random number between minus one and one**. A **positive** weight makes its input push the next neuron up, and a **negative** weight makes its input push the next neuron down.

- The example input is **F1 of 320 and F2 of 2000 hertz**. A very low F1 with a very high F2 is the pattern of **/i/**, so the correct answer is **dean**.

**Section 13: Forward Flow and the Output Distribution**

- **Forward propagation** carries the input through the network one layer at a time. Each neuron computes an **inner product**, which means multiplying each incoming number by its weight and adding the results.
  - First hidden layer: For the top neuron, F1 is multiplied by its weight, F2 is multiplied by its weight, and the two products are added to give that neuron's activation.
  - Later layers: The other neurons do the same with their own weights, so the first hidden layer produces three numbers. Those numbers pass through the next weights to produce two numbers, then four, then five at the output.
  - These calculations never have to be done by hand. What matters is that every step is an inner product.

- The output is a **probability distribution** over the five words, with values of **.5, .1, .1, .1 and .2**, which add up to one. The network gives its highest probability to **done**, so with random weights it is an **untrained network** that gets the answer wrong.
  - Target: The correct answer is given as the target distribution **0, 0, 1, 0, 0**, with all of the probability on dean.
  - Learning: **Stochastic gradient descent** then changes each weight, so that the next time the network sees 320 and 2000, the inner products lead to a high probability for dean.
  - The network starts out guessing, the target shows what the correct guess was, and learning adjusts every weight so the correct guess wins next time.

**Section 14: The Same Network in PyTorch**

- **PyTorch** describes the network as a list of layers.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

net = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Linear(2, 3, bias=False),
    nn.Linear(3, 2, bias=False),
    nn.Linear(2, 4, bias=False),
    nn.Linear(4, 5, bias=False),
)
```

- **Sequential** means the network has **one layer after another, in order**. **Linear** means the layer **computes inner products** of the weights and the activations from the previous layer.
  - nn.Linear(2, 3): The line reads as **take input from 2 neurons, and have 3 neurons**. The next line takes input from those 3 and has 2, then 2 in and 4 out, then 4 in and 5 out.
  - Bias set to False: Turning off the **bias** keeps every layer purely linear, which matches the diagram of the network. This code only describes the network and does not train it.

- **Only layers that perform calculations get a line of code.** The **input layer** has no incoming weights and computes no inner products, because it only takes in numbers. The input layer therefore never gets its own line, and the first line describes the **first hidden layer**.
  - Total layers: This network has **five layers in total**, which are the input layer, three hidden layers and the output layer.
  - Hidden layers: It has **three hidden layers**, and the **last line is always the output layer**.
  - "How many layers" and "how many hidden layers" are different questions, so the question must be read carefully.

- Changing the network means changing the numbers in the lines. To **add a hidden layer of 3 neurons right before the output**, the first three lines stay the same. The old last line, 4 in and 5 out, becomes **4 in and 3 out**, and a new last line takes **3 in and gives 5 out**. Read aloud, the network becomes 2 in and 3 out, 3 in and 2 out, 2 in and 4 out, 4 in and 3 out, and 3 in and 5 out.
  - Quick check: The first number on each line must match the second number on the line before it, because each layer takes its input from the layer before it.

- The same reading works for any network written this way. A network described as 4 in and 3 out, 3 in and 8 out, 8 in and 4 out, 4 in and 4 out, and 4 in and 5 out takes **4 numbers as input**, has **four hidden layers** with 3, 8, 4 and 4 neurons, and chooses among **5 categories**, one for each output neuron.
  - Diagram: Drawn as a network, it has a column of **4 input neurons**, then hidden columns of **3, 8, 4 and 4 neurons**, then a column of **5 output neurons**, with every neuron connected to every neuron in the next column.
  - Target distribution: When the first category is correct, the answer places a **1 in the first slot and 0 everywhere else**.
