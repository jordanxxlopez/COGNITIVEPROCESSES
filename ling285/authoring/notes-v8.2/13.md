**Section 1: Principles of Mass-Spring Systems**

- Every vibrating system in speech follows a small set of principles, and the principles are consistent with each other.
  - Mass and stiffness: **Increasing the mass lowers the frequency, and increasing the stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - Number of modes: **A system with n masses has n modes**, and each higher mode has a higher frequency, because more parts move against each other and more springs are stretched and compressed.
  - Added mass: **Adding mass at a location lowers the frequency more when that location moves more.** A mass that does not move has no effect, a mass that moves a little has a small effect, and a mass that moves a lot has a large effect.
  - Added stiffness: **Adding stiffness at a location raises the frequency more when that location is stretched and compressed more.** Both of these effects depend on where the change is made.
  - Motion and stretching: **Where movement is greatest, there is no stretching, and where stretching is greatest, there is no movement.**
  - Harmonics and formants: In a **fixed-fixed system**, the frequencies are called **harmonics**, and they are **whole-number multiples** of the base frequency. In a **fixed-free system**, the frequencies are called **formants**, and they are **odd multiples** of the base frequency.

- These principles prevent a common mistake. If both the mass and the stiffness of a system are increased, it might seem that the two effects simply **cancel out**. That is true only for a **single mass on a single spring**. In a system with many masses and springs, a location that moves a lot is hardly stretched, and a location that is stretched a lot hardly moves, so only one of the two changes matters at that location.
  - Picture a spot on a string that never moves but is strongly stretched. Added mass there has no effect, while added stiffness raises the frequency, so the frequency goes up.
  - Picture a spot that moves up and down the most. Added stiffness there has no effect, while added mass slows it down, so the frequency goes down.

**Section 2: More Masses, More Modes**

- Adding masses adds modes, and the same shapes keep repeating. In **Mode 1**, everything moves together. In **Mode 2**, the system splits into two halves that move against each other. In **Mode 3**, the middle moves against the sides. The more the parts move against each other, the more the springs are stretched and compressed, so **higher modes are stiffer and vibrate faster**.
  - A higher mode does not use stiffer springs. It makes the same springs stretch more.

**Section 3: Why Fixed-Fixed Frequencies Are Whole-Number Multiples**

- In a string fixed at both ends, the first mode is **one half-wave**, a single bump that goes from no motion at one end, to maximum motion in the middle, to no motion at the other end. Each higher mode is built by fitting **more of these half-wave packets** into the same length.
  - Mode 2: The mode **contains two packets**, with one half moving up while the other half moves down. Each packet behaves like a copy of Mode 1, so Mode 2 has **twice the stiffness** and **twice the frequency**.
  - Mode 3 and Mode 5: Mode 3 **contains three packets**, and Mode 5 **contains five**, so the fifth mode has five times the frequency of the first.
  - Result: The frequencies increase in equal steps. If the first harmonic is 100 hertz, the next ones are 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz.

- The numbers do not have to start at 100. A voice whose base frequency is **250 hertz** has its next harmonics at 500, 750 and 1000 hertz, because each harmonic is still a whole number of copies of the first.
  - Exam answer: **The fifth mode contains five copies of the first mode**, so it has five times the stiffness and five times the frequency.

**Section 4: Harmonics of Strings and Membranes**

- A string fixed at both ends vibrates in all of these modes at once. Its **spectrum**, with **frequency** on the horizontal axis and **amplitude** (strength) on the vertical axis, has **evenly spaced spikes**, one for each harmonic. The **vocal folds** are fixed on all sides, just behind the Adam's apple, so they behave like a fixed-fixed system and produce harmonics.

- The vocal folds form a **membrane** (a thin sheet of tissue), and the same packet counting works on a surface.
  - First mode: A single bump spans the whole membrane.
  - Second mode: Two bumps fit across the membrane, so the frequency is twice as high.
  - Fourth mode: Four ups and downs fit across the membrane, so the frequency is four times as high.

**Section 5: One Fixed End and One Free End**

- A **building** is **fixed at the base** and **free at the top**. In strong winds or an earthquake, engineers want it to sway only in **Mode 1**, with every floor moving together. That motion can feel slightly unpleasant on a high floor, but it is safe. In a higher mode, some floors would move one way while other floors moved the other way, and the whole structure could break apart.

- A string can be set up the same way, fixed at the bottom and free at the top. Its first mode is no longer a full half-wave. It goes from **no motion at the fixed end** to **maximum motion at the free end**, which is only **one quarter of a wave**.

**Section 6: Why Fixed-Free Frequencies Are Odd Multiples**

- In a fixed-free system, each mode is built from **quarter-wave packets** (pieces that are each a quarter of a wave long), and each packet runs from no motion to maximum motion. **Mode 1 contains one packet**, **Mode 2 contains three**, **Mode 3 contains five** and **Mode 4 contains seven**.
  - Result: With a base of 100 hertz, the modes are at **1, 3, 5 and 7 times 100 hertz**, which are 100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz.

- **Two packets can never fit.** Two quarter-wave packets would go from no motion, to maximum motion, to no motion again. That would place a point of no motion at the top as well as the bottom, which would make the system **fixed at both ends**, not fixed-free. Adding one more packet, for **three** in total, ends with maximum motion at the top again, so three is the next possible mode.
  - In short, the free end must always be moving, and only an odd number of quarter-wave packets ends with motion at the free end.

- The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called **formants**. **A formant is a frequency at which a system vibrates when it is fixed at one end and completely free at the other.** Formants are **odd multiples** of the lowest one.
  - F1: This label names the first formant.
  - F2: This label names the second formant.
  - F3: This label names the third formant.

**Section 7: Counting Quarter-Waves**

- Counting quarter-wave packets gives every formant from the first one. If **F1 is 500 hertz**, then **F2 is three times F1, which is 1500 hertz**, and **F3 is five times F1, which is 2500 hertz**.
  - Each step from one formant to the next is twice the first formant, because each new mode adds two more quarter-wave packets.

**Section 8: The Air in the Vocal Tract Is a Fixed-Free System**

- The air inside the mouth and throat forms a tube with two different ends.
  - Glottis end: The **vocal folds** are closed, so the air there **cannot move back and forth**, and that end is **fixed**.
  - Lip end: The **lips are open**, so the air can move back and forth freely, and that end is **free**.
  - Comparison: The air in the vocal tract is therefore a fixed-free system, like a building. **The air in the vocal tract vibrates the same way a building sways**, and the mathematics describing them is almost identical.
  - Its frequencies are therefore **formants**, which are odd multiples of the lowest one. Speaking changes the stiffness and mass of the air at different locations in the tract, which moves the formants. That is how listeners hear different vowels and consonants.

**Section 9: Compression and Motion Alternate in an Air Column**

- The air in the tube can be modeled as lines of particles, where each particle represents billions of air molecules.
  - Spring behavior: Where the lines **crowd very close together and then spread far apart**, the air is compressing and expanding, which means it is acting like a **spring**.
  - Mass behavior: Where the lines **move back and forth** without changing their distance from each other, the air is acting like a **mass**. This is easiest to see at the **free end, at the lips**, where the air simply moves in and out.
  - Crowding and spreading indicate springiness, and sliding without crowding indicates mass.

- The modes of the air match the modes of a building.
  - Mode 1: The only point of no motion is the fixed end. The air near the fixed end is strongly compressed, and the air near the lips moves a lot without being compressed. A building in Mode 1 behaves the same way, with a lot of compression near the ground and a lot of motion without stretching near the top.
  - Mode 2: There are **two points of no motion**, one at the fixed end and one inside the tube.
  - Mode 3: There are **three points of no motion**. The air around each of them is strongly compressed and expanded, so those are the springiest locations.

- The same reasoning answers questions about a building's higher modes. In **Mode 4** of a building, **springiness is greatest at the fixed base and at the three points where the building crosses its resting position**, four locations in all, because those points do not move. The locations between them move the most and have the least springiness.
  - Location of maximum movement: Raising the stiffness there **changes nothing**, because the structure is not acting like a spring there.
  - Location of no movement: Raising the stiffness there makes the frequency **rise sharply**, because that location is very springy.

- The same two-step reasoning answers a question about a string fixed at both ends. Consider a small spot where **Mode 3 crosses its middle line** while **Mode 4 is at the top of a bump**, and increase both the mass and the stiffness of that spot.
  - Mode 3: The string does not move at that spot but is stretched the most, so only the added stiffness matters, and **Mode 3's frequency goes up**.
  - Mode 4: The string moves the most at that spot and is hardly stretched, so only the added mass matters, and **Mode 4's frequency goes down**.

- Adding mass and stiffness at the **very top of a building** lowers the frequency of **every mode**, including Mode 4. The top is the free end, so it has the most motion and almost no springiness in every mode. Only the added mass matters there, and added moving mass lowers the frequency. **The top of the building corresponds to the lip end of the vocal tract.**

**Section 10: Vowel Shapes Locate Constrictions**

- A **constriction** (narrowing) is a location where the tongue or lips narrow the vocal tract. Locations are measured from the **glottis**, the space between the vocal folds at the fixed end.
  - Done: There is **no significant constriction**, and the vocal tract is fairly uniform along its whole length.
  - Dean: The constriction occurs under the roof of the mouth, about **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**, producing a **front high vowel**.
  - Don: The constriction occurs far back in the throat, about **one-third of the way from the glottis**, producing a **low back vowel**.
  - Dune: The constriction occurs **at the lips** because of **lip rounding**, producing a **back high vowel**.
  - Dan: The tongue is positioned low and toward the front of the mouth, producing a **front low vowel**.

**Section 11: Why a Constriction Can Raise One Formant and Lower Another**

- **Compressing air raises its stiffness.** Compressing the air in a tire makes the tire stiffer, so a constriction at a location where the air is strongly compressed **raises the frequency**.

- **Packing air together raises its mass**, because compressing it into a smaller volume raises its **density**. A constriction at a location where the air moves a lot therefore **lowers the frequency**.

- Every constriction therefore **adds both mass and stiffness**, but that does not mean constrictions have no effect on frequency. **Along a vibrating string or a vibrating column of air, the locations that move the most alternate with the locations that are compressed and expanded the most.** At any one location, in any one mode, either the mass or the stiffness is the change that matters.
  - Any constriction question can be answered in two steps. First, determine whether the air at that location is mostly moving or mostly being compressed in the mode in question. Second, decide whether the added mass or the added stiffness matters there.

**Section 12: /u/, /i/ and /a/**

- For **/u/**, **lip rounding** creates a constriction at the **free end**. The air at the free end moves a lot and is hardly compressed in both Mode 1 and Mode 2, so the added **mass** matters in both. **F1 and F2 both go down.**
  - Measurement: A speaker who moved from the central vowel into /u/ produced exactly this pattern, with **both formants dropping** in the recording.

- For **/i/**, the constriction is about **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - Mode 1: The air at that location moves a lot, so the added mass matters and **F1 goes down**.
  - Mode 2: The air at that location is strongly compressed, so the added stiffness matters and **F2 goes up**.
  - Measurement: A speaker who moved from the central vowel into /i/ showed the **first formant dropping sharply** and the **second formant rising**.
  - The same constriction is at a location of motion for F1 and at a location of compression for F2, so one formant falls while the other rises.

- For **/a/**, the constriction is about **one-third of the way from the glottis**, which reverses the /i/ pattern.
  - Mode 1: That location is near the fixed end, where the air is strongly compressed, so the added stiffness matters and **F1 goes up**.
  - Mode 2: The air at that location moves a lot, so the added mass matters and **F2 goes down**.

- The reasoning also works in reverse. **If a constriction raises a formant a lot, the air at that location must be very springy and hardly moving in that mode.** **If a constriction lowers a formant a lot, the air at that location must be moving a lot.**

- A measured **schwa**, produced with no constriction, had formants near **500, 1500 and 2500 hertz**, which are the odd multiples that a uniform fixed-free tube predicts.

**Section 13: The Source Supplies Harmonics and the Filter Shapes Their Heights**

- Speech uses **two vibrating systems at the same time**.
  - Source: The **vocal folds** are a fixed-fixed system, so they produce **harmonics**. The lowest harmonic is called the **base frequency**, or **F0**, and the others are its multiples. In this example, the harmonics are **100 hertz apart**.
  - Filter: The **air in the vocal tract** is a fixed-free system, so its frequencies are the **formants**. For the schwa, the formants are near **500, 1500, 2500 and 3500 hertz**.

- **The formants determine the heights of the harmonics.** All the harmonics are still present, but **harmonics near a formant are tall, and harmonics far from any formant are short**. The outline of the harmonic heights, called the **envelope**, rises to a peak at each formant.
  - **The harmonics stay the same from vowel to vowel, but their heights change, and the envelope is what makes each vowel distinct.**
  - In other words, the vocal folds determine which frequencies are present, and the shape of the vocal tract determines which of them are loud.

**Section 14: The /i/ and /a/ Filters**

- For **/i/**, the filter keeps **F1 low and F2 high**. The tall harmonics are grouped at the very bottom of the spectrum, a long stretch of short harmonics follows, and the next tall group appears much higher up. The two lowest formants are therefore **far apart**.

- For **/a/**, the filter **raises F1 and lowers F2**. The first two groups of tall harmonics are **close together** in the lower part of the spectrum, and the higher formants appear farther up.

- The same harmonics from the source are present under both vowels, and only the filter differs. Moving the constriction moves the formants and changes which harmonics are loud.

**Section 15: A Spectrogram Places Successive Spectra in Time**

- **A spectrum describes one instant.** Speech, however, is not one frozen vowel. The vocal tract keeps moving, and constrictions keep forming and releasing, so speech is **time-varying** (it changes over time).

- **A spectrogram places many spectra side by side over time.**
  - Time: Time is on the **horizontal axis**.
  - Frequency: Frequency, which was on the horizontal axis of each spectrum, is now on the **vertical axis**.
  - Amplitude: Amplitude, which was the height of each spike, is now shown as **darkness**. The louder a frequency is, the darker it appears.

- The formants appear as **dark bands** that move as the speaker moves. In the sequence **/ia/**, the two lowest bands start **far apart** during /i/, with F1 low and F2 high. As the speaker moves into /a/, **F1 rises and F2 falls**, so the bands **come close together**.
  - Reading a spectrogram means following the dark bands, because those bands are the formants changing over time.

**Section 16: A Sentence Over Time**

- **Where were you a year ago?** is a useful sentence for seeing the source and the filter working together over time. The vocal folds, as the source, keep producing harmonics, while the vocal tract, as the filter, keeps changing shape, so the envelope over the harmonics changes from moment to moment.
  - Spectrogram: These changes appear as **dark bands that rise and fall**, because the dark bands are the formants and darkness shows amplitude.
  - Vowels: Where the vowel sounds like **/i/**, the two lowest bands spread far apart. During **you**, all the bands drop because lip rounding forms a constriction at the lips.
- **A spectrum shows one frozen instant, but a spectrogram shows how the formants move**, and real speech is always moving. Reading a sentence like this one means following each band and asking which constriction moved it.

**Section 17: Wide Harmonic Spacing Makes Formants Harder to Identify**

- Formants can only appear through the harmonics, so the spacing of the harmonics matters. With harmonics every **100 hertz** and formants at **300 and 2100 hertz**, there are **many harmonics** under each formant peak, so the formants clearly **shape the amplitudes** of many harmonics and are easy to see.

- With a higher base frequency, the harmonics are spaced farther apart, for example every **250 hertz**. Fewer harmonics land on each formant peak, and a formant can fall **between two harmonics**, so **it becomes very hard to tell the harmonics apart from the formants**.
  - Toddler: A **toddler's** harmonics can be at 400, 800 and 1200 hertz, which is why listeners can find a toddler's vowels hard to identify unless they know the child.
  - Soprano: A **soprano's** harmonics can start at 800 hertz and continue at 1600 and 2400, so the words of an opera singer are often very hard to follow.

- **The formants form a smooth envelope, and the harmonics are samples of it** (single points along that outline). A higher F0 means fewer samples, so the spectrogram no longer shows clean formant bands.
  - The harmonics work like the points used to trace a curve. With many points, the curve is obvious, and with only a few points, the curve is hard to see.
