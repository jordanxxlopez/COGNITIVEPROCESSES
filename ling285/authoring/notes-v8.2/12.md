**Section 1: Dynamic Systems in the Economy**

- The **2026 Nobel Prize in economics** was awarded to **Robert Hall** and **Michael Woodford** for the **microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization**. **Stabilization** means returning a system to its equilibrium and keeping it there. This is the same role that **balancing feedback** plays, and the same role a spring plays when it pulls a mass back to rest.
  - In their model of the economy, **unemployment and inflation rise and fall in turn**, like the two curves of an oscillator. One curve behaves like the position, which starts high, becomes negative and returns. The other behaves like the velocity, which is shifted relative to the position.
  - The goal of stabilization policy is to **reduce the size of these rises and falls**, so that the economy avoids very high unemployment and runaway inflation.

- Woodford's theory is called **Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium**, or **DSGE**. Each word in the name has a meaning that carries over from dynamical systems.
  - Dynamic: The economy is a system that changes over time according to rules.
  - Stochastic: The model can handle **random shocks**, such as the start of a war, a factory fire or a large company's CEO being caught embezzling. This is similar to stochastic gradient descent, where one unusual data point can push the estimate far off, but later data pull it back toward the average.
  - Equilibrium: The goal is a stable state that people can live in, without large, sudden jumps.

- **Macroeconomic** and **microeconomic** describe two levels of the same economy.
  - Macroeconomic: These quantities describe a whole economy, such as the inflation rate or the number of people who are unemployed.
  - Microeconomic: These events are single choices, such as one person buying something.
  - Explaining large patterns from many small events is the same kind of problem as speech. In speech, many small vibrations in the vocal tract add up to a meaning that a listener understands without noticing any single vibration.

**Section 2: Masses, Springs and Their Modes**

- A mass-spring system vibrates because **the spring tends to return to equilibrium**, while **a moving mass tends to keep moving**. The vibration can occur **longitudinally** (back and forth along the spring) or **transversely** (up and down between two walls), and both kinds of motion behave the same way. Both are useful, because the vocal folds are easiest to understand with transverse motion, while the air in the vocal tract is easiest to understand with longitudinal motion.
  - Mass: **Increasing the mass lowers the frequency**, so the system completes fewer cycles per second.
  - Stiffness: **Increasing the stiffness raises the frequency**, so the system completes more cycles per second.

- A system with **two masses** has **two modes** (ways of vibrating). It can also vibrate in both at once, with the masses rising and falling together while also moving against each other.
  - Mode 1: The masses move together, and **Mode 1 is slower** because the middle spring is carried along without being stretched or compressed, so in practice the system uses **only two springs**.
  - Mode 2: The masses move against each other, and **Mode 2 is faster** because all **three springs** are stretched and compressed, and more working springs means **more stiffness** and a **higher frequency**.
  - Stiffer middle spring: Making the **middle spring stiffer** leaves **Mode 1 unchanged**, because that spring is never stretched in Mode 1. The same change makes **Mode 2 rise**, because in Mode 2 the middle spring is stretched and compressed throughout the motion.

- A system with **three masses** has **three modes**. In Mode 1, all three masses move together. In Mode 2, the middle mass stays still while the outer two move against each other. In Mode 3, the middle mass moves against both outer masses. The more the springs are stretched and compressed, the higher the mode's frequency.
  - Mode 1 and Mode 3: Making **Mass 2**, the middle mass, heavier **lowers the frequency of Mode 1 and Mode 3**, because the middle mass moves in both modes.
  - Mode 2: The same change has **no effect on Mode 2**, because in Mode 2 the middle mass does not move.
  - **A mass affects the frequency only through its movement.** A mass that does not move has no effect, a mass that moves a little has a small effect, and a mass that moves a lot has a large effect.

**Section 3: More Masses and the String**

- **Each added mass adds one more mode**, and the same patterns repeat as the chain gets longer.
  - Mode 1: Everything moves up and down together.
  - Mode 2: The system splits into **two parts** that move against each other. With an odd number of masses, such as three, five or seven, the middle mass stays still in Mode 2.
  - Mode 3: The system splits into **three parts**, with the middle moving one way and the two sides moving the other way.
  - Higher modes have more parts moving against each other, so the springs are stretched and compressed much more, and those modes vibrate faster.

- Adding more and more masses and springs produces a **string**, like the string of a guitar or the strings inside a piano. **Every small piece of a string has weight, which is its mass, and every small piece resists being stretched or compressed, which is its springiness.** The same mode patterns appear in the string, with everything moving together in Mode 1, the two halves moving against each other in Mode 2, and the middle moving against the sides in Mode 3.
  - In musical instruments, these frequencies are heard as **notes**.
  - The ear can pick out the notes of many instruments at the same time, because the ear itself is built of masses and springs that respond to the masses and springs vibrating in the world.

**Section 4: Harmonics of a String Fixed at Both Ends**

- A string fixed at both ends with its first mode at **100 hertz** has its next modes at **200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz**. Every mode is an **exact multiple** of the first, and these multiples are called **harmonics**.

- The multiples come from **how many copies of the first mode fit into each higher mode**. The first mode is a single bump that swings from a maximum to a minimum and back.
  - Second mode: The mode contains **two** of those bumps, with one side moving up while the other moves down, so it has twice the frequency.
  - Third and fifth modes: The third mode contains **three** bumps, and the fifth contains **five**, so each mode's frequency is the first frequency multiplied by the number of copies.
  - Example: If the first mode is 100 hertz, the fifth mode is 500 hertz.

- A real string vibrates in **many modes at once**, and their sum produces an irregular-looking motion, much like the waveform of speech. A single mode on its own is a **pure tone**, which sounds so harsh that it is used for sirens. Everyday sounds contain many modes at the same time.

- A **spectrum** shows this mixture. **Frequency** is on the horizontal axis, and **amplitude** (how much vibration there is) is on the vertical axis. For this string, the spectrum has spikes at 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz, which means all five frequencies are present in the sound.

- The same reasoning predicts what happens when one piece of the string is made **heavier**, for example by thickening the exact middle of the string.
  - Mode 1: The middle moves a lot, so the added mass **lowers** the frequency.
  - Mode 2: The middle does not move, so the added mass has **no effect**.
  - Mode 3 and Mode 4: In Mode 3 the middle moves again, so the frequency drops. In Mode 4 the middle is still again, so the frequency does not change. Moving up through the modes, the effect **alternates** between lowering the frequency and having no effect.

- **Motion and stretching alternate along a vibrating string.** Where a piece of the string moves the most, it is carried up and down and is hardly stretched. Where the string is stretched the most, such as right next to a fixed end, there is **no movement**. Along the string, these locations keep alternating.
  - **A useful shortcut is that springiness is greatest at the points of no movement.** In **Mode 5** of a string fixed at both ends, the string does not move at the **two fixed ends** or at the **four points where it crosses its middle line**, so those **six locations** have the most springiness.
  - The reverse question has the reverse answer. The **most motion**, and therefore the **least springiness**, occurs at the tops of the **five bumps**.

**Section 5: Principles of Mass-Spring Systems**

- All of these results follow from a short set of principles. They are not separate facts to memorize, because the principles are consistent with each other.
  - Mass and stiffness: **Increasing the mass lowers the frequency, and increasing the stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - Number of modes: **A system with n masses has n modes of vibration**, and each higher mode has a higher frequency.
  - Added mass: **Adding mass at a location lowers the frequency more when that location moves more.** How much a location moves depends on where it is.
  - Motion and stretching: **Where movement is greatest, there is no stretching, and where stretching is greatest, there is no movement.**
  - Added stiffness: **Adding stiffness at a location raises the frequency more when that location is stretched and compressed more.** This also depends on where the location is.
  - Fixed-fixed system: The harmonics are **multiples of the base frequency**, because each mode contains a whole number of copies of the base mode.
  - Fixed-free system: The frequencies are **odd multiples of the base frequency**, because each mode contains an odd number of copies of the base mode.

**Section 6: A Local Change Affects Two Modes Differently**

- Changing one small piece of a string can move two modes in **opposite directions**. Consider a location where **Mode 4 moves the most** and where **Mode 5 crosses its middle line**, so that Mode 5 does not move there. Both the **mass** and the **stiffness** of that small piece are increased.
  - Mode 4: The piece is moving, so the added mass **lowers** the frequency. The piece is barely stretched, so the added stiffness has no effect.
  - Mode 5: The piece does not move, so the added mass has no effect. The piece is stretched the most, so the added stiffness **raises** the frequency.
  - Result: The same change **lowers Mode 4's frequency and raises Mode 5's frequency**. Each mode is worked out separately by asking whether the location is moving or stretching in that mode.

- **Perturbation theory** predicts how a small change in one location affects how something vibrates. It was first worked out with **drums**, which are also made of countless masses and springs. In the late nineteenth century, before cameras could capture such fast motion, researchers placed small amounts of **wax** on different parts of a drum.
  - When wax was placed where the drum was predicted to vibrate a lot, the extra mass made the drum vibrate more slowly, causing its frequency (pitch) to **drop**.
  - When wax was placed where the drum was predicted not to move, it had **no effect** on the sound.

**Section 7: The Vocal Folds as a Membrane**

- The **larynx** contains the **vocal folds**, which form a **membrane** (a thin sheet of tissue) similar to a drum, so they have many modes of vibration. In Mode 1, all the masses move up and down together. In Mode 2, the two halves move against each other. Higher modes divide the surface into more and more parts.

- The **scientific method** depends on clear theories, usually written as **differential equations**, whose solutions produce predictions that can be measured. The theory here is that the vocal folds vibrate in many modes at once, so a measured voice should have a spectrum with **equidistant harmonics** (evenly spaced harmonics).
  - Measurement: A voice was recorded and analyzed in **Praat**. Its lowest harmonic was about 130 hertz, and the next harmonics were close to two and three times that value. **The measurement matched the prediction.**
  - Each person's lowest frequency is different, but the harmonics above it are always close to multiples of it.

- Changes to the mass and stiffness of the vocal folds change the voice. Heavy smokers can develop **nodules** (small growths) on their vocal folds. A nodule adds mass at one location and changes the stiffness of the tissue around it. That changes how the folds vibrate, which is part of why a heavy smoker's voice sounds different.

**Section 8: One Fixed End and One Free End**

- A **building** is a system that is **fixed at one end and free at the other**, because its base is fixed to the ground while its top can sway in strong winds or an earthquake.
  - Mode 1: Structural engineers design buildings to absorb that motion in **Mode 1**, with the whole building swaying together. On a high floor this can feel unpleasant, but it is safe.
  - Higher modes: Engineers design buildings never to vibrate in a higher mode, where some floors would move one way and other floors the other way, because that would break the structure.

- The **air inside the vocal tract** is also a fixed-free system. At the glottis, the **vocal folds are closed**, so the air cannot flow past them and **cannot move**, which makes that end **fixed**. At the lips, the air can move in and out freely, so that end is **free**. Unlike a building, the air in the vocal tract vibrates in **all of its modes**. Speaking changes the mass and stiffness of the air at different locations, which is how listeners identify which vowel, consonant and word is being produced.

- A fixed-free system has a different spectrum from a fixed-fixed one. **Its frequencies increase in odd steps, at 100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz**, instead of including every multiple.
  - First mode: The mode goes from **no motion** at the fixed end to **maximum motion** at the free end, which is only a quarter of a full wave.
  - Next modes: The next possible mode contains **three** of these quarter-wave pieces, so its frequency is three times the first. The mode after that contains **five**, so its frequency is five times the first.
  - Even multiples: Two pieces would end with no motion at both ends, which is a fixed-fixed pattern, so even multiples never occur.

**Section 9: Air in a Fixed-Free Tube**

- The air in the vocal tract can be modeled as a **chain of masses and springs** that is fixed at the glottis and free at the lips. The masses represent the air's inertia (its tendency to keep moving), and the springs represent its springiness.
  - First formant: The lowest mode of this chain corresponds to the **first formant**, **F1**.
  - Second formant: The next mode corresponds to the **second formant**, **F2**. The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called **formants**, while the frequencies of a fixed-fixed system are called harmonics.
  - The second mode vibrates **three times faster** than the first, because it contains three of the first mode's quarter-wave pieces.
  - The masses near the free end move very freely in both modes.

- Three locations along the tube are important for vowels.
  - /a/: The constriction is **one-third of the way from the glottis**.
  - /i/: The constriction is **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - /u/: The constriction is at the **free end, at the lips**.

**Section 10: Vowel Shapes and Constrictions**

- Images taken while people speak show where constrictions (narrowings) occur in the vocal tract, which extends from the closed glottis to the open lips. A **constriction** is a location where the tongue or lips narrow the tract. The **glottis** is the space between the vocal folds at the bottom of the tract, so distances are measured from that fixed end.
  - Done: There is **no significant constriction** in the vocal tract, so air flows freely along its whole length.
  - Dean: The constriction occurs under the roof of the mouth, about **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**, producing a **high front** vowel.
  - Don: The constriction occurs far back in the throat, about **one-third of the way from the glottis**, producing a **low back** vowel.
  - Dune: The constriction occurs **at the lips** because of **lip rounding**, producing a **high back** vowel.
  - Dan: The tongue is positioned low and toward the front of the mouth, producing a **low front** vowel.

**Section 11: What a Constriction Does to the Air**

- **A constriction increases both the stiffness and the mass of the air.** Compressing air makes it **stiffer**, in the same way that compressing the air in a tire makes the tire stiffer. A constriction also packs the air into a smaller volume and raises its **density**, which makes the air more **massive** and harder to move.

- It might seem that raising mass and stiffness together would **cancel out**, because more mass lowers the frequency and more stiffness raises it. **That is false** for any system with many masses and springs. It is true only for a single mass on a single spring.
  - In a system with many masses and springs, each location is either mostly **moving** or mostly **stretching** in a given mode.
  - Location with no motion and a lot of stretching: The added mass has no effect, so only the added stiffness matters, and the frequency **rises**.
  - Location with a lot of motion and no stretching: The added stiffness has no effect, so only the added mass matters, and the frequency **falls**.
  - Speech itself proves that the effects do not cancel, because constrictions at different locations clearly change the frequencies of the voice.

**Section 12: /u/ Loads the Moving Lip End**

- For **/u/**, **lip rounding** creates a constriction at the **free end**, which raises the mass and the stiffness of the air there.
  - Mode 1: The air at the free end moves a lot and is hardly compressed, so **motion matters most**, and the added mass lowers **F1**.
  - Mode 2: The air at the free end also moves a lot, so **motion matters most** again, and the added mass lowers **F2**.
  - Result: The added stiffness has no effect in either mode, because the air at the lips is moving, not stretching. **Both F1 and F2 go down.**

- This was tested by having a speaker start from the central vowel, whose formants were near 500, 1500 and 2500 hertz, and then move into **/u/**. **Both formants dropped**, as predicted, because the rounded lips added mass to the air exactly where it moves the most.

**Section 13: /i/ Has Opposite Effects on F1 and F2**

- For **/i/**, the constriction is about **two-thirds of the way from the glottis**, and that one location behaves differently in the two modes.
  - Mode 1: The air at this location moves a lot and is hardly compressed, so **motion matters most**. The added mass **lowers F1**.
  - Mode 2: The air at this location is strongly compressed, so **springiness matters most**. The added stiffness **raises F2**.
  - Result: The same constriction pushes the two formants apart, with **F1 going down and F2 going up**.

- This was tested by having a speaker go from the central vowel into **/i/**. In the recording, the **first formant dropped sharply** and the **second formant rose**, as the mass-spring model predicted.

**Section 14: /a/ Reverses the Two Effects**

- For **/a/**, the constriction is about **one-third of the way from the glottis**, and the pattern is reversed.
  - Mode 1: This location is near the fixed end, where the air is strongly compressed, so **springiness matters most**. The added stiffness **raises F1**.
  - Mode 2: The air at this location moves a lot, so **motion matters most**. The added mass **lowers F2**.
  - Result: For **/a/**, **F1 goes up and F2 goes down**, the opposite of /i/, so the two formants move closer together.

- The general rule works in both directions. **If a constriction raises a formant a lot, the air at that location must be springy and hardly moving in that mode**, so the added stiffness is what mattered. **If a constriction lowers a formant a lot, the air at that location must be moving a lot**, so the added mass is what mattered.

**Section 15: The Principles Applied to Air**

- The same principles of mass-spring systems explain the vowels. **Increasing the mass lowers the frequency, and increasing the stiffness raises the frequency**, but each change matters only where it can act. **Added mass matters where the air moves, and added stiffness matters where the air is compressed and expanded.** Because the vocal tract is fixed at the glottis and free at the lips, its formants are **odd multiples** of the lowest one, since each higher mode contains an odd number of copies of the quarter-wave base mode.

**Section 16: Compression and Motion Alternate in an Air Column**

- The air column can be modeled as **lines of air particles**, where each particle represents billions of real air molecules. The closed vocal folds are at the **fixed end**, and the open lips are at the **free end**.
  - Compression: Where the lines of particles crowd together and spread far apart, the air is **compressing and expanding**, which indicates **springiness**.
  - Motion: Where the lines move back and forth without changing their spacing, the air is acting as a **mass**. This is easiest to see at the **free end**, where the air simply moves in and out.
  - Fixed end: The air there cannot move, but it is strongly compressed and expanded.

- The three lowest modes of the air match the modes of a building. **F1** has one point of no motion, at the fixed end. **F2** has two, one at the fixed end and one inside the tube, and **F3** has three. Between every pair of no-motion points, locations of maximum motion and locations of maximum compression alternate.

- For the **schwa**, the central vowel with no constriction, the tube behaves like a uniform fixed-free system, so F2 should be three times F1 and F3 should be five times F1. A measured schwa had formants near **500, 1500 and 2500 hertz**, which are the odd multiples the theory predicts.

**Section 17: Where Were You a Year Ago?**

- The question **Where were you a year ago?** applies all of the constriction rules at once. Each sound places a constriction at a different location, so the formants move up and down continuously instead of staying at fixed values.
  - /i/-like sound: The tongue forms a constriction near the front of the mouth, so **F1 goes down and F2 goes up**. That location is a location of motion in Mode 1 and a location of compression in Mode 2.
  - you: **Lip rounding** forms a constriction at the lips, so **F1 and F2 both go down**. The air at the free end is moving in every mode.
  - /a/-like sound: The tongue moves back and down, so **F1 rises and F2 falls**. That location is a location of compression in Mode 1 and a location of motion in Mode 2.
- **Speech is a continuous series of constrictions, not a list of separate vowels**, so the frequencies of the air in the vocal tract are always changing. The same principles of masses and springs predict which way each formant moves at every moment.
