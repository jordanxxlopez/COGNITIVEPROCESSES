## p2
- **Speech uses two vibrating systems, and the first one is the vocal folds.** The **vocal folds** are a **membrane**, which is a vibrating sheet. The vocal folds are **fixed on all sides** and are made of many masses and springs.
  - The vocal folds vibrate in many modes at once. In Mode 1, all the masses move up and down together. In Mode 2, half move up while the other half move down. Higher modes make more complicated patterns.
  - All the modes add together into one combined motion. This combined motion is what actually happens in the throat while a person talks.
- **Because the vocal folds are fixed-fixed, their frequencies are harmonics.** **Harmonics** are whole-number multiples of the lowest frequency.
  - The lowest harmonic is called **F0**. Each person has a different F0, but the other harmonics are always multiples of that person's F0.

## p3
- **The second vibrating system is the air in the vocal tract, which is fixed at one end and free at the other.**
  - The air is **fixed at the glottis**, because the closed vocal folds keep the air there from moving back and forth.
  - The air is **free at the lips**, where it can move in and out.
  - A fixed end and a free end allow only the **odd harmonics**, so the frequencies of the air are one, three, five and seven times the lowest frequency.

## p4
- **Each fixed-free mode is built from quarter-wave pieces.** Each piece goes from no motion at a still point to the most motion at a moving point.
  - **Mode 1 holds one piece, Mode 2 holds three pieces, Mode 3 holds five pieces, and Mode 4 holds seven pieces.**
  - Two pieces would end with a still point at the free end, which is impossible. So only odd numbers of pieces fit, and that is why the formants are odd multiples of the first formant.

## p5
- **In the vocal tract, the air moves along the tube, not up and down.** This back-and-forth motion along the tube is **longitudinal** motion. Each formant is one mode of this longitudinal motion. F1 is the lowest mode, and F2 is the next mode.
  - The modes look like a building's modes turned on their side. The fixed glottis end is on the left, and the free lip end is on the right.

## p6
- **The closed end is always springy, and the open end is always massy, in every mode.** Air can be squeezed against a closed end. Air has nothing to squeeze against at an open end.
  - Near the **closed end**, the thin layers of air come very close together and then far apart. The air **compresses and expands**, so it acts like a **spring**.
  - Near the **open end**, the thin layers of air stay the same distance apart and just move back and forth. The air acts like a **mass**.
  - In higher modes, springy places and massy places **take turns** more and more times along the tube.
  - In plain terms, the lip end swings like a heavy door, and the throat end squeezes like a pillow.

## p7
- **Any constriction question can be answered with the same three steps.** Take a spot inside the tube where, in **Mode 3**, the lines of air bunch up and spread apart but hardly move back and forth. Squeezing the tract at that spot raises **F3**.
  - The first step is that squeezing air always **increases both its mass and its stiffness**, the same way squeezing a tire makes it stiffer.
  - The second step is that, at this spot in Mode 3, the air is acting like a **pure spring**. It compresses and expands but does not move back and forth, so the extra mass does not matter.
  - The third step is that only the extra stiffness counts, and **raising stiffness raises frequency**, so **F3 goes up**.
- **The answer is built one step at a time, not memorized.** Each new spot and each new mode needs the same steps. Each step rests on the step before it, which is how any good argument is built, in science and in art.

## p8
- **Images taken during speech show where each vowel squeezes the air.** The vocal tract is a tube from the closed glottis to the open lips.
  - The vowel in **done** has **no constriction** at all.
  - **dean** squeezes the air near the front, about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - **Don** squeezes the air far back in the throat, about **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - **dune** squeezes the air **at the lips** by rounding them.
  - **Dan** is a low front vowel.

## p9
- **A constriction changes both the inertia of the air and its stiffness.** **Inertia** is the air's resistance to being moved, which is its mass. In the mass-spring model, a constriction is drawn as **heavier masses and stiffer springs** at that spot.
  - A constriction always raises both mass and stiffness. Moving places and springy places take turns, so **the effect depends on what the air is doing at that spot in each mode**.
  - **When stiffness is what matters at a spot, the frequency goes up. When mass is what matters, the frequency goes down.**

## p10
- **The same spot in the tube can act differently in the two modes, so each mode must be checked separately.** A spot can be massy in Mode 1 and springy in Mode 2. Then one constriction lowers F1 and raises F2 at the same time.
  - That is why a vowel is identified by the pair of formants, not by one formant alone.

## p11
- **For /u/, F1 and F2 both go down.** /u/ squeezes the air at the lips. At the open end, the air moves back and forth with almost no compression in **every** mode, so the air there acts as a pure mass. The extra stiffness does not matter, and the extra mass does.
  - The schwa has reference values of about **500 and 1500 hertz**. A measured /u/ had an F1 in the low two hundreds, well below 500.

## p12
- **For /i/, F1 goes down and F2 goes up.** /i/ squeezes the air about two thirds of the way from the glottis.
  - In **Mode 1**, the air at that spot moves back and forth, so it acts like a mass. The extra mass counts, and **F1 goes down**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the air at that spot compresses and expands, so it acts like a spring. The extra stiffness counts, and **F2 goes up**.
  - A speaker going from schwa into /i/ showed exactly this, with **F1 going down and F2 going up**.

## p13
- **For /a/, F1 goes up and F2 goes down.** /a/ squeezes the air about one third of the way from the glottis.
  - In **Mode 1**, that spot is close enough to the closed end that the air is mostly springy. The extra stiffness counts, and **F1 goes up**.
  - In **Mode 2**, that spot sits between two springy regions, so the air there mostly moves. The extra mass counts, and **F2 goes down**.
  - A speaker producing /a/ showed **F1 going up and F2 going down**. In a typical /a/, F1 and F2 are fairly close together.

## p14
- **All three vowels follow one rule. Find whether the air at the constriction mostly moves or mostly compresses in each mode, then decide whether the extra mass or the extra stiffness counts.**
  - At the lips, the air is massy in both modes, so /u/ lowers both formants.
  - Two thirds along the tube, the air is massy in Mode 1 and springy in Mode 2, so /i/ lowers F1 and raises F2.
  - One third along the tube, the air is springy in Mode 1 and massy in Mode 2, so /a/ raises F1 and lowers F2.

## p15
- **The vocal folds are the source, and their mass and stiffness set F0 and the spacing of the harmonics.**
  - **Big, heavy, loose vocal folds** have a large mass and a small stiffness, so their **F0 is low**, around 100 hertz, with harmonics at 200, 300 and so on.
  - **Tighter, lighter vocal folds** have a higher F0, such as 250 hertz, with harmonics at 500 and 750 hertz.
  - **Very tight, light vocal folds** have an even higher F0, such as 350 hertz, with harmonics at 700 and 1050 hertz.
- **F0 keeps changing while people talk.** Only a robot keeps F0 constant. Speakers change F0 by tightening and loosening the vocal folds with the muscles in the larynx, and listeners use those changes to understand meaning.
  - When **"The dog slept here"** is a statement, F0 falls at the very end. When the same words are a question, F0 rises at the end.
  - The way F0 moves also tells listeners whether a person is being sarcastic, pretending, or even lying.
- **F0 can be measured in Praat.** Take a short piece of speech, view a spectral slice, and pick the lowest harmonic. One measured voice had F0 around 170 hertz, and the next harmonic was at about twice that value, as the theory predicts.

## p16
- **The air in the vocal tract is the filter, and the formants depend on where the constriction is.** Typical values show the patterns.
  - **Schwa** has F1 near **500** and F2 near **1500** hertz.
  - **/u/** has F1 near 300, F2 near 1000 and F3 near 2000 hertz, so all of them are lower.
  - **/i/** has F1 near 300 and F2 near 2200 hertz, so they are very far apart.
  - **/a/** has F1 and F2 both near 800 to 900 hertz, so they are very close together.
- **The main rule is that a formant makes the nearby harmonics loud, and harmonics far from any formant are muted.** During a schwa, harmonics near 500 hertz are loud. The farther a harmonic is from 500, the weaker it is. Between two formants the harmonics get weaker, and near the next formant they get stronger again.
  - **The vowel shape is the filter. The voice supplies the harmonics, and the filter chooses which harmonics survive.**

## p17
- **The lower F0 is, the more harmonics the formants can shape.** Heavy, loose vocal folds give harmonics every 100 hertz. Many harmonics sit under each formant, so the shaped spectrum follows the formant peaks smoothly and is easy to read.
- **With very light, tight vocal folds, the harmonics are far apart, so a formant can be missed.** A formant at 500 hertz may have **no harmonic near it to make loud**. Sparse harmonics can miss a formant or make it unclear.
  - An **opera singer** is an extreme case. The harmonics are so far apart that the formants do not line up with any harmonic, so listeners struggle to hear which vowel is being sung. Singers are even trained to shape the mouth so the formants line up with the harmonics.
  - A **small child** of two or three has an F0 near 500 hertz, so very few harmonics fall near the formants. A child's speech is very hard to understand unless the listener is a caretaker who is used to it.
  - A simple way to see it is that the formants are the same in every case, but a high voice gives the listener only a few harmonics to hear them through.

## p18
- **A spectrogram shows how speech changes over time.** **Time** runs along the horizontal axis. **Frequency** runs up the vertical axis. **Darkness** shows how much energy, or amplitude, there is.
  - Each moment of the spectrogram is one spectrum turned on its side, and the spectra are lined up one after another in time.

## p19
- **"Where were you a year ago?" brings every idea about speech together in one short sentence.** The vocal folds supply harmonics the whole time. Every new sound changes where the tongue and lips squeeze the air, so the formants keep moving from one moment to the next.
  - On a spectrogram of the sentence, **time** runs along the bottom, **frequency** runs up, and the **dark bands are the formants**.
  - Where the vowel sounds like **/i/**, **F2 rises high and F1 stays low**, because the squeeze is at a spot that is springy in Mode 2 and massy in Mode 1.
  - During "you," the formants **drop**, because rounding the lips adds mass right where the air moves the most, and that lowers all of them.
  - Through "a year ago," F2 goes up and then down again as the tongue moves from one vowel to the next.
- **All the formants keep changing during speech, so speech is not a string of frozen vowels.** Every rise and fall of a formant can be predicted with the same mass-spring steps. Find the constriction, then check whether the air there is moving or compressing in each mode.
  - This constant change is why a network that sees two formants from one frozen moment cannot understand real speech. Speech recognition needs systems that take in whole stretches of sound over time.
  - In plain terms, a spectrogram of a real sentence is a moving record of the tongue and lips, and reading it means following the dark bands as they rise and fall.

## p20
- **Listeners identify a consonant from the vowel that comes after it, as a spliced recording proves.** A speaker said **ma** and **na**. The **m** part of "ma" was cut out and joined to the **a** part of "na." The consonant came from "ma," so the result should sound like "ma," but **listeners clearly hear "na."**
- **The m or n is heard more from the vowel than from the nasal sound itself.** The vowel's formants are different depending on the consonant before the vowel.
  - After **m**, one formant of the vowel stays fairly **steady**.
  - After **n**, that formant **drops sharply** at the start of the vowel.
  - So the vowel carries the information about the consonant. Listeners decide which consonant they heard **during the vowel**, after the consonant is already over.
- **Listeners take in whole stretches of speech and decide about the sounds that came first using the sounds that follow.** This was one of the first striking results in speech perception, published in the **1950s** by the linguist **André Malécot**.
  - For about seventy years, speech recognition machines treated each slice of sound by itself, and they **failed**, despite billions of dollars of effort.
  - A machine that understands speech has to use the sound that follows to decide about the sound that came before. That is why modern speech recognition uses large language models built from many neural networks.

## p21
- **A neural network can learn to recognize vowels from formants.** The network has **two inputs**, **F1 and F2**, measured from real recordings of five vowels. The network's job is to decide which vowel was spoken.
  - **The network's knowledge is stored in its weights.** The weights are learned from the data.

## p22
- **The vowel network has five outputs and three hidden layers.** There is one output neuron for each word, **done, dune, dean, Don and Dan**. The **three hidden layers** have **3, 2 and 4 neurons**.
  - Information enters at the input, a little like sound reaching the ear.
  - Each hidden layer turns the numbers it receives into new numbers, a little like the stages of processing in the brain.
  - The output layer gives the network's decision about which vowel it heard.
- **Every neuron connects to every neuron in the next layer, so the network is fully connected.** This version is **purely linear**, which means it has **no activation functions**. Each neuron just passes on the result of its inner product.

## p23
- **Before training, every weight is a random number between minus one and one.** A **positive** weight makes its input push the next neuron up. A **negative** weight makes its input push the next neuron down.
- **The example input is F1 of 320 hertz and F2 of 2000 hertz.** A very low F1 with a very high F2 is the pattern of **/i/**, so the correct answer is **dean**.

## p24
- **Forward propagation carries the input through the network one layer at a time.** Each neuron computes an **inner product**, which means multiplying each incoming number by its weight and adding the results.
  - For the top neuron of the first hidden layer, F1 is multiplied by its weight, F2 is multiplied by its weight, and the two results are added.
  - The first hidden layer produces three numbers. Those three numbers go through the next weights to make two numbers, then four, then five at the output.
  - The calculations never need to be done by hand. What matters is that every step is an inner product.
- **The output is a probability distribution, and with random weights the network is wrong.** The five outputs are **.5, .1, .1, .1 and .2**, which add up to one. The highest probability is on **done**, but the correct answer is dean.
  - The correct answer is given as the target **0, 0, 1, 0, 0**, which puts all the probability on dean.
  - **Stochastic gradient descent** changes each weight, so that the next time the network sees 320 and 2000, the inner products give a high probability for dean.
  - In plain terms, the network starts by guessing, the target shows the right answer, and learning nudges every weight so the right answer wins next time.

## p25
- **PyTorch describes the network as a list of layers.** **Sequential** means the network has **one layer after another**. **Linear** means the layer **does inner products** of weights and the previous activations.
  - **nn.Linear(2, 3)** means **take input from 2 neurons and have 3 neurons**. The next lines read 3 in and 2 out, then 2 in and 4 out, then 4 in and 5 out.
  - Setting the **bias** to False keeps every layer purely linear, which matches the diagram of the network. This code only describes the network. It does not train it.
- **Only layers that do calculations get a line of code.** The **input layer** has no weights coming into it and does no inner products. The input layer only receives numbers, so it never gets its own line. The first line describes the **first hidden layer**.
  - This network has **five layers in total**, which are the input layer, three hidden layers and the output layer.
  - This network has **three hidden layers**, and the **last line is always the output layer**.
  - "How many layers" and "how many hidden layers" are different questions, so read each question carefully.
- **To add a hidden layer of 3 neurons right before the output, change only the end.** The first three lines stay the same. The old last line, 4 in and 5 out, becomes **4 in and 3 out**. A new last line takes **3 in and gives 5 out**.
  - A quick check is that each line's first number must match the previous line's second number, because each layer takes its input from the layer before it.
- **The same reading works for any network written this way.** A network written as 4 in and 3 out, 3 in and 8 out, 8 in and 4 out, 4 in and 4 out, and 4 in and 5 out takes **4 numbers as input**, has **four hidden layers** with 3, 8, 4 and 4 neurons, and chooses among **5 categories**.
  - Drawn as a network, it has a column of **4 input neurons**, then hidden columns of **3, 8, 4 and 4 neurons**, then a column of **5 output neurons**.
  - When the first category is correct, the target puts a **1 in the first slot and 0 in every other slot**.
