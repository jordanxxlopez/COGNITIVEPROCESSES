## p3
- **A dynamical system is a system whose state changes over time according to rules.** Dynamical systems appear in two places in this course. One is **animal behavior**, such as an octopus defending its home or a person speaking. The other is **AI learning**, where a program changes its internal numbers as it is trained.
- **Every dynamical system can be described with three parts, elements, relations and a goal.** The **elements** are the pieces that make up the system. The **relations** are the ways those pieces are connected and coordinated. The **goal** is what the whole coordinated system is working to achieve.
  - These three parts let very different systems be described in the same terms. The system can be an animal's body, a vocal tract or a machine learning program.

## p4
- **An octopus defending its den is a dynamical system in behavior.** Its **elements** are its eight **arms** and the **ground** it sits on and pushes against.
- **Its relations are the coordinated connections between each arm, the ground and the fish.** These relations are **planned by a neural system**. That system lives in the octopus's head and also throughout the rest of its body. So the octopus does not control its arms from one central point alone.
- **Its goal is to keep the den.** The octopus coordinates its arms, its grip on the ground and the movement of the fish so that it can protect its home.

## p5
- **Human speech is also a dynamical system, and it has many more elements than the octopus.** The **elements** are the **many parts of the tongue**, the **lips**, the **velum**, the **larynx**, the **palate** and the **pharyngeal wall**.
  - The tongue is not one single element. Its tip, body and root can each move somewhat independently, so it counts as many parts.
  - The **velum** is the soft back part of the roof of the mouth. It raises to close off the nasal passage or lowers to let air flow through the nose. The **larynx** is the voice box in the throat, which holds the vocal folds. The **palate** is the hard roof of the mouth. The **pharyngeal wall** is the back wall of the throat behind the tongue.
- **The relations between all these parts are planned by the brain.** Producing a single word requires the lips, tongue, velum and larynx to move at precisely coordinated times.
- **The goal of the speech system is to convey a thought.** All of this coordinated movement exists so that an idea in one person's mind can reach another person.
  - The real-time MRI recording shows the system in action. Over about three seconds of speech, the lips, jaw and tongue change shape and position continuously. So speech is many parts moving together over time, not a series of frozen positions.

## p6
- **All daily voluntary actions, such as walking or reaching, are dynamical systems.** The involuntary actions of the heart, the pancreas and neurons are dynamical systems too. Each involves parts whose activity changes over time in coordinated ways.
  - The elements can be as big as **organs** or parts of organs. They can be as small as **cells** and the **chemicals** inside cells. So **biology, chemistry and physics** all study dynamical systems.
  - The **speech system** is a **very complex dynamical system**. It combines many body parts, many muscles and brain planning, all working toward one goal.
- **There are so many dynamical systems in nature that one can be used to study another.** An octopus arm and a human tongue are extremely different on the surface. Yet they share an **underlying similarity** in how their elements, relations and goals are organized.
  - This is **going up Diotima's ladder**. Studying many different dynamical systems moves understanding from individual cases toward the general principles they all share.

## p7
- **The octopus example comes from a 2023 paper in Marine Biology by Jean Alupay, Jennifer Mather and Khalil Iskarous.** The paper is titled "A syntactic analysis of a complex motor action" and is about the octopus arm slap. The researchers studied an octopus of the genus **Abdopus**, filmed in **tidal pools in Okinawa, Japan**, as it **slapped at fish** in the wild. A slap is a quick arm strike against a fish near the den. In total, 19 slaps were analyzed.
  - The arm movements were measured in two **reference frames**. An **external reference frame** measures position relative to the surroundings. An **animal-centered reference frame** measures position relative to the octopus's own body.
- **The octopus built each slap out of two kinematic primitives, rotation and translation of its hydrostatic arms.** Kinematic primitives are basic building-block movements. Rotation means turning the arm around a point. Translation means moving the arm from one place to another without turning it.
  - A **muscular hydrostat** is a body structure with no bones. It is supported by muscles pushing against each other around an incompressible core. The octopus arm is one, so it can bend at any point along its length.
  - The octopus combined these primitives **serially**, one after another, and **in parallel**, at the same time.
- **Combining a small number of primitives keeps the movement flexible while controlling only a few degrees of freedom.** A **degree of freedom** is one independent way a system can move. An octopus arm could bend in almost unlimited ways. Limiting control to a few combined primitives makes the movement manageable. The authors named this **flexible rigidity**.
- **The slap supports Flash and Hochner's embodied organization view of motor behavior.** Embodied organization means that much of the organization of movement comes from the structure of the body itself, not only from commands in the brain. The slap also supports their idea that **motor primitives combine syntactically**.
  - **Syntax** in language is the set of rules for combining words into sentences. Calling the slap **syntactic** means small movement units are combined by rules into a larger action, the way words are combined into sentences. So one dynamical system, the octopus arm, is used to study another, human language.
- **The octopus uses sensory feedback about the moving fish together with feed-forward motor primitives.** **Feedback** is information from the senses during the movement that allows correction. **Feed-forward** control sends out a pre-planned movement without waiting for correction. Together they let the octopus stay in **dynamic equilibrium** with a changing environment.
  - The paper's keywords are **Octopus, Muscular hydrostat, Motor control** and **Abdopus**. The book Octopus, The Ocean's Intelligent Invertebrate, by Jennifer Mather, Roland Anderson and James Wood, is a broader source on octopus behavior.

## p8
- **AI is a model of five animal psychological skills, knowing, recognizing, attending, acting and learning.** It is a system built to imitate abilities that animals, including humans, already have.
  - **Knowing** is holding stored information about the world, such as how words are used. **Recognizing** is identifying what something is when it is encountered. **Attending** is focusing on the parts of the input that matter most. **Acting** is producing an output, such as a movement for an animal or a word for a language model. **Learning** is changing internal knowledge based on experience so that future performance improves.
- **The cloze procedure is an early example of measuring one of these skills in humans.** Wilson Taylor introduced it in "Cloze Procedure," subtitled A New Tool for Measuring Readability, in Journalism Quarterly in Fall 1953.
  - In a **cloze task**, words are removed from a passage and readers fill in the blanks. The more easily readers guess the missing words, the more readable and predictable the text is. The article reported three pilot studies and two experiments comparing cloze results with two readability formulas.
  - Filling in a missing word from its context is the same basic skill that modern language AI performs. It predicts a word from the words around it.

## p9
- **In GPT, each word is represented as a vector, a list of thousands of numbers.** These vectors are called **x's** or **tokens**. In "chickens cackle and ducks," each of the four words is its own tall column of numbers, one column per token.
- **All of the AI's knowledge is stored in a few trillion numbers called weights, or w's.** The weights are not a dictionary or a list of facts. They are numbers whose values together determine how the system responds to input.
- **The AI combines the W's with the x's using mostly addition, subtraction, multiplication and division.** The complexity comes from the enormous number of these simple calculations. No single operation is complicated.
- **These calculations produce a probability distribution function over possible next words.** A **probability distribution** gives each possible word a probability, and all the probabilities add up to one. The distribution is the system's way of saying "this is my guess for the next word."
  - For "chickens cackle and ducks," the curve has small bumps over many words and a tallest peak over **"quack."** Words like "a," "cat" and "Zulu" get only a little probability.
- **The way the W's combine with the x's is itself a dynamical system.** Learning is a simpler dynamical system than this prediction process. So learning is the easier one to work through first.

## p10
- **The weights come from a training process that starts with no knowledge at all.** The approach is summed up as "Fake it till you make it!" Training repeats a sequence of steps.
  - The system **starts with random W's**. Any values at all can be used as the starting point.
  - The system is given a sequence of words, such as "chickens cackle and ducks." It is asked to **predict the next word** by combining its W's with the input vectors.
  - With random weights, the prediction is poor. The tallest peak might sit over "cat," with only low, uneven probability on the other words, including "quack."
  - We already know the correct next word is "quack." The **target distribution** puts all of the probability on "quack" as a single sharp spike and zero on every other word. The system is told to **change its W's** so that its output looks more like this target.
  - The system is exposed to **more sequences** and asked to predict again, **billions of times**. Each time its W's are changed to **improve its guessing**.
- **This repeated cycle of guessing, comparing and adjusting is how LLMs learn and know.** A large language model gets all of its knowledge from gradually correcting random weights over billions of examples.

## p11
- **AI learning depends on two tricks, each captured by a phrase in an image generated by ChatGPT.**
  - The first trick is **"Fake It Till You Make It."** The system begins with **random weights** and makes predictions right away. Those first predictions are essentially meaningless. The system does not wait until it has knowledge before acting. It acts first and gains knowledge along the way.
  - The second trick is **"Learn by Making Mistakes."** Each wrong prediction produces an **error**, the difference between what the system guessed and what was correct. The system uses that error to adjust its weights. So every mistake becomes information that improves the next prediction.

## p12
- **Finding an average seems very easy with a short list of numbers.** The average of 10, 12 and 14 is found by adding them and dividing by how many there are. The sum is 36, and 36 divided by 3 gives 12.
- **This simple formula does not work for complex things like speech, language, biological molecules and scenes.** Those are exactly what AI works with. For them, there is no short list of numbers to add up and divide. So a method that improves an estimate step by step is needed.
- **The averaging method used here learns from the data one piece at a time.** Start with a **list of numbers**. Make a **wild guess**, called x, about their average. Take one number from the list and check how far it is from x. Move x **a bit closer** to that number. Repeat with another number, and then another.
  - This is **observation-based error learning**. Each observation reveals an error in the current guess. The guess is corrected in the direction of that observation.
  - This is the same logic as training an LLM, applied to a much simpler problem. The random starting guess for x plays the role of the random starting weights. Moving x toward each observed number plays the role of changing the weights toward the correct next word.

## p13
- **SGD, stochastic gradient descent, estimates an average by learning from the data one observation at a time.** The list is [78, 67, 98, 85, 76, 68, 98, 72]. Its true average, its **essence**, is 80.25, found by adding the eight numbers to get 642 and dividing by eight. That is the target the learning process should approach.
- **SGD does not start by calculating that average. It starts by picking any random number x as the estimate.** At **time 0**, the estimate is set to 130, which is far above every number in the list. So x[0] is 130.
  - The number in brackets is the **time step**. The value x[0] is the estimate at time 0. The value x[1] is the estimate after one update, and so on.
- **The learning rate, written γ, is set to 25 percent.** The learning rate controls how large each correction is. It is the fraction of the error that the estimate moves on each step.
  - Plotted against the update number, the estimate at time 0 is a single point at 130. The true average of 80.25 sits far below it as a flat horizontal line.

## p14
- **The first step of learning is to randomly pick one number d from the data.** The first observation drawn is 98. So d[0] is 98.
- **The discrepancy, also called the loss, is the difference between the current estimate and the observation.** The estimate is 130 and the observation is 98. So the loss is 32. The loss measures how wrong the estimate is compared with this piece of data.
  - The discrepancy is positive, so the estimate is 32 units too high compared with this observation. The average should be **close to the data**. So an estimate far from an observed value needs to change.
  - Plotted together at update 0, the estimate is at 130 and the observation is at 98, below it. The true average of 80.25 is lower still.

## p15
- **The correction step subtracts 25 percent of the discrepancy from the current estimate.** The discrepancy is 32 and the learning rate is 0.25. So the correction is 8. Subtracting 8 from 130 gives the new estimate. So x[1] is 122.
- **The estimate moves toward this observation, but not all the way.** It moves one quarter of the distance, from 130 to 122. Moving only part of the way keeps any single observation from taking over the estimate.
  - Plotted over updates, the estimate line drops from 130 at update 0 to 122 at update 1. It is heading down toward the average line at 80.25.

## p16
- **The same procedure repeats with a new random observation each time.** Draw a number. Compute the discrepancy. Subtract 25 percent of that discrepancy from the estimate.
- **On the second update, the random observation is 85.** The current estimate is 122. The discrepancy is 37. The correction is 25 percent of 37, which is 9.25. Subtracting it gives 112.75. So x[2] is 112.75, and the estimate moved toward the observation.

## p17
- **On the third update, the random observation is 76.** The estimate of 112.75 is still above the observation, so the discrepancy is positive. The discrepancy is 36.75. The correction is 25 percent of 36.75, which is 9.1875. Subtracting it gives about 103.563. So x[3] is about 103.563.

## p18
- **On the fourth update, the random observation is 68.** The discrepancy between 103.563 and 68 is 35.563. The correction is 25 percent of that, about 8.891. Subtracting it gives 94.672. So x[4] is 94.672.
- **Across the first four updates, the estimate falls steadily.** It went 130, 122, 112.75, 103.563 and 94.672. Every observation so far has been below the estimate. So every correction has pushed the estimate down toward the average line at 80.25.

## p19
- **On the fifth update, the random observation is 98, which is higher than the current estimate of 94.672.** A **datum** is a single data point. The discrepancy is now **negative**, because the estimate is below the observation. It is 94.672 minus 98, which is negative 3.328.
- **The rule still says to subtract 25 percent of the discrepancy.** The correction is a negative number, about negative 0.832. Subtracting a negative number is the same as adding. So a negative discrepancy makes the subtraction **move x upward**. The estimate rises slightly from 94.672 to 95.504, toward the high observation.
  - The same single rule handles both cases. When the estimate is above an observation, the discrepancy is positive and the estimate goes down. When the estimate is below an observation, the discrepancy is negative and the estimate goes up. In both cases the estimate moves toward the observation.
  - Plotted over updates, the estimate line bends slightly upward at update 5 after four straight drops. This is the first sign that the path does not move smoothly in one direction.

## p20
- **On the sixth update, the random observation is 72, which is below the estimate again.** The discrepancy between 95.504 and 72 is 23.504. The correction is 25 percent of that, which is 5.876. Subtracting it gives 89.628. So x[6] is 89.628.

## p27
- **SGD consists of two steps.** First, **make an initial estimate or guess**, which can be completely random. Second, **use data to improve the estimate**, one observation at a time, by moving the estimate part of the way toward each observation.
- **Each word in the name describes part of the method.**
  - **Stochastic** means a **random observation** is used. At each step, the data point for the correction is picked at random. This is why the estimate is sometimes pushed down and sometimes pulled up.
  - **Gradient** means a **loss-related direction**. The discrepancy tells how wrong the estimate is and which way it needs to move. A positive discrepancy means move down. A negative discrepancy means move up.
  - **Descent** means **reducing the mistake**. Every correction makes the error for the observed data point smaller.
- **The path jitters, but its tendency is toward the average.** Individual steps can move away from the true average, as happened when 98 was drawn. Over many updates, the random pushes up and down balance out, and the estimate settles near 80.
  - This is observation-based error learning in its simplest form. The same method, applied to trillions of weights instead of a single number x, is how LLMs change their W's to predict the next word.

## p28
- **A second data set makes the relationship between the estimate and the loss easier to track.** The data are [62, 70, 78, 82, 90, 98]. They add up to 480, and 480 divided by 6 is 80. So the average is 80.
- **The mean motion of SGD describes how the estimate moves on average.** Each random observation is sometimes above and sometimes below the average. So on average the estimate is pulled toward the mean of the data, 80. Replacing the random observation with the average gives the rule for the mean motion. The new estimate x[t plus 1] equals the old estimate x[t] minus 25 percent of the distance between the old estimate and 80.
  - Here t stands for any time step. So this one rule describes every update.
- **The loss at each time step is one half of the discrepancy squared.** Squaring makes the loss positive whether the estimate is too high or too low. So both kinds of mistake count as error. The loss is zero only when the estimate equals 80 exactly.
  - The rate at which this loss changes as x changes equals the discrepancy, x[t] minus 80. This is why the discrepancy is the **gradient**, the loss-related direction. Subtracting a fraction of it moves the estimate in the direction that lowers the loss.
- **The starting point is set far above the average, at x[0] equal to 140.** At time 0, the discrepancy is 60. So the loss is one half of 3600, which is 1800. On the first update, the estimate moves 25 percent of the way toward 80, from 140 to 125. The loss falls to one half of 2025, which is 1012.5. On the second update, the estimate falls to 113.75, and the loss falls to about 569.5.
  - Each update keeps 75 percent of the previous discrepancy. So the discrepancy shrinks from 60 to 45 to 33.75 and keeps shrinking. The estimate curve falls quickly at first and then flattens as it approaches the target line at 80. It reaches about 82 by update 12. The loss curve falls from 1800 at update 0 to nearly 0 by update 12.
  - The mean motion is **smooth** with no jitter, unlike the stochastic path. This is because it uses the average of the data instead of a random observation at each step. In this case, the **estimate goes down and the loss goes down** together.

## p29
- **The same data set and the same learning rate of 25 percent can be used with only the initial condition changed.** The **initial condition** is the starting value of the estimate. This time the estimate starts far below the average, at x[0] equal to negative 100.
  - At time 0, the discrepancy is negative 180. Squaring makes the loss positive and very large. It is one half of 32400, which is 16200.
- **On the first update, subtracting 25 percent of a negative discrepancy moves the estimate upward.** The estimate goes from negative 100 to negative 55. The loss falls to 9112.5. On the second update, the estimate rises again, to negative 21.25. The loss falls to about 5125.8.
- **The estimate rises toward 80.** It climbs from negative 100 to about 74 by update 12. The discrepancy shrinks in size at every step. So one half of the discrepancy squared falls toward 0, from 16200 at update 0 to nearly 0 by update 12.
- **The direction changes, but the learning does not.** Starting above the average, the estimate moves down. Starting below the average, the estimate moves up. In both cases the loss decreases. So learning is defined by the loss going down, not by the estimate moving in any particular direction.

## p30
- **SGD can be run for 300 epochs, meaning 300 update steps, with three initial conditions and four learning rates.** The three initial conditions are IC of 130, well above the average, IC of 80, right at the average, and IC of 10, well below it. Every run settles around 80, the average of the data.
  - With a **learning rate of 10 percent**, the run starting at 130 falls and the run starting at 10 rises. Both reach the neighborhood of 80 within about the first 25 to 50 epochs. After that, all three runs wiggle gently within a few units of 80, roughly between 75 and 85.
  - With a **learning rate of 50 percent**, the runs starting at 130 and 10 reach the neighborhood of 80 within the first few epochs. After that, all three runs jump up and down widely, roughly between 70 and 95. They never settle.
  - With a **learning rate of 1 percent**, the runs move very slowly and very smoothly. After 300 epochs, the run starting at 130 has only come down to about 82. The run starting at 10 has only climbed to about 77. So neither has fully reached the average.
  - With a **learning rate of 80 percent**, the runs reach the average region almost immediately. After that, all three runs jump violently, roughly between 68 and 98, which is nearly the full range of the data. Each update moves the estimate most of the way to whatever observation was just drawn. So the estimate mostly tracks individual data points instead of their average.
- **The initial condition affects only the first part of each run.** After enough epochs, all three runs behave the same way. The learning rate determines how fast they arrive and how much they jitter once they get there.

## p31
- **The learning rate creates a fundamental learning tradeoff between speed and accuracy.**
  - When γ **increases**, learning **speed increases**. Each correction is larger, so the estimate reaches the average region in fewer steps. At the same time, **accuracy decreases**. The large corrections make the estimate jump around the average instead of settling on it.
  - When γ **decreases**, learning **speed decreases**. Each correction is small, so the estimate needs many steps to reach the average. At the same time, **accuracy increases**. The small corrections keep the estimate steady and close to the true average once it arrives.
- **With a single learning rate, the fastest learning and the most accurate result cannot be had at the same time.** The 1 percent run is accurate but slow. The 50 and 80 percent runs are fast but noisy.
- **Thousands of people, for generations, have made their living finding better ways to optimize.** To optimize means to adjust a learning process so that it works as well as possible. The goal is to find methods where **speed increases and accuracy increases** together. Then systems like LLMs can learn quickly while ending up with accurate weights.
