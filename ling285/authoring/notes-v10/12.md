## p2
- **The same math that describes a vibrating spring also describes the ups and downs of an economy.** The **2026 Nobel Prize in economics** went to **Robert Hall** and **Michael Woodford** for the **microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization**.
  - **Stabilization** means bringing a system back to its equilibrium and keeping it there.
  - Stabilization is the same job that **balancing feedback** does, and the same job a spring does when it pulls a mass back to rest.
- **In their model, unemployment and inflation rise and fall in turns, like the two curves of an oscillator.**
  - One curve acts like the position of a mass. It starts high, goes negative, and comes back.
  - The other curve acts like the velocity, which is the flipped version of the first curve.
  - The goal of stabilization policy is to **make these ups and downs smaller**, so the economy avoids high unemployment and runaway inflation.
- **Woodford's theory is called Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium, or DSGE, and each word has a meaning.**
  - **Dynamic** means the economy changes over time by following rules.
  - **Stochastic** means the model can handle **random shocks**, such as a war starting or a company leader being caught stealing money. Stochastic gradient descent works the same way, because one strange data point can push the estimate off, and later data pull it back toward the average.
  - **Equilibrium** means the goal is a steady state without wild jumps.
- **Large patterns can come from many tiny events.** **Macroeconomic** quantities describe a whole economy, such as inflation or the number of people without jobs. **Microeconomic** events are single choices, such as one person buying something.
  - Speech has the same structure. Many tiny vibrations in the mouth add up to a meaning that a listener understands without tracking each vibration.

## p3
- **A mass-spring system vibrates because the spring pulls toward rest and the moving mass keeps going.** The same vibration can happen in **longitudinal** motion, left and right along the spring, or in **transverse** motion, up and down between two walls. Both kinds of motion follow exactly the same rules.
  - Understanding both directions matters, because the vocal folds and the air in the vocal tract each vibrate most clearly in a different direction.

## p4
- **Mass and stiffness push frequency in opposite directions.**
  - **Raising the mass lowers the frequency**, so the system goes back and forth fewer times per second.
  - **Raising the stiffness raises the frequency**, so the system goes back and forth more times per second.
  - A heavier mass is harder to turn around, and a stiffer spring pulls back harder. That is why the two changes have opposite effects.

## p5
- **Two masses have two modes, and Mode 2 is faster because more springs are stretching.** In **Mode 1**, the masses move together. In **Mode 2**, the masses move against each other. The system can also do both at once.
  - In **Mode 1**, the middle spring moves with the masses without stretching, so effectively Mode 1 has **only two springs**.
  - In **Mode 2**, all **three springs** stretch and compress. More working springs give **more stiffness** and a **higher frequency**.
- **A spring only matters in a mode where that spring stretches.** Suppose the **middle spring is made stiffer** while the system vibrates.
  - **Mode 1 does not change**, because the middle spring never stretches in Mode 1.
  - **Mode 2 gets faster**, because the middle spring stretches and compresses all the time in Mode 2.

## p6
- **Three masses have three modes, and a mass only matters in a mode where that mass moves.** In Mode 1, all three masses move together. In Mode 2, the middle mass stays still while the two outer masses move against each other. In Mode 3, the middle mass moves against both outer masses.
- **Making the middle mass heavier changes some modes and not others.** The middle mass is called **Mass 2**.
  - **Mode 1 gets slower**, because Mass 2 moves in Mode 1.
  - **Mode 3 gets slower**, because Mass 2 moves in Mode 3.
  - **Mode 2 does not change**, because Mass 2 does not move in Mode 2.
- **The amount a mass changes the frequency depends on how much that mass moves.** A mass that does not move has no effect. A mass that moves a little has a small effect. A mass that moves a lot has a large effect.

## p7
- **Every added mass adds one more mode, and the mode patterns repeat as the chain grows.**
  - **Mode 1** always has everything moving up and down together.
  - **Mode 2** always splits the system into **two parts** that move against each other. With an odd number of masses, such as three, five or seven, the middle mass stays still in Mode 2.
  - **Mode 3** always splits the system into **three parts**, with the middle moving one way and the sides moving the other way.
  - Higher modes have more parts moving against each other, so the springs stretch more and the frequency is higher.
- **A chain of more and more masses and springs becomes a string.** A string is found in a guitar or inside a piano.
  - Every small piece of a string has weight, which is its **mass**.
  - Every small piece resists being stretched or squeezed, which is its **springiness**.
  - The string has the same mode patterns. Everything moves together in Mode 1, the two halves move against each other in Mode 2, and the middle moves against the sides in Mode 3.
- **The frequencies of a string are heard as notes.** The ear can pick out the notes of many instruments at once, because the ear is also built from masses and springs that respond to the masses and springs vibrating in the world.

## p8
- **A string fixed at both ends has modes at exact multiples of its first mode.** With the first mode at **100 hertz**, the next modes are at **200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz**. These multiples are called **harmonics**.
- **The multiples come from counting how many copies of the first mode fit into each higher mode.** The first mode is one bump that swings from up to down and back.
  - The second mode holds **two** bumps, one side going up while the other side goes down, so the second mode has twice the frequency.
  - The third mode holds **three** bumps, and the fifth mode holds **five** bumps. The frequency multiplies by the number of bumps that fit.
  - So if the first mode is 100 hertz, the fifth mode is 500 hertz.
- **A real string vibrates in many modes at once.** All the modes add together into one irregular motion, which looks a lot like the waveform of speech.
  - A single mode by itself makes a **pure tone**. A pure tone sounds so harsh that it is used for sirens. Normal sounds contain many modes at once.
- **A spectrum shows which frequencies a sound contains.** **Frequency** runs along the horizontal axis, and **amplitude**, which is how much vibration there is, runs up the vertical axis. For this string, the spectrum has spikes at 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 hertz.

## p9
- **All of these results come from a short list of principles about masses and springs.** The principles connect to each other, so they do not need to be memorized as separate facts.
  - **Raising the mass lowers the frequency, and raising the stiffness raises the frequency.**
  - **A system with n masses has n modes of vibration**, and each higher mode has a higher frequency.
  - **Adding mass lowers the frequency in proportion to how much that mass moves.** How much the mass moves depends on where the mass is.
  - **Where there is the most movement there is no stretching, and where there is the most stretching there is no movement.**
  - **Adding stiffness raises the frequency in proportion to how much that spring stretches and compresses.** This also depends on where the spring is.
  - In a **fixed-fixed system**, the harmonics are **whole-number multiples of the base frequency**, because the first mode fits a whole number of times into each mode.
  - In a **fixed-free system**, the frequencies are **odd multiples of the base frequency**, because the first mode fits an odd number of times into each mode.

## p10
- **Making one spot on a string heavier lowers some modes and leaves others the same.** Suppose the very middle of the string is made thicker, which adds mass there.
  - In **Mode 1**, the middle moves up and down a lot, so the added mass **lowers** the frequency.
  - In **Mode 2**, the middle does not move, so the added mass changes **nothing**.
  - In **Mode 3**, the middle moves again, so the frequency drops. In **Mode 4**, the middle is still again, so nothing changes.
  - Going up through the modes, the effect **alternates** between lowering the frequency and having no effect.
- **Movement and stretching take turns along a vibrating string.** Where a piece of string moves the most, it is carried up and down and is hardly stretched. Where the string is stretched the most, such as right next to a fixed end, there is **no movement**.
  - **The shortcut is that the most springiness is at the points with no movement.**
  - In **Mode 5** of a string fixed at both ends, the string does not move at the **two fixed ends** or at the **four points where it crosses its middle line**. Those **six places** have the most springiness.
  - The opposite question has the opposite answer. The **most movement**, and the **least springiness**, is at the tops of the **five bumps**.

## p11
- **One small change to a string can make one mode lower and another mode higher.** Choose a spot where **Mode 4 moves the most** and where **Mode 5 crosses its middle line**, so Mode 5 does not move at that spot. Then raise both the **mass** and the **stiffness** of that spot.
  - **For Mode 4**, the spot is moving, so the extra mass **lowers** the frequency. The spot is barely stretched, so the extra stiffness does nothing.
  - **For Mode 5**, the spot does not move, so the extra mass does nothing. The spot is stretched the most, so the extra stiffness **raises** the frequency.
  - So the same change **lowers Mode 4 and raises Mode 5**.
- **Each mode is checked separately with one question, which is whether the spot is moving or stretching in that mode.** If the spot is moving, the mass is what matters. If the spot is stretching, the stiffness is what matters.
- **This reasoning about small local changes is called perturbation theory, and it was first tested on drums.** A drum is also made of countless masses and springs. In the late nineteenth century, before cameras could record fast motion, small amounts of **wax** were stuck onto different spots on a drum.
  - Wax on a spot predicted to vibrate a lot added moving mass, and listeners heard the drum's frequency **drop**.
  - Wax on a spot predicted not to move made **no difference** to the sound.

## p12
- **The vocal folds are a membrane, so they have many modes of vibration, like a drum.** The **larynx** holds the **vocal folds**. In Mode 1, the whole surface moves up and down together. In Mode 2, the two halves move against each other. Higher modes divide the surface into more and more parts.
- **Changing the mass or stiffness of the vocal folds changes the voice.** People who smoke heavily can develop **nodules** on their vocal folds. A nodule adds mass at one spot and changes the stiffness nearby, so the folds vibrate differently, and the voice sounds different.

## p13
- **The measured voice matched the prediction of evenly spaced harmonics.** Science states a theory, usually with **differential equations**, solves it to get a prediction, and then measures. The theory here is that the vocal folds vibrate in many modes at once, so a voice should have a spectrum with **evenly spaced harmonics**.
  - A voice recorded and examined in **Praat** had its lowest harmonic at about 130 hertz.
  - The next harmonics were close to two and three times that value.
  - Each person's lowest frequency is different, but the harmonics above it are always close to multiples of it.

## p14
- **A building is fixed at the bottom and free at the top.** In strong wind or an earthquake, the top of a building can sway.
  - Engineers design buildings to sway in **Mode 1**, with the whole building moving together. On a high floor, Mode 1 feels unpleasant, but it is safe.
  - Engineers design buildings to never vibrate in a higher mode. In a higher mode, some floors would move one way and other floors the other way, which would break the building.
- **The air inside the vocal tract is also fixed at one end and free at the other end.**
  - At the bottom, the **vocal folds are closed**, so the air there **cannot move**, and that end is **fixed**.
  - At the lips, the air can move in and out easily, so that end is **free**.
  - Unlike a building, the air in the vocal tract vibrates in **all of its modes**. Speaking changes the masses and springs of different parts of the air, and that is how listeners tell vowels, consonants and words apart.
- **A fixed-free system has frequencies only at odd multiples, such as 100, 300, 500 and 700 hertz.**
  - The first mode goes from **no motion** at the fixed end to **lots of motion** at the free end. That is one quarter of a wave.
  - The next possible mode holds **three** of these quarter pieces, so its frequency is three times the first. The mode after that holds **five**, so its frequency is five times the first.
  - Two quarter pieces would end with no motion at both ends, which is a fixed-fixed pattern. So even multiples never happen.

## p15
- **The air in the vocal tract can be modeled as a chain of masses and springs that is fixed at the glottis end and free at the lip end.** In this model, the masses stand for the air's inertia, which is its resistance to being moved, and the springs stand for its springiness.
  - The lowest mode of the chain is like the **first formant**, written **F1**. The next mode is like the **second formant**, written **F2**.
  - The frequencies of a fixed-free system are called **formants**. The frequencies of a fixed-fixed system are called **harmonics**.
  - The second mode vibrates **three times faster** than the first, because the second mode holds three quarter-wave pieces.
  - The masses near the free end move very freely in both modes.
- **Three places along the tube matter for vowels.**
  - **/a/** is made by squeezing the air **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - **/i/** is made by squeezing the air **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - **/u/** is made by squeezing the air at the **lips**, which is the free end.

## p16
- **Images of the vocal tract show where each vowel squeezes the air.** A **constriction** is a place where the tongue or lips narrow the tract and squeeze the air. The **glottis** is the tiny space between the vocal folds at the fixed end, so distances are measured from the glottis.
  - In **done**, the central vowel, there is **no constriction**, and air flows freely along the whole tract.
  - In **dean**, a **high front** vowel, the air is squeezed under the roof of the mouth, about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **Don**, a **low back** vowel, the air is squeezed far back in the throat, about **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **dune**, a **high back** vowel, the lips are rounded, so the air is squeezed **at the lips**.
  - **Dan** is a **low front** vowel.

## p18
- **Squeezing air raises both its stiffness and its mass.**
  - Squeezing air makes it **stiffer**, the same way pressing on a tire makes the tire stiffer.
  - Squeezing air also packs it into a smaller space, which raises its **density**. Denser air is more **massive** and harder to move.
- **Raising mass and stiffness together does not cancel out in a system with many masses and springs.** Canceling out only happens for a single mass on a single spring.
  - In a system with many masses and springs, each spot is either mostly **moving** or mostly **stretching** in a given mode.
  - At a spot with no motion and lots of stretching, only the extra stiffness counts, so the frequency **goes up**.
  - At a spot with lots of motion and no stretching, only the extra mass counts, so the frequency **goes down**.
  - Speech proves that the effects do not cancel, because constrictions in different places clearly change the frequencies of the voice.

## p19
- **For /u/, rounding the lips lowers both F1 and F2.** Rounding the lips squeezes the air at the **free end**, which raises the mass and the stiffness of the air there.
  - In **Mode 1**, the air at the free end moves a lot and hardly compresses, so **motion matters most**. The extra mass lowers **F1**.
  - In **Mode 2**, the air at the free end again moves a lot, so **motion matters most**. The extra mass lowers **F2**.
  - The extra stiffness does not matter in either mode, because the air at the lips is moving, not stretching.
- **A recording confirmed the prediction.** A speaker started from the central vowel, with formants near 500, 1500 and 2500 hertz, and then said /u/. **Both formants dropped**, because the rounded lips added mass right where the air moves the most.

## p20
- **For /i/, F1 goes down and F2 goes up.** The air is squeezed about **two thirds of the way from the glottis**, and that one spot acts differently in the two modes.
  - In **Mode 1**, this spot has lots of motion and hardly any compression, so **mass matters most**. The extra mass **lowers F1**.
  - In **Mode 2**, this spot has lots of compression, so **springiness matters most**. The extra stiffness **raises F2**.
  - In plain terms, the same squeeze lands on a moving spot for F1 and on a springy spot for F2, so one formant falls while the other rises.
- **A recording confirmed the prediction.** A speaker went from the central vowel into /i/. The **first formant dropped a lot**, and the **second formant went up**.

## p21
- **For /a/, F1 goes up and F2 goes down, which is the reverse of /i/.** The air is squeezed about **one third of the way from the glottis**.
  - In **Mode 1**, this spot near the fixed end has lots of compression, so **springiness matters most**. The extra stiffness **raises F1**.
  - In **Mode 2**, this spot has lots of motion, so **mass matters most**. The extra mass **lowers F2**.
  - So F1 and F2 move toward each other, and the two formants end up close together.
- **The same reasoning works backward.**
  - If a constriction raises a formant a lot, the air at that spot must be **springy and hardly moving** in that mode.
  - If a constriction lowers a formant a lot, the air at that spot must be **moving a lot** in that mode.

## p22
- **The mass-spring principles explain every vowel.** Raising mass lowers frequency, and raising stiffness raises frequency, but each change only matters where it can act.
  - **Extra mass matters where the air moves.**
  - **Extra stiffness matters where the air compresses and expands.**
  - The vocal tract is fixed at the glottis and free at the lips, so its formants are **odd multiples** of the lowest one.

## p23
- **The air in the tube can be modeled as lines of air particles.** Each particle stands for billions of real air molecules. The closed vocal folds sit at the **fixed end**, and the open lips sit at the **free end**.
  - Where the lines bunch close together and then spread far apart, the air is **compressing and expanding**, which is called **springiness**.
  - Where the lines move back and forth without changing their spacing, the air is acting as a **mass**. This is easiest to see at the **free end**, where the air simply moves in and out.
  - At the fixed end, the air cannot move, but the air there compresses and expands a lot.
- **The modes of the air match the modes of a building.**
  - **F1** has one point of no motion, at the fixed end.
  - **F2** has two points of no motion, one at the fixed end and one inside the tube.
  - **F3** has three points of no motion. Between them, the air takes turns between places of most motion and places of most compression.
- **The schwa matches the prediction for a uniform tube.** The **schwa** is the central vowel with no constriction. F2 should be three times F1, and F3 should be five times F1. A measured schwa had formants near **500, 1500 and 2500 hertz**, which are those odd multiples.

## p24
- **A short question like "Where were you a year ago?" uses all of the constriction rules at once.** Each sound squeezes the air at a different place, so the formants move up and down the whole time.
  - When the tongue makes an **/i/**-like squeeze near the front of the mouth, **F1 goes down and F2 goes up**, because that spot moves in Mode 1 and is springy in Mode 2.
  - When the lips round for "you," **F1 and F2 both go down**, because the free end moves in every mode.
  - When the tongue moves back and down, as in an **/a/**-like sound, **F1 goes up and F2 goes down**, because that spot is springy in Mode 1 and moves in Mode 2.
- **Speech is a continuous series of constrictions, not a list of separate vowels.** The frequencies of the air in the vocal tract are always changing, and the mass-spring rules predict which way each formant moves at every moment.
