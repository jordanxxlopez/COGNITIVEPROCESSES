## p3
- **Real-time MRI films the inside of the head while a person is talking.** The images are **midsagittal**, a side-view slice taken straight down the middle of the head, from the nose to the back of the neck. The face points to the left. The lips are on the left edge, and the spinal column is the bright vertical band on the right. Each recording runs about half a minute and includes the speaker's sound, so every movement can be matched to the speech it produces.
  - **Air shows up black** because it gives off almost no signal. Soft tissue such as the tongue shows up gray or white. So the dark spaces are the open airways. These are the mouth cavity, the nasal passages above the roof of the mouth, and the **pharynx**, the air tube at the back of the throat behind the tongue.
  - The large rounded gray mass filling most of the mouth is the **tongue**. Above it is the **hard palate**, the bony roof of the mouth. Hanging from the back of the hard palate is the **velum**, or soft palate. The velum can be raised or lowered to close or open the path from the throat into the nose.
- **When the recordings play, the tongue, lips, jaw and velum all move at once and very quickly.** Every consonant and vowel we hear is the result of the speaker shaping this tube of air in a particular way. **Phonetics** is the study of these speech sounds. The images give a direct way to see the shapes behind them.

## p4
- **A fricative is a consonant made by bringing two parts of the mouth very close together without closing them completely.** Air is pushed through the narrow gap. The squeezed air becomes turbulent. This produces the hissing or buzzing noise in sounds like f and s. A fricative can be held for as long as there is breath.
- **English fricatives are organized along two dimensions, place of articulation and voicing.** Place of articulation is where in the mouth the narrow gap is formed. Voicing is whether the **vocal folds** in the larynx vibrate while the sound is made.
  - A **voiceless** sound is made with the vocal folds apart and still. The only noise is the hiss of air through the gap.
  - A **voiced** sound is made with the vocal folds vibrating. The hiss is combined with a buzz. A hand on the throat feels vibration during "zzzz" and no vibration during "ssss."
- **The English fricatives fall into four places of articulation. Each place has a voiceless member and a voiced member.**
  - **Labial** fricatives are f (voiceless) and v (voiced). Labial refers to the lips. For f and v, the lower lip is raised against the upper front teeth.
  - **Dental** fricatives are both spelled th. The voiceless one starts "think." The voiced one starts "this." The tip of the tongue sits against or just between the upper teeth.
  - **Alveolar** fricatives are s (voiceless) and z (voiced). The **alveolar ridge** is the bony ridge just behind the upper front teeth. The tongue tip is raised close to it.
  - **Postalveolar** fricatives are sh (voiceless) and its voiced partner written j, the sound in the middle of "measure." The gap is formed just behind the alveolar ridge, a little farther back than for s and z.
- **The four MRI images line up with the four places. The constriction moves backward from one place to the next.** In the labial image, the lower lip is pulled up toward the upper teeth. In the dental image, the front of the tongue is pushed forward toward the teeth. In the alveolar image, the tongue tip is raised toward the ridge behind the upper teeth. In the postalveolar image, the narrowing sits slightly farther back.
  - Each pair is made with the **same place and the same manner**. The pairs are f and v, the two th sounds, s and z, and sh and j. The only difference within a pair is whether the vocal folds vibrate.

## p5
- **A stop is a consonant made by closing the vocal tract completely at some point.** Air cannot get out through the mouth. Air pressure builds up behind the closure. When the closure is released, the trapped air bursts out. English has two kinds of stops, **oral stops** and **nasal stops**.
  - In an **oral stop**, also called a **plosive**, the velum is raised against the back wall of the throat. This seals off the nose. With both the mouth and the nose closed, pressure builds and is released as a small burst of air.
  - In a **nasal stop**, the mouth is closed in exactly the same way, but the velum is lowered. Air flows out through the nose the whole time. So there is no pressure build-up, and the sound has a humming quality.
- **The stops fall into three places of articulation. Each place has a voiceless oral stop, a voiced oral stop and a nasal stop.**
  - At the **lips**, p is voiceless, b is voiced, and m is the nasal. The MRI images for p, b and m all show the two lips pressed together.
  - At the **alveolar ridge**, t is voiceless, d is voiced, and n is the nasal. The images for t, d and n show the tongue tip pressed against the ridge behind the upper teeth.
  - At the **velum**, k is voiceless, g is voiced, and ng is the nasal, the sound at the end of "sing." A sound made at the velum is called **velar**. The images for k, g and ng show the back of the tongue humped up against the soft palate.
- **The nasal images differ from the oral images in the position of the velum.** In m, n and ng, the velum hangs down away from the back of the throat. This leaves an open passage into the nose. In p, t and k, the velum is raised to seal that passage. The short clips show each closure forming and releasing in real time. So a stop is an event in time. First the closure, then pressure or nasal airflow, then the release.

## p6
- **The fricatives and stops fit into one table built on three properties, manner of articulation, place of articulation and voice.** Manner of articulation is how the air is shaped. It can be a narrow gap, as in a fricative. It can be a full closure with the nose sealed, as in an oral stop. It can be a full closure with the nose open, as in a nasal stop.
  - The places run from the front of the mouth to the back. They are **labial, dental, alveolar, postalveolar** and **velar**.
  - The fricatives fill the labial, dental, alveolar and postalveolar columns, each with a voiceless and a voiced member. The oral stops fill the labial, alveolar and velar columns, with voiceless p, t and k and voiced b, d and g. The nasal stops fill the same three columns, and all of them are voiced.
- **Every one of these consonants is a combination of three values.** The sound t is a **voiceless alveolar oral stop**. The sound z is a **voiced alveolar fricative**. Changing just one value produces a different sound. Changing t from voiceless to voiced gives d. Changing d from an oral stop to a nasal stop gives n.
- **The empty cells are also informative.** None of these English consonants is a dental or postalveolar stop. None is a velar fricative. So the sounds of English are not a random collection of noises. They are a **highly structured system** of shared properties.
  - Every English speaker already uses this system every time they talk, without ever having been taught the table. So a speaker's knowledge of English contains a great deal of hidden structure, organized along place, manner and voice.

## p7
- **Artificial intelligence is a model of five psychological skills that animals have.** The five skills are **knowing, recognizing, attending, acting** and **learning**.
  - Knowing is having information stored in a usable form. Recognizing is identifying something from the input that arrives. Attending is selecting which parts of the input to focus on. Acting is producing a behavior or output. Learning is changing over time based on experience.
- **The way AI models each of these skills is at least slightly based on how humans perform them.** Many people say AI is a purely technical innovation that appeared out of nowhere. That view is mistaken, because AI grew out of ideas about human and animal psychology. Each part of an AI system can be traced back to a human skill it was built to imitate.

## p8
- **How AI knows something connects to a 1953 paper by Wilson Taylor.** The paper is titled "Cloze Procedure," with the subtitle A New Tool for Measuring Readability. It appeared in the Fall 1953 issue of **Journalism Quarterly**, a journal devoted to research in mass communication.
  - The paper was the first full statement of a method and its theory. It reported **three pilot studies and two experiments**. In them, cloze results were compared with two existing **readability formulas**, which are formulas that estimate how easy a piece of writing is to read.
- **Taylor's question was whether people's ability to restore deleted parts of a message could measure how readable that message was.** A tool built to measure human understanding of language ended up describing what it means for a system to know something.

## p9
- **Taylor described cloze procedure as a new psychological tool for measuring the effectiveness of communication.** The method is straightforward, and the data are easily turned into numbers. The findings held up in testing.
  - At first the tool was treated as a new approach to **readability**. Its results repeatedly agreed with the **Flesch** and **Dale-Chall** formulas, the two standard devices for estimating readability. Then cloze procedure was tested directly against those formulas.
  - Taylor expected that if future research supported these results, the tool would have many uses in other fields involving communication.
- **Taylor credited Charles Osgood and Melvin Marks, both experimental psychologists, for starting and helping with the work.** Others who advised or helped included Lee Cronbach, an educational psychologist and statistician, Dean Wilbur Schramm, Charles Swanson and George Klare, who had both written on readability, and the research associates Kalmer Stordahl and Clifford Christensen.
- **At the center of the method is a unit of measurement called a cloze.** It is pronounced like the verb "close." The name comes from the word **closure**.
  - **Closure** is a term from **Gestalt psychology**, the branch of psychology that studies how we perceive whole patterns. Closure is the human tendency to **complete a familiar pattern that is not quite finished**. The standard example is a broken circle. We see it as a whole circle by filling in the gap in our minds.
  - The three black circles beside Taylor's text each have a wedge cut out. The wedges point inward toward a common center. Nothing in the figure is a triangle, yet a bright white triangle appears to sit on top of the circles. The visual system completes the triangle from the partial information the wedges provide.

## p10
- **The same principle applies to language.** A person can complete a broken circle because its shape is so familiar that it can be recognized even when much of it is missing. Given the sentence "Chickens cackle and ___ quack," almost anyone can instantly supply the word "ducks."
  - If the word a person supplies is the same as the word that was removed, that person scores **one cloze unit**.
- **Filling that single blank requires a surprising amount of knowledge.** The sentence is a complex pattern built out of many smaller patterns.
  - A reader must know the **meanings** of all five words. A reader must know their **forms**, the patterns of letters in each word. A reader must know the meanings of **combinations** of words.
  - The reader must also notice that the sentence has two parallel halves. The first half pairs an animal with its sound, "chickens cackle." So the second half calls for the animal that goes with "quack." The reader guesses what the whole sentence means and then completes the pattern to fit that meaning.
- **Taylor gave two formal definitions.** A **cloze unit** is any single successful attempt to reproduce a part deleted from a message, by deciding from the remaining context what the missing part should be. A message is any language product. **Cloze procedure** is a method of intercepting a message from a transmitter, a writer or speaker, deleting parts of it, and giving the damaged message to receivers, readers or listeners. Their attempts to make the patterns whole again produce cloze units that can be counted.
- **AI shows its knowledge of something by completing, or clozing, sequences.** A system that can reliably fill in the missing parts of language has captured the patterns of that language.
  - The same test works for people. A teacher tests a student's knowledge of a skill by asking the student to complete some sequence, such as finishing a sentence or supplying the next step in a procedure.
  - AI takes this idea to its limit. From the AI point of view, **knowledge is nothing more than completion**. A system counts as knowing English if, given part of an English sequence, it can correctly produce what comes next. No further ingredient is required.

## p11
- **GPT takes in a sequence of words and predicts what word should come next.** The words are the input. So the first question is what a word looks like to a computer.
  - To a computer, a word is simply **a bunch of random numbers**. Each word in the vocabulary gets its own set of numbers. The only requirement at the start is that **no two words share the same set**. The numbers do not need to mean anything at first.
- **A list of numbers like this is called a vector.** The vector for one word could hold 1024 numbers. In the diagram, the words "chickens," "cackle," "and" and "ducks" are each drawn as a tall vertical bar, one vector per word. The input to GPT is a row of vectors, one for each word.
- **Given "chickens cackle and ducks ___," a system that knows English produces an output vector of 10,000 numbers.** Each number is the probability that a particular word of English is the next word. The output is drawn as one long horizontal bar, with labels for a few of the words, "a," "cat," "quack" and "Zulu."
  - A system that knows English gives a high probability to "quack," since ducks are the animal that quacks. Words like "cat" and "Zulu" get very low probabilities, since they make no sense in that position.
  - The output does not choose a single word directly. It gives a probability for every word in the vocabulary at once. The most likely words are the best completions. So the system's knowledge of English shows up entirely in how well it fills the blank.

## p12
- **The list of probabilities GPT produces is called a pdf, short for probability distribution function.** It describes how likely each possible next word is. The input vectors are called the **x's**. So the central question is how the system turns the x's into the pdf.
- **GPT transforms the inputs through recognizing by computing.** It recognizes the pattern in the input words. That recognition is carried out entirely through computation. The computing is mostly just **addition, subtraction, multiplication and division**. This is the same **grade school arithmetic** used to add up a grocery bill. The difference is that GPT performs these operations an enormous number of times in a row.
- **The knowledge the system uses to turn the x's into the pdf is stored in a bunch of numbers called w's, or weights.** The x's change every time a new sentence comes in. The w's stay fixed while the system is being used. The arithmetic combines the incoming x's with the stored w's, and the result is the pdf.
  - The knowledge is located **in** the weights. If the weights are changed, the system's predictions change. So its knowledge has changed.
  - Today's AI systems have **about a couple of trillion weights**. Every piece of knowledge the system has, including the fact that ducks quack, is spread across those trillions of numbers.

## p13
- **Trillions of weights are not too many numbers to resemble human knowledge.** A person knows that chickens cackle but ducks quack. That knowledge has to be stored somewhere.
  - It is stored in the **synapses**, the connections between the **neurons** of the brain. A synapse is the point where one neuron passes a signal to another. The strength of each connection helps determine what the brain does with incoming information.
- **The human brain has about 150 trillion synapses.** That is roughly 75 times the 2 trillion weights of today's largest AI systems. In both cases, knowledge is stored in a huge number of connection strengths rather than in a list of written facts. The weights play the role that synapses play in the brain.

## p14
- **The claim that knowledge is completion strikes many people as nonsensical.** Among them is **Yann LeCun**, a leading AI researcher. He argues that this view does not make sense, and he raised **a billion dollars** on the basis of that position.
- **Anyone who has used an AI system can judge the question directly.** What matters is whether the system feels like one whose notion of knowledge is **totally different** from our own. One reasonable answer is that it is **not totally different** from ours. Its answers, completions and explanations are close enough to human knowledge that the two cannot be entirely separate.
- **Another term for completion is prediction, and that word sits better with people.** Humans constantly **predict** and **expect** what will happen next. It might be the next word in a sentence, the next note in a song, or the next event in a situation. So knowledge as completion is knowledge as prediction, which human minds do all the time.

## p15
- **AI learns by making mistakes, and humans may not be that different.** How AI learns is one of the most central issues in AI.
  - The cartoon titled "How much you learn," from the r/GetMotivated page on Reddit, has three stacks of books. The stack labeled **"From theory"** is the shortest. The stack labeled **"From practice"** is taller. The stack labeled **"From mistakes"** is by far the tallest. People learn the most from their mistakes, which matches the way AI systems learn.
- **The main learning algorithm in AI is called Stochastic Gradient Descent, or SGD.** It sounds intimidating, but it can be understood through a very easy example, **averaging**.

## p16
- **Computing an average in the ordinary way is simple.** The average of 10, 12 and 14 is found by adding the numbers and dividing by how many there are. The sum is 36, and 36 divided by 3 is 12.
- **This direct method does not work for the complex things AI deals with.** Those things include speech, language, biological molecules and scenes. For them, no simple formula can be applied once to get the answer. So a different approach is needed.
- **The averaging method used in AI works in a way that extends to those complex cases.** Start with a list of numbers. Make a **wild guess**, called x, about their average. Then improve x through exposure to the data. Take one number from the list. Compare it to the current value of x. Move x **a bit closer** to that number. Repeat with another number, and then another.
  - This is **observation-based error learning**. The estimate starts out wrong. Each observation reveals how wrong it is. The estimate is corrected a little each time.

## p17
- **The goal is to find the average, the essence, of the list [78, 67, 98, 85, 76, 68, 98, 72].** The average is a single number that best represents all the values. Added up, the numbers give 642, and 642 divided by 8 is 80.25. SGD reaches an estimate close to this value without ever doing that full calculation.
- **SGD reaches the average in five repeated steps.**
  - First, pick any random number x as the estimate of the average. This is time 0, so the estimate is written x[0]. Suppose the number picked is 130. The guess is deliberately far from the true value.
  - Second, randomly pick a number d from the data. The data point reveals how far the data is from the estimate. Suppose the number picked is 98, so d[0] is 98.
  - Third, measure the size of the mistake. The mistake is called the **Loss**. It is the difference between the current estimate and the observed data point. Here it is 130 minus 98, which is 32.
  - Fourth, improve the estimate by subtracting a small part of the Loss from it. Here that small part is 10 percent of the Loss, which is 3.2. So x[1] is 130 minus 3.2, which is 126.8.
  - Fifth, repeat the whole cycle. Pick another number d from the data and use it the same way to improve x[1] into x[2], then x[2] into x[3], and so on.
- **The same rule works when the estimate starts too low.** Suppose the estimate were 10 and the data point drawn were 78. The Loss would be 10 minus 78, which is negative 68. Subtracting 10 percent of a negative number means adding. So the estimate rises by 6.8, from 10 to 16.8. The estimate is always pushed toward the data, whichever side it starts on.
- **The whole algorithm does two things.** It makes an initial estimate or guess. Then it uses data to improve that estimate.
  - **Stochastic** means **random**, since each data point is picked at random from the list. **Gradient** refers to the **Loss**, since the size and direction of each correction depend on how large the mistake is and which way it points. **Descent** means **reducing**, since each step reduces the mistake by moving the estimate closer to the data.

## p18
- **The fraction of the Loss subtracted at each step is called the learning rate.** In the example it was 10 percent. The four graphs apply SGD to the same list with four learning rates, 10 percent, 50 percent, 1 percent and 80 percent.
  - In every graph, the vertical axis is the estimate x, from 0 to 140. The horizontal axis is **epochs**, from 0 to 300. Each epoch is one update step. Each graph has three runs from three **initial conditions (IC)**, three different starting guesses. The red run starts at 130, the green run at 80, and the blue run at 10.
- **With a learning rate of 10 percent, the red run drops steeply from 130 and the blue run climbs steeply from 10.** Both reach the neighborhood of 80 within roughly the first 25 to 50 epochs. The green run starts near 80 and stays there. After that, all three runs overlap and wobble slightly around 80.
- **With a learning rate of 1 percent, all three runs are smooth, with almost no wobble, but learning is very slow.** After 300 epochs, the red run has only come down to about 82. The blue run has only climbed to about 76. So neither has fully reached the average.
- **With a learning rate of 50 percent, the red and blue runs jump to the neighborhood of 80 within the first few epochs, so learning is very fast.** Afterward, all three runs **bounce up and down widely**, roughly between 70 and 95, and never settle. **With 80 percent, the runs arrive even faster, but the bouncing is even larger**, roughly between 65 and 97.
- **The bouncing comes from the random single data points used at each step.** Each update pulls the estimate toward just one number from the list. Those numbers range from 67 to 98. With a small learning rate, each pull moves the estimate only slightly, so the pulls toward high and low values average out. With a large learning rate, each pull moves the estimate most of the way toward whichever number was drawn. At 80 percent, if the estimate is 80 and the number drawn is 98, the estimate jumps by 80 percent of the 18-point gap, which is 14.4, landing at 94.4. The next draw may throw it far back down.

## p19
- **The four graphs reveal the fundamental learning tradeoff.** The learning rate is written with the Greek letter γ (gamma).
  - When γ is **increased**, learning **speed increases**. The estimate reaches the neighborhood of the answer in just a few steps. At the same time, **accuracy decreases**. The estimate keeps jumping around instead of settling close to the true average.
  - When γ is **decreased**, learning **speed decreases**. The estimate creeps toward the answer and may still be far from it after hundreds of steps. At the same time, **accuracy increases**. The estimate moves smoothly and stays close to the true value once it gets there.
- **Speed and accuracy pull against each other.** So a single learning rate cannot make both as good as possible at once. The 10 percent rate sits between the extremes. It reaches the average fairly quickly while wobbling only a little.
- **For generations, thousands of people have made their living by finding better ways to optimize learning.** Optimizing means adjusting how learning is carried out so that **speed and accuracy both increase** at the same time. This gets around the tradeoff that a single fixed learning rate cannot escape.
