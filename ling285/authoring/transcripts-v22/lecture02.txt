4|**Fricatives** squeeze the air through a narrow gap so that it hisses, and they are organized by two separate choices. One choice is the **place of articulation**, where the narrowing happens, which can be labial, dental, alveolar or postalveolar. The other choice is **voicing**, whether the vocal folds vibrate. >> So f and v share the labial place and differ only in voicing, and the same holds for s and z, and for sh and the sound in beige. >> Feeling the throat while sliding from one sound of a pair to the other makes the voicing difference easy to notice.
5|**Stops** close the vocal tract completely and then let the air go. They are made at three places, the lips for p, b and m, the alveolar ridge for t, d and n, and the soft palate, the velar place, for k, g and ng. >> In each row the first stop is voiceless and the second is voiced. >> The third one is nasal, because the velum is lowered and the air escapes through the nose while the mouth stays closed.
6|**English consonants have far more structure than anyone notices when speaking.** Putting fricatives and stops into one table reveals three dimensions, which are voicing, place of articulation and **manner of articulation**, meaning how the air is let out. >> Fricatives let the air through a narrow gap, **oral stops**, also called plosives, block the air completely and then release it through the mouth, and **nasal stops** block the mouth while the air goes out through the nose. >> Nasal stops in English are always voiced. || Every consonant sits at one place in this table, so each one is described by a choice of voicing, a choice of place and a choice of manner. >> For example, n and d are both voiced and both alveolar, and they differ only because n is a nasal stop while d is an oral stop. >> Speakers use all of this structure without ever having been taught it, which says a lot about how much they already know.
7|**AI is a model of several psychological skills that animals have**, namely knowing, recognizing, attending, acting and learning. How AI models each of these skills is at least partly based on how humans do them. >> So AI did not come out of nowhere as a purely technical invention. >> Each piece of an AI system can be traced back to an idea about how minds and brains work.
8|**The question of how AI knows anything starts with a test called the cloze procedure.** It was invented as a way of measuring how readable a piece of writing is, by deleting words and asking readers to fill them in. >> The name comes from the word closure, the idea that people mentally close up gaps in something that is almost complete.
9|**Closure is a principle from Gestalt psychology.** In the figure of three broken circles, people see a whole triangle even though no triangle is drawn, because the mind fills in what is missing. The cloze procedure applies the same principle to language. >> Given chickens cackle and blank quack, almost anyone fills in ducks right away. >> Filling that gap correctly requires knowing the meanings of the words, how they combine and the structure of the whole sentence.
10|**For AI, knowledge simply is the ability to complete a sequence.** A person's knowledge of a skill can be tested by asking them to finish some sequence, and AI takes this idea all the way, treating completion as all that knowing is. >> If a system can reliably fill in the missing piece, it is treated as knowing the pattern. >> Another word for completion is prediction, and people already predict and expect what comes next all the time.
11|**GPT works by taking in a bunch of words and predicting the next one.** To a computer, each word starts out as a list of random numbers, and the only requirement is that different words get different lists. A list of numbers like this is called a **vector**. >> Given chickens cackle and ducks blank, a system that knows English gives back one long vector with a probability for every word of English being the next word. >> That output is a **probability distribution**, so a word like quack should get a high probability while a word like cat or Zulu should get almost none.
12|**The system turns the input vectors into the probability distribution by computing, and the computing is mostly plain grade school arithmetic.** It adds, subtracts, multiplies and divides. >> The knowledge that turns the input numbers into the output probabilities is stored in another set of numbers called **weights**. >> Today's AI systems have an enormous number of these weights, which is where everything they know is kept.
13|Having so many weights can seem too strange to resemble human knowledge, but human knowledge is stored in a similar way. Knowing that chickens cackle while ducks quack is stored in the **synapses**, the connections between the neurons of the brain. >> The brain has an enormous number of synapses, even more than the weights of today's AI systems. >> So a huge store of connection strengths is exactly where a brain keeps its knowledge too.
14|The idea that knowledge is completion can sound nonsensical at first. The best test is personal experience, by asking whether talking with an AI system feels like talking with something whose knowledge is totally different from one's own. >> Whatever the system's notion of knowledge is, it does not seem totally different from a human one. >> Calling completion **prediction** makes the idea feel more natural, since people constantly predict and expect what will happen next.
15|**One of the most central issues in AI is how it learns, and it learns by making mistakes.** People may not be so different, since much of what people learn comes from their mistakes rather than from theory or practice alone. >> The main learning algorithm of AI is called **stochastic gradient descent**. >> It sounds intimidating, but it can be understood through a very easy example, which is finding an average.
16|Averaging a short list of numbers is easy, by just adding them up and dividing. But that direct method does not work for complex things like speech, language, biological molecules or scenes, which are the kinds of things AI deals with. So a different averaging method is built that learns from the data a little at a time. >> It starts with a wild guess about the average, then looks at one number from the list and moves the guess a bit closer to that number. >> Then it repeats with another number, which is called **observation-based error learning**.
17|**Stochastic gradient descent finds the essence of a list of numbers by improving a guess step by step.** It begins with a random guess as the first estimate of the average. It then picks one number from the data at random, and the difference between the current guess and that number is the mistake, which is called the **loss**. >> The guess is improved by subtracting a small fraction of the loss, so that it moves a little toward the number it just saw. >> Then another number is picked at random and the same thing happens again, over and over. || The name says what the algorithm does. **Stochastic** means random, because the data points are picked randomly, **gradient** refers to the loss, and **descent** means reducing it. >> So the whole algorithm is two ideas, which are making an initial guess and then using the data to improve that guess.
18|Running the algorithm from three very different starting guesses, one too high, one about right and one too low, reveals how the **learning rate** matters. The learning rate is the fraction of the loss subtracted at each step. >> With a small learning rate, all three guesses creep toward the true average slowly but smoothly and end up very close to it. >> With a large learning rate, all three jump to the right neighborhood almost at once, but then they keep bouncing around it noisily and never settle. || So no matter where the guess starts, the algorithm finds the average. The starting guess only changes the path, while the learning rate changes how fast and how precisely it gets there.
19|**Learning has a fundamental tradeoff between speed and accuracy.** Raising the learning rate makes learning faster but less accurate, and lowering it makes learning more accurate but slower. >> There is no setting that gives both at once, which is the disappointing part. >> Generations of people have made their living by finding better ways to optimize, so that learning becomes faster and more accurate at the same time.
