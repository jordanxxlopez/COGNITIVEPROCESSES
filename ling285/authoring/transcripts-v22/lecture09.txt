3|**Differential equations are the mathematical way of writing down dynamical systems.** A differential equation states how a quantity changes over time, given its current state, just as the rules of the x and v system do. Newton first hid this idea in a coded message, and it can be paraphrased as saying that the laws of nature are expressed by differential equations. >> So spring systems, cooling coffee, the air in the vocal tract and learning algorithms can all be written in the same kind of mathematics. >> Both inference and learning in large language models are differential equations too.
4|**A viscous spring gives negative, or balancing, feedback, and Euler's method predicts what it will do.** In a system where the change of x is minus one third of x, a starting value well above zero falls by a third of itself in the first step. The following step takes away a third of that smaller value, so the drop is smaller, and each later step is smaller still. >> So x moves toward zero quickly at first and then more and more slowly, without ever overshooting. >> Finding x at each moment this way, by plugging the current value back into the rule again and again, is **Euler's method**, which predicts what the world will do using only arithmetic.

10|**A neural network is a system that takes in a huge list of numbers and returns a decision.** For a picture of a face, the input is the millions of pixel values and the job is to say which of many known people it is. For speech, the input is the numbers of a sound wave and the job is to say which speech sound it is. >> The output is a **probability distribution**, one probability for each possible answer, and the probabilities must add up to one. >> For five vowels, a distribution that gives most of its weight to the third vowel and none to the last says the network is fairly sure of the third vowel and sure it is not the last.
11|**The knowledge of a neural network is held in its weights.** The input vector, such as the pressure values of a speech wave, is combined with these weights through many calculations, and the result is a decision about what was heard. Real systems have enormous numbers of weights and calculations. >> The weights are learned by faking it until making it, starting from random weights. >> The network is given a signal, such as the vowel in Rud, and in **forward propagation** it uses its useless random weights to produce a distribution that does not match reality. || The network is then told the right answer as a distribution with a one for Rud and zeros everywhere else, so it realizes it made a big **error**, or **loss**. It improves its weights to reduce that loss, then gets another signal, and this is repeated billions of times. >> The remaining question is which calculations are actually done on the signal to turn it into a decision.
12|**Understanding how people perceive can help explain how neural networks use their weights.** The goal is to understand how a network turns, for example, a speech wave of a z into the decision z rather than s or a. Knowing how biological neural networks perceive speech and faces gives clues to how artificial networks model the same thing. >> Two theories of animal perception have shaped the design of neural networks, which are **templatic perception** and **hierarchical perception**.
13|**Templatic perception compares what comes in with stored templates.** In this theory of face perception, the eye receives light from a face, and the brain holds a stored template of every face the person knows. The brain compares the incoming face with each template, and the template most similar to the input is what gets perceived, along with its name. >> For AI, this means that the weights of a neural network are like perceptual templates. >> So the network decides by measuring how similar its weights are to the input it receives.
14|**Hierarchical perception happens in stages.** Psychologists and neuroscientists have long known that the input is turned into one new representation, which is turned into another, and then another, until a final decision is made. >> Each stage builds on the result of the stage before, rather than jumping straight from the raw signal to the answer.
15|**Neural networks are templatic and hierarchical perceivers at once.** The templatic part compares a template of numbers with an input of numbers to see how similar they are, and the way to compare two lists of numbers is the **inner product**. To take an inner product, the two lists are multiplied position by position and all the products are added up. >> If the inner product is near zero, the lists are dissimilar, and if it is large and positive, the lists are similar. >> So comparing an input with two different templates, the template whose inner product with the input is larger is the one the input most resembles.
16|**A neural network combines both ideas in inference and in learning.** In **inference**, the network is given a signal vector, and its weights are compared with it through inner products over and over, stage after stage, much like Euler's method steps forward again and again. In **learning**, the network is given a signal whose identity is known, it tries to infer that identity with its random weights and fails, and the weights are improved with stochastic gradient descent. >> So template matching is how the network decides, the stages are how it builds up the decision, and stochastic gradient descent is how its templates are learned.
