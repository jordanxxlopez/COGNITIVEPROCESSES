3|**A neural network takes a huge list of numbers and returns a probability distribution as its decision.** The input might be the pixels of a face, to be matched to one of many people, or the numbers of a speech wave, to be matched to one of the sounds of a language. The output gives one probability for each possible answer, and those probabilities must add up to one. >> With five vowels in an agreed order, a distribution that puts most of its probability on the third vowel and none on the last one says the network leans toward the third vowel and rules out the last. >> A network can be understood by what it does, how it walks and how it swims, before learning why it is called a neural network.
4|**The knowledge of a network lives in its weights, and the weights are learned by faking it until making it.** The input vector, such as the pressure numbers of a speech wave, is combined with the weights through many calculations to produce a decision. At the start the weights are random, so when the network is given the vowel in Rud, forward propagation produces a distribution that does not reflect reality. >> The network is told that it should have put all of its probability on Rud, so it sees that it made a big error, or loss. >> It improves its weights to reduce the loss, and then the whole thing is repeated with another signal, billions of times.
5|**Two huge questions remain about neural networks.** The question of **learning** asks exactly how a network learns its weights. The question of **inference** asks exactly how a network combines its weights with an input. >> Inference is answered first, because learning contains inference as one of its parts.
6|**A single neuron computes a weighted sum that is called an inner product.** Cajal discovered that the brain is made of separate neurons, connected by synapses, and in an artificial neuron each incoming connection carries a **weight**, a positive or negative number. The neuron multiplies each incoming activation by the weight on its connection and adds all the products together. >> A positive weight lets an active input push the neuron up, and a negative weight lets it push the neuron down. >> This weighted sum goes by several names, including inner product, scalar product and dot product.
7|**Brain chemistry and electricity were gradually turned into arithmetic.** A series of works over many decades took what was known about how neurons behave and expressed it with simple numbers and calculations. >> The early idea was that a neuron's activity can be modeled with simple mathematics, later work built machines that learn, and still later work developed networks of many neurons processing in parallel. >> Each step kept the same core idea, that neurons combining their inputs can be written as multiplying and adding.
8|Most engineers and neuroscientists believe modern AI has nothing to do with how humans think, since the brain is full of chemistry and electricity while AI is full of numbers and arithmetic. A few people instead see modern AI as a long history of tinkering with how arithmetic can imitate the chemical and electrical activity of the brain, and believe those attempts have succeeded. >> These are beliefs rather than facts, and they will not be on the exam.
9|**A multilayer perceptron passes numbers through several layers to reach a decision.** It has an input layer, several hidden layers and an output layer, and every neuron in one layer is connected by a weight to every neuron in the next. Each hidden neuron takes an inner product of the previous layer's activations with its own weights, and that number becomes its **activation**. >> The activations of one layer become the inputs to the next layer, so the inner products are taken again and again until the output layer is reached. >> At the output, the final numbers are turned into probabilities that add up to one, and the largest probability is the network's decision.
10|**Finding the activation of one hidden neuron is a matter of one inner product.** The neuron's activation is found by multiplying each input value by the weight on its connection to that neuron and then adding up all of those products. >> So a neuron connected to five neurons in the layer before it has exactly five weights. >> If its weights are w1 through w5 and the incoming activations are x1 through x5, its activation is w1 times x1 plus w2 times x2, and so on up to w5 times x5.

12|**Learning by faking it until making it starts from random weights and a known answer.** An input whose correct answer is known is fed through the network with random weights, and the output distribution comes out wrong. The true answer is written as a distribution with a one in the correct position and zeros everywhere else. >> The weights are then changed so that the output moves closer to that true distribution, using stochastic gradient descent, which in neural networks is called **backpropagation**. >> This is repeated over and over with billions of input and output pairs, which is what the huge data centers spend their time doing.
13|**Stochastic gradient descent can be written in Python to estimate an average.** The list includes one obviously mistaken value that is far larger than all the others, and a direct average would be pulled far off by that one error. >> With a small learning rate, each data point only nudges the estimate a little, so the single mistaken value pulls the estimate up for a moment but the many normal values soon pull it back. >> So learning a little from each observation makes the estimate end up near the true center of the data, without the mistaken value taking over.
