2|A **mass-spring system** is a mass hooked up to a spring, and it vibrates because of what each part wants. **The spring wants to be at its equilibrium position**, its resting length, so whenever it is stretched or squished it pulls or pushes to get back there. **A mass that is moving wants to keep on moving**, so once it gets going it does not stop on its own. >> When the mass is pulled out and let go, the spring pulls it back toward equilibrium. >> The mass is still moving when it gets there, so it keeps on moving past equilibrium and squishes the spring, which then pushes it back the other way. >> The mass overshoots again and again, so the system vibrates. Friction from the table would slowly stop it, but the model leaves friction out, so in the model it vibrates forever. || The same system can be pictured in **two directions**, and it is important to be able to picture it both ways. In the **longitudinal** picture the mass moves left and right along the line of the spring, and in the **transverse** picture the mass sits between two walls with a spring on each side and goes up and down. >> Pulling the mass up in the transverse picture stretches the springs, and because springs hate being extended, they pull the mass back down. >> When the mass passes the middle, the springs are not stretched at all, but the mass is moving, so it keeps on moving and stretches them on the other side. || **Both pictures move in exactly the same way** and at the same rate, because they have the same mass and the same total stiffness. Some systems are easier to think about longitudinally and others transversely, but both describe the same vibration. >> So the rate of vibration is set by the mass and the stiffness, not by which direction the mass happens to move.
3|Two things set how fast a mass-spring system vibrates, and they are the **mass** and the **stiffness** of the spring. The number of back-and-forth cycles the system completes each second is called its **frequency**. >> Each comparison changes only one of these two things, so its effect is easy to see. || **A heavier mass vibrates more slowly**, and this matches everyday experience, since heavy branches sway back and forth slowly while little branches sway back and forth quickly. A heavy mass is hard to get moving and hard to turn around, so each trip back and forth takes longer. >> So making the mass much heavier, while the spring stays the same, makes the whole system vibrate at a noticeably lower rate. || **A stiffer spring makes the system vibrate faster.** Stiffness is harder to picture than mass, but the idea is that the stiffer something is, the faster it wants to go back, because a stiff spring pulls back hard and turns the mass around sooner. >> So making the spring much stiffer, while the mass stays the same, makes the whole system vibrate at a noticeably higher rate. >> Both rules hold whether the system is pictured longitudinally or transversely. || The two rules point in **opposite directions**, because raising the mass lowers the frequency while raising the stiffness raises the frequency. >> These two rules are what explain everything about modes, strings and, eventually, speech.
4|With **two masses**, the system has two walls, a spring from each wall to a mass, and one spring between the two masses. All the masses are equal and all the springs are equal, and this system can vibrate in **two different ways**, which are called **modes**. **A mode is just another word for a way of vibrating**, and the two modes are told apart by asking whether the masses go together or against each other. >> In the first mode, both masses are pulled up and let go, and they go up and down together, or in the longitudinal picture they move left and right together. >> In the second mode, one mass is pulled down and the other up, and they move against each other, so whenever one goes up the other goes down. || A system can also vibrate in **both modes at the same time**. If both masses are pulled up but one higher than the other, both start falling together, which is the first mode, while the middle spring also pushes them against each other, which is the second mode. >> The result moves together and against each other at once, and real systems, including the ones used in speech production, do this all the time. || **The second mode vibrates faster than the first**, even though both use the same two masses and the same three springs. The explanation comes from asking what each spring is actually doing in each mode. >> In the first mode the masses move together, so the middle spring never stretches or compresses. It just rides along like a rigid bar, so effectively the first mode has only two springs. >> In the second mode the masses move against each other, so the middle spring is stretched and squished over and over, and all three springs are working. >> More springs working means more stiffness, and more stiffness means more pull and so a higher frequency. Nothing about the springs themselves changed, because the two modes simply use the springs differently. || This foundation has to be followed step by step rather than memorized, because each later idea builds on it. >> The general rule is that the more the masses go against each other, the more the springs stretch and compress, so the higher the frequency of that mode.

6|With **three masses**, the system has **three modes**, one more than with two masses, and each mode is a different way the masses move relative to each other. In the first mode all three go back and forth together, and this mode has the lowest frequency. In the second mode the middle mass stays perfectly still while the two outer masses move against each other, and this is the middle frequency. In the third mode the middle mass moves opposite to the two outer masses, and this is the highest frequency. >> The second mode can be started by carefully leaving the middle mass untouched and pulling the two outer masses apart. >> The third mode is easiest to see in the transverse picture, with the middle going one way while the sides go the other. || The frequencies follow the same reasoning as with two masses. The first mode is very slow because the masses move together, so the two middle springs are doing nothing, and since their job is to stretch and compress, which is what raises frequency, they add no stiffness when they just ride along. >> In the second mode the springs do stretch and compress, but not as much as they could. >> In the third mode everything is going against everything else, so there is lots of stretching and lots of compression, which uses the most stiffness and makes it the fastest. >> The masses and springs are identical in all three modes, so only the way the masses move relative to each other changes the frequency. || All three modes can also happen **at the same time**, and however the system is started, it vibrates as some mix of its pure modes. >> Learning an instrument means learning how to excite some modes and not others, and those favored modes are heard as notes. >> With four masses the same logic continues, giving four modes that range from all the masses moving together to every mass moving against its neighbors.
7|The pattern holds for any number of masses, since **N masses give N modes**. One mass has only one way to vibrate, two masses have two ways, three have three, and so on. Moving to higher modes, more parts move against each other, so there are more reversals in the pattern and the frequency gets higher. >> In chains of separate masses like these, the mode frequencies are generally not whole-number multiples of the lowest frequency. || Comparing the same mode across chains of different lengths reveals the same idea repeating. Every first mode has all the masses going up and down together, every second mode has the left side going against the right side, and every third mode has the middle going against the sides. >> When the number of masses is odd, the middle mass in the second mode is frozen while the sides go against each other around it. || Packing in more and more masses and springs, all the way to an infinite number, gives a **string**, like a guitar or piano string. Every little piece of a string has mass, and every little piece stretches like a spring, so a string vibrates in exactly the same kinds of modes. >> In the lowest mode the whole string goes up and down together with hardly any stretching between its pieces, and that mode gives the lowest note. >> In higher modes more and more parts of the string move against each other, and a string plucked without any skill vibrates in many modes at once. || A system can also be **fixed on one side and free to sway on the other**, and a **building** is like this, because it is fixed to the ground at the bottom and free to sway at the top in high winds or earthquakes. >> Civil engineers design buildings to sway in the first mode, all together, and never in higher modes, where some floors would move one way while others move the other way. >> Buildings are not made so stiff that they cannot move at all, because they are designed to sway safely in their lowest mode.
8|A **string fixed at both ends** vibrates in many modes. In the lowest mode the whole string goes up and down together, and each higher mode has more sections moving against each other and completes more cycles in the same amount of time. Frequency is measured in **Hertz**, which means times per second. >> For a string fixed at both ends, each mode is a **whole-number multiple** of the lowest one, so the second mode is twice the lowest, the third is three times the lowest, and so on. Modes that line up this way are called **consecutive harmonics**. >> The motion is slowed way down so that it can be followed by eye. || A real string vibrates in **all of its modes at the same time**, so its actual motion is the sum of all of them. Adding the separate motions together at every point gives one complicated, changing shape, which is what an actual plucked string does. || The **spectrum** of a sound tells which frequencies are in it and how much of each there is. >> For a string moving in five modes at once, the spectrum has five spikes, one at the frequency of each moving mode. >> If the string vibrated only in its third mode, there would be no movement in the other modes, so the spectrum would have just one spike, at the frequency of the third mode. That pattern is like a one-hot encoding, with one active slot and every other slot at zero. >> So the spectrum turns a complicated motion into a simple list of which modes are moving and how strongly.
9|**The vocal folds are even more complicated than strings.** They are a bunch of muscle forming a surface, which is called a **membrane**, and a membrane spreads its masses and springs across two directions instead of along one line. The same logic of modes still applies to them. >> In the first mode all the masses go up and down together, and in the second mode the left half goes against the right half. >> In the third mode the middle is stuck while the sides go against each other, and higher modes divide the surface into more and more parts. || The frequencies given to the membrane's modes in the picture are assigned for teaching, so they are not the natural frequencies this particular rectangle would really have. >> Adding all of the motions together gives one combined surface, and measured at one point, its spectrum has equal spikes, one for each mode. || In real vocal folds, **all the modes happen at the same time**. Producing a voice makes the vocal folds vibrate in many modes at once, and that is what creates a spectrum full of spikes.
10|**The scientific method depends on very clear statements about how systems work.** These statements are usually written as **differential equations**, which describe how quantities change over time, and solving the equations gives a prediction about how the world will behave when it is actually measured. >> The simple spring equation is one example, because solving it predicts that a pulled spring goes back and a pushed spring also goes back. || For the voice, the theory says that **the vocal folds are a membrane** built from many masses and springs, so they vibrate in many modes at once. Their equations are the same type as the spring equations, only more of them and slightly more complicated, so the intuition built from masses and springs is enough to reason with. The prediction is that **any laryngeal vibration signal will have a spectrum with evenly spaced spikes**, and these evenly spaced spikes are called **harmonics**. || **This prediction was tested in class.** Two students each made a sound with the mouth wide open, so that no particular vowel was being made, and the spectrum of each voice was examined in the program Praat. >> In the first student's voice the spikes came at a low frequency and its multiples, evenly spaced just as predicted. >> In the second student's voice the first spike was about twice as high, and the following spikes were again evenly spaced, only farther apart. >> So the prediction held for both voices, and a prediction built from intuition about masses and springs turned out to be precise and real. || The difference between the two voices follows from the mass rule. The second voice has a higher pitch, so that student's vocal folds are probably smaller and thinner, and thinner folds have less mass, so they vibrate at a higher frequency. >> This is part of how people are recognized by their voices, since becoming familiar with someone means becoming familiar with the spectrum of their voice. >> How a person says particular vowels also matters, and that depends on the air above the vocal folds rather than the folds themselves.
11|A string can also be **fixed at one end and free at the other**, like a building that is fixed to the ground and sways at the top. At the fixed bottom the string cannot move, and a point that never moves is called a **node**. At the free top the string moves the most, and a point of largest motion is called an **antinode**. >> So every mode has to start with a node at the bottom and end with an antinode at the top. || This changes which modes are possible, because **only the odd harmonics occur**, meaning one, three, five and seven times the lowest frequency. The modes at two and four times the lowest frequency are missing, because those patterns cannot end with free motion at the top. >> The first mode has no still point inside the string, and its motion grows steadily from the fixed bottom to the free top, like a building swaying as a whole. >> Each higher mode adds more still points inside, with more sections swinging against each other. || Freeing one end also lowers the whole system. For a string of the same length and wave speed, switching from fixed at both ends to fixed at one end and free at the other **halves the lowest frequency**. >> When both ends are fixed, the string has every whole-number multiple of the lowest frequency. >> When one end is fixed and the other is free, the string has only the odd multiples.
