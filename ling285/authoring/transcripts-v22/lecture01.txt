3|**Speech is one of the most complex skills a person ever learns, and almost nobody teaches it.** Children learn to move their speech organs in extremely complicated ways with no instructions and hardly any way to see what their tongue is doing. By the time they are two to four years old, they speak with a wide vocabulary and language that is close to an adult's. >> Reading and writing whole words come later, around six or seven, and they need a great deal of explicit teaching. >> So the skill that seems harder, speaking, is learned almost for free, while the skill that seems simpler, writing, has to be drilled. || The field that studies how the tongue, lips, velum and other parts of the vocal tract move is called **articulatory phonetics**. MRI images make these movements visible, even though a speaker can never watch them directly. >> Voice assistants and other speech technology have recently come to imitate this human ability in ways almost nobody expected. >> So the course studies speech twice, once as a human skill and once as something AI systems now model.
4|**Acoustic phonetics** studies the air inside the vocal tract and how that air vibrates. The air sits between the tongue and the rest of the vocal tract, and for the sake of understanding it, the curve of the tract can be ignored and the air pictured as a straight column. >> The arrows on the MRI image straighten that bent passage out into a simple tube. >> The vibration of the air in that tube is what turns the movements of the tongue and lips into the sounds a listener hears.
5|**AI cannot simply be ignored, even though it causes real harm.** It has psychological, economic, social and environmental costs, and some scientists warn of even worse dangers, but it also has real benefits, such as helping with medical treatments. One of the biggest dangers is that **people know everything about how these systems are built and understand almost nothing about how they work.** >> The number of people who actually know how these systems operate is very small. >> So understanding AI matters for scientists who use it, for politicians who need to control it and for lawyers who argue about it. || The course exists because a creative thinker can help explain these systems well enough to gain their benefits while avoiding their harms. >> That kind of understanding, rather than fear or blind use, is the goal.
6|The course has specific goals and general ones. The specific goals are to appreciate speech as a human cognitive skill, to understand basic principles of physics through speech, to understand how modern AI systems model human cognition, and to sharpen careful reasoning through elementary programming. >> The general goal is to think creatively across fields, since the course draws on physics, psychology, linguistics, biology, economics, computer science and neuroscience. >> It also aims to deepen understanding of the world people now live in and to make people more skillful users of modern AI.
7|**Diotima's ladder describes two very different ways of knowing.** At the bottom are daily experience, habit, memorization and repetition, where similar things are treated as the same and slightly different things are treated as different. At the top are creativity, insight, focus and imagination, where different things can turn out to be the same and similar things can turn out to be different. Each step up the ladder comes from asking a question and working out its answer. >> For thousands of years, people could fake it and do well without ever climbing the ladder. >> That is no longer possible, because job and graduate school interviews now look for exactly the abilities at the top of the ladder, such as posing problems, solving them and reading critically.
8|**Speech can be learned by doing it.** Saying a long f and then sliding into a v, a long th as in thing and then the th in that, a long s and then a z, and a long sh and then the sound in the middle of beige, makes the differences easy to feel. >> Within each pair, the only thing that changes is whether the vocal folds vibrate, and that difference is called **voicing**. >> Placing a hand on the throat makes it obvious, since the buzz switches on for v, z and the others and switches off for f, s and the others. || Moving from one pair to the next changes something else, which is **where in the mouth** the air is squeezed. That difference is called the **place of articulation**. >> So each of these sounds is described by two separate choices, one about the vocal folds and one about where in the mouth the narrowing happens.
9|**Fricatives** are sounds made by squeezing the air through a narrow gap, so that it becomes turbulent and hissy. They line up in a grid where one direction is the place of articulation and the other is voicing. >> At the **labial** place the lower lip touches the upper teeth, giving f and v. >> At the **dental** place the tongue tip is at the teeth, giving the two th sounds, and at the **alveolar** place the tongue is just behind the teeth on the ridge, giving s and z. >> At the **postalveolar** place the tongue is a little farther back, giving sh and the sound in beige. || Each column of the grid has one voiceless sound and one voiced sound, so voicing and place combine freely. >> The MRI images under each column let the place be identified by looking at where the tongue or lips make the narrowing.
10|**Stops** are made by closing the vocal tract completely and then releasing the air. They come in three places, with p, b and m at the lips, t, d and n with the tongue tip at the alveolar ridge, and k, g and ng with the back of the tongue raised against the soft palate, which is the **velar** place. >> In each row, the first sound is voiceless and the second is voiced, just like the fricatives. || The third sound in each row, m, n and ng, is a **nasal stop**. The mouth is closed just as for b, d or g, but the **velum** is lowered so that the air escapes through the nose. >> So n and d share their place and their voicing, and they differ only in whether the air goes out through the nose or is blocked and then released through the mouth. >> That gives three separate ways to tell consonants apart, which are voicing, place of articulation and the manner of letting the air out, with nasality as part of the manner.
