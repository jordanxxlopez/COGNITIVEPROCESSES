2|Speech has **two major vibrating systems**, and the first is the **vocal folds**, a membrane fixed on all sides and made of a whole bunch of masses and springs. In the first mode all the masses go up and down together, in the second mode half of them go up while the other half go down, and higher modes split the membrane into more and more parts going against each other. >> This is literally what happens in the throat during speech, and measuring the spectrum of real voices confirms it. || Because the vocal folds are fixed on all sides, their frequencies are **harmonics**, which are whole-number multiples of the lowest one. **The lowest harmonic of all is called F0.** Each person has a different F0, but every voice has its harmonics stacked at multiples of its own F0. >> The frequencies given to the membrane's modes in the picture are assigned for teaching rather than worked out for the membrane. >> Added together, the separate motions make one complicated surface, and its spectrum at a pickup point has equal spikes, one for each mode.
3|The second vibrating system is fixed differently, so it follows a different rule, and a **string fixed at the bottom and free at the top** is the simplest version of it. At the fixed bottom the string cannot move, so that point is a **node**, and at the free top it moves the most, so that point is an **antinode**. >> So every mode must have a node at the bottom and an antinode at the top. || Because of those ends, **only the odd harmonics occur**, at one, three, five and seven times the lowest frequency. With equal excitation of each mode, the spectrum at the free end has equal spikes at those odd multiples. >> For a string of the same length and wave speed, switching from fixed at both ends to fixed at only one end halves the lowest frequency.
4|A **quarter-wave packet** is the piece of a wave that runs from a node to an antinode. That piece is exactly what joins the fixed bottom to the free top, so each mode is built out of these packets. The first mode holds one packet, the second holds three and so vibrates three times as fast, and the third and fourth hold five and seven. || The counts are always odd, because the string must still end with an antinode at the top. Adding one packet would leave a node there, so packets get added two at a time. >> More packets in the same length means shorter waves and faster vibration. >> The packets are pieces of one standing pattern, so they do not travel along the string.
5|The second vibrating system is the **air in the vocal tract**. Picture the space inside the mouth and throat straightened out into a tube, with the flesh of the tongue, the palate and the throat forming its walls. The air is **fixed at the glottis**, the space between the vocal folds, because the folds are closed and the air cannot move back and forth there, and it is **free at the lips**, where it can go in and out. >> So the air in the vocal tract is fixed at one end and free at the other, and its frequencies are formants that fall at odd multiples. || The air can be modeled as a mass-spring chain of identical masses in a uniform tube, fixed at one end and free at the other. The first mode corresponds to **F1**, the lowest air mode of the tube, and the second mode corresponds to **F2**, which vibrates three times faster than the first. >> Three spots are marked, measured from the glottis, with /a/ one third of the way up, /i/ two thirds of the way up, and /u/ at the lip end.
6|The air does **two different things** at different places, and both can be seen directly. Where the slivers of air come very close together and then move far apart, the air is compressing and expanding, which is **springiness**, and that happens near the closed end, because air can be squeezed against something closed. Where the slivers stay equally spaced and just move back and forth, the air is acting like a **mass**, and that happens near the open end, because there is nothing there to squeeze against. || In the first mode, the air is very springy at the closed end, then less and less springy and more and more like a mass toward the open end. In higher modes springiness and mass alternate along the tube, and they repeat more times the higher the mode is. >> In every mode, the air at the open end acts like a mass, going back and forth with hardly any expansion. >> And the air near the closed end always acts like a spring.
7|Different sections of the vocal tract can be compared by asking, for each one, whether the air there is mostly springy or mostly acting as a mass. A section near the closed end is very springy, a section where the slivers just move back and forth together is acting as a mass, and a section where the slivers bunch up and spread out again is springy. >> None of this has to be memorized, since it can be read directly from how the air moves. || **Constricting the tract at a springy spot raises the formant of that mode.** A constriction always increases both mass and stiffness, because squeezing air makes it stiffer, the way squeezing a tire does, and bunching it up makes it effectively heavier. >> At a spot in the third mode where the air is acting like a pure spring, there is no back-and-forth movement, only compression and expansion. >> So the added mass does not matter there and only the added stiffness counts, which means the third formant goes up. >> This chain of reasoning has to be built fresh at each spot, because the situation is slightly different everywhere.
8|Real-time **MRI** images of people speaking reveal where each vowel squeezes the air. >> In Dean the tongue is high and front, which makes a constriction two thirds of the way from the glottis. >> In Dune the tongue is high and back, with a constriction at the lips, and in Done the tongue is central with no constriction at all. >> In Don the tongue is low and back, squeezing the air far back in the throat, one third of the way from the glottis, and in Dan the tongue is low and front. || **Combining these images with the model** is where theory meets reality. The model treats the air as masses and springs, fixed on one side and free to move on the other, and a constriction is drawn as stiffer springs and heavier masses at that spot. >> Where each vowel puts its constriction decides which formants go up and which go down.
9|**Compressing air raises its stiffness**, so constricting at a place with lots of compression raises the frequency. **Bunching up air raises its mass, or density**, so constricting where the air moves a lot adds mass and lowers the frequency. >> So making a constriction increases both mass and stiffness. || That does not mean the effects cancel and constrictions leave frequency unchanged. **The parts of a vibrating medium that move the most alternate with the parts that compress and uncompress the most**, so at any given spot in a mode, one effect wins. >> If the air there is mostly moving, the added mass wins and the formant goes down. >> If the air there is mostly compressing, the added stiffness wins and the formant goes up. >> The same constriction can do opposite things to different formants, because each mode has its own pattern of motion and compression.
10|The same spot along the tract can play different roles in the first and second modes. Near the lips both modes allow strong motion, but farther inside the tube the second mode has an extra change in its pattern, so a spot can be moving in one mode and compressing in the other. >> That is why the effect of a constriction has to be worked out separately for F1 and for F2. >> F1 and F2 are resonances of the air in the tract, not harmonics of the vocal folds.
11|For **/u/**, the lips squeeze the air at the free end, which is called **loading the free end**, and that raises both the mass and the stiffness of the air at the lips. **The air at the open end acts like a mass in every mode**, going back and forth with hardly any squeezing, because there is nothing there to squeeze against. >> So the added stiffness does not matter, because there is no compression or expansion there. >> The added mass does matter, because the air there is moving a lot, so both F1 and F2 go down. || This is what happens in the mouth for the vowels in dune, moon and through. Measurements match it, since starting from schwa, a student's /u/ had an F1 far below the F1 of schwa.
12|For **/i/**, the air is squeezed two thirds of the way from the closed end, and the two modes are doing different things at that spot. In the first mode the air there is mostly moving back and forth, so it acts like a **mass**. In the second mode the air there is mostly compressing, so it acts like a **spring**. || A constriction always raises both mass and stiffness, so the outcome depends on which one matters in each mode. F1 goes down because the air is moving and the added mass wins, while F2 goes up because the air is compressing and the added stiffness wins. >> So wherever stiffness is what matters, frequency goes up, and wherever mass is what matters, frequency goes down. >> Moving from schwa into /i/ in a recording, F1 drops and F2 rises, exactly as predicted.
13|For **/a/**, the air is squeezed far back, about one third of the way from the closed end. In the first mode that spot is close to the closed end, and near the closed end there is always springiness, so the added stiffness wins and F1 goes up. In the second mode everything alternates, with compression near the closed end, compression further up, and the motion of the mass in between, right where /a/ squeezes, so the added mass wins and F2 goes down. >> So /a/ is the opposite of /i/, with F1 up and F2 down. >> Students who measured their own /a/ found a high F1 with an F2 not far above it, which fits the prediction.
14|Following one bit of air at a time separates the two features of each air mode. **Motion** is how far a bit of air travels back and forth, while **compression** is how much the spacing between neighboring bits of air changes. >> A group of air elements can travel far while staying evenly spaced, and elsewhere the elements can barely travel while their spacing changes a lot. >> In the schwa tube and in the separate rows for the first three modes, these two features alternate along the length, and that is what decides how each constriction affects each formant.
15|The **vocal folds** differ from person to person, and the rules for mass and stiffness predict their F0. Big, loose, heavy vocal folds have high mass and low stiffness, so their F0 is low and their harmonics are packed closely together. Tighter, lighter vocal folds give a higher F0, and very tight, light vocal folds give an even higher F0, with the harmonics spaced farther apart each time. >> So a lower F0 packs more harmonics into the same range of frequencies, and a higher F0 spaces them farther apart. || **F0 is not fixed during speech.** Only a robot keeps F0 constant, while real speech changes F0 all the time, in every vowel. >> The dog slept here, said as a statement, ends with F0 going down, and said as a question, it ends with F0 going up. >> Speakers change F0 by changing the stiffness of the vocal folds, and listeners use those changes to understand meaning, and even to tell when someone is sarcastic or pretending.
16|The air in the vocal tract has its own formants, and for schwa, with no constriction, they sit at the evenly spaced odd multiples that a uniform tube predicts. The exact values vary with how long the vocal tract is and how heavy and stiff the air is. **Formants are all about where the constriction is in the vocal tract.** || The formants interact with the harmonics by one **core rule**, which is that **a formant encourages the closest harmonics to be audible, while harmonics far from that formant are muted.** Harmonics near a formant get amplified, and harmonics farther away get weaker until they cannot be heard. >> So the shape of the vowel is the filter, the voiced source supplies the harmonics, and the filter chooses which harmonics survive. >> Compared with schwa, /u/ has both formants low, /i/ has a very low F1 and a very high F2, and /a/ has its first two formants close together.
17|**The interaction of source and filter is really important.** Picture a person with heavy, loose vocal folds, whose harmonics are closely spaced, saying schwa. There are lots of harmonics near the first formant, so the ones closest to it are amplified and those farther away get weaker, and near the second formant the harmonics are amplified again. >> So the lower F0 is, the more harmonics the formants have to shape. || Now picture very light, very stiff vocal folds, whose harmonics are widely spaced. The first formant may have no harmonic close by to amplify, so **sparse harmonics can miss a formant or make it look ambiguous**. >> **Opera singers** have such a high F0 that their formants barely line up with any harmonics, so listeners can hardly hear which vowels they are making. >> **Very small children** also have a very high F0, so their formants and harmonics line up so poorly that only their caretakers can easily follow them. || **F0 can be measured in Praat** by taking a spectral slice of a vowel and selecting the lowest harmonic. For a measured male voice, the next harmonic came out at about double the first, so the theory holds.
18|**A spectrum captures one instant, and a spectrogram stacks many spectra over time.** Time runs along the horizontal axis, frequency runs up the vertical axis, and darkness tells how much energy there is, with high amplitude black, medium gray and low white. So a formant appears as a dark band at its frequency, and its rise and fall over time can be followed. || Speech is not one steady vowel, so the formants are constantly changing. In the spectrogram of /ia/, the lowest band rises from /i/ to /a/, so F1 goes up, and the next band falls, so F2 goes down. >> These are the same directions as the vowel targets, since /i/ has a low F1 and a high F2 while /a/ has a high F1 and a lower F2.
19|A recording of **"Where were you a year ago?"** follows the same rules as single vowels, because real speech is a stream of changing sounds rather than one held vowel. Where F2 rises high, the speaker is making a sound like /i/, since squeezing the air near two thirds of the way up raises F2 and lowers F1. >> So /i/ is the classic vowel where F1 is low and F2 is high. >> In you, all the formants go down, just as /u/ predicts, and across the whole phrase the formants keep going up and down as each sound is made.
20|**One experiment reveals how complex listening to speech really is.** A speaker recorded the syllables ma and na, the m sound was cut out of ma, the a vowel was cut out of na, and the two pieces were pasted together. Since the consonant came from ma, the result should sound like ma, but **it sounds like na**. || **Listeners decide which consonant they heard mostly from the vowel that follows it.** In the a that follows m, the formants stay fairly stationary, while in the a that follows n, one formant drops sharply. That drop in a formant inside the vowel tells the listener n, so it overrides the m that was pasted in front. || **Speech perception does not decide sound by sound, slice by slice.** Listeners take in a whole stretch of speech and then decide what they heard, often deciding about a consonant later in time, during the vowel. >> For decades, speech recognition machines failed because they were built to recognize each little piece separately, the way people think they listen. >> An AI that understands speech has to be complex enough to use later information to decide about earlier sounds, and that is why large language models were needed for speech recognition.
21|A **neural network** can be trained on formants to decide which vowel is being spoken. The formants from each student's vowels in lab become the network's input, and the **W's**, which are the weights, contain the network's knowledge. >> Training adjusts the weights until the network makes good decisions about which vowel was produced. >> This is where the speech science and the AI come together, since F1 and F2 measured from spectrograms become the numbers the network learns from.
22|This network only multiplies and adds, with **no activation functions** between the layers. It takes two inputs, passes them through three hidden layers, and ends in five outputs, and the two inputs are F1 and F2, the frequencies of the first two modes of the air's vibration. >> Each hidden layer does **inner products**, multiplying the incoming numbers by its weights and adding them up, and so makes a new representation of the input. >> That is like information arriving at the ear and being processed by one part of the brain after another before the brain decides what it heard. >> The five outputs are /done/, /dune/, /dean/, /Don/ and /Dan/, with one neuron for each vowel.
23|The example input has a very low F1 and a very high F2, and **a very low F1 with a very high F2 is the pattern for /i/**, so a smart network should decide dean. Before training, every weight is a random number between negative one and one. >> A positive weight pushes the next neuron up, and a negative weight pushes it down. >> The size of a weight sets how strongly its input counts, and these random weights carry no knowledge about vowels yet.
24|In **forward flow**, the input flows forward through the network one layer at a time. Each neuron in the first layer multiplies F1 and F2 by its own weights and adds the products, and none of these calculations ever have to be done by hand. Those numbers pass through the next weights and the next, until the last layer gives the output, which is a probability distribution over the five words. >> The output puts the most probability on done and spreads the rest over the other four words, and since the values add up to one, it is a good probability distribution. >> But it says done when the vowel was /i/, so the weights are bad, the network is stupid before training, and the result is met with an angry face. || **Training is what fixes the bad weights.** The network is told the right answer as a distribution with all of its probability on dean and none on any other word, and **stochastic gradient descent** then changes each weight. >> The next time the same formants come in, the inner products should make the value for dean higher than all the others.
25|The same network can be written in **Python** using the **PyTorch** library. The code imports torch and torch.nn as nn, and it builds the network inside **nn.Sequential**, which means a sequence of layers that the numbers pass through one after another. Each layer is an **nn.Linear**, which says how many numbers the layer takes in and how many it gives out. >> The first layer takes two inputs and makes three outputs, the next takes three and makes two, the next takes two and makes four, and the last takes four and makes five, one for each vowel. >> Each layer's output size has to match the next layer's input size, so that the numbers can flow all the way through. || Setting the bias option to False removes the **bias**, an extra constant added to a neuron's sum, so each layer only multiplies and adds, just as in the diagram. >> This code is only a declaration, with no training, because it describes the network the way the machine sees it without feeding in formants or changing any weights.
