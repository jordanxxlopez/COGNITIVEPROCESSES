2|**The harmonic oscillator is a loop of two quantities, x and v, that keep changing each other.** The change of x over time equals v, so v drives x. The change of v over time equals the negative of x, so x drives v, but in the opposite direction. >> The dot over a letter means how much that quantity increases or decreases as time goes on. >> When x is high, v is pushed down, and when v is negative, x falls, so the two keep chasing each other around the loop.
3|**This loop is the basic dynamical system behind every oscillation, or vibration.** Each variable pushes on the other, so there is no single cause at the start and no single effect at the end. >> Whenever x reaches the middle, v is at its largest and carries x past it, and whenever v reaches zero, x is at its farthest point and turns v around. >> So the system never comes to rest on its own, and it keeps swinging back and forth.
4|**Vibration needs two physical effects working together**, a spring and a mass. The **spring** is the springiness found in many things, and its strength is called its **stiffness**. The **mass** is what carries motion once something is moving. >> Vibration is what is felt when a car hits a bump, it is music, it is the boom and bust of the economy, and it is the bouncing estimate of stochastic gradient descent with a high learning rate. >> It is also the motion of the air in the vocal tract and of the vocal folds during speech.
5|**A slice of air in the vocal tract behaves like a spring and a mass.** Where the bits of air are squeezed close together the pressure is high, and where they are spread far apart the pressure is low. >> Squeezed air pushes back to spread out again, which is its springiness, and moving air keeps moving, which is its mass. >> So the air in the mouth has both ingredients that vibration needs.
6|**A spring by itself gives what is called negative or balancing feedback.** In a system where the change of x is a negative fraction of x, a positive x makes x fall and a negative x makes x rise. Starting from a positive value, x falls by a fraction of its current size, then by a fraction of the smaller size, and so on, so it drifts toward zero more and more slowly. >> Starting from a negative value, it rises toward zero in the same gradual way. >> These are systems that move only in order to reach their **equilibrium**, the natural resting position, and the size of that fraction is the stiffness, so a stiffer spring gets there faster.
7|**A mass that is moving just keeps on moving.** A car traveling at a constant velocity covers the same distance every moment, without anything pushing it forward. >> This is the idea of inertia, worked out by Galileo, Descartes and Newton, that a mass in motion stays in motion. >> So mass is the opposite kind of effect from a spring, since a spring wants to stop at a place while a mass wants to keep going.
8|**A spring stops at a place, while a mass stops only after a process.** A spring with no mass, pulled away from its natural position, simply moves back and stops right at that position. Once a mass is attached, the mass reaches the natural position while it is still moving, so it keeps going and overshoots. >> The spring then pulls it back, and it overshoots again in the other direction. >> That combination of a spring that wants to go back and a mass that wants to keep moving is exactly what produces vibration.
9|**There is a cosine in the mouth.** The air pressure at a spot inside the vocal tract rises and falls in the smooth wave of a cosine, which is the same wave the x and v loop produces. >> But in a vowel the air does not vibrate at a single frequency. >> Several of these waves happen at the same time, and together they make up the sound of the vowel.
10|**Vowels are described by tongue height and tongue backness.** High vowels like the ones in dean and dune have the tongue raised, and low vowels like the ones in Dan and Don have it lowered. Front vowels like dean and Dan have the tongue pushed forward, while back vowels like dune and Don have it pulled back, with done in the central region. >> So dean and dune are both high vowels, and they differ because dean is front while dune is back, with the lips rounded as well. >> Changing the shape of the tract in these ways changes how the air inside it vibrates.

13|**Perturbation theory asks what happens to the frequency of a mass-spring system when one of its parts is changed.** If the mass is increased, the frequency decreases, because a heavier mass is harder to turn around. If the stiffness is increased, the frequency increases, because a stiffer spring pulls back harder. >> So raising the mass makes the system vibrate more slowly, and raising the stiffness makes it vibrate more quickly. >> These two rules are the basis for understanding how hearing and speech work.
14|**Hearing is explained by the two perturbation rules.** Inside the ear, the **basilar membrane** is heavy and not very stiff at one end, and light and very stiff at the other end. >> Since frequency goes down with mass and up with stiffness, the heavy, loose end vibrates at low frequencies and the light, stiff end vibrates at high frequencies. >> Each part of the membrane is connected to neurons, so the brain can tell which frequencies were heard from where along the membrane the vibration happened.
15|**Learning faces the same tradeoff between speed and accuracy.** A higher learning rate makes learning faster but less accurate, and a lower learning rate makes it more accurate but slower. >> With a high learning rate, the estimate keeps bouncing around the right answer, which is the oscillation of stochastic gradient descent. >> Raising the stiffness of a spring and raising the learning rate both make the system reach its goal faster.
