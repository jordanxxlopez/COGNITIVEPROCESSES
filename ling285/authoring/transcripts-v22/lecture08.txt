3|**A spring stops at a place, but a mass-spring system stops only after a process.** A spring with no mass, pulled away from its natural position, goes straight back to that position and stays there. Once a mass is attached, the mass arrives at the natural position while it is still moving, so it keeps going, overshoots and stretches or squishes the spring on the other side. >> The spring then pulls it back, it overshoots again, and the cycle repeats. >> So the spring supplies the goal of going back to equilibrium, and the mass supplies the motion that carries it past that goal, and together they vibrate.
4|**Perturbation theory says that raising the mass lowers the frequency and raising the stiffness raises it.** A heavier mass is harder to turn around, so the system vibrates more slowly, while a stiffer spring pulls back harder, so it vibrates more quickly. When people speak, they change the effective mass and stiffness of different portions of air in the vocal tract, differently for each vowel and consonant. >> So when the air is set vibrating by the vocal folds, it vibrates at many frequencies at once, and which frequencies they are depends on where and how much the air is squeezed. >> For a listener to understand speech, the ear has to work out which low, middle and high frequencies are present, so that the brain can tell, for example, that one set of frequencies means scene while another set means son.
5|**Sound reaches the brain through the ear.** Waves of vibration in the air arrive at the outer ear and travel inward to the inner ear, to a coiled part called the **cochlea**. The cochlea is the part that sends signals to the brain about which frequencies are in the sound. >> The many other names of the parts of the ear do not need to be memorized, but the cochlea does.
6|**Inside the cochlea is the basilar membrane.** Sound vibrations pass from the eardrum through three tiny bones into the coiled cochlea, and running along the inside of that coil is a long membrane called the **basilar membrane**. >> Uncoiled, the membrane would be a long strip, and its properties change gradually from one end to the other. >> That gradual change along its length is the key to how the ear separates frequencies.
7|**Hearing is explained by mass and stiffness changing along the basilar membrane.** One end of the membrane is heavy and not very stiff, while the other end is light and very stiff. Since frequency goes down with mass and up with stiffness, the heavy, loose end vibrates at low frequencies and the light, stiff end vibrates at high frequencies, with the frequencies in between laid out along the membrane. >> Each part of the membrane is hooked up to neurons that send signals to the brain. >> So the brain knows which frequencies were heard from where along the membrane the vibration happened, and this discovery won a Nobel Prize.
8|**The vocal folds are a mass-spring system that speakers can tune.** They are small muscles in the larynx, and like all muscle they have mass and stiffness, so other muscles attached to the surrounding cartilage can stretch them to make them stiffer or bunch them up to make them effectively heavier. >> Stiffer folds vibrate faster and heavier folds vibrate slower, so this control lets people sing, signal questions, mistrust, belief, surprise and emotion in English, and change the meanings of words in tone languages. >> The rate at which the vocal folds vibrate is called **pitch**, or **F0**, the fundamental frequency. || **The vocal folds vibrate because of air pressure and suction.** When air is taken into the lungs and the vocal folds are closed, pressure builds up beneath them until it blows them apart, and air flows between them. >> Just like a shower curtain being pulled inward, the flowing air sucks the folds back together, and then the pressure below blows them apart again. >> This repeats many times per second, and the suction is the same **Bernoulli force** that lifts airplanes.
9|**The harmonic oscillator can be solved step by step with simple arithmetic.** Starting with x at its high point and v at zero, each step uses the current v to nudge x forward a little, and then uses the new x to nudge v forward a little. Repeating this over and over builds up two lists of numbers, one for x and one for v. >> Plotted over time, x falls from its high point through zero to a low point and climbs back, while v dips negative and comes back up, a quarter of a cycle out of step. >> So a few lines of adding and multiplying trace out the same vibration that the reasoning in words predicts.
10|**The order of the updates matters.** In the explicit version, both new values are computed from the previous pair of x and v, rather than updating x first and then using the new x for v. With the same step size, the swings of x and v in this version grow larger and larger with every cycle. >> That growth is not something a real spring would do, because it is an error caused by the method and the size of the step. >> Taking smaller steps makes the error smaller, which is one more version of the tradeoff between speed and accuracy.
11|**Euler's explicit method solves differential equations using nothing but arithmetic.** Each new value is the old value plus its rate of change multiplied by a small time step. >> Rules about how things change over time can therefore be turned into a simple repeated calculation that a person or a computer can carry out. >> Calculations like this, done by hand, were once used to plan real space flights, and the same idea runs inside the computers that simulate speech, weather and much more.
