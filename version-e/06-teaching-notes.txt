2|**Psycholinguistics** aims to **discover the psychological processes by which humans acquire and process language**. The questions cover comprehension, production, representation, and acquisition. **Comprehension** is understanding a message; **production** is forming one. **Representation** concerns the stored and active information that makes language possible, while **acquisition** concerns how those abilities develop through learning.||**Comprehension:** how people understand **spoken and written language**. **Speech production:** how people **produce language**.||**Representation:** how language is represented **in the mind and brain**. **Acquisition:** how people **learn language**. The four terms distinguish understanding language, producing it, representing it, and learning it.
3|The two smallest units introduced are **phonemes** and **morphemes**. **Phonemes are the shortest segments of speech that, if changed, change the meaning of the word**.||The example **bit** separates the **b, vowel, and t sounds**. Replacing one of those sounds can distinguish it from another word; the phoneme itself need not have a meaning.||**Morphemes are the smallest units with meaning or grammatical function.** The sounds in **bit** can be separated into a beginning consonant, a vowel, and a final consonant. A change in one sound can produce a different word, whereas a morpheme must itself contribute meaning or grammatical function.
4|**Morphemes are the smallest units of language that have meaning or grammatical function.** The examples are **table: one morpheme; bedroom: two morphemes; bedrooms: three morphemes**.||**Bed** and **room** contribute meanings. The plural ending **-s** contributes grammatical information even though it is not a standalone word.||**Bedrooms** contains **bed + room + s**: two meaningful parts combine into bedroom, then the ending adds plural meaning. The count follows those contributions rather than the number of spoken syllables.
5|One striking feature of words is **how many we know and how rapidly we acquire them**. **Well-read adults can understand more than 50,000 different words**.||The **lexicon is our knowledge of the forms of words, which we use to make contact with meaning**. The **mental lexicon** proposes that forms are stored for reuse, an idea reaching back to the **nineteenth century**. An **auditory word representation** supports recognizing a heard form, a **concept** supplies its meaning, and a **motor word representation** supports producing the spoken form. The model links hearing, understanding, and speaking through those representations.||**Lichtheim observed neuropsychological dissociations between written and spoken word recognition.** The **spoken-word processing model** links **auditory word representations, concepts, and motor word representations**, with spoken input entering the auditory side and motor representations supporting output.
6|In the **word superiority experiment**, a **letter string flashes briefly and is then masked**. The sequence is **fixation → letter string, about 50 ms → mask → D/K choice**.||The same target letter **K** occurs in three conditions: **WORK, a word; K, a letter alone; and ORWK, a nonword**. The participant identifies which letter was present. After **WORK** disappears behind the mask, the participant chooses whether the relevant letter was **D or K**. The same K is harder to identify alone or in **ORWK**, even though the response still concerns just that letter.||**K is identified most accurately when it appears in the word.**
7|In **bottom-up word recognition**, **letters or phonemes are identified from features**, and **words are identified from letters or other constituents**. Activation proceeds from incoming evidence toward a word candidate.||In **top-down processing**, **activation from words feeds back to constituents**. A word-level candidate can support identification of its letters or phonemes.||Bottom-up information can activate a letter from its features and then a word from its letters. Top-down feedback returns support from the likely word to its uncertain component, helping the reader settle on the letter.
8|The **phonemic restoration effect** occurs when **phonemes are perceived in speech even though their sound is covered up**. Listeners **fill in missing phonemes based on sentence context and the portion of the word presented**.||With a masking **cough**, listeners may be unable to tell **when the cough takes place** relative to the missing segment. The experienced speech can seem complete despite the covered sound. A cough replaces part of the speech, yet the sentence can sound complete. The surrounding word and sentence make a particular missing sound expected, so the listener experiences that sound as part of the utterance.||This is **top-down processing**. **The meaning of words that follow the missing phoneme also influences which phoneme is perceived**, so restoration uses linguistic context rather than only the immediately available sound.
9|In the **word superiority effect**, a stimulus that is **a word, letter, or nonword is flashed briefly**, then **followed by a mask**. **Two letters are presented**, and the task is to **pick the letter that was in the flashed stimulus**.||**Letters are easier to recognize when contained in a word than when alone or in a nonword.** Word context improves identification of the constituent.||Both letter alternatives can complete a real word. The mask interrupts the brief letter-string display before the choice appears. The participant then chooses the letter that was actually in the string, and word context makes that identification more accurate.
10|The **interactive activation model** connects **feature, letter, and word levels**. Features activate compatible letter candidates, which in turn support compatible words.||The diagram includes word candidates such as **WEAR, WORK, WORD, and WEAK**. They compete according to the support provided by the visible features and letters. Features supporting **W**, **O**, and **R** help activate words compatible with those letters. A strong candidate such as **WORK** can send support back toward K while competing word candidates lose support.||**Word activation feeds back to constituents**, helping resolve uncertain letters. Compatible candidates support one another, while competing candidates can inhibit one another. This connects bottom-up evidence with top-down word knowledge.
11|Word-recognition research collects data from public sources to create **a large representative sample of utterances or written text, called a corpus**. A corpus indicates the frequency of **words, different meanings, and grammatical constructions**.||This is useful because much of language comprehension concerns **prediction**, and the ability to perceive written words depends partly on **how frequently they appear in the language**. A **corpus** is a collected sample of language use. Counting a word's occurrences relative to the sample's size gives its frequency; the common words in the list occur much more often than less frequent words.||The **Speer (2022), wordfreq** estimates list occurrences per million: **the 53,703; to 26,915; and 25,704; of 25,119; a 22,909; in 18,621; I 12,303; is 11,749; for 10,233; that 10,233**. The data are pooled across several corpora.
12|**What does it mean for a word to be frequent “in the language,” and what would you count to find out?** Counts of superlatives over the years depend on which words, sources, and periods are included.||The **Google Books Ngram Viewer** samples printed books over time. The frequency describes how often the word occurs in those books.||A frequency is a count relative to some amount of language. For books across years, the count concerns occurrences in the books included for each period, which is the language experience represented by that graph.
13|The **word frequency effect** means **responding more rapidly to high-frequency words**. The example contrasts a faster response to **home** with a slower response to **hike**.||The estimates are **home: 646 occurrences per million; hike: 9**, using **Speer (2022), wordfreq**. In a **lexical-decision task**, participants read words and nonwords silently and respond **yes when the string is a word**. **HOME** is accepted quickly, **HIKE** more slowly, and **HIKM** receives **no**. In **lexical decision**, the question is whether the string is a real word. **HOME** and **HIKE** both require yes, but the more frequent HOME is accepted faster; **HIKM** requires no.||During reading, **low-frequency words are looked at longer**. Reaction time and fixation duration therefore provide related evidence about frequency and processing.
14|**Words learned early in life are recognized and named faster, even when frequency is matched.** This is the **age-of-acquisition, or AoA, effect**.||The early/later pairs are **doll/thesis**, **puppy/clause**, and **cookie/audit**. Their **Zipf frequencies** are approximately **4.06/4.05**, **4.00/3.99**, and **4.07/4.04**, respectively.||The **Zipf scale** expresses **log10 frequency per billion words**. **Age of acquisition** is when a word was learned. Matching frequency means the paired words occur at approximately the same rate, while differing in how early they became part of the person's vocabulary.
15|**Lexical ambiguity means words have more than one meaning.** **“I saw her duck”** can mean seeing her bird or seeing her lower her head or body.||**Some meanings are more likely.** As **Traxler (2012)** puts it, **“Many words have multiple meanings, but these meanings are not all created equal.”**||**Duck** can name the animal or describe the action of lowering the body. The same word form therefore activates more than one possible meaning, and the sentence helps determine which is intended.
16|**Words can have more than one meaning**, as in **“I saw her duck.”** A context involving birds supports the animal reading; a context involving avoiding something supports the action reading.||**“These meanings are not all created equal” (Traxler, 2012):** more frequent meanings can be more accessible.||A bird-related setting favors the noun interpretation of **duck**. A setting involving avoiding something favors the action, so understanding depends on connecting the word with the surrounding situation.
17|**Meaning dominance is the fact that some meanings of words are used more frequently than others.** It affects how meanings are accessed.||**Biased dominance** occurs when meanings have **different dominance**: **TIN** favors **metal** over **food container**. **Balanced dominance** occurs when meanings have **about the same dominance**: **CAST** can mean **a member of a play** or **a plaster cast**.||A **dominant meaning** is the meaning used more often. **Biased** ambiguity has one strongly favored meaning, whereas **balanced** ambiguity has alternatives with more similar frequencies.
18|The **dependent variable is fixation duration**. In **“the cast worked…”**, **both meanings of cast are activated**, and readers **look longer at cast than at the control word cook**.||In **“the tin was…”**, the **dominant meaning of tin** is favored. Readers can read this **biased ambiguous word as fast as a control word**.||**Fixation duration** is how long the eyes remain on the word. The balanced meanings of **cast** compete enough to lengthen viewing, while the dominant meaning of **tin** can be accessed quickly in a neutral setting.
19|**Meaning frequency is not the only factor determining accessibility; context also plays a major role.** In **“The miners went to the store and saw that they had beans in a tin,”** the context indicates the **less frequent container meaning**.||**Both meanings become activated**, and processing takes longer than the control **cup**. Context supports the subordinate meaning while the dominant metal meaning can remain available.||In **beans in a tin**, the sentence requires a container, not the metal as a material. Selecting that less frequent meaning takes additional processing while the common meaning is also activated.
20|**Components of language are not processed in isolation.** To explain sentence meaning, distinguish **semantics: meanings of words and sentences**, from **syntax: rules for combining words into sentences**.||**“The cats won’t eat”** is acceptable. Changing it to **“The cats won’t bake”** creates a **semantic anomaly** in the intended situation, while changing it to **“The cats won’t eating”** creates a **syntax error** in verb form.||**Semantics** asks what the sentence means; **syntax** concerns how its words are grammatically arranged. **Won't bake** has the expected grammatical form but an odd meaning for cats, whereas **won't eating** uses the wrong verb form.
21|Patient evidence distinguishes functions associated with **Broca’s area in the frontal lobe** and **Wernicke’s area in the temporal lobe**.||**Broca’s aphasia** includes **slow, labored, ungrammatical speech** and problems understanding some sentence structures. **“The apple was eaten by the girl”** can be understood with plausible roles; **“The boy was pushed by the girl”** requires syntax to establish who acted on whom. In **“The boy was pushed by the girl,”** word order and grammar establish that the girl did the pushing. Both people could plausibly push, so identifying their roles requires using the sentence structure.||**Wernicke’s aphasia** includes **meaningless speech** and difficulty **understanding speech and writing**, including both example sentences. The contrast relates different patterns of impairment to syntax and semantics.

The schematic comparison shows an **acceptable sentence**, a **semantic violation associated with N400**, and a **syntactic violation associated with P600**. **N400** names a negative response around 400 milliseconds after the critical word; **P600** names a positive response around 600 milliseconds. The letter indicates polarity and the number indicates approximate timing.||The horizontal axis is **time after the critical word in milliseconds**, and the vertical axis is **microvolts, negative plotted upward**. N400 is around **400 ms** and P600 around **600 ms**. The curves are labeled **schematic, not real data**.
23|The **P600 response is associated with structures in the frontal lobe**, and frontal damage can reduce it in the comparison described. Its example contrasts **“The spoiled child throws the toy on the floor”** with **“The spoiled child throw the toy on the floor”**: the second creates a grammatical agreement problem.||The **N400 response is associated with structures in the temporal lobe**, and temporal damage can reduce it. Its example contrasts **“Whipped cream tastes sweet”** with **“Whipped cream tastes anxious”**: the second creates a semantic anomaly.||The graphs plot **microvolt amplitude over milliseconds**. **Anxious** conflicts with the expected meaning of whipped cream tasting sweet, producing the semantic response. **Child throw** conflicts with the expected agreement in **child throws**, producing the syntactic response.
24|**Parsing is the mental grouping of words in a sentence into phrases**, a central process for determining sentence meaning.||**Garden-path sentences begin by appearing to mean one thing, then end up requiring another interpretation.** After **“After the musician played the piano…”**, **“she left the stage”** or **“she bowed to the audience”** fits the first parse. At first, **the piano** seems to be what the musician played. The words **was wheeled** require the piano to become the thing being wheeled away, so the phrase boundary must move to after **played**.||**“…was wheeled off the stage”** instead requires **“After the musician played, the piano was wheeled off the stage.”** The piano becomes the subject of the later clause.||This is **temporary ambiguity**: the initial words are ambiguous, but **the meaning is made clear by the end of the sentence**. The later input requires reparsing.
25|**“The horse raced past the barn fell.”** Initially, raced can be interpreted as the sentence’s main action, making fell difficult to attach.||The complete reading is **“The horse [that was raced past the barn] fell.”** The reduced relative clause describes the horse; **fell** is the main verb.||**Raced past the barn** describes which horse is meant: the horse that someone raced. The horse's main action in the full sentence is **fell**, which becomes clear only after revising the first interpretation.
26|**“The horse raced past the barn fell”** and **“The burglar discovered behind the bushes fled”** both create garden paths through an initially plausible main-verb reading.||For the burglar sentence, the complete interpretation is **“The burglar [who was discovered behind the bushes] fled.”** Discovered belongs to the reduced relative description, while **fled** supplies the main action.||**Discovered behind the bushes** describes the burglar who was found there. The burglar then **fled**, so discovered belongs inside the description rather than serving as the main action of the complete sentence.
27|**Sentences with temporary ambiguity help reveal the mechanisms operating during parsing.** In the **syntax-first approach**, words are initially grouped into phrases by **rules based on syntax**.||If readers realize something is wrong with the parse, they **take other information into account to reinterpret the sentence**. **Late closure** means the parser assumes the **new word is part of the current phrase**, adding words there for as long as possible. **Late closure** keeps a new word in the phrase currently being built. In the piano example, this initially attaches piano to played, and the later continuation forces that attachment to be changed.||The sequence is **read the next word → attach it by syntax rules → check whether the parse works**. If it works, continue; if not, **reanalyze using other information**.
28|The piano sentence initially permits **“[After the musician played the piano]…”**. Piano is attached to played as its object.||Continuing with **was** and **wheeled** makes that grouping fail: **“[After the musician played the piano] [was]”** cannot support the intended complete interpretation. The repaired sentence has two parts: **after the musician played** establishes the earlier event, and **the piano was wheeled off the stage** states what happened next. The repair changes grouping rather than changing the words.||The repair changes the boundary to **“[After the musician played] [the piano was wheeled off the stage].”** The same words are regrouped so piano becomes the subject of the main clause.
