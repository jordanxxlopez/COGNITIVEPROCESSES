2|**“You have never once been in contact with the world—only with your brain’s reconstruction of it.”** The perceptual experience is the result of the brain’s processing of sensory information.||A **reconstruction** is the experienced interpretation produced from sensory input. It can be useful because the environment contains recurring relationships that make some interpretations much more plausible than others.||**“So why is the reconstruction almost always right?”** The explanation connects that processing with regularities in the world. Sensory evidence and knowledge of those regularities help the brain select a useful interpretation even when the input is ambiguous.
3|**“What do you see?”** The black-and-white pattern by **R. C. James** contains a **Dalmatian dog**. At first, its patches may not appear to form a recognizable animal.||The example separates the physical pattern from its interpretation. Recognizing the dog supplies an organization for patches that previously seemed disconnected, making the same sensory information easier to understand.||Once the dog is recognized, the patches are organized as parts of the dog and its surroundings. The retinal pattern remains the same, but knowing what to look for changes the experienced organization.
4|**Perception is the experience every other cognitive process is built on top of.** It provides information that attention, memory, language, and reasoning can use.||Calling perception a foundation means that later processing uses information perception has already organized. What is remembered or described is therefore an interpretation of sensory input.||What is attended, remembered, described, or reasoned about therefore depends partly on how sensory information has been perceived. Perception provides an interpreted experience for those other processes to use.
5|**Perception is experience resulting from stimulation of the senses.** It is **the gateway to every other cognition**: attention, memory, language, and reasoning all take perception as input.||Four properties matter. **Perceptions change when information is added. Perception involves a process much like reasoning or problem solving. Perceptions occur in conjunction with actions. Prior knowledge shapes how we resolve ambiguous stimuli—usually correctly, sometimes not.** **Ambiguous** means compatible with more than one interpretation. Adding information or bringing relevant knowledge to the input can change which interpretation becomes most plausible.||The same retinal image can be experienced differently before and after knowing what to look for. **Perception depends jointly on the incoming sensory signal and on the knowledge the perceiver brings to it.**
6|**Perception is fast and effortless, so it feels automatic**, but the processing supporting that experience is largely invisible to us. Roughly **half the primate cortex is devoted to vision alone**, and some human perceptual abilities may have no equivalent elsewhere.||**Machine perception has repeatedly fallen short.** Each generation of systems solves a previous failure and inherits a new one. Formal reasoning—**chess, proofs, and algebra**—became manageable for machines decades before reliable object recognition. Feeling effortless describes the experience, not the amount of processing required. The difficulty of building a perceiving machine makes the hidden work of everyday recognition easier to appreciate.||The **DARPA Urban Challenge (2007)** demonstrates the difficulty of robot vehicles interpreting a changing environment. If perception were simple, it would have been the easy part of artificial intelligence; instead it has been a persistent challenge.
7|In **2007**, machine vision relied on **hand-written rules and sensor fusion**. Vehicles finished a **60-mile urban course**, slowly and on a **closed course**.||**Now**, deep networks trained on **millions of labeled images** match or exceed human accuracy on benchmark object recognition. **Still**, four problems remain: **novel viewpoints, occlusion, unusual lighting, and scenes whose context is odd**. **Occlusion** means that something is hidden by another object, and a **novel viewpoint** is an unfamiliar viewing angle. Both change the available image even when the object's identity remains the same.||The gap is in **interpretation under ambiguity**, not just sensing: cameras can out-resolve the eye. A system can label a thousand stop signs and still miss one with stickers on it. Humans often adjust to a strange scene, while a trained network can fail confidently.
8|**Why is it so hard to build a perceiving machine?** The visual system solves problems involving **inverse projection, hidden or blurred objects, and different viewpoints** before we notice there was a problem.||These are related recognition problems: determining the source of an image, recognizing an incompletely visible object, and preserving identity when the viewing angle changes. Each requires more than matching one complete picture.
9|The **inverse projection problem** is **the task of determining which object produced a particular image on the retina**. We start with the retinal image and work outward to its source.||At a given viewing arrangement, an object produces a projection, but **one image could have come from infinitely many objects**. **A small object nearby and a huge object far away can produce identical retinal images**; different sources along a projection line can fit the same evidence. **Projection** maps a three-dimensional scene onto a two-dimensional retinal image. **Inverse projection** works in the opposite direction, from the image back toward a possible three-dimensional source.||Perception is therefore **an inference about what most likely caused the image**, rather than a unique source being read directly from the image.
10|**Most objects in a normal scene are partly occluded by other objects**, yet people identify them from fragments. We also recognize objects that are **blurred, poorly lit, or seen at the edge of vision**.||The visual system treats **a partial view as a partial view**, rather than as a new shape. It must determine which edges belong to the same object even though that ownership is not explicitly labeled in the image. An **occluder** is the object blocking the view. Completion groups the visible portions as parts of something continuing behind it, explaining how a partly hidden object can still appear coherent.||**Perceptual completion** means the visual system **extends contours behind an occluder**. A wheelbarrow behind a wall is still experienced as **one wheelbarrow**, not as unrelated visible fragments.
11|**Viewpoint invariance is the ability to recognize an object as the same object across changes in viewing angle.** Every rotation produces a physically different retinal image.||We have never seen most objects from most angles, yet recognize them anyway. This is difficult for machine vision but occurs readily in young children. **One object can have many projections while remaining “the same.”**||**Invariance** means that recognition remains stable despite a change in the image. The stable property is the recognized identity; the light pattern reaching the eye can be different at each angle.
12|There are **two directions of processing**: information flows **up from the receptors** and **down from what you already know**. These are bottom-up and top-down contributions.||The directions describe where an influence begins. Sensory receptors supply bottom-up information, while knowledge already available to the perceiver supplies top-down influence on how that information is understood.||The incoming signal supplies sensory evidence, while knowledge, expectations, and attention influence how that evidence is interpreted. Both can contribute to the same perception, rather than defining two completely separate kinds of experience.
13|**Bottom-up processing starts at the “bottom” of the system, where environmental energy stimulates the receptors.** It is also called **data-driven processing**.||The sequence is **light reflected from Mary → image on the retina → signals in the optic nerve → signals in the brain**. More generally, **stimulus → receptors → neural signal → representation**.||The incoming chain does not require already knowing Mary. A **receptor** responds to environmental energy and begins converting it into neural activity. The representation at the end of the chain depends on processing that starts with this stimulation.
14|**Top-down processing originates in the brain, at the “top” of the perceptual system.** It is also called **knowledge-driven processing**.||The perceiver brings **knowledge—what you already know about Mary; expectation—what you predict you are about to see; and attention—which features you sample**. These influence the interpretation of incoming evidence.||**Bottom-up and top-down are two contributions to perception.** Knowledge supplies familiar possibilities, expectation favors what is anticipated, and attention affects what is sampled. These are ways existing mental activity can influence the interpretation of a new input.
15|**Direct perception theories** emphasize that **perception comes from the stimuli available in the environment**. Parts can be identified and assembled before recognition; the information in light can be rich enough to specify the world. **Gibson** emphasizes an **optic array** available to a moving observer that specifies layout.||**Constructive perception theories** emphasize that people **actively construct perceptions using expectations**. Knowledge, experience, and context do part of the work: **the percept is built, not received**. Illusions show that the percept can depart from the stimulus. An **optic array** is the patterned light available to the observer. Moving changes that pattern and reveals information about layout; constructive explanations emphasize how interpretation also draws on what the observer knows.||The combined position is that **perception is constructive, but constrained by information the environment reliably provides**.
16|**Irving Biederman’s recognition-by-components theory (1987)** proposes that we perceive objects by first perceiving **elementary volumetric features**. These building blocks, called **geons**, combine into recognizable objects.||The stages move from identifying features and components to recognizing their arrangement as an object. The same kinds of parts can form different objects when arranged differently. **Volumetric** means three-dimensional. A geon is a basic three-dimensional component, and its arrangement with other components helps distinguish one recognized object from another.||Approximately **36 geons** are proposed, and **three geons in the right arrangement identify most common objects**. Recognizing components can therefore support a **partial or novel view** without requiring an exact match to one full image.
17|An **image region lifted out of a photograph and shown on its own** may be difficult to identify. The isolated patch supplies only its local shape, shading, and texture.||The isolated region has less identifying information than the full scene. Its uncertainty can be reduced when its position and surrounding objects indicate what kind of thing would fit there.
18|In **Torralba and Oliva’s** demonstration, **the circled blobs are pixel-for-pixel identical**, yet they are perceived as **a shoe, a car, and a pedestrian**.||**Orientation and context did the identifying, not the shape.** The same region receives a different identity according to the scene around it. The local evidence remains identical while the surrounding scene changes. Context therefore changes the candidate identity assigned to the region, which is a top-down contribution to recognition.||**Knowledge affects perception** because scene information makes different objects plausible in different positions and settings. Recognition cannot be explained by the isolated blob’s features alone.
19|**Speech segmentation is the ability to tell where one word ends and the next begins.** Speech has almost no physical gaps that consistently mark word boundaries. The apparent spaces between words depend on interpreting the signal.||In **“I hope it’s in my desk,”** the physical sound does not insert a clean gap at each printed word boundary. An unfamiliar language can therefore sound like **an unbroken stream**. **Segmentation** divides a continuous signal into meaningful units. Knowing the language helps the listener identify likely words even when silence does not neatly separate them.||**Understanding which word you heard from context is top-down processing.** Language knowledge helps organize continuous acoustic input into words.
20|In **spectral rotation**, the same sentence is played **once rotated in frequency and once intact**. In the **rotated version on the left**, only the longest pauses stand out; in the **intact version on the right**, words feel temporally separate.||**The temporal envelope of the sound is the same in both**. The frequency structure changes, but new pauses have not been inserted between words.||The **temporal envelope** describes how the sound's strength changes over time. Keeping that timing pattern while changing frequency structure helps separate physical pauses from the listener's experience of word boundaries.
21|The **direct pathway model** is an early account in which **nociceptors in the skin send pain messages straight to the brain**. A purely bottom-up account predicts that **pain intensity should track tissue damage**.||**Expectation, attention, and distraction all change how much pain is experienced.** Identical stimulation can produce different pain depending on what the person believes is happening. **Nociceptors** are sensory receptors involved in signaling potentially damaging stimulation. **Nociception** refers to that processing; the experienced pain also reflects the expectation and attention effects described here.||In **Atlas et al. (2012, Journal of Neuroscience)**, when participants could see pain medication being delivered, **activity increased in frontal regions and decreased in areas associated with nociception**. The experience therefore includes cognitive influences as well as incoming stimulation.
22|**Perception as inference** addresses ambiguity: **if the image is ambiguous, the percept has to be a bet** about what caused it. More than one source can fit the available evidence.||An **inference** selects an explanation from evidence. In perception, the evidence is the sensory input, and the explanation is the object or event that could have produced it.||**Helmholtz’s unconscious inference** and **Bayesian inference** describe how that selection can use experience and current input. The system favors an interpretation rather than deriving a uniquely specified object from the retinal image alone.
23|**Helmholtz’s theory of unconscious inference, around 1860**, is a top-down account: **some perceptions result from unconscious assumptions we make about the environment**. We use what we know to inform what we see.||The **likelihood principle** says **we perceive the world in the way that is most likely, given our past experience**. This addresses the inverse projection problem, where many objects can produce the same image. The **likelihood principle** favors the interpretation most consistent with past experience. The process need not feel like reasoning because the assumptions are applied without conscious inspection.||The inference is **unconscious and involuntary**: we cannot inspect it or decline it. **“Unconscious” means inaccessible, not Freudian**; knowing about an illusion does not necessarily switch off its appearance.
24|For retinal image **(a)**, source **(b)** is **two overlapping rectangles**. Source **(c)** is **a rectangle and an oddly notched shape, aligned exactly right**. Both are geometrically consistent with the image.||Almost everyone chooses **(b)**. The notched alternative requires a precise coincidence, and **coincidences are rare in our experience**.||The notched arrangement requires separate edges to line up just right. Overlapping rectangles provide an ordinary cause of the same visible boundaries, so experience favors that interpretation.
25|**Bayesian inference makes the likelihood principle quantitative.** A **prior probability** is what you believed before the stimulus arrived, built from exposure to how the world usually is—for example, **light usually comes from above**.||The **likelihood** is how well each candidate interpretation would explain the stimulus actually received. **Both a bump and a dent can fit the shading**. The **prior** is the starting probability, the **likelihood** is the support the current evidence gives a candidate, and the **posterior** is the updated probability. They play different roles in the same inference.||The **posterior** combines prior and likelihood to give an updated interpretation. Formally, the posterior is **proportional to prior × likelihood**, with normalization. A strong prior can favor one reading of an ambiguous image, and different environmental experience can change those priors.
26|**The stimulus** is a disc **light at the top and dark at the bottom**. **The likelihood** is ambiguous: **a bump lit from above and a dent lit from below produce the identical image**. Neither explanation is uniquely selected by that evidence.||**The prior** favors light coming from above, a common pattern in the environment. **The posterior** therefore favors seeing **a bump**, immediately and without a conscious choice. Shading depends on both shape and illumination. The light-from-above assumption helps resolve that joint ambiguity by favoring the shape consistent with the usual direction of light.||**Turn the picture upside down**: bumps can become dents and dents bumps. Rotation changes which interpretation fits the **light-from-above prior**, even though the depicted object’s physical shape has not changed.
27|**Perceptual organization** is the process by which the visual field is **carved into objects before they are identified**. Regions and contours must be grouped into candidate objects.||**Organization** establishes which features belong together; **recognition** establishes what the resulting object is. Grouping contours into a unit therefore helps make that unit available for identification.||The image does not explicitly label which features belong together. **Gestalt laws of perceptual organization** describe systematic ways the mind groups the field into coherent units.
28|In **Wundt’s structuralism**, **overall experience is built by combining basic elements called sensations**. Perception involves **adding up sensations**.||Movement would be constructed by **summing snapshots of an object in different locations over time**, and a face would be the sum of the dots composing it. **Muybridge’s sequence of stills** makes the motion-as-snapshots idea concrete. A **sensation** is an elementary component of experience in this account. The structuralist proposal explains the whole by combining those components, which makes the organization of that combination the central issue.||The claim is that perception can be analyzed into parts and the whole is nothing more than those parts added together. Gestalt psychologists challenged whether that addition explains the organized experience.
29|The **Gestalt psychologists, around 1912**, rejected the idea that **perceptions are formed by adding up sensations**. **The mind groups patterns according to laws of perceptual organization**.||In **apparent movement**, movement is perceived even though no object physically moves through the experienced path. In the ring demonstration, the changing gap appears to move around the ring. **Apparent movement** means movement is experienced without an object physically traversing the perceived path. The organization across successive displays creates the motion percept.||**The whole is different from the sum of its parts—not merely greater, different.** The experienced organization includes a property that cannot be explained as literal motion of an object between positions.
30|**Good continuation** means that **lines tend to be seen as following the smoothest path**. Points connecting into **straight or smoothly curving lines** are seen as belonging together.||A contour disappearing behind an object is experienced as **continuing behind it**. In the rope display, perception follows a continuous route through crossings rather than switching arbitrarily between segments. At a crossing, good continuation favors following the smooth route rather than turning abruptly onto another line. The principle groups segments as one continuing contour.||Following **one cable in a tangle** or **one line on a subway map** uses the same organizing principle.
31|**Prägnanz**, also called **simplicity or good figure**, means that a stimulus pattern is organized so the resulting structure is **as simple as possible**.||The **Olympic symbol** is seen as **five overlapping circles**, rather than **nine irregular closed shapes fitted together**, even though that alternative is also consistent with the lines. **Prägnanz** favors a simple overall organization. Five complete circles provide a simpler description of the Olympic pattern than a set of unusual pieces fitted together.||Both Gestalt and Helmholtz select among interpretations that fit: **Gestalt picks the simplest; Helmholtz picks the most likely**. In ordinary scenes those choices often agree.
32|**Similarity** means **similar things appear grouped together**. In panel **(a)**, all the dots are red; in **(b)**, varying the color makes the display reorganize into columns. The geometry stays the same while the coloring differs.||Grouping can depend on **color, shape, size, orientation, and lightness**. A feature relationship can change which elements are perceived as a unit without changing their positions. The objects do not need to move for their grouping to change. Changing a shared feature such as color makes some elements appear to belong together more strongly than others.||**Grouping happens before recognition: organization first, identification second.** The field is already organized into candidate objects by the time those objects are identified.
33|In the beach scene, **good continuation** makes **the horizon and waterline appear as continuous lines behind the figure**. **Similarity** groups **sand with sand, water with water, and sky with sky**.||**Prägnanz** favors **three simple horizontal bands**, rather than a mosaic of colored patches. **Figure–ground** separates **one person against a background**. **Figure–ground** distinguishes an object from what lies behind it. The person can be the figure while the horizon, surfaces, and colors are grouped into a coherent background.||**Common region and proximity** are also present. Several grouping principles can operate together in the same scene; there are more than four Gestalt laws.
34|The **Gestalt position** treats the laws as **intrinsic—built into the visual system**. The role of experience is minor, so the laws should be **universal across cultures and present very early in life**.||An experience-based challenge is that **smooth contours and similar-looking surfaces really do tend to belong to the same object**. The principles may reflect **statistical regularities the system has absorbed**.||**Gestalt laws are accurate because the environment is regular.** **Intrinsic** means part of the system itself, and **innate** means built in rather than acquired through learning. The theoretical contrast concerns where grouping principles come from.
35|**The world is not random. Perception exploits that.** Recurring arrangements and properties provide regularities that make some interpretations more probable than others.||A **regularity** is a pattern that recurs often enough to guide an expectation. Using that pattern helps perception choose among possibilities when the immediate image leaves uncertainty.||These include **physical regularities**, such as common orientations and lighting, and **semantic regularities**, such as objects typical of a kitchen. Perception can use them without a conscious decision on each occasion.
36|Perception is shaped by **knowledge of regularities in the environment**, connecting modern accounts with Helmholtz’s unconscious inference.||**Physical regularities are regularly occurring physical properties**: **verticals and horizontals are more common than obliques; light comes from above; surfaces are mostly uniformly colored; objects tend to be opaque and rigid**. A physical regularity concerns properties such as orientation or illumination. A semantic regularity concerns meaning and function, such as which objects belong in a scene used for cooking.||**Semantic regularities are characteristics associated with the functions carried out in different types of scenes**. **Kitchens contain stoves, kettles, and sinks; offices contain screens and chairs; streets contain cars, signs, and pedestrians**.||Physical regularities concern **how the world is put together**. Semantic regularities concern **what the world is used for** and which objects are probable in that setting.
37|The **oblique effect** means **people perceive verticals and horizontals more easily than other orientations**: better acuity, faster detection, and more accurate orientation judgments.||The environment is full of verticals and horizontals, including **forests and rock formations as well as buildings**. This is not merely an artifact of modern cities. **Oblique** means tilted away from vertical or horizontal. The perceptual advantage for common orientations connects the kinds of edges frequently encountered with what the visual system detects most readily.||**More cortical neurons are tuned to vertical and horizontal than to oblique orientations.** An environmental regularity is associated with both a behavioral advantage and a neural-tuning bias.
38|The **light-from-above assumption** is that **light comes from above**, as it usually does in the environment. **Shading is therefore read as depth**.||**Light on top, shadow below → a bump protruding toward you. Shadow on top, light below → an indentation.** These readings depend on the assumed illumination. A **bump** protrudes toward the viewer; an **indentation** recedes into the surface. The same light–dark pattern can support either shape when a different light direction is assumed.||Panels **(a) and (b)** are **the same image, one rotated 180 degrees**. Rotation changes which depth reading the prior favors, producing opposite percepts from the same photographed pattern.
39|The sand sculpture permits two readings: **shapes raised out of the sand or carved into it**. The light and shadow support an interpretation of the surface’s depth.||The photograph contains shading rather than a direct label identifying raised and recessed surfaces. An assumed light direction supplies the information needed to choose a depth reading.
40|**One image, one rotation, two incompatible percepts.** The sand did not change; **the photograph was turned and nothing else**.||What changed is **which interpretation is consistent with light arriving from above**. Raised-looking shapes can become carved-looking shapes, and the reverse can occur too.||The reversal comes from interpreting the reoriented shading. The display changes relative to the assumed overhead light, making a different surface arrangement appear consistent with it.
41|A **scene schema** is **knowledge of what a given kind of scene typically contains and where those things typically are**. It is built through **repeated exposure to scenes of that type**.||Imagining a kitchen commonly brings up **a stove, a sink, and cabinets**, even when those objects were not specified. The schema supplies expectations in imagined and actual kitchens. A **schema** is organized knowledge about a familiar kind of situation. A scene schema connects a setting with the objects and locations that ordinarily occur there.||We approach a scene expecting **a short list of likely objects in likely places**. An unfamiliar room’s **door handle** is sought in plausible positions rather than on the ceiling.||**Physical regularities constrain what shapes and surfaces are possible; semantic regularities constrain what objects are probable, given where you are.**
42|**Palmer (1975): Participants saw a scene, then a briefly flashed object.** Objects **congruent with the scene—a loaf of bread in a kitchen—were identified far more accurately than incongruent ones, such as a mailbox in the same kitchen**. Scene context changes recognition accuracy.||**Potter (1976): Pictures were presented in rapid succession, around an eighth of a second each.** Observers could still say whether a described target scene had appeared. **The gist of a scene arrives faster than its details.** **Congruent** means fitting the scene, while **incongruent** means conflicting with it. The Palmer comparison concerns how that fit changes object identification; Potter's **gist** is the rapidly available overall meaning of a scene.||**Torralba and Oliva: Identical image regions read as a shoe, a car, and a pedestrian according to the scene around them.** The same local patch receives different interpretations with different surrounding context.||**The scene supplied a short list of probable objects. The blob was matched against that list**, rather than every object the observer knows. Context helps constrain which identity fits the available region.
43|**“Perceptions occur in conjunction with actions.”** Perception guides what we do, and what we do changes the information available to perception.||Action changes what can be sensed next. A new viewing angle or a closer inspection supplies new evidence, which can then guide the next movement.||Moving around an object, reaching for it, or changing the viewing angle supplies evidence unavailable in one stationary image. **We perceive in order to act, and we act in order to perceive better.**
44|**Perception is not a still photograph delivered to a stationary observer.** We **move around objects, pick them up, turn them over, and change our vantage point**.||Movement helps resolve ambiguity: near and far objects move differently across the visual field; moving around an occluder reveals a hidden part; and **a sequence of viewpoints specifies shape that no single viewpoint does**.||In **motion parallax**, **near objects sweep past while distant ones drift slowly**, as when looking from a moving car. The relative speeds provide depth information.||**Perceiving and acting are continuous with each other.** **Motion parallax** is a difference in apparent movement produced as the observer moves. Nearby and distant surfaces shift at different rates, providing information about their relative depth.
45|The **ventral “what” pathway** runs **occipital lobe → temporal lobe** and supports **identifying objects**. Damage can impair recognition while leaving visually guided reaching relatively intact. It is also called the **perception pathway**.||The **dorsal “where/how” pathway** runs **occipital lobe → parietal lobe** and supports **locating objects and guiding action toward them**. Damage can impair reaching and grasping while recognition survives. It is also called the **action pathway**. **Ventral** names the route toward temporal regions; **dorsal** names the route toward parietal regions. The functional distinction links object identification with the former and spatially guided action with the latter.||**Ungerleider and Mishkin (1982)** established the division through **lesion work in monkeys**. **Milner and Goodale** argued that **“how” fits better than “where.”** The streams interact: the division is one of emphasis, not complete independence.
46|**Patient D.F. had ventral damage following carbon monoxide poisoning.** Asked to **report a slot’s orientation or match it with a held card**, she could not do it accurately. Asked to **post the card through the same slot**, she rotated her hand accurately and posted it.||**Mirror neurons are premotor neurons that fire both when an action is performed and when the same action is observed.** Perceiving an action and producing it can therefore draw on shared machinery. A **dissociation** separates two abilities that might otherwise seem like the same skill. D.F.'s accurate posting with impaired orientation judgment separates using visual information for action from explicitly reporting it.||D.F. demonstrates that **knowing what something is and being able to act on it are separable abilities**. Orientation information supported her action even when she could not use it for an explicit judgment.
47|**The image underdetermines the world.** Inverse projection, occlusion, and viewpoint mean that a retinal image can fit many possible scenes. **Perception resolves the ambiguity** using knowledge, expectation, attention, and context.||**Both directions contribute:** bottom-up and top-down processing are contributions to perception. **Perception is inference:** Helmholtz’s likelihood principle and Bayesian inference combine prior beliefs with current evidence. **Underdetermines** means the image does not settle on one unique external cause. Grouping and prior knowledge constrain the alternatives so that a coherent object or scene can be experienced.||**Organization precedes recognition:** Gestalt grouping organizes the field into candidate objects. **The priors come from the world:** physical and semantic regularities shape assumptions that are usually useful because the environment itself is regular.
48|Helmholtz’s theory of unconscious inference and the Gestalt laws of organization are **not fundamentally incompatible**. Both concern **how one interpretation gets selected from many that fit the image**.||**Helmholtz selects the most likely; the Gestaltists select the simplest.** In the great majority of ordinary scenes, the simplest interpretation is also the most likely, so the accounts can agree.||The **simplest** interpretation minimizes unnecessary complexity; the **most likely** interpretation fits familiar environmental patterns. Those criteria can favor the same grouping even though the theories explain the choice differently.
49|**“You have never once been in contact with the world—only with your brain’s reconstruction of it. So why is the reconstruction almost always right?”**||**The reconstruction is right because the world is regular, and the assumptions were built from it.** Perception uses sensory evidence together with expectations suited to recurring patterns in the environment. An **illusion** is a systematic difference between the display's physical properties and how it appears. The same assumptions that ordinarily help can produce that difference in an unusual arrangement.||Those assumptions usually support an accurate interpretation. When a display violates them, the same ordinarily useful processes can produce a systematic illusion.
