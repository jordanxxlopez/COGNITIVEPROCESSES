2|**Attention is the ability to focus on specific stimuli or locations in our environment.** **Selective attention** means attending to one thing while ignoring others; **divided attention** means paying attention to more than one thing at a time.||**Distraction** is one stimulus interfering with the processing of another stimulus. **Attentional capture** is rapid shifting of attention, usually caused by a **loud noise, bright light, or sudden movement**.||When one conversation is followed and another ignored, attention is selective. Following more than one activity uses divided attention; an irrelevant sound becomes a distraction when it interferes, and a sudden sound captures attention when it pulls focus toward itself.
3|**Selective attention is the ability to focus on one message and ignore others.** We do not attend to a large fraction of the information in the environment.||**We filter out some information and promote other information for further processing.** A message can therefore be physically present without receiving the same processing as the attended stream.||At a party, many voices reach the listener, but one conversation is followed closely. The attended voice is easier to understand and keep up with while the other conversations recede into the background.
4|**Colin Cherry (1953)** used **dichotic listening**, with one message presented to the left ear and another to the right ear at the same time. The headphones deliver **“The meaning of life is…”** to one ear and **“The yellow dog chased…”** to the other.||The participant is told to follow one of those messages. **Shadowing** means repeating that message aloud as it arrives: the listener says **“The yellow dog chased…”** while the other voice continues speaking. Repeating the selected words keeps attention on that message.||The voice being repeated is the **attended message**. The competing voice is the **unattended message**: it is still playing, but the listener tries not to follow it. The task asks whether attention can stay with the shadowed sentence while the other sentence is filtered out.
5|In dichotic listening, **participants could not report the content of the message in the unattended ear**, but **knew there was a message and knew the speaker’s gender**. This concerns selective listening in the **cocktail-party effect**.||**Information in the unattended ear is being processed at some level.** A **change in gender** or a **change to a tone** can be noticed.||**Information in the unattended ear is being processed at some level.** The listener may know that the ignored speaker was male or female yet be unable to repeat what that speaker said. A switch from speech to a tone is a noticeable change in the sound even when the ignored sentence's meaning is not available.
6|Models of selective attention ask **where the attention filter occurs: early in processing or later in processing**. They differ in the analysis available to unattended information.||The **early-selection model** is **Broadbent’s filter model (1958)**. The **intermediate-selection model** is **Treisman’s attenuation theory (1964)**. **Late-selection models** include the evidence discussed by **MacKay (1973)**.||**Early** and **late** refer to the location of selection in the processing sequence. Early selection chooses before meaning is worked out; late selection allows meaning to be processed before choosing what will guide the response.
7|**Broadbent’s filter model is an early-selection model: it filters the message before incoming information is analyzed for meaning.**||The initial **sensory memory** stage **holds all incoming information for a fraction of a second before transfer to the next stage**. Both messages can initially be registered.||Both voices first enter the brief sensory stage. The message selected by the filter proceeds to the detector, which works out its meaning, and the resulting information can continue to the later memory stage.
8|In **Broadbent’s filter model**, the **filter identifies the attended message based on physical characteristics**. **Only the attended message is passed on to the next stage**.||Physical characteristics can include **ear of presentation, pitch, or voice**. Selection therefore does not require first understanding every message.||Selection occurs **before meaning analysis**. If attention is directed to the right-ear message, **ear of presentation** can tell the filter which stream to pass. The selection uses where the sound arrived rather than what the sentence means.
9|The **detector** in Broadbent’s model **processes information to determine higher-level characteristics of the message**, including meaning.||Its input is the message that has already passed the filter. “All information” at this stage means the **selected information reaching the detector**, not both original streams.||A message blocked by the filter does not continue to the detector. For the selected words **“The yellow dog chased…”**, the detector works beyond the physical sound to identify the words and their meaning. That analysis occurs after the filter has admitted the attended message.
10|The next memory stage in **Broadbent’s filter model receives the output of the detector**. It **holds information for approximately 10–15 seconds and may transfer it to long-term memory**.||This store comes after selection and higher-level analysis. It is different from the initial sensory stage, which briefly holds incoming information before the filter. The first sensory stage briefly holds the incoming sounds; the later store holds information after the attended message has been selected and understood. Information that continues into long-term memory can remain available beyond that brief holding period.||The sequence is **incoming information → sensory memory → filter → detector → later memory**. The model’s attention claim depends on where the restriction occurs along that route.
11|**Broadbent’s model is also called a bottleneck model because the filter restricts information flow.** The restriction concerns which message continues, rather than slowing every message equally.||The model **lets information through based on specific physical characteristics**. Only the selected channel receives the later analysis.||**Early selection** places the restriction before meaning. A **bottleneck** is a narrow point that allows only part of the incoming information to continue. In Broadbent's account, the attended voice passes through that point while the competing voice is blocked before meaning analysis.
12|A shortcoming of a strict Broadbent filter is that **participants’ names get through**. In **Moray (1959)**, listeners’ names were presented to the unattended ear; **about one third detected their own name**.||Participants can also **shadow meaningful messages that switch from one ear to another**. In **Gray and Wedderburn’s “Dear Aunt Jane” experiment (1960)**, the **left ear received “Dear—4—Jane”** and the **right, shadowed ear received “8—Aunt—2.”** The phrase **“Dear Aunt Jane”** requires taking **Dear** from the left ear, **Aunt** from the right, and **Jane** from the left. Listeners follow the meaningful phrase even though its words alternate between physical channels.||Participants reported **“Dear Aunt Jane.”** Following meaning across ears challenges the proposal that selection completely excludes content outside one physical channel.
13|**Treisman’s attenuation theory is an intermediate-selection model.** The attended message can be separated from the unattended message **early in the information-processing system**, and **selection can also occur later**.||The **attenuator analyzes the incoming message in terms of physical characteristics, language, and meaning**. It reduces the unattended stream’s strength rather than completely removing it.||**Attenuation** means reducing strength. The unattended voice is turned down rather than silenced, so a sufficiently important word can still become noticeable while the attended sentence remains easier to follow.
14|In **Treisman’s attenuation theory**, the **attended message is let through the attenuator at full strength**. It has stronger input to the following **dictionary unit**.||The full-strength message is more likely to activate relevant words. Attention therefore creates a processing advantage for the selected stream.||The attended voice reaches the dictionary unit with enough strength to activate its words readily. This lets the listener follow the selected sentence continuously instead of repeatedly losing it to the weaker competing voice.
15|In **Treisman’s attenuation theory**, the **unattended message is let through at a much weaker strength**. It is **attenuated, not shut off**.||Reduced-strength input can still activate a word if that word’s **activation threshold** is sufficiently low. Common or important words may therefore become available from weak input.||An important word can become active even in the turned-down message. A person's own name is the familiar example: its low threshold allows weaker input to activate it.
16|Treisman’s **dictionary unit contains words, each with a threshold for being activated**. **Words that are common or important have low thresholds; uncommon words have high thresholds**.||The threshold determines how much activation is required. A person’s own name can have a low threshold and therefore respond even to attenuated input.||An **activation threshold** is the amount of input needed for a word to become active. A low threshold is easier to reach, so important or common words can be recognized from less input than unfamiliar words.
17|**Unattended inputs are attenuated, not shut off.** Selection follows an ordered hierarchy: **physical cues → syllabic pattern → specific words → grammatical structure → meaning**.||These levels describe increasingly organized analysis of the incoming signal. Weakening the stream limits how effectively it supports later processing without requiring complete elimination.||**Physical cues** include properties of the voice; **syllabic pattern** concerns the organization of speech sounds. Specific words, grammar, and meaning involve progressively more organized linguistic information.
18|In **late-selection models**, **selection of stimuli for final processing does not occur until after information has been analyzed for meaning**.||In **MacKay (1973)**, participants heard **“They were throwing stones at the bank”** in the attended ear. The unattended ear received either **“river”** or **“money.”**||With **river** in the ignored ear, **bank** is more readily understood as the side of a river. With **money**, the same attended word is more readily understood as a financial institution.
19|In **MacKay (1973)**, participants chose which test sentence was closest in meaning to the attended message: **“They threw stones toward the side of the river yesterday”** or **“They threw stones at the savings and loan association yesterday.”**||**The meaning of the biasing word shifted participants’ choice**, although they were **unaware of the presentation of the biasing words**. River favored the river interpretation; money favored the financial interpretation.||The listener hears the attended sentence but does not report hearing the biasing word. Even so, **river** favors the river-side test sentence and **money** favors the savings-and-loan test sentence.
20|A **late-selection account** proposes that **all inputs receive full processing, but only important, task-relevant inputs enter short-term memory and eventually lead to a response**.||The sequence places **perceptual processing before the selective filter**, followed by later memory and response. Meaning can therefore be analyzed without every analyzed message becoming reportable.||Both messages can be understood before one is chosen for the response. The important or task-relevant message is retained, while the other message can influence interpretation without becoming the message the person reports.
21|**Broadbent (1958), filter model:** selection is **early, before meaning is analyzed**. **Only the attended channel** gets through, selected on physical features such as **ear, pitch, or voice**. Shadowing shows that unattended content often cannot be reported.||**Treisman (1964), attenuation theory:** selection is **intermediate, early and late**. Unattended input is **turned down**, while **low-threshold words** can still activate. Own-name detection and **“Dear Aunt Jane”** motivate that flexibility. Broadbent's filter **blocks** the unattended stream, Treisman's attenuator **weakens** it, and late selection places the choice **after meaning**. These different routes explain why an ignored word is unavailable in some conditions yet can affect interpretation in others.||**Deutsch and Deutsch (1963), and MacKay (1973), late selection:** information is analyzed for **meaning before selection controls response and awareness**. An unattended biasing word shifting sentence interpretation supports this later meaningful influence.
22|**There is no single answer to the early–late controversy.** Under some conditions **early selection can be demonstrated**, and under other conditions **late selection** can be demonstrated.||Researchers therefore focus on **understanding the factors that control attention**. Task demands and available processing capacity help explain why selection differs across conditions.||A demanding task can leave little processing available for a competing message. An easier task can leave more available, allowing unattended information to influence what is understood or how quickly a response is made.
23|**Automatic processes are processes that do not demand attentional control.** The **visual-search task** provides an example when a distinctive feature is detected readily among other items.||A target that stands out can require less deliberate item-by-item inspection than a difficult search. Automaticity concerns control and resource demands under those task conditions.||A target with a distinctive visible feature can stand out among distractors. The observer need not deliberately inspect and reject every item before locating it.
24|**Automatic processes do not demand attentional control**, but they can **interfere with more effortful, attention-demanding processes**.||In the **Stroop task**, reading the word can interfere with naming the **ink color** when word meaning and color conflict. The automatic response is not the one required by the current instruction.||When the word **RED** is printed in blue ink, the required answer is **blue**. Reading activates **red**, which competes with the instructed color-naming response and slows it down.
25|**Processing capacity** is **how much information a person can handle at any given moment**. **Perceptual load** is **the difficulty of a given task**.||**High-load, difficult tasks use higher amounts of processing capacity. Low-load, easy tasks use lower amounts.** **Capacity** is the amount available; **load** is how much the task uses. A low-load task leaves more capacity for an irrelevant stimulus, whereas a high-load task uses more of that capacity on the main task.||When the main task uses more capacity, less remains available for irrelevant information. When it uses less, spare capacity can support distractor processing. That spare capacity allows task-irrelevant stimuli to be processed and interfere with the response.
26|In **Forster and Lavie (2008)**, **presentation of a task-irrelevant stimulus slows response time**. The distraction effect is **stronger for easier tasks than for harder tasks**.||Under the harder, higher-load condition, **less perceptual capacity remains** for the irrelevant stimulus. Under the easier condition, more is available to process it.||The irrelevant image draws more processing during the easier search, slowing the target response. During the harder search, attention is more fully occupied with the target, and the distractor produces a smaller delay.
27|**Overt attention** shifts from one place to another **by moving the eyes**. **Covert attention** shifts **while the eyes stay fixed**.||**Saccades are rapid eye movements from one place to another. Fixations are short pauses on points of interest.** An **eye tracker** measures those movements and pauses. Looking directly at a new object is an overt shift. Keeping the eyes on one point while attending off to the side is a covert shift; the attended side location remains in peripheral vision rather than moving onto the fovea.||**Central vision** is the area being looked at; objects there fall on the **fovea**. **Peripheral vision** is off to the side. The **fovea has much higher resolution than the periphery**.
28|**Stimulus salience** concerns **areas that stand out and capture attention**. It is a **bottom-up process** depending on physical characteristics of the stimulus. **Color and motion are highly salient**.||Selection can be driven by the image itself through **light–dark differences, color, contrast, and motion**. A bright patch against a dark background or an object that suddenly moves stands out through its physical properties. The attention shift is bottom-up because the conspicuous stimulus pulls attention toward itself.||**Attentional capture is an involuntary shift of attention due to stimulus salience.** A sudden movement can draw attention even when it is unrelated to the observer’s current goal.
29|**Top-down determinants of eye movements** include **scanning based on cognitive factors and the meaning of features**. What the observer knows and seeks affects where they look.||A **scene schema is knowledge about what is contained in typical scenes**. Schemas **help guide fixations from one area of a scene to another**.||Searching for an object uses knowledge of where that object would normally be found. The eyes are guided toward likely locations by the current goal and scene knowledge.
30|A **scene schema** supplies knowledge about **what is contained in typical scenes** and helps **guide fixations from one area to another**.||**Võ and Henderson (2009)** compared viewing behavior for **semantically consistent and inconsistent objects**. An object that violates the expected scene contents is viewed differently from one that fits.||**Fixation duration** is the time the eyes remain on an item. A scene-consistent object fits what is expected in that setting. A scene-inconsistent object violates that expectation and can hold the gaze longer while the observer works out what it is doing there.
31|**Eye movements are influenced by the task. Scanning can be based on task demands.** In meal preparation, gaze goes toward objects needed for the current or next action.||A useful object can receive a fixation even when another feature is more visually conspicuous. The goal determines what information needs to be sampled.||During meal preparation, gaze goes to the object needed for an upcoming step. The eyes can move toward the next useful item before the hand reaches for it, coordinating visual information with action.
32|**Precueing means directing attention without moving the eyes.** In **Posner et al. (1978)**, participants responded **faster to a light at an expected location than at an unexpected location**, even while their eyes stayed fixed.||The cue changes which location receives priority before the target appears. The response-time difference provides evidence for **covert attention**.||The participant keeps looking at the fixation point while a cue directs attention to one side. A target at the expected side is responded to more quickly, even though the eyes have not turned toward it.
33|In **Posner et al. (1978)**, **precueing** produced faster responses at an **expected location** than an **unexpected location** while the eyes stayed fixed.||A **valid cue** indicates the location where the target appears. An **invalid cue** directs attention elsewhere, so responding includes redirecting attention to the actual target.||After a valid cue, attention is already at the target's location. After an invalid cue, attention must shift from the suggested location to the actual one, making the response slower.
34|The **same-object advantage** shows that we can **covertly attend to specific objects**. **When attention is directed to one place on an object, its enhancing effect spreads to other places on that object**.||In **Egly et al. (1994)**, a cue at **A** is followed by a target at the cued position, an **invalid same-object position**, or an **invalid different-object position**.||The two uncued positions are **equally far from the cue**. After one end of an object is cued, a target at its other end can be detected faster than a target equally far away on a different object. The benefit follows the object's grouping, not just the nearest point in space.
35|Whether **C has an advantage over B** depends on how **A, B, and C** belong to the perceived objects. In the changed arrangement, **C has no advantage over B**.||When **A and C are on the same object again**, the relevant same-object relationship returns. The advantage changes with the object arrangement.||**C has no advantage over B** when A and C no longer belong to the same object. When **A and C** belong to the same object, attention directed to A also benefits C. Rearranging the objects so they no longer share that grouping removes C's advantage over B.
36|**Practice enables people to simultaneously do two things that were difficult at first.** **Schneider and Shiffrin (1977)** divided attention between **remembering a target and monitoring rapidly presented stimuli**.||The **memory set contained one to four target characters**. **Test frames could contain random-dot patterns, a target, or distractors**.||The participant first keeps a small set of target characters in mind, then watches rapidly appearing frames for any of those characters. Remembering the targets and checking the frames must be coordinated at the same time.
37|The procedure begins by **presenting a target stimulus in the memory set**, followed by **a series of 20 rapidly presented test frames**.||The participant decides **whether a target from the memory set was present in a frame**. Current input must be compared with the retained target while distractors are excluded.||Each of the **20 frames** must be checked against the target set. A target frame calls for detection of a remembered character; distractor and random-dot frames should not be treated as a target.
38|**After about 600 trials, the task had become automatic for participants** in the reported condition. Performance improved toward a stable high level through practice.||Repeated trials improved the coordination of remembering the target and monitoring test frames.||At first, checking each frame against the target set requires deliberate effort. With extensive practice in the trained task, target detection becomes more fluent and demands less conscious control.
39|**Schneider and Shiffrin suggest that practice made it possible for participants to divide their attention.** **Automatic processing occurs without intention and uses only some of a person’s cognitive resources**.||**Practice results in automatic processing** in the trained conditions. Component operations require less deliberate coordination, leaving more capacity available for other demands. A practiced component can run with less deliberate attention while another activity is carried out. Turning conscious attention back onto each component of an already fluent skill can disrupt its smooth execution.||For skilled performers, **paying attention to their performance has sometimes been argued to degrade performance**. Deliberately monitoring a highly practiced component can interfere with its fluent execution.
40|Practice can make dividing attention possible, but **automatic processing is task dependent**. A task can remain sufficiently hard that automatic processing is not achieved even with practice.||**Automatic processing occurs without intention and uses some cognitive resources**.||A familiar task can still require attention when its demands increase. Practice reduces effort for the trained operations, but coordinating a second difficult activity can still create interference.
