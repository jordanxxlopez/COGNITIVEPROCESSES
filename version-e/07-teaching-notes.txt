2|**Sentences with temporary ambiguity help us understand the mechanisms operating during parsing.** In the **syntax-first approach**, grouping words into phrases is governed initially by **rules based on syntax**. The parser initially keeps extending the phrase it is already building. When a later word no longer fits, it revises that grouping so the complete sentence can be understood.||If readers realize something is wrong with that grouping, they **take other information into account to reinterpret the sentence**.||**Late closure** means the parser assumes a **new word is part of the current phrase**, adding each new word there for as long as possible. Later input can reveal that this initial attachment needs repair.
3|The **syntax-first approach** is motivated by distinguishing **syntax from semantics**. The **parser is assumed to function automatically and follow simple rules**, seeking a simple parse.||With **late closure**, a new word is treated as part of the current phrase for as long as possible. A syntactic tree represents the resulting organization into words, phrases, and their relationships.||A **parse** is a grouping of words into grammatical relationships. The syntax-first account starts with an automatic grouping and brings in a more effortful repair when the continuing sentence makes that grouping fail.
4|In the **interactionist approach to parsing, semantics and syntax both influence processing as one reads a sentence**.||**“The spy saw the man with the binoculars”** permits **“[The spy saw the man] [with the binoculars]”**, where the spy uses them, or **“[The spy saw] [the man with binoculars]”**, where the man has them. With the spy, the binoculars can be the instrument used to see the man. With the bird, that interpretation is less plausible, so the phrase is more readily understood as describing the man who has the binoculars.||**“The bird saw the man with the binoculars”** favors the man having binoculars. **Semantics influences how we interpret the relationship between the words**, rather than contributing only after a syntax-only interpretation has failed.
5|**Our interpretation of a sentence is also influenced by the meaning of a scene we may be observing.** **Tanenhaus and coworkers (1995)** used the **visual-world paradigm** to measure processing while participants viewed a scene.||**Eye movements change when information suggests that revision of the sentence interpretation is necessary.** Gaze indicates which objects or destinations are being considered.||**Syntactic and semantic information are used simultaneously** in the interactionist account. The **visual-world paradigm** follows the listener's gaze among visible objects while an instruction is spoken. Objects in view help determine which meaning of the unfolding instruction is sensible.
6|**“Place the apple on the towel in the box.”** The ambiguity is whether **“the relevant apple is the one on the towel”** or the instruction means **“move the apple onto the towel.”**||With **two apples**, listeners expect the speaker to let them know **which apple** is intended. **On the towel** can identify the apple already resting there rather than specify its destination.||When two apples are visible, **on the towel** can tell the listener which apple to choose. The phrase describes the apple's current location, and **in the box** gives the destination for moving it.
7|With **only one apple**, the other interpretation is also considered: **the apple should be moved to the other towel**. The listener has less need for a phrase distinguishing one apple from another.||With only one apple, identifying which apple is meant requires less information. **On the towel** can initially sound like a destination, until **in the box** requires the listener to revise where the apple should go.||**Upon hearing “the box,” the person looks back at the apple**, reflecting revision of the destination interpretation.
8|We use **language while interacting with the environment** and use **knowledge of the environment to predict what we are about to read or hear**.||**“Getting himself and his car to work on the neighboring island was time consuming. Every morning he drove for a few minutes, and then boarded the…”** A likely completion is **ferry**.||A **ferry** fits the combination of a car, an island, and boarding a means of transport. The situation makes that continuation expected before the word itself appears.
9|**Experience with sentence constructions influences predictions about how a sentence will be organized.** **Fine et al. (2013)** investigated whether readers change those predictions through experience with new constructions.||In the **moving-window paradigm**, participants read **one word at a time on a computer screen**, pressing the **space bar** to reveal the next word. The words **who were** make the soldiers' description explicit. Without them, warned can initially look like the main action, so reaching **conducted** requires the reduced description to be recognized and the grouping revised.||Compare **“The experienced soldiers warned about the dangers conducted the midnight raid”** with **“The experienced soldiers who were warned about the dangers conducted the midnight raid.”**||Initially, the reduced construction had a **higher reading cost**. After exposure, the difference became **nonsignificant**, supporting adjustment of parsing expectations through experience.
10|**Stories are more than the sum of the meanings of individual sentences.** Creating a coherent story includes **making inferences: determining what the text means by using our own knowledge**.||**“John was trying to fix the birdhouse. He was pounding the nail when his father came out to watch him and help him do the work.”** The passage does not explicitly name the instrument. The text mentions pounding a nail but not a hammer. Knowledge about that activity supplies the likely instrument, making the story more complete in the reader's understanding than its literal wording alone.||Readers can later endorse **“John was using a hammer to fix the birdhouse when his father came out…”** Knowledge supplied the likely hammer. This connects **unconscious inference** with the **constructive nature of memory**, where an inferred detail can be attributed to the original wording.
11|A **situation model is a mental representation of what a text is about**. It represents **events as if experiencing the situation**, often from the **protagonist’s point of view**, rather than only recording phrases, sentences, and paragraphs.||**Mental representations as simulations** involve the **perceptual and motor characteristics of the objects and actions in a story**. A nail hammered **into a wall** is represented horizontally because that orientation fits the action. Identifying a matching picture is faster when its orientation agrees with the event being represented.||After **“He hammered the nail into the wall,”** identifying a **horizontally oriented nail** is faster than identifying an orientation inconsistent with that situation.
12|**“The ranger saw the eagle in the sky.”** This situation supports an eagle with **extended wings**, even though that configuration is not separately spelled out.||A picture matching the implied configuration produces **faster reaction time** than a mismatching one.||Readers **create perceptual representations matching the situation**. The eagle in the sky is represented with its wings spread for flight. A matching picture fits that represented event more readily than a picture showing a configuration that does not fit it.
13|In the **physiology of simulations**, approximately the **same cortical areas are activated by actual movements and by reading related action words**, but **activation is more extensive for actual movements**.||The color-coded maps compare **blue: foot movements and leg words; red: finger movements and arm words; green: tongue movements and face words**.||Reading a word about an action can recruit part of the neural system used in performing that kind of action. The foot, hand, and tongue examples connect action-word meaning with the corresponding movement-related areas.
14|**We access information about situations as we read.** **Ross Metusalem et al. (2012)** measured **N400 amplitude**, a semantic-response measure, while participants read short passages.||**“The band was very popular and Joe was sure the concert would be sold out. Amazingly, he was able to get a seat down in front. He couldn’t believe how close he was when he saw the group walk out onto the [stage/guitar/barn] and start playing.”**||**Stage is expected; barn is unrelated; guitar is related.** **Guitar generates a smaller N400 than barn**, indicating some activation from the concert scenario even though guitar is not the correct completion.||The **situation model** activates event-related knowledge beyond one expected next word. **Stage** is the expected place for the band to walk out. **Guitar** is an incorrect completion but belongs to concert knowledge, whereas **barn** is unrelated, so guitar produces a smaller N400 than barn.
15|**Conversation is the most common form of language production:** two or more people talking to each other. It is **dynamic and rapid**, involves **shared knowledge**, and requires considering **what the other person is saying and what they know about the topic**.||The **given–new contract** means a speaker constructs sentences containing **given information, which the listener already knows**, together with **new information**. **Given information** is already shared; **new information** adds something the listener does not yet know. Once that new detail is established in the conversation, later utterances can treat it as shared information.||**New information can become given information** later. Common ground changes during conversation as the exchange establishes what both people know.
16|Coordination between speakers includes **using similar grammatical constructions**, called **syntactic coordination**.||**Syntactic priming** occurs when **production of a particular construction by one person increases the chance that another person will use that construction**. This aligns the grammatical form of their statements.||Alignment can **reduce computational load in conversation**. Both **gave the book to the boy** and **gave the boy the book** express a transfer. Syntactic priming makes a speaker more likely to reuse the grammatical arrangement just heard, even with new content.
17|In **syntactic-priming experiments**, two people converse about a task. Researchers test whether **a grammatical construction used by one person causes the other person to use it**.||One person can be a **confederate**, who uses a controlled prime such as **“The girl gave the book to the boy”** or **“The girl gave the boy the book.”** A **confederate** is a person taking part in the study under the experimenter's direction. Their scripted sentence supplies the grammatical form that may influence the participant's next description.||The participant **picks a matching card and describes a card to the other person**.
18|In **78% of trials**, **the form of the participant’s description matched the form of the confederate’s priming statement**.||This supports the idea that **speakers are sensitive to other speakers’ linguistic behavior and adjust their behavior to match**. The match concerns syntactic form rather than repetition of an identical message.||The participant often uses the same kind of sentence construction as the preceding speaker. Reusing an available form makes it easier to coordinate a new message with the ongoing exchange.
19|The **Sapir–Whorf hypothesis** proposes that **language influences thought**. The nature of a culture’s language can affect how people think, associated here with **Whorf (1956)**.||**Winawer and coworkers (2007)** found differences between cultures in responding to **blue squares based on how the colors were categorized**. Russian uses **siniy** for darker blue and **goluboy** for lighter blue. Russian gives lighter and darker blues separate basic names. Responses are faster when the matching task crosses that named boundary than when the alternatives remain within the same category.||**Russian participants responded more quickly when the bottom squares belonged to different categories.** The language’s category boundary is associated with faster discrimination when the comparison crosses that boundary.
20|**Differences in how names are assigned to colors can affect the ability to tell colors apart.** **Gilbert et al. (2006)** tested differences between processing in the **left and right hemispheres**.||Language is typically associated more strongly with the **left hemisphere**. If language influences color perception, the effect is predicted to be stronger when colors appear in the **right visual field**, whose input initially projects leftward.||The task asks **which side contains the odd color**. The **right visual field** initially sends information to the left hemisphere, where language is typically more strongly represented. A stronger cross-category advantage on that side connects the color judgment with language-related processing.
21|Nearby space is often described **egocentrically**: **to your left or right, in front of you, or behind you**. These directions change with the speaker’s orientation.||In **Tzeltal, a Mayan language spoken in Chiapas**, **absolute directions** are used more pervasively. Similar systems occur in some unrelated languages in **Bali and Queensland**. **Egocentric** directions depend on the observer: turning around changes which direction is left. **Absolute** directions remain tied to the environment, so east stays east after the observer turns.||**“Raise your east hand”** illustrates the difference. Determining east depends on the environment and current orientation, rather than naming a permanently fixed left or right hand.
22|**Tzeltal has a historically landmark-based system.** Things are described as **uphill or downhill from one another**, rather than simply to the speaker’s left or right. **Uphill and downhill function as cardinal directions**, making the system **absolute**.||People in some cities also use **uptown or west**, as in New York, although Manhattan’s westward alignment is closer to northwest. The comparison concerns how pervasively those directions are used. **Uphill** and **downhill** operate as stable environmental directions rather than momentary left and right. A person must track their orientation to use those terms correctly in a new position.||In the field example, a Tenejapan in an unfamiliar city at night asks whether a hot tap is the **uphill, southern** or **downhill, northern** tap. **Levinson et al. (2002)** connect these practices with maintaining spatial orientation.
23|In the **chips task**, participants saw a card with **a large and small dot on Table 1**. After a **30-second delay**, they were **rotated 180 degrees** and led to **Table 2**, choosing a matching arrangement from **four cards**. There were **eight trials per participant**.||**Relative responses** preserved **egocentric coordinates**; **absolute responses** preserved **environmental direction**. Other choices were coded **untypable**. Rotation makes the two reference frames produce different matches. After the half-turn, an arrangement preserving the same left–right relation conflicts with one preserving the same environmental direction. Choosing a card therefore reveals which reference frame guided the remembered arrangement.||**Dutch responses were predominantly relative; Tzeltal responses favored absolute direction**, matching the language-related reference-frame tendencies.||Among **consistent Tzeltal coders**, defined as using one response style on at least **six of eight trials**, **over 80% were classified as absolute thinkers**. This is a participant-level summary distinct from pooling every response in the stacked bars.
24|Language has a long tradition of **formal description similar to artificial intelligence**. Much experimental psychology in the **1950s and 1960s** resisted explanation in terms of **internal states**.||**Skinner’s Verbal Behavior** tried to explain language learning through **operant conditioning**. **Chomsky’s 1959 review** challenged that account and argued for internal cognitive structure. **Operant conditioning** explains learning through consequences, while an internal-structure account explains how organized knowledge supports novel sentences. The debate concerns how language learning and productivity are explained.||The debate was an important part of the **cognitive revolution**, in which internal representations and operations became legitimate scientific explanations of behavior.
25|**Substrate dependence** means that **details of how language is implemented in the brain may be unavoidable in a theory of language**.||The physical system can constrain learning and processing through **memory limitations, errors, and associative-learning mechanisms**. Those details may therefore contribute to the explanation rather than be irrelevant background.||A **substrate** is the physical system that carries out a process. Substrate dependence means that the brain's learning mechanisms and processing constraints can matter to how language works.
26|Chomsky’s formal approach is associated with the **computational level of analysis**. **David Marr**, a visual neuroscientist, distinguished **three levels: computational, algorithmic, and implementational**.||The **computational level** specifies the problem and purpose. The **algorithmic level** specifies representations and operations used to solve it. The **implementational level** concerns how those operations are physically realized.||The **computational** question is what problem is being solved and why. The **algorithmic** question is how representations and operations solve it, and the **implementational** question is how a physical system carries out those operations.
27|For **cash registers**, the **computational level** asks: **given item prices, determine the amount due; given payment, compute the change due**. It identifies the function to be performed.||The **algorithmic level** uses **addition for price totals and subtraction for change**. It specifies operations that accomplish the function. Adding item prices and subtracting the amount due from the payment describe operations. The purpose is to determine totals and change; the register's physical machinery supplies one implementation of those operations.||At the **implementational level**, different devices or programs can carry out those calculations. The abstract functions can be described without first selecting particular hardware.
28|**Type I problems** are problems that **break down cleanly across Marr’s levels**. Treating language as Type I allows work at the **computational level, including Universal Grammar**, and the **algorithmic level, including language-specific processes**, without initially explaining implementation.||Implementational concerns include **errors, memory limitations, and associative learning**. Whether those can be separated from an adequate language account is the theoretical issue. A **Type I** account separates the abstract problem and operations from their physical implementation. For language, the issue is whether explaining the formal system can proceed independently of the brain's particular learning and processing properties.||**Chomsky’s critique predates Marr’s theoretical work by about a decade.** Both influenced artificial intelligence and the **GOFAI**, or symbolic-AI, tradition.
29|A **letter-prediction model** can find words in a continuous sequence. **“Manyyearsagoaboyandgirllivedbytheseatheyplayedhappily…”** becomes **“many years ago a boy and girl lived by the sea they played happily…”**.||In **Elman’s model**, **prediction error on the y-axis goes down within words** and rises near less predictable transitions.
