2|Cognitive psychology **“in the wild”** studies behavior with **no experimenter present**. The naturalistic driving work collected data in **privately owned vehicles** using **unobtrusive instrumentation**. **Naturalistic** means that driving occurs in the drivers' ordinary environment. Cameras and other equipment record what drivers are doing as real traffic events unfold.||Equipment included **hidden radar**, a **face camera behind smoked glass**, and other cameras approximately **one inch by one inch**.||The design is **quasi-experimental**: observe naturally occurring crashes and examine **what was happening before the crash**. The recordings make the period before the event available for analysis.
3|The **100-car study** collected data in a **naturalistic setting** to obtain **crash, pre-crash, near-crash, and conflict data**, together with **distributions of driver performance**.||**One hundred drivers** used their **own or leased cars with specialized instrumentation on public roads**, as close to unobserved as possible. They drove for an extended period, **up to 13 months**, without an experimenter present. A **baseline** is a period of ordinary driving used as a reference. The recordings include both everyday travel and hazardous events, with the driver continuing normal use of the vehicle.||Drivers were **not coached or instructed to perform specific actions other than driving normally**. Instrumentation was **unobtrusive and inconspicuous to other drivers, but not invisible**. Normal-driving periods provide a comparison with hazardous events.
4|The naturalistic **100-car database** contained **42,300 hours of driving data**, approximately **two million miles**, **82 crashes and collisions**, **761 near crashes**, **8,295 incidents**, and **20,000 matched periods of normal baseline driving**.||A **crash or collision** means contact with another **vehicle, fixed object, pedestrian, pedalcyclist, or animal**. A **near crash** is a conflict requiring a **rapid, severe evasive maneuver to avoid a crash**. A crash involves contact, a near crash requires an urgent severe maneuver to avoid contact, and an incident requires a less severe evasive response. The event categories follow what happens on the road.||An **incident** requires an evasive maneuver of **lesser magnitude than a near crash**. These categories separate event severity rather than treating every conflict as the same outcome.
5|In the **100-car results**, **inattention to the forward roadway** was identified as a primary contributing factor in many crashes and collisions. **80% of crashes and 65% of near crashes involved at least one form of inattention within three seconds before conflict onset**.||**93% of lead-vehicle conflict crashes and minor collisions involved looking away**. In **86% of those events**, headway at onset was **greater than 2.0 seconds**.||**Headway** is the time gap to the vehicle ahead. Looking away can leave a developing conflict unseen even when the initial gap is more than two seconds.
6|**Secondary tasks that are moderately to very complex, and driver drowsiness, carry the highest associated crash risk** in the distraction analysis.||The graphs compare **crash, near-crash, incident, and baseline** periods using **time eyes off the forward roadway in seconds**, **number of glances away**, and **mean length of the longest glance away**.||**Eyes off the forward roadway** measures time looking away from the road ahead. A long continuous glance creates a different viewing pattern from several brief glances, even when both involve off-road looking.
7|The naturalistic-driving material describes **video recorders placed in cars** and reports that **accident risk is about four times higher during cell-phone use**.||**Strayer and Johnston (2001)** used a **simulated driving task**. Participants on a cell phone **missed twice as many red lights and took longer to apply the brakes**. The **same pattern occurred with a hands-free cell phone**.||A hands-free phone removes the need to hold the handset but leaves the conversation to be understood and answered. Those language demands compete with noticing the red light and responding by braking.
8|**Cartwright-Finch and Lavie (2007)** presented a rapidly flashed **cross stimulus five times**. Participants indicated **which arm was longer**, with one arm slightly longer than the other.||**On the sixth trial, a square was added. Only 10% of participants saw it.** Attention remained engaged with the cross judgment. The participant repeatedly judges the cross's longer arm and continues that task when the square is added. The square is visible in the display, but the unexpected item can go unnoticed while attention remains on the cross.||**Inattentional blindness** is failure to perceive an unattended stimulus even when a person might be looking directly toward it. Attention remains focused on the cross judgment while the square is missed.
9|**Change blindness** occurs when **differences between two versions of a picture are not immediately apparent**. The **task of identifying differences requires concentrated attention and search**.||The observer must register relevant information and compare it across views. Simply receiving both pictures is not sufficient. The changed object can be visible in each picture, yet its difference may not stand out. Detecting the change requires noticing its earlier and later states as different.||This differs from inattentional blindness: change blindness concerns **a difference across versions**, while inattentional blindness concerns **an unexpected stimulus missed while attention is elsewhere**.
10|In **Levin and Simons (1997)**, **change detection** is tested during a visual event. Viewers can fail to notice a change while continuing to understand the overall event.||Detecting the change requires attention to the relevant feature and comparison with its earlier state. A viewer can follow the event’s overall meaning while missing a change in a particular person or object.||Following the overall event does not mean every detail is retained. A change in a particular person or object can be missed while the larger action continues to make sense.
11|**Binding is the process by which features such as color, form, motion, and location are combined to create perception of a coherent object**.||The visual system must establish which properties belong together. Detecting redness, roundness, and movement separately does not yet identify one red moving ball.||Binding connects with the earlier question of how **Gestalt and direct-perception accounts** explain an organized visual field. A red moving ball has a color, a shape, and a direction of movement. **Binding** makes those properties belong to the same perceived ball rather than leaving redness, roundness, and motion unattached.
12|**How an object’s individual features become bound together—the binding problem—is addressed by Treisman’s feature integration theory, or FIT.**||The sequence is **object → preattentive stage → focused-attention stage → perception**. The first stage **analyzes the object into features**; the next **combines those features**.||In **feature integration theory**, the preattentive stage registers the features and focused attention brings them together. The resulting perception includes which features belong to which object.
13|During FIT’s **preattentive stage**, the **object is analyzed into features**. The operation is described as **automatic**, involving **no deliberate effort or attention**, with the observer **unaware of the process**.||Separate feature registration precedes their combination. A color and a shape can both be available without the correct color–shape pairing being secure.||The system can register that red and green are present before their objects are securely identified. Focused attention is then needed to attach each color to the correct shape and location.
14|During the **focused-attention stage**, **attention plays a key role and features are combined**. This follows the preattentive analysis of an object into components.||Accurate perception requires assigning those properties to the correct object. Knowing which colors and shapes appeared is not the same as knowing which color belonged to each shape.||Correct binding means perceiving the actual color–shape combination. Seeing both a red feature and a circular feature is insufficient if the red belonged to a triangle and the circle was a different color.
15|In the **preattentive stage**, objects are analyzed into **separate features**. A **rolling red ball** includes **red color, round shape, and movement to the right**. Different mechanisms process these features, which are initially represented separately.||In **Treisman and Schmidt (1982)**, participants reported **combinations of features from different stimuli**. These are **illusory conjunctions**. An **illusory conjunction** combines real features into the wrong object. Reporting a red circle when the display contained a red triangle and a differently colored circle recombines the color and shape.||The description **“free-floating” features** means that features are available without secure assignment to their objects. An error can recombine displayed properties rather than invent a feature that never occurred.
16|The display contains a **yellow vertical rectangle, a gray horizontal rectangle, and a blue oval**. Each has separable **color, form, and orientation** properties.||Correct binding attaches yellow to the vertical rectangle, gray to the horizontal rectangle, and blue to the oval. Focused attention combines the features in those object-specific pairings.||Combining features from different objects is a **binding error**. The blue belongs to the oval, not to either rectangle. Preserving those particular assignments is what makes the perception three correctly identified objects rather than a list of colors and shapes.
17|The stimulus contains black digits **1 and 8**, together with **a small red triangle, a large outlined blue triangle, a yellow circle, and a green outlined circle**.||Reporting the **black digits first** directs attention away from the colored objects. Features can remain available without receiving enough focused attention for correct pairing.||Reporting **1 and 8** first occupies attention that could otherwise be directed to the colored shapes. A later report can contain their colors and forms but combine them incorrectly.
18|The rectangle-and-oval pattern separates **feature registration** from **object identity**. The features include **yellow, gray, blue, vertical, horizontal, and oval**, but accurate perception requires their original combinations.||The correct pairings are **yellow vertical rectangle, gray horizontal rectangle, and blue oval**. Combining existing properties differently would produce an illusory conjunction.||A yellow horizontal rectangle would combine the color of one displayed rectangle with the orientation of the other. Both features were present, but that particular conjunction was not.
19|In **Treisman and Schmidt (1982)**, **the image was flashed for one fifth of a second**, or **200 milliseconds**, followed by a **random-dot mask**.||Participants were asked to **report the black numbers first**, then **report what they saw at each of the four locations where the shapes had been**.||The **random-dot mask** is a pattern shown immediately after the brief image. The participant reports the numbers and then reconstructs what appeared at the four shape locations from that brief exposure.
20|In **18% of trials**, participants reported **objects made up of a combination of features from two different stimuli**, such as **a small red circle or a small green triangle**.||A **small red circle** combines a displayed color and size with the shape of another item. The error concerns which features belong together, rather than the appearance of a completely new feature.||Those colors and shapes were present, but not in the reported pairing. This is an **illusory conjunction**, rather than necessarily an invented color or form.
21|FIT predicts **illusory conjunctions**. In a brief display where participants **reported digits first and then letters and their colors**, they **mixed up letters and colors on 39% of trials**, while reporting **a letter or color absent from the display on 15%**.||The first is a **binding error**; the second is a **feature-report error**. This is a different display from the 18% colored-shape example. The **39%** result concerns mixing displayed letters and colors; the **15%** result concerns reporting a feature absent from the display. The **18%** result belongs to the separate colored-shape example, so the percentages describe different displays and errors.||FIT also predicts **object-based attention**. Illusory conjunctions can occur even when stimuli **differ greatly in shape and size**, because features have not yet been bound together through focused attention.
22|In the **focused-attention stage**, **“free-floating” features are combined**. Attention plays a key role in making their correct object assignment available.||Attending to an object supports combining its color, form, and location rather than merely knowing that those features occurred somewhere.||**Free-floating** means that a feature has not yet been securely assigned to a particular object. Focused attention joins the correct color, shape, and position into one object representation.
23|**R.M., a patient with Balint’s syndrome**, had an **inability to focus attention on individual objects** and reported a **high number of illusory conjunctions**.||R.M. could report combinations that mixed features from different objects. Difficulty focusing on an individual object makes it harder to attach its properties to that object correctly.||**FIT** assigns focused attention the role of combining features correctly. R.M. could report combinations that mixed features from different objects. Difficulty focusing on an individual object makes it harder to attach its properties to that object correctly.
24|FIT emphasizes **mostly bottom-up processing**, but **top-down processing contributes when participants are told what they will see**, such as **“You are being shown a carrot, a lake, and a tire.”**||**Top-down processing combines with feature analysis to help perception become accurate.** Knowledge constrains likely combinations of properties. Knowing that a carrot, lake, and tire are expected supplies likely object identities and feature combinations. Directing attention to the objects rather than the digits also makes their actual color–shape pairings easier to report.||In **Treisman and Schmidt (1982)**, when participants **ignored the black numbers and focused on the objects**, they could **correctly pair shapes and colors**. Changing attention also improves binding without changing the objects themselves.
25|**Attention enhances neural responding**, and **attentional processing is distributed across a large number of brain areas**.||A **topographic map is a spatial map of visual stimuli on the visual cortex**. Each stimulus point produces activity at a specific cortical location; **neighboring stimulus points cause activity at neighboring cortical points**.||**Topographic** means that spatial relationships are preserved in the map. Two nearby points in the visual field produce responses in nearby cortical positions.
26|A **topographic map** preserves orderly relationships between positions in the visual field and positions in visual cortex. **Nearby visual positions remain nearby within the cortical representation**.||The **left and right visual fields project mainly to opposite hemispheres**, while positions within the mapped areas retain systematic spatial relationships.||The map represents where the stimulus is in the visual field. Looking to a selected location can strengthen its corresponding response without changing where that location is represented in the map.
27|During a **search task**, **fMRI detects cortical activity**. **Attention to an expected direction of motion causes activity to increase in a number of brain areas**.||An **expected direction of motion** is the motion feature the observer is trying to find. Preparing to detect it increases activity in multiple areas involved in processing the relevant information.||The effect is **distributed across the cortex**. An **expected direction of motion** is the motion feature the observer is trying to find. Preparing to detect it increases activity in multiple areas involved in processing the relevant information.
28|Visual patterns at **A, B, and C on the disk** produce spatially organized activity. **When this activity is transmitted to higher visual areas, the observer perceives the patterns at locations A, B, and C**.||Each labeled position on the disk has a corresponding neural representation. 29|**Datta and DeYoe (2009)** used **fMRI** while an observer paid attention to different locations on a disk. The display can remain similar while the selected location and its activity pattern change.
30|**Datta and DeYoe created attention maps** and **trained classifiers to predict where someone was paying attention** from measured activity patterns.||**After training classifiers, they predicted where someone was paying attention with 100% accuracy in the task.**||A **classifier** learns which activity patterns go with which attended locations. It can then use a new measured pattern to choose the location the participant is attending to in the task.

32|**William James (1890)** describes attention as **“the taking possession by the mind, in clear and vivid form, of one out of what seem several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought.”**||**“Focalization, concentration of consciousness are of its essence.”** Attention can prioritize either an external object or a train of thought.
