**Sperling was studying visual sensory memory**, which is also called **iconic memory**. **Before his study, people thought that visual sensory memory could hold only 4 to 5 items**, because that is the number people managed in whole report.
  - The older view took the whole report score as the size of the store.

**The other conditions in his study showed two things** about this belief. **It is true that people can only report 4 to 5 items before memory decays**, or fades away. **But sensory memory actually encodes the whole scene**, because partial report revealed that nearly every row was available at first.
  - The limit lies in how much can be reported in time, not in how much was registered.

Sperling’s results establish that **sensory memory has a large capacity but fast decay**. **Within just 1 second, most of sensory memory decays**, and what remains is **only what was moved to short-term memory through attention**.
  - The large capacity gives attention a full snapshot to choose from.
  - The fast decay means attention has to choose quickly, and whatever it does not pick is lost.

Sperling also measured how availability changes with the **delay of the tone**. With **no delay**, about **82%** of the letters were available, and availability fell quickly over the next few tenths of a second. By a delay of about **1 second**, partial report had dropped to roughly the **whole report** level, about **36%**.
  - The advantage of cueing a single row disappears within about a second.

The delay is the time between the display and the tone, and participants saw nothing during that time. The decline therefore reflects sensory memory fading after the letters were gone, which is the fast decay of iconic memory.
  - Nothing new was shown during the delay, so the loss cannot be blamed on new input.

This decay also explains why whole report is limited. Naming letters takes longer than the icon lasts, so by the time someone reaches the fifth or sixth letter, most of the icon has already faded.
  - The whole report limit of about four or five letters is set by decay rather than by capacity.

The **visuospatial sketchpad** **holds visual and spatial information**. **Visual** information concerns appearance, such as shape and color, while **spatial** information concerns location and arrangement, such as where objects are relative to one another.
  - The sketchpad keeps both kinds of information available for a short time.

The sketchpad is **involved in the process of visual imagery**, which means forming or holding a picture in the mind without seeing it. Planning a route, picturing how furniture fits in a room and mentally turning an object all rely on the sketchpad.
  - Each of these tasks works on an image rather than on words.

The sketchpad is connected to the **central executive** and to **long-term knowledge**. The executive decides what the sketchpad holds, and long-term knowledge supplies stored images and layouts that can be brought into it.
  - Familiar scenes can be pictured because long-term memory feeds them into the sketchpad.

The sketchpad and the phonological loop are separate parts, so a visual task and a verbal task can run together with little interference. A picture in the sketchpad leaves the loop free for words, and words in the loop leave the sketchpad free for images and places.
  - The sketchpad can also transform images, such as turning a mental picture to a new angle, so it manipulates information as well as storing it.

**Fixation** and **functional fixedness** also get in the way of the **two-string problem**, where the task is to **tie together two strings** that hang from the ceiling. The strings hang too far apart to reach both at once, so while holding one string, a person cannot reach the other.
  - A few objects are in the room, including a pair of **pliers**.

The answer is to **use the pliers to create a pendulum**. Tying the pliers to one string turns it into a **pendulum**, which is a weight that swings back and forth.
  - The person sets it swinging, grabs the other string and catches the first one as it swings near.

**The usual function of pliers gets in the way of seeing them as a weight**. People think of pliers as a gripping tool, and that familiar use hides the fact that pliers are heavy, which is the property the solution needs.
  - The solver also has to imagine motion and picture a hanging string as something that can swing toward them.

The pliers are in plain view the whole time. The difficulty lies in how they are represented, as a gripping tool rather than as a heavy object that can swing.
  - Fixation is a problem of representation rather than a problem of missing materials.

**Illusory correlations** occur when **a correlation appears to exist, but either does not exist or is much weaker than assumed**. A **correlation** is a relationship in which one thing tends to go along with another.
  - An illusory correlation is a relationship we believe in that the evidence does not support.

**Illusory correlations can occur when we expect two things to be related, so we fool ourselves into thinking they are related even when they are not**. What we expect guides what we notice, and what we notice guides what we believe.
  - **Stereotypes** are **oversimplified generalizations about a group or class of people that often focus on the negative**.
  - **A stereotype about a group may lead people to pay particular attention to behaviors associated with that stereotype**, and **this attention creates an illusory correlation that reinforces the stereotype**.
  - People notice and remember the cases that fit and miss the many cases that do not.

Illusory correlation is **related to the availability heuristic**. **Selective attention to stereotypical behaviors makes these behaviors more available** in memory.
  - Those easily recalled examples are then taken as proof of the stereotype, so it seems more accurate than it is.

In the second version, the same disease is expected to kill 600 people, but the outcomes are described in terms of people who will die. This wording makes it the loss frame.
  - With **Program C**, **400 people will die** for certain.
  - With **Program D**, there is **a 1/3 probability that no one will die and a 2/3 probability that 600 people will die**.

**78% of people chose Program D**, the gamble, and 22% chose Program C instead. Program C has the same outcome as Program A, since saving 200 of 600 people means that 400 die.
  - Program D is also the same gamble as Program B.
  - Only the wording changed, yet most people switched from the sure option to the gamble, which is the framing effect.
