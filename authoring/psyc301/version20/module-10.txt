2|A **problem** is **an obstacle between a present state and a goal**, in which **the solution is not immediately obvious**. The **present state** is the way things are now, and the **goal** is the way we want them to be. >> The **obstacle** is whatever stops us from going straight from the present state to the goal. || The **sliding-tile puzzle** has exactly this structure. The starting board holds eight numbered tiles and one empty space in a scrambled order, with **8, blank, 6** across the top, while the goal board holds the same pieces in order, with the empty space in the top left followed by **1** through **8**. >> The two boards differ only in where the pieces sit. || Knowing the goal is not the same as knowing how to get there. The finished board is easy to describe, but a tile can only move into the empty space, so reaching the goal takes many moves made in the right order. >> That movement rule is the obstacle in this puzzle. || The words **not immediately obvious** separate a problem from a routine task. A problem means that the path has to be worked out, so problem solving calls for thinking rather than simply following instructions. >> A task with an obvious path is an exercise, not a problem.
3|According to **Koffka (1935)**, **Gestalt psychologists** studied **learning, problem solving, and even attitudes and beliefs**, as well as **perception**. They **took a perceptual approach** to every one of these topics. >> To them, solving a problem resembles seeing a figure, because the same elements lead to a solution or to a dead end depending on how they are organized into a whole. || For Gestalt psychologists, **problem solving** had two parts. The first part is **representing the problem in the mind**, which means how the solver mentally arranges its elements, and the second part is **restructuring**, which means **reorganizing the problem’s representation** so that it is seen in a new way. >> A solution often appears only after the second part has happened. || **Köhler’s circle problem** asks, **if the length of the circle’s radius is r, what is the length of line x?** A rectangle sits in one quarter of the circle, and line **x** is one of the rectangle’s diagonals. >> **Different people might represent this problem differently**, and **their representations are likely to differ from the picture**. >> When x is seen as a lone line, its length seems hard to work out. || The answer comes from **restructuring** the picture. The rectangle’s other diagonal runs from the circle’s center to the corner that touches the circle, so it is a radius with length **r**. >> A rectangle’s two diagonals are always equal, so x equals the radius as well. \[x=r\]
4|**Solutions are often obtained by first perceiving the object and then representing it in a different way**, and this is called **restructuring of the problem’s representation**. The elements of the problem do not change, but the way the solver arranges them does. >> Restructuring changes the solver’s view rather than the problem itself. || **Gestalt psychologists related restructuring to insight**. **Insight** is a **sudden realization of a problem’s solution**, and it involves **discovering a crucial element** that had been missed. >> Gestalt psychologists **thought that solutions come to people all of a sudden**, because the solver sees the problem the same way until something sets off a new arrangement. >> That moment is called the **Aha experience**, which is the feeling of the right representation clicking into place. || **Other researchers disagree that all problem solving works this way**, because **much of our problem solving does not require Aha experiences**. Plenty of problems are solved one step at a time, with each step bringing the solver a little closer. >> Insight is one route to a solution and methodical work is another, which raises the question of how to tell them apart.
5|**Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987)** **designed an experiment to distinguish between insight and noninsight problems**. The **insight problems** included the **triangle problem** and the **chain problem**, and the **noninsight problems** were **algebra** problems. >> Comparing the two kinds of problems lets the feeling of progress be measured for each. || They expected opposite patterns for the two kinds of problems. **In insight problems, participants should not be very good at predicting how near they are to a solution**, because if insight comes suddenly, the solver should feel far away right until the answer arrives. >> **Noninsight problems involve a more methodical process**, so **participants should know how close they are to the solution**. >> In those problems, every finished step brings the answer visibly closer. || Participants gave **warmth judgments every 15 seconds**, rating how close they felt to the answer on a scale from cold to hot, and **hot ratings indicated that they were getting close to the solution**. Checking only whether people solved the problem would not reveal whether the answer came suddenly or gradually. >> Warmth ratings track the feeling of closeness over time. >> Steadily rising warmth means gradual progress, while low warmth followed by a sudden jump means insight.
6|Two **insight problems** used by **Metcalfe and Wiebe** reveal what kind of restructuring insight requires. In each one, the obvious approach fails, and the answer appears only when the parts are seen differently. >> Both problems look simple, which makes the obvious approach hard to give up. || The **triangle problem** asks how to **move three of the blue dots to make the triangle point to the bottom of the page**. The triangle has ten dots arranged in rows of one, two, three and four, and seven of the dots stay where they are while three of them move. >> The top dot goes below the middle of the bottom row, and the two ends of the bottom row go up beside the row of two dots. >> The rows now run four, three, two and one, so the triangle points down. || The **chain problem** asks how to **join four short pieces of chain into a single closed loop**, where each piece has three links. **Opening a link costs 2 cents, closing a link costs 3 cents, and there are only 15 cents to spend**. >> The obvious plan opens one link on each piece, which costs 20 cents and goes over budget. >> The answer is to open all three links of one piece and use those three open links to join the other three pieces into a loop, for a total of exactly 15 cents. \[3(2+3)=15\] || Neither answer comes from gradual improvement. Each one requires leaving the obvious approach behind and seeing the parts differently, such as treating one piece of chain as a set of connectors. >> This is the kind of restructuring that Gestalt psychologists linked to insight.
7|**Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987)** compared **warmer and colder ratings** for insight and noninsight problems as the moment of solution approached. The question was whether people could feel themselves getting closer. >> The two kinds of problems produced very different patterns of ratings. || For the **algebra** problems, which were the noninsight problems, warmth showed a **gradual increase**. Ratings climbed steadily over the last minute and reached the top at the solution, so **noninsight problems were solved gradually**. >> Each finished step is visible progress, so warmth builds as the steps add up. || For the **insight** problems, warmth showed a **big jump from 3 to 7**. Ratings stayed low until just before the answer and then leapt to the top at the moment of solution, so **insight problems were solved suddenly**. >> No step-by-step progress can be felt, so warmth stays low until the restructuring happens. || The results confirm the prediction that insight and noninsight problems feel different from the inside. Participants felt cold only seconds before solving the insight problems, so insight does arrive suddenly, as Gestalt psychologists proposed. >> Their sense of being close was a poor guide to how close they really were.
8|**Gestalt psychologists claimed that one of the major obstacles to problem solving is fixation**. **Fixation** is the **tendency to focus on a specific characteristic of the problem that keeps people from arriving at a solution**. >> **Functional fixedness** is one kind of fixation, which means **restricting the use of an object to its familiar functions**. >> We see an object as having one purpose and overlook its other possible uses. || The **candle problem** tests functional fixedness. A person faces a **vertical corkboard mounted on the wall** and receives a **candle**, a **box of matches** and a **box of tacks**, and the task is to **mount the candle on the corkboard so that it will burn without dripping wax on the floor**. >> The answer is to empty the matchbox, tack it to the board and set the candle in it like a shelf. || **Seeing the matchboxes as containers inhibited people from using them as supports**, because full boxes looked only like containers. **Participants who were given empty matchboxes were twice as likely to solve the problem**. >> An empty box no longer looks like a container in use, so its flat bottom and shape are easier to notice as useful. >> A small change that doubles the success rate reveals how strongly the familiar function held people back.
9|**Fixation** and **functional fixedness** also get in the way of the **two-string problem**, where the task is to **tie together two strings** that hang from the ceiling. The strings hang too far apart to reach both at once, so while holding one string, a person cannot reach the other. >> A few objects are in the room, including a pair of **pliers**. || The answer is to **use the pliers to create a pendulum**. Tying the pliers to one string turns it into a **pendulum**, which is a weight that swings back and forth. >> The person sets it swinging, grabs the other string and catches the first one as it swings near. || **The usual function of pliers gets in the way of seeing them as a weight**. People think of pliers as a gripping tool, and that familiar use hides the fact that pliers are heavy, which is the property the solution needs. >> The solver also has to imagine motion and picture a hanging string as something that can swing toward them. || The pliers are in plain view the whole time. The difficulty lies in how they are represented, as a gripping tool rather than as a heavy object that can swing. >> Fixation is a problem of representation rather than a problem of missing materials.
10|In the **two-string problem**, **once a hint was provided by accidentally setting a string in motion, most participants were able to solve it**. The experimenter bumped one string so that it swung, and seeing it swing suggested a pendulum without giving the answer away. >> **In Gestalt terms, the solution occurred when participants restructured their representation**. >> The hint changed how the problem was represented, not what was available in the room. || A **mental set** is a broader form of fixation. It is a **preconceived notion about how to approach a problem**, and it is **based on a person’s past experiences with the problem or with similar problems**. >> A method that worked before gets used again, even when a better method is available. || The **water-jug problem** reveals how a mental set works. The task is to **figure out how to obtain a required volume of water, given three empty jars for measuring**. >> After solving several problems with one formula, people keep using it on new problems, even when a simpler method would work.
11|Each **water-jug problem** **specifies the capacities of jugs A, B and C and a final desired quantity**. **The task is to figure out how to use jugs with these capacities to measure out the desired quantity**, measured in quarts. >> Only filling, pouring and emptying are allowed, so the answer has to come from combining the jug sizes. || In problem 1, the jugs hold **21, 127 and 3** quarts, and the target is **100** quarts. The solver fills jug B with 127 quarts and pours from B into A until A is full, which leaves 106 quarts in B. >> The solver then pours from B into C until C is full, empties C and fills it once more, which leaves exactly 100 quarts in B. \[B-A-2C=127-21-2(3)=100\] || The same formula, B minus A minus twice C, solves each of the early problems. Participants started applying it automatically, and that automatic habit is a **mental set**. >> The habit forms because the formula keeps working. || The later problems test that habit. Problem 7 has jugs of **15, 39 and 3** quarts and a target of **18**, and although the long formula still works, simply adding A and C is much easier. \[A+C=15+3=18\] >> Problem 8 has jugs of **28, 59 and 3** quarts and a target of **25**, which A minus C solves directly. \[A-C=28-3=25\] >> **The mental set inhibited participants from using the simpler solutions**, and people who had not worked through the earlier problems found the short methods far more often. || A mental set speeds up solving when problems are alike. Once the problems change, though, the same set hides the easier paths. >> Past success can therefore become an obstacle in its own right.
12|**Newell and Simon (1956)** wrote the **Logic Theorist**, an early computer program that proved theorems in logic by hunting for a chain of valid steps. Their approach to problem solving was an **information-processing approach**. >> A program that solves problems by searching suggests that human solving can be described in the same step-by-step terms. || In this approach, **problem solving** is **a process that involves search**. The Gestalt tradition looked at **the initial structure of a problem and the new structure achieved when it is solved**, while Newell and Simon looked instead at what happens in between, which is a **search that occurs between the posing of the problem and its solution**. >> They described this search as **searching for a way to reach a goal** and **getting around roadblocks**. || Every **problem space** contains three kinds of states. The **initial state** holds **the conditions at the beginning of the problem**, and **intermediate states** hold the conditions partway through, after some moves but before the answer. >> The **goal state** is **the solution of the problem**. || To solve a problem is to travel through its space. Starting from the initial state, each move reaches another intermediate state, until the search finally arrives at the goal. >> Problem solving becomes a matter of finding a path through this space.
13|**Operators** are **rules that specify which moves are allowed and which are not**, and operators **take the problem from one state to another**. Every legal move applies an operator, and each operator turns the current state into a new one. >> Without such rules a problem would have no structure, because anything could happen at any step. || The **Tower of Hanoi** has especially clear operators. In the **initial state**, three disks sit on the first of three pegs, with the largest at the bottom and the smallest at the top, and in the **goal state** the same stack sits on the third peg in the same order. >> The task is to move the whole stack from the first peg to the third. || Three rules set the legal moves. The first rule is that **disks are moved one at a time** from one peg to another, and the second rule is that **a disk can be moved only when there are no disks on top of it**. >> The third rule is that **a larger disk cannot be placed on top of a smaller disk**. || These rules are what make the problem hard. The goal is easy to picture, but the stack cannot move all at once, so smaller disks keep moving out of the way and the middle peg serves as a temporary parking spot. >> The solver has to plan steps in between rather than heading straight for the goal.
14|Solving the **Tower of Hanoi** with a larger stack takes a long series of moves. Small disks move again and again to clear the way for larger ones, and a larger disk can move only once every smaller disk above it has gone elsewhere. >> The same pattern repeats at every level of the stack. || The number of moves grows enormously as disks are added. **With 64 disks, solving the puzzle would take a trillion years**, **even if each move were made in one second and every move were correct**. >> The rules never change, yet the number of moves explodes. || A problem space can become huge even when its rules stay simple. The goal state is easy to describe, but the route to it, through many intermediate states, can be extremely long. >> Each larger disk has to wait until the pegs are arranged so that its move is legal. >> Meanwhile the smaller disks are stacked and restacked many times.
15|The **sliding-tile puzzle** has an **initial state** and a **goal state**. In the initial state the eight tiles and the empty space are scrambled, starting with **8, blank, 6** across the top, and in the goal state the empty space is in the top left and the tiles run in order from **1** to **8**. >> Both states contain exactly the same pieces. || The **operators** in this puzzle are tile moves. A tile beside the empty space can move into it, and each such move shifts the empty space to where the tile was, which creates a new state. >> Only the tiles touching the empty space can move at any moment. || Every arrangement of the eight tiles and the empty space is a separate state in the problem space. The same tiles appear in every state, and just one arrangement is the goal, so a solver cannot check every state and the search has to be guided. >> Strategies that pick promising moves at each step are needed to reach the goal without wandering through the entire space.
16|A **search tree** lays out the problem space of a sliding-tile puzzle. Each state is a point in the tree, and each legal move links one state to the next. >> Following a path down the tree is the same as making a series of moves. || The starting board allows **three possible moves**, so the tree splits three ways after the first step. Each of those new states allows further moves, so the tree splits again, and the branching continues at every level. >> Every level multiplies the number of states to consider. || The tree grows wider with every step, because each state opens several new ones. After just a few levels there are dozens of states, and only one path leads to the goal. >> The size of the tree is what makes blind searching hopeless. || Trying every branch would mean facing an enormous number of possibilities. A good strategy follows the branches that bring the board closer to the goal and leaves the rest unexplored. >> Choosing which branches to follow is the real skill in solving the puzzle.
17|**According to Newell and Simon, we have to search the problem space to find a solution**, and one useful strategy for doing so is **means-end analysis**. **Means-end analysis** aims to **reduce the differences between the initial and goal states**. >> At each point, the solver asks how the current state differs from the goal and which move would shrink that difference. || Means-end analysis relies on **subgoals**, because **subgoals create intermediate states closer to the goal**. A **subgoal** is a smaller goal that must be met before the main goal can be reached. >> When no move can close the main difference directly, the solver sets a subgoal that removes the obstacle. >> Subgoals break one hard problem into a series of easier ones. || **For the Tower of Hanoi, applying means-end analysis reduces the size of the difference between the initial and goal states**. The main difference is that the whole stack is on the wrong peg, and the largest disk cannot move until the disks above it are cleared, so clearing them becomes the first subgoal. >> Means-end analysis steers the search toward the goal, so the solver does not have to try every possible move.
18|**Means-end analysis** solves the **Tower of Hanoi** through a chain of subgoals, and each subgoal removes one obstacle to moving the largest disk. Working through them in order turns a confusing puzzle into a series of manageable steps. >> Every subgoal answers the question of what is blocking the next important move. || **Subgoal 1 is to free up the large disk**. The smallest disk moves from the first peg to the third, and the middle disk moves from the first peg to the middle peg. >> The large disk is now uncovered and free to move. || **Subgoal 2 is to free up the third peg**. The smallest disk moves from the third peg onto the middle disk, so the third peg is empty and the large disk has a place to go. >> The two smaller disks now sit legally together on the middle peg. || **Subgoal 3 is to move the large disk onto the third peg**. The large disk moves from the first peg to the third, where it is in its final place. >> The same strategy can then move the two smaller disks onto it. || Some subgoal moves seem to go away from the goal. Putting the smallest disk on the third peg and then taking it off again looks wasteful. >> That detour is what frees the space the large disk needs, and means-end analysis accepts such detours when they remove an obstacle.
19|**One of the main contributions of Newell and Simon’s approach** is that it **provided a way to specify the possible pathways from the initial state to the goal state**. Spelling out the states and operators maps every route through a problem, so problem solving could be described precisely and even programmed into a computer. >> That precision allowed researchers to compare strategies and predict where people would get stuck. || **There is more to problem solving than specifying the problem state**, because **how a problem is stated can affect its difficulty**. Two versions of a problem can share the same states and operators, yet one version can still be much easier. >> The easier version uses wording or a presentation that leads the solver to a representation exposing the key relationship. || The **mutilated checkerboard problem** is a clear case, because how hard it is depends heavily on how the board is presented. Presentation shapes the representation the solver adopts, and that representation decides how hard the search becomes. >> The same states and operators can therefore produce very different amounts of difficulty.
20|A **checkerboard** has **64 squares** and **can be completely covered by placing 32 dominoes on the board**, **with each domino covering 2 squares**. Each domino covers two neighboring squares, either side by side or one above the other. >> Thirty-two dominoes at two squares each cover all 64 squares. || The **mutilated checkerboard problem** asks, **if two opposite corners are eliminated, can the remaining 62 squares be covered with 31 dominoes?** The numbers seem to fit, since 31 dominoes cover 62 squares, but the answer is **no**. >> The arithmetic alone suggests a covering that turns out to be impossible. || The colors of the squares hold the key. Every domino covers **one dark square and one light square**, because neighboring squares are always different colors, and opposite corners share the **same color**, so removing them leaves **32 squares of one color and 30 of the other**. >> Thirty-one dominoes would need 31 squares of each color, so a full covering is impossible. || Placing dominoes one at a time would take a very long time and could never prove that a covering is impossible. Once the board is represented by its colors, a single count settles the question. >> A pure search approach would struggle to reach this answer, while the right representation reaches it at once.
21|**The key to solving the mutilated checkerboard problem** is **understanding two things**. The first is that **each domino covers two squares**, and the second is that **these squares must be of different colors**. >> Once both facts are in view, the impossibility follows from a simple count. || **Conditions differed in how much information was provided about the squares**. **Condition 1** used a **blank board** with no colors, which gave no hint at all, and **condition 2** used a **colored board** of dark and light squares, which gave a visual hint. >> **Condition 3** wrote the **words black and pink** on the squares, which drew attention to the two colors. >> **Condition 4** wrote the **words bread and butter** on the squares. || The problem was **easier to solve when the information provided pointed toward the correct representation of the problem**. The **bread and butter condition emphasized the difference between the squares**, since bread and butter naturally come in pairs, one of each. >> **Participants solved this condition twice as fast as the blank condition**. || The board and the dominoes were identical in all four conditions. The labels changed only how easily solvers came to see each domino as pairing one square of each kind. >> A small change in wording guided people toward the representation that solves the problem.
22|The **think-aloud protocol** is a method for studying problem solving in which participants **say aloud what they are thinking while solving a problem**. They are **instructed not to describe what they are doing, but to put new thoughts into words** as those thoughts come. >> The **goal** is to discover **what information the participant is attending to while solving the problem**. >> Researchers can then follow what the solver notices and when, instead of seeing only the final answer. || With the **mutilated checkerboard problem**, think-aloud protocols reveal a **shift in how the solver perceives the elements of the problem**. **Participants get the problem after realizing that the words bread and butter are important**. >> Before that moment they talk about laying down dominoes, and afterward they talk about pairing the two kinds of squares. || This shift is **similar to the Gestalt idea of a representational shift**. The board and the rules do not change, but the solver’s mental representation reorganizes, which is restructuring, and the answer follows. >> Think-aloud protocols capture restructuring while it happens, rather than only its outcome.
