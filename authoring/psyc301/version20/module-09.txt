2|The **modal model** of short-term memory turned out to be **too simple**. **Short-term memory is not just passive storage**, because it also depends on **“control processes,”** so a fuller model has to include them. >> **Control processes** are the things we actively do with information, such as choosing it, repeating it and changing it. >> In the modal model, short-term memory was mostly a waiting room where information stayed until it was used or lost. || **Baddeley and Hitch (1974)** proposed **working memory**, which resembles short-term memory but covers much more. **Working memory** is a **limited-capacity system for the temporary storage and manipulation of information for complex tasks such as comprehension, learning and reasoning**. >> **Storage** keeps information on hand for a short time. >> **Manipulation** means working on that information, for example by changing it, combining it or calculating with it. || Complex tasks call for storage and manipulation at the same moment. To **comprehend** a sentence, we hold its opening while linking it to the words that come next, and to **learn**, we keep new material active while tying it to what we already know. >> To **reason**, we keep facts in mind while working toward a conclusion. || Storage and manipulation draw on one shared, **limited capacity**. Heavy processing leaves less room for holding information, and holding a lot leaves less room for processing. >> This is why a mental task gets harder as more has to be juggled at once.
3|**Multiplying 43 times 6 in your head** requires storage and active processing at the same time. The full calculation gives 258. \[43\times6=258\] The work runs in five steps, and each step depends on something the earlier steps left in memory. >> The first step is to picture the whole problem, 43 times 6, and keep it in mind so that later steps can return to it. >> The second step is to multiply 3 by 6, which gives 18. \[3\times6=18\] >> In the third step, the person holds the 8 in memory as the ones digit while carrying the 1 over to the 4. >> In the fourth step, the person multiplies 6 by 4 to get 24 and then adds the carried 1 to get 25. \[6\times4=24,\qquad24+1=25\] >> The fifth step combines the 25 with the 8 that has been held all along, which gives **258**. || The problem uses both **storage** and **active processes**. Keeping the 8 while the work continues is storage, and carrying, multiplying and adding are active processes that change the information. >> The 8 must last through several steps, so the answer depends on storage and processing working together, which is exactly the job of working memory. || Losing any stored piece breaks the answer. If the 8 slips away during the work on the tens, or if the carried 1 is never added, the result comes out wrong. >> Mental arithmetic is therefore a direct test of working memory.
4|**Short-term memory** in the **Atkinson and Shiffrin** model and **working memory** in the **Baddeley and Hitch** model differ both in how they are built and in what they are for. The contrast explains why working memory replaced the older idea. >> The older model describes a single holding place, while the newer model describes a set of cooperating parts. || In the Atkinson and Shiffrin model, short-term memory is a **single component for all types of information**, so words, pictures and sounds all share one store. This store is **mainly used for holding information for a short time**. >> Holding is its main job, with little attention to working on the information. || Working memory in the Baddeley and Hitch model has **three components**. The **central executive** directs and coordinates the system, the **visuospatial sketchpad** holds visual and spatial information, and the **phonological loop** holds verbal and auditory information. >> Baddeley **added** a fourth part, the **episodic buffer**, **in 2000**, as a place where information from different sources can be combined. || Working memory is **used for the manipulation of information during complex cognition**, including reasoning, comprehension and learning. If there were only one store, any two tasks held at once should get in each other’s way. >> With separate components, a verbal task and a visual task can run together with little interference, since each has its own store.
5|Each **component of working memory** has its own specific job. Knowing what each part does makes it possible to predict which tasks will interfere with each other. >> Tasks that need the same part compete, while tasks that need different parts can share the system. || The **phonological loop** deals with verbal and auditory information, and it has two parts. The **phonological store** **holds verbal and auditory information**, such as words and speech sounds, and the **articulatory rehearsal process** is **responsible for the rehearsal that keeps items in the phonological store from decaying**. >> **Articulatory** refers to speech movements, so this process silently repeats the items like an inner voice. >> Without that rehearsal, items in the store fade within a few seconds. || The **visuospatial sketchpad** **holds visual and spatial information**. Visual information is about appearance, such as shape and color, while spatial information is about position and layout, such as how a room is arranged. >> Both kinds of information are held in the same component. || The **central executive** **pulls information from long-term memory, coordinates the other components, and directs and maintains attention**. In **Baddeley’s working memory model**, the executive is linked to the phonological loop, which holds **verbal and auditory information**, and to the sketchpad, which holds **visual and spatial information**. >> The executive stores nothing itself. >> It decides what the two stores hold, brings in long-term knowledge and keeps attention on the task.
6|The **working memory model of Baddeley and Hitch (1974)** describes how information moves through the system. **Input** from the world enters **sensory memory**, and from there it passes to the **central executive**, which trades information back and forth with **long-term knowledge**. >> The executive is the hub through which new input reaches the rest of working memory. || The executive also trades information in both directions with the **phonological loop** and the **visuospatial sketchpad**. A spoken word goes to the phonological loop, and a shape goes to the sketchpad. >> Long-term knowledge links directly to both of these stores as well. || Working memory is a group of interacting parts inside a larger memory system. The executive controls what each store holds and does, and working memory keeps drawing on stored knowledge such as word meanings and familiar images. >> Working memory can also send new information into long-term storage. >> Because information flows both ways, working memory can interpret new input using what we already know, and it also adds to that knowledge.
7|Three effects reveal how the **phonological loop** operates, and each one points to speech sounds as the code it uses. Together they explain why verbal memory behaves the way it does. >> Each effect changes something about sound or speaking and finds a matching change in memory. || In the **phonological similarity effect**, **letters or words that sound similar are confused**. **Phonological** means having to do with the sounds of language. >> Letters such as B, D and G sound alike, so their sound codes overlap and they get mixed up. || In the **word-length effect**, **memory for lists of words is better for short words than for long words**. **It takes longer to rehearse long words and to produce them during recall**. >> In a fixed amount of time, fewer long words can be rehearsed, so fewer of them stay active. || In **articulatory suppression**, **speaking interferes with rehearsal, so memory is reduced**. Saying something aloud, such as “the, the, the,” takes over the rehearsal process. >> The items to be remembered are no longer refreshed, so they fade from the store. || All three effects point in the same direction. If verbal information were stored as images or as meanings, these effects would be hard to explain. >> Verbal information is held as sound and kept alive by an inner voice. >> When that voice is slow, busy or confused by similar sounds, memory suffers.
8|In the **phonological similarity effect**, **words that sound similar are confused by people**, and **Conrad (1964)** tested this effect using letters. **Participants saw target letters that were quickly flashed**, and **then participants wrote them down**. >> **Half of the time the letters were similar, and half of the time they were not**. >> The letters disappeared so fast that writing them down depended on memory. || **Mistakes were made**, and the mistakes followed a clear pattern. Participants were **unlikely to replace a letter with one that looked like the target**, such as writing **F** for **E**, and they were **likely to replace it with one that sounded like the target**, such as writing **B** for **E**. >> The letters E and F share strokes but have different-sounding names. >> The names of E and B end in the same vowel sound, which is why they were confused. >> Lists of similar-sounding letters led to more errors than lists of distinct-sounding letters. || The participants were holding the letters by sound. They only ever saw the letters and never heard them, yet a visual code would have produced confusions between letters that look alike. >> A sound code produces confusions between letters whose names sound alike. >> The errors followed sound, so the letters were held as sound codes in the phonological loop.
9|The **phonological similarity effect experiment** established that **people confuse letters that sound similar**, **even though the information was presented visually**. The letters came in through the eyes, but the confusions still came from how the letters sound rather than how they look. >> Sight was the input, yet sound was the code that memory kept. || The experiment **also revealed that the phonological loop is necessary for the conversion**, **not just for holding information**. No one has to decide to name a letter silently, because seeing it brings up its spoken name, and that name is what memory keeps. >> This automatic **recoding** turns a visual input into a phonological code. || The phonological loop therefore does two jobs in this task. It converts written letters and words into sound, and then it stores and rehearses that sound code until recall. >> This is why seen items produce sound-based errors. || Recoding explains why written material behaves like spoken material in memory. Once letters become names, the same inner voice that rehearses heard words rehearses them too. >> Errors then follow sound, however the letters first arrived.
10|In the **word-length effect**, **when memorizing words, a person can remember fewer words if the words are long**. **Baddeley and colleagues (1975, 1984)** found that **people remembered more of the short words than the long words**. >> Long words take more time to say, so fewer of them can be rehearsed before they fade. || **Ellis and Hennelly (1980)** found that **Welsh and English bilingual children** have a **shorter digit span in Welsh than in English**. The cause is that **Welsh numbers take longer to pronounce**. >> The children know the same numbers in both languages and only the speaking time differs, which makes the comparison especially clean. >> The Welsh place name **Llanfairpwllgwyngyllgogerychwyrndrobwllllantysiliogogogoch** is a well-known example of a very long Welsh word. || **The number of words a person can say in 1.5 to 2.0 seconds is likely to be that person’s digit span**. The phonological loop holds roughly what can be rehearsed in that window before it fades. >> Short words fit easily and long words fit less well, so span depends on speaking time rather than on a fixed number of items. || The limit of the phonological loop is therefore a limit of time. More items fit when each one is quick to say, and fewer fit when each one takes longer. >> This time limit explains why the same person can have different spans in different languages.
11|**Articulatory suppression** happens **when a person speaks while memorizing**, which **keeps the phonological loop busy**. The person **gets worse at remembering**, and **the word-length and phonological similarity effects are reduced**. >> Both effects depend on the loop, so occupying the loop weakens both. || The **word-length effect** shrinks because rehearsal is blocked. Short words normally win because they can be rehearsed faster, but when speaking takes over rehearsal, neither short nor long words get rehearsed. >> The advantage of short words therefore fades away. || The **phonological similarity effect** shrinks because the conversion into sound is blocked. Seen letters are normally turned into sound codes, which causes sound-based confusions. >> With the loop busy, visually presented letters are not turned into sound, so sound similarity matters less. || These findings **support the concept of a phonological loop**. Repeating “the, the, the” fills the speech system that rehearsal needs, and a single manipulation removes both effects. >> Rehearsal and recoding must therefore rely on the same speech-based system. >> If the two effects came from separate systems, one manipulation would not remove both of them.
12|The **visuospatial sketchpad** **holds visual and spatial information**. **Visual** information concerns appearance, such as shape and color, while **spatial** information concerns location and arrangement, such as where objects are relative to one another. >> The sketchpad keeps both kinds of information available for a short time. || The sketchpad is **involved in the process of visual imagery**, which means forming or holding a picture in the mind without seeing it. Planning a route, picturing how furniture fits in a room and mentally turning an object all rely on the sketchpad. >> Each of these tasks works on an image rather than on words. || The sketchpad is connected to the **central executive** and to **long-term knowledge**. The executive decides what the sketchpad holds, and long-term knowledge supplies stored images and layouts that can be brought into it. >> Familiar scenes can be pictured because long-term memory feeds them into the sketchpad. || The sketchpad and the phonological loop are separate parts, so a visual task and a verbal task can run together with little interference. A picture in the sketchpad leaves the loop free for words, and words in the loop leave the sketchpad free for images and places. >> The sketchpad can also transform images, such as turning a mental picture to a new angle, so it manipulates information as well as storing it.
13|The **sentence experiment** of **Brooks (1968)** puts the **phonological loop** and the **visuospatial sketchpad** in competition. Participants **memorized a sentence** and kept it in mind, and then they stepped through it word by word and **indicated whether each word was or was not a noun**. >> In **condition 1**, they **indicated their answers by speaking**, saying yes or no. >> In **condition 2**, they **indicated their answers by pointing** to yes or no on a sheet. >> The task stayed the same, and only the way of responding changed. || For the participants, **pointing was easier than speaking**, because **the phonological loop was busy processing the sentence, but the sketchpad was free**. A sentence is verbal, so it sat in the phonological loop, and spoken answers also need the loop, so they competed with the sentence. >> Pointing relies on the sketchpad, which had nothing else to do, so there was no competition. || Holding a sentence and sorting its words keeps the phonological loop busy the whole time. A second verbal demand interferes with that work, while a spatial response does not. >> The pattern points to separate verbal and spatial resources.
14|The **F task** of **Brooks (1968)** tests the same two parts of working memory with a picture instead of a sentence. Participants **memorized a shape**, a block letter **F**, and kept the image in mind. >> Starting at a corner marked with a star, they mentally moved around the F and **indicated whether each corner was an inside corner or an outside corner**. >> In **condition 1**, they **indicated their answers by speaking**, saying in or out. >> In **condition 2**, they **indicated their answers by pointing**, going down a numbered list of corners and pointing to **OUT** or **IN** in each row. || Here **speaking was easier than pointing**, which is **the opposite of the sentence experiment**, because **the sketchpad was busy with the image, but the phonological loop was free**. The F is a visual image, so it sat in the sketchpad. >> Pointing means looking and moving the hand to places on a sheet, which needs the same spatial resources as the image. >> Speaking uses the phonological loop, which was free to help. || The two Brooks tasks produce a reversal. Speaking was harder for the sentence and easier for the image, while pointing was the reverse. >> A single store could not produce this crossover, so the two kinds of memory must be separate.
15|**Tasks are easier when the information held in mind and the operation performed on it involve different types of short-term memory**. One type is **verbal or phonological**, and the other type is **visual or spatial**. >> A verbal memory with a spatial response, as in pointing during the sentence task, was easy. >> A visual memory with a verbal response, as in speaking during the F task, was also easy. >> When the memory and the response used the same system, performance dropped. || **The two types of short-term memory are therefore somewhat independent**, and **at the least, they have separate capacities**. Filling the phonological loop leaves the sketchpad with room to spare, and filling the sketchpad leaves the loop with room to spare. >> Each system has its own limit that the other does not use up. || Each part interferes with itself far more than with the other. Two verbal demands compete with each other, and so do two visual and spatial demands, while a verbal demand paired with a visual and spatial one lets both run well. >> Listening to directions while driving uses both systems, and trouble comes where their demands overlap.

17|**Mental rotation** means turning a mental image to a new orientation. **Shepard and Metzler (1971)** showed participants pairs of three-dimensional objects built from cubes, and participants judged whether the two were the same object or mirror images of each other. >> In **picture-plane rotation** pairs, one object is turned flat within the page, much like the hand of a clock. >> In **depth rotation** pairs, one object is turned as if it were spun around a vertical axis. >> Mirror-image pairs can never be rotated into a match, so rejecting them depends on finishing the rotation. || Reaction time, or **RT**, grew steadily with the **angle of rotation** between the two objects. Pairs at **0 degrees** took about **1 second**, and pairs at **180 degrees** took about **4.5 seconds**. >> Between those two angles, reaction time rose in a straight line. || Participants **mentally rotated** one object to line it up with the other, so a larger difference in angle needed a longer rotation. Each extra degree added about the same amount of time, as if the image turned at a steady speed through every position in between, the way a real object would. >> Depth rotations produced the same straight-line pattern, so the mental image is handled as a three-dimensional object. >> Mental rotation is active manipulation carried out in the visuospatial sketchpad.
18|The **central executive** **does the real work of working memory**, and it has several jobs. It is responsible for **directing and maintaining attention**, which covers **focusing**, **dividing** and **switching** attention. >> Focusing keeps relevant information active, dividing spreads resources over two tasks, and switching moves resources from one task to another. >> The executive also **controls the suppression of irrelevant information**, so that distractions do not use up working memory. >> It **coordinates the sketchpad and the phonological loop** so that both can serve a task, and it **performs calculations**, as in mental arithmetic. || The executive is **often split into updating, shifting and inhibition**, following the work of **Miyake and colleagues (2000)**. **Updating** replaces old contents of working memory with new, relevant information, and **shifting** means moving between tasks or mental sets. >> **Inhibition** means holding back unwanted thoughts or responses. || **The idea of a central executive is often criticized as theorizing a homunculus in the brain**. A **homunculus** is a little person in the head who makes every decision, so saying that an executive controls everything names the control without explaining it. >> Dividing the executive into updating, shifting and inhibition gives specific functions that can be measured separately and that vary between people.
19|The **episodic buffer** is **a fourth component, added to the working memory model by Baddeley in 2000**. It has a **limited capacity of about 4 chunks**, where a **chunk** is a meaningful unit that may contain several pieces. >> The buffer **binds information from the phonological loop, the visuospatial sketchpad and long-term memory into integrated episodes**. >> **Binding** means joining separate pieces of information into one combined memory. || In the updated model, the **central executive** connects to the **phonological loop**, the **episodic buffer** and the **visuospatial sketchpad**. The buffer links with both the loop and the sketchpad, and all three of these parts connect to **long-term knowledge**. >> The buffer sits between the two stores so that their contents can be combined. || Two findings explain **why the episodic buffer was needed**. **People can recall about 16 words of a meaningful sentence**, which is **far beyond their span for unrelated words**, because meaning and grammar join the words into a few large chunks that the original model had nowhere to keep. >> **Some amnesic patients can immediately recall a story they have just heard**, even though **amnesia** is a severe loss of long-term memory. >> Since these patients could not store the story in long-term memory, it must have been held as a meaningful episode in a working memory component large enough to contain it.
20|**Working memory and the brain** can be studied through **individual differences**, meaning the ways in which people differ from one another. **Vogel and colleagues (2005)** first **determined participants’ working memory capacity**, sorted them into a **high-capacity group** and a **low-capacity group**, and then **measured ERP responses**. >> An **ERP**, or **event-related potential**, is electrical brain activity recorded from the scalp and timed to events in the task. || Participants were **shown either simple or complex stimuli** in trials that had four phases. A **cue** arrow first pointed left or right to mark which side to remember, and then a brief **memory display** of colored bars at different angles appeared. >> A blank **delay** followed, and the ERPs recorded during this delay tracked how much each person was holding. >> A **test display** then asked whether the angle of a red bar had changed. || In the **simple** condition, the cued side had only **red bars**, which were the targets. In the **complex** condition, it had red bars plus **blue bars**, which were **distractors** to be ignored. >> If a person screens out the blue bars, brain activity should reflect only the red ones. >> If the blue bars get in, activity should rise as though more items were being held.
21|**Vogel and colleagues (2005)** compared brain activity in **high-capacity** and **low-capacity** participants across three conditions. In the first condition there were **two items**, meaning two red bars, and in the second there were **four items**, meaning four red bars. >> **Two items with two distractors** made up the third condition, in which two red bars appeared with two blue bars to ignore. || **Activity continues well into the retention interval**, after the memory display is gone. This activity reflects the items being held in visual working memory, so the more items a person holds, the more activity appears. >> The size of this activity therefore works as a measure of how much is in memory. || In **high-capacity** individuals, **activity is greater when there are four relevant items than when there are two relevant items**. **Adding irrelevant blue items has no effect**, so two targets with two distractors look like two targets alone. >> These people keep the distractors out of working memory. || In **low-capacity** individuals, **adding irrelevant items increases activity**, so two targets with two distractors produce activity close to that for four targets. These people let the distractors in, where they take up space meant for targets. >> The groups differ mainly in how well they control what enters working memory, not in how much raw space they have.
22|**Vogel and colleagues (2005)** also related each participant’s **memory capacity** to that person’s **filtering efficiency**. **Filtering efficiency** is an **EEG** measure of how well someone keeps irrelevant items out of working memory, and **EEG** records the brain’s electrical activity from the scalp. >> Filtering efficiency asks how much the distractors added to brain activity. || **Capacity is correlated with the EEG measure of filtering efficiency**, so **people with higher capacity are also less distracted by the irrelevant items**. The people who hold more items are the same people who keep unneeded items out. >> Holding more and filtering better go together. || This changes what a difference in capacity means. High capacity depends on control over what gets in, as well as on how much can be held. >> People with high capacity screen out distractors, which leaves their limited space for relevant information. >> People with low capacity admit distractors, which fill working memory with items they do not need. || Better attentional control could therefore free working memory for the information that matters. A person’s effective capacity reflects both the size of the store and the strength of the filter. >> Improving the filter would let the same store hold more of what the task needs.
