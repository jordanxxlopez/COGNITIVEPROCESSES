2|A **problem** is **an obstacle between a present state and a goal**, where **the solution is not immediately obvious**. >> The **present state** is how things are right now. >> The **goal** is how we want things to be. >> The **obstacle** is whatever keeps us from getting directly from one to the other. || The **sliding-tile puzzle** captures this structure. >> The starting board has eight numbered tiles and one empty space, arranged as **8, blank, 6** in the top row, **5, 4, 7** in the middle row and **2, 3, 1** in the bottom row. >> The goal board has the empty space in the top left, followed by **1, 2** in the top row, **3, 4, 5** in the middle row and **6, 7, 8** in the bottom row. >> Both boards have exactly the same pieces. Only their positions differ. || Knowing the goal is different from knowing how to reach it. >> The finished arrangement is easy to describe. >> The obstacle is the rule that tiles can move only into the empty space, so reaching the goal takes many moves in the right order. >> The phrase solution not immediately obvious separates a problem from a routine task. A problem requires figuring out the path, which is why problem solving involves thinking rather than simply following instructions.
3|**Gestalt psychologists** were interested in **learning, problem solving, and even attitudes and beliefs**, along with **perception**, according to **Koffka (1935)**. >> They **took a perceptual approach** to all of these. >> In the Gestalt view, solving a problem works much like seeing a figure. The same elements can lead to a solution or to a dead end depending on how they are organized into a whole. || **Problem solving for Gestalt psychologists** had two parts. >> **Representing the problem in the mind**, which is how the solver mentally organizes the problem’s elements. >> **Restructuring**, which is **reorganizing the problem’s representation** to see it in a new way. || **Köhler’s “circle” problem** asks this question. **If the length of the circle’s radius is r, what is the length of line x?** >> The figure has a circle with a horizontal and a vertical radius, and a rectangle drawn in one quarter of the circle. Line **x** is a diagonal of the rectangle running from the center to a side of the rectangle. >> **Different people might represent this problem differently**, and **people’s representations are likely to differ from the picture**. As long as x is seen as a lone line, its length seems hard to find. || The solution comes from **restructuring**. >> The rectangle has a second diagonal, running from the center of the circle to the corner of the rectangle that touches the circle. >> That second diagonal is a radius, so its length is **r**. >> The two diagonals of a rectangle are always equal, so line x is also equal to the radius. \[x=r\]
4|**Solutions are often obtained by first perceiving the object and then representing it in a different way**. This is the **restructuring of the problem’s representation**. >> The elements of the problem stay the same. >> The way the solver organizes them changes. || **Gestalt psychologists related restructuring to insight**. **Insight** has several features. >> It is a **sudden realization of a problem’s solution**. >> It involves **discovering a crucial element** of the problem that was missed before. >> Gestalt psychologists **thought solutions come to people all of a sudden**. The solver keeps looking at the problem the same way until something triggers a new organization. >> This sudden realization is the **“Aha” experience**, the feeling that comes when the right representation clicks into place. || **Other researchers disagree that all problem solving works this way**. >> **Much of our problem solving does not require “Aha” experiences**. >> Many problems are solved step by step, with each step bringing the solver a little closer. >> Insight is one way of solving problems, and methodical step-by-step solving is another, so the question becomes how to tell them apart.
5|**Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987)** **designed an experiment to distinguish between insight and noninsight problems**. >> **Insight problems** included the **triangle problem** and the **chain problem**. >> **Noninsight problems** were **algebra** problems. || The **hypothesis** made opposite predictions for the two kinds of problems. >> **In insight problems, participants should not be very good at predicting how near they are to a solution**. If insight arrives suddenly, the solver should feel far from the answer right up until it appears. >> **Noninsight problems involve a more methodical process**, so **participants should know how close they are to the solution**. Each completed step brings the answer visibly closer. || The measure was **warmth judgments every 15 seconds**. >> Participants rated how close they felt to the solution, from cold to hot. >> **“Hot” ratings indicated that they were getting close to the solution**. >> Measuring only whether people found the answer would not reveal whether it came suddenly or gradually. Warmth ratings give a moment-by-moment record. >> A steady rise in warmth means gradual progress, while a flat line followed by a sudden jump means insight.
6|Two **insight problems** used by **Metcalfe and Wiebe** reveal the kind of restructuring insight requires. || The **triangle problem** asks how to **move three of the blue dots to make the triangle point to the bottom of the page**. >> The triangle has ten dots, with one dot at the top, then rows of two, three and four dots. >> Seven dots stay where they are, and only three move. >> Move the top dot to below the middle of the bottom row. Move the two end dots of the bottom row up to the ends of the two-dot row. >> The rows now hold four, three, two and one dots from top to bottom, so the triangle points down. || The **chain problem** asks how to **join four short pieces of chain into a single closed loop**. Each piece has three links. **Opening a link costs 2 cents, closing a link costs 3 cents, and there are only 15 cents**. >> The obvious approach opens one link on each piece and joins them in a ring, which takes four openings and four closings and costs 20 cents, more than the budget. >> The solution is to open all three links of one piece, which costs 6 cents. >> Those three open links then join the other three pieces into a loop, and closing them costs 9 cents. \[3(2+3)=15\] >> The total is exactly 15 cents. || Neither solution comes from gradual, step-by-step improvement. Each requires breaking away from the obvious approach and seeing the elements differently, such as treating one piece of chain as a set of connectors.
7|**Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987)** compared **“warmer/colder” ratings** over time for insight and noninsight problems. The graph plots **median warmth rating**, from **cold, at 0**, to **solved, at 7**, against **time before the solution**, from 60 seconds before to the moment of solution. || For **algebra**, the noninsight problems, warmth showed a **gradual increase**. >> Ratings sat at about **3** at 60 and 45 seconds before the solution. >> They rose to about **4** at 30 seconds, **5** at 15 seconds and **7** at the solution. >> **Noninsight problems were solved gradually**. Each completed step is visible progress, so warmth rises as the steps accumulate. || For **insight** problems, warmth showed a **big jump from 3 to 7**. >> Ratings stayed at about **2** from 60 to 30 seconds before the solution. >> They rose only to about **3** at 15 seconds before the solution, then jumped to **7** at the solution. >> **Insight problems were solved suddenly**. No step-by-step progress is visible, so warmth stays low until the restructuring happens. || The results confirm the hypothesis. >> Insight and noninsight problems feel different from the inside. >> Participants reported feeling cold just seconds before solving insight problems, so insight really does arrive suddenly, as the Gestalt psychologists proposed.
8|**Gestalt psychologists claimed that one of the major obstacles to problem solving is fixation**. **Fixation** is the **tendency to focus on a specific characteristic of the problem that keeps people from arriving at a solution**. >> **Functional fixedness** is a kind of fixation. It is **restricting the use of an object to its familiar functions**. >> We see an object as having one purpose and miss other ways it could be used. || The **candle problem** tests functional fixedness. >> A person is in a room with a **vertical corkboard mounted on the wall** and is given a **candle**, a **box of matches** and a **box of tacks**. >> The task is to **mount the candle on the corkboard so it will burn without dripping wax on the floor**. >> The solution is to empty the matchbox, tack it to the corkboard and stand the candle in it as a shelf. || **Seeing the matchboxes as containers inhibited using them as supports**. >> When the boxes held matches and tacks, people thought of them only as containers. >> **Participants who were given empty matchboxes were twice as likely to solve the problem**. >> An empty box no longer looks like a container in use, so it is easier to see as a separate object that can serve as a platform. Doubling the solution rate with this small change reveals how strongly the familiar function was holding people back.
9|**Fixation** and **functional fixedness** also block the **two-string problem**. The task is to **tie together two strings** hanging from the ceiling. >> The strings are too far apart to reach at the same time. >> Holding one string, the person cannot reach the other. >> A few objects, including a pair of **pliers**, are available in the room. || The solution is to **use the pliers to create a pendulum**. >> Tie the pliers to the end of one string, which turns the string into a **pendulum**, a weight that swings back and forth. >> Set it swinging, take hold of the other string and catch the swinging one when it comes close. || **The usual function of pliers gets in the way of seeing them as a weight**. >> People think of pliers as a tool for gripping. >> That familiar function makes it hard to notice that pliers are also heavy, which is the property the solution needs. >> The solution also requires imagining motion. The solver has to see a hanging string as something that can swing toward them, so both the weight and the motion must be recognized.
10|In the **two-string problem**, **once a hint was provided by “accidentally” setting a string in motion, most participants were able to solve it**. >> The experimenter brushed against one string so that it swung. >> Seeing the string swing suggested the idea of a pendulum without stating the answer. >> **In Gestalt terms, the solution occurred when participants restructured their representation**. The hint changed how the problem was represented, not what was available to solve it. || A **mental set** is another obstacle and a broader form of fixation. >> It is a **preconceived notion about how to approach a problem**. >> It is **based on a person’s past experiences with the problem or similar problems**. >> A method that worked before gets applied again, even when a better method is available. || The **water-jug problem** reveals mental set. >> The task is to **figure out how to obtain a required volume of water, given three empty jars for measuring**. >> A person who has solved several problems with one formula keeps reaching for that formula on new problems, even when a simpler method would work.
11|Each **water-jug problem** **specifies the capacities of jugs A, B and C and a final desired quantity**. **The task is to figure out how to use the jugs with these capacities to measure out the desired quantity**. The table lists eight problems, with capacities in quarts. >> Problem 1 has jugs of **21, 127 and 3** and a desired quantity of **100**. >> Problem 2 has **14, 163 and 25**, with a target of **99**. Problem 3 has **18, 43 and 10**, with a target of **5**. Problem 4 has **9, 42 and 6**, with a target of **21**. >> Problem 5 has **20, 59 and 4**, with a target of **31**. Problem 6 has **20, 50 and 3**, with a target of **24**. >> Problem 7 has **15, 39 and 3**, with a target of **18**. Problem 8 has **28, 59 and 3**, with a target of **25**. || For problem 1, the steps run as follows. >> Fill jug B with **127** quarts. >> Pour from B into A until A is full. That removes 21, leaving **106**, which is B minus A. >> Pour from B into C until C is full. That removes 3, leaving **103**. >> Pour from B into C a second time, leaving **100**. \[B-A-2C=127-21-2(3)=100\] || The same formula, B minus A minus 2C, solves the early problems, so participants learned to apply it automatically and developed a **mental set**. >> Problem 7 can be solved the long way, but it also has a much simpler solution. \[A+C=15+3=18\] >> Problem 8 also has a simple solution. \[A-C=28-3=25\] >> **The mental set inhibited participants from using the simpler solutions**. Participants who had not solved the earlier problems found the simpler solutions much more often. The habit speeds up solving when problems are alike but blinds solvers to easier paths when problems change.
12|**Newell and Simon (1956)** created the **Logic Theorist**, an early computer program that proved logical theorems by searching for a sequence of valid steps. They took an **information-processing approach** to problem solving. || They **described problem solving as a process that involves search**. >> Gestalt psychologists focused on **the initial structure of a problem and the new structure achieved when it is solved**. >> **Newell and Simon described problem solving as a search that occurs between the posing of the problem and its solution**. >> It is **“searching for a way to reach a goal”** and **“getting around road-blocks.”** >> If a computer could solve problems by search, human problem solving could be described in the same step-by-step terms. || A **problem space** has three parts. >> The **initial state** is **the conditions at the beginning of the problem**. >> **Intermediate states** are the conditions reached along the way, after some steps but before the solution. >> The **goal state** is **the solution of the problem**. || Solving a problem means moving through the problem space. >> The solver starts at the initial state, and each step leads to a new intermediate state. >> The search ends when the goal state is reached, so problem solving becomes the task of finding a route through this space.
13|**Operators** are **rules that specify which moves are allowed and which are not**. Operators **take the problem from one state to another**. >> Every legal move applies an operator. >> Each operator changes the current state into a new one. Without such rules, a problem would have no structure, since anything could happen at any step. || The **Tower of Hanoi** is a classic problem with clear operators. >> In the **initial state**, three disks are stacked on the first of three pegs, with the largest at the bottom and the smallest at the top. >> In the **goal state**, the same stack sits on the third peg, in the same order. || Three rules define the legal moves. >> **Rule 1. Disks are moved one at a time** from one peg to another. >> **Rule 2. A disk can be moved only when there are no disks on top of it**. >> **Rule 3. A larger disk cannot be placed on top of a smaller disk**. || The rules make the problem challenging. >> The goal state is easy to picture, but the stack cannot be moved all at once. >> The smaller disks have to keep moving out of the way, and the middle peg serves as a temporary holding place. The rules force the solver to plan intermediate steps rather than moving straight to the goal.
14|The full **Tower of Hanoi** solution for a larger stack takes many steps. The sequence of moves shifts the disks back and forth among the three pegs many times. >> Smaller disks move repeatedly to clear the way for larger ones. >> Each larger disk can only move after every smaller disk above it has been moved elsewhere. >> The pattern repeats at every level of the stack. || The number of moves grows enormously as disks are added. >> **With 64 disks, solving it would take a trillion years**, **even if each move were made in one second and every move were correct**. >> The rules stay the same, but the number of required moves explodes. || This makes the size of the problem space clear. >> The goal state is easy to describe. >> The path to it, through many intermediate states, can be extremely long, so problem spaces can become enormous even when the rules stay simple. || The Tower of Hanoi makes this concrete. Each larger disk waits until the pegs are arranged so that its move is legal, and the smaller disks are stacked and restacked on the other pegs many times along the way.
15|The **sliding-tile puzzle** has an **initial state** and a **goal state**. >> In the initial state, the top row is **8, blank, 6**, the middle row is **5, 4, 7** and the bottom row is **2, 3, 1**. >> In the goal state, the top row is **blank, 1, 2**, the middle row is **3, 4, 5** and the bottom row is **6, 7, 8**. || The **operators** in this puzzle are tile moves. >> A tile next to the empty space can move into it. >> Each move shifts the empty space to where the tile was, which produces a new state. || Every arrangement of eight tiles and one empty space is a different state in the problem space. >> The same eight tiles appear in every state, and only one arrangement is the goal state. >> The solver cannot check every possible state, so search must be guided. Strategies that choose promising moves at each step are needed to reach the goal without wandering through the whole space. >> Many arrangements can be reached from the starting board, and each one counts as a separate point in the problem space.
16|A **search tree** maps out the problem space of a sliding-tile puzzle. Each state appears as a node, and each legal move appears as a line to a new node. >> At the top, numbered **1**, is the starting state, with the tiles arranged as **2, 8, 3** in the top row, **1, 6, 4** in the middle row and **7, blank, 5** in the bottom row. >> Each line leading down from a state is a tile sliding into the empty space. || The start has **three possible moves**, which lead to states **2, 3 and 4**. >> In state 2, the 7 moves right. In state 3, the 6 moves down. In state 4, the 5 moves left. >> Each of these states allows further moves, which branch into states **5 through 9**, and those branch further. || The tree widens at every level because each state allows several moves. >> By the bottom levels, there are dozens of states, numbered up to **46**. >> Only one path leads to the state marked **goal**. >> A solver who tried every branch would face an enormous number of possibilities, so a good strategy focuses on branches that move closer to the goal.
17|**According to Newell and Simon, we have to search the problem space to find a solution**. A useful search strategy is **means-end analysis**. >> **Means-end analysis** aims to **reduce the differences between the initial and goal states**. >> At each point, the solver asks what is different from the goal and which move would reduce that difference. || Means-end analysis works through **subgoals**. **Subgoals create intermediate states closer to the goal**. >> A **subgoal** is a smaller goal that has to be reached before the main goal can be. >> If no move can reduce the main difference directly, the solver sets a subgoal that removes the obstacle. >> Subgoals turn one hard problem into a series of easier ones. || **For the Tower of Hanoi, applying means-end analysis reduces the size of the difference between the initial and goal states**. >> The big difference is that the whole stack is on the wrong peg. >> The largest disk cannot move until the disks above it are cleared, so clearing those disks becomes the first subgoal. >> Means-end analysis guides search toward the goal, so the solver does not have to try every possible move.
18|**Means-end analysis** solves the **Tower of Hanoi** through a series of subgoals. Each one removes a specific obstacle to moving the largest disk. || **Subgoal 1 is to free up the large disk**. >> In step (a), the smallest disk moves from the first peg to the third peg. >> In step (b), the middle disk moves from the first peg to the middle peg. >> The large disk on the first peg is now uncovered, so it can move. || **Subgoal 2 is to free up the third peg**. >> In step (c), the smallest disk moves from the third peg onto the middle disk on the middle peg. >> The third peg is now empty, so the large disk has somewhere to go, and the smaller disks are stacked legally on the middle peg. || **Subgoal 3 is to move the large disk onto the third peg**. >> In step (d), the large disk moves from the first peg to the third peg. >> The large disk is now in its final position, and the same strategy can be repeated to move the two smaller disks onto it. || Some steps that achieve subgoals seem to move away from the goal. >> Moving the smallest disk to the third peg and then off again looks like wasted effort. >> It is necessary to free up the space the large disk needs, and means-end analysis accepts such temporary detours when they remove an obstacle.
19|**One of the main contributions of Newell and Simon’s approach** is that it **provided a way to specify the possible pathways from the initial state to the goal state**. >> Defining states and operators maps out every route through a problem. >> This made problem solving something that could be described precisely and even programmed into a computer. >> Precision made it possible to compare different strategies and to predict where people would struggle. || **There is more to problem solving than specifying the problem state**. **How a problem is stated can affect its difficulty**. >> Two versions of the same problem can have identical states and operators. >> One version can still be much easier to solve, because its wording or presentation helps the solver adopt a representation that reveals the key relationship. || The **mutilated-checkerboard problem** is a clear example. Its difficulty depends heavily on how the board is presented. >> Presentation shapes which representation the solver adopts, and the representation shapes how hard the search becomes.
20|A **checkerboard** has **64 squares** and **can be completely covered by placing 32 dominoes on the board**, **with each domino covering 2 squares**. >> Each domino covers two neighboring squares, either side by side or one above the other. >> Thirty-two dominoes times two squares each gives exactly 64. || The **mutilated-checkerboard problem** asks this question. **If two opposite corners are eliminated, can the remaining 62 squares be covered with 31 dominoes?** >> The area seems to match, since 31 dominoes cover 62 squares. >> The answer is **no**. || The key is the colors of the squares. >> Every domino covers **one dark square and one light square**, because neighboring squares always have different colors. >> The two opposite corners have the **same color**, so removing them leaves **32 squares of one color and 30 of the other**. >> Thirty-one dominoes would need to cover 31 squares of each color, so a complete covering is impossible. || Representation matters here. Trying to place dominoes one by one would take a very long time and would never prove that a covering is impossible. Once the solver represents the board by its colors, the answer follows from counting in one step, an insight a pure search approach would struggle to reach.
21|**The key to solving the mutilated-checkerboard problem** is **understanding two things**. >> **Each domino covers two squares**. >> **These squares must be of different colors**. || **Conditions differed in how much information was provided about the squares**. >> **Condition 1** used a **blank board**, with no colors at all, which gave no hint. >> **Condition 2** used a **colored board**, with dark and light squares, which gave a visual hint. >> **Condition 3** put the **words “black” and “pink”** on the squares, which drew attention to the two colors. >> **Condition 4** put the **words “bread” and “butter”** on the squares. || The problem was **easier to solve when the information provided pointed toward the correct representation of the problem**. >> The **“bread and butter” condition emphasized the difference between the squares**. Bread and butter naturally go together in pairs, one of each. >> **Participants solved this condition twice as fast as the blank condition**. >> The labels did not change the problem itself. They changed how easily solvers built the representation in which each domino pairs one square of each kind.
22|The **think-aloud protocol** is a method for studying problem solving. Participants **say aloud what they are thinking while solving a problem**. >> They are **instructed not to describe what they are doing, but to verbalize new thoughts** as they occur. >> The **goal** is to find out **what information the participant is attending to while solving the problem**. >> Instead of seeing only the final answer, researchers can track what the solver notices and when. || In the **mutilated-checkerboard problem**, think-aloud protocols reveal a **shift in how the solver perceives the elements of the problem**. >> **Participants get the problem after realizing that the words “bread” and “butter” are important**. >> Before that moment, they talk about placing dominoes. After it, they talk about pairing the two kinds of squares. || This shift is **similar to the Gestalt idea of a representational shift**. >> The board and the rules stay the same. >> The solver’s mental representation reorganizes, which is restructuring, and the solution follows. >> The protocols therefore capture restructuring as it happens, rather than only its result.
