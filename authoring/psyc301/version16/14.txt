2|Emotions are part of how decisions get made. **People with damage to an area of their prefrontal cortex, who suffer from flattened emotions, have impaired decision making.** >> Flattened emotions are weakened emotions, so the person feels much less than usual. >> With less emotion, decisions get worse rather than better. >> Emotion therefore contributes to making good choices. || **Anxious people tend to avoid making decisions that could potentially lead to large negative consequences.** This pattern is **risk avoidance**. >> A choice that carries the chance of a large loss feels too threatening to an anxious person. >> The anxious person passes up that choice, even when it might turn out well. || **Optimistic people are more likely to ignore negative information and focus on positive information.** >> **Too much optimism can lead to poor decision making.** >> Ignoring the negative information means deciding without the whole picture. >> A decision made with part of the information missing is more likely to go wrong. || Each emotional state changes which information a person weighs. >> Too little emotion impairs the decision itself. >> Anxiety pulls attention toward possible large losses. >> Too much optimism pulls attention away from negative information.
3|**Expected emotions** are **emotions that people predict they will feel concerning an outcome**. >> Before choosing, a person imagines how a good result or a bad result would feel. >> That predicted feeling then guides the choice. || **Expected emotions are one of the determinants of risk aversion**, the **tendency to avoid taking risks**. >> Someone who expects to feel very bad after a loss is less willing to accept a chance of that loss. >> The predicted feeling, more than the outcome itself, pushes the person toward the safer option. || **People inaccurately predict their emotions.** >> People tend to believe that **a particular loss will have a greater impact than a gain of the same size**. >> That belief **increases risk aversion**, because a loss is expected to hurt more than an equal gain would help. >> As a result, people turn down risks that would be reasonable if gains and losses were weighed evenly. || The link between expected emotions and risk works through prediction. >> A person cannot feel an outcome before it happens, so the choice rests on a guess. >> When the guess makes losses feel bigger than equal gains, the person avoids risks more than the real feelings would justify.
4|**Kermer and colleagues (2006)** **compared people’s expected emotions with their actual emotions**. >> Participants were **given $5**. >> They were **told that a coin flip would decide whether they won an additional $5 or lost $3**. >> They **rated their happiness before the experiment started**, which gave each person a baseline. || Participants then **predicted how they would feel if they won or lost**. >> Before the flip, they expected losing $3 to drop their happiness by about 4 points below baseline. >> They expected winning $5 to raise it by only about 2 points. >> The smaller amount of money was expected to matter about twice as much as the larger one. || After the flip, the real changes were much smaller. >> Losing lowered happiness by only about 1 point, and winning raised it by about 1.2 points. >> People **overestimated their negative feeling**. >> The prediction missed far more for the loss than for the gain, since the expected drop of about 4 points shrank to about 1 point while the expected rise of about 2 points only shrank to about 1.2 points. || **We usually have coping mechanisms that get activated, but we don’t consider them when predicting emotions.** >> The coping mechanisms soften the real feeling after a loss. >> Leaving them out of the prediction is why losing was expected to hurt so much more than it did.
5|**Incidental emotions** are **emotions that are not specifically related to decision making**. >> They **may be related to one’s general disposition or personality**, which is a person’s usual emotional tendency. >> They may come from **some recent experience**, which can leave a mood behind. >> They may come from **one’s general environment or surroundings**, such as the weather. || **Simonsohn (2007)** analyzed **university admissions decisions**. >> **Applicants’ academic attributes were more heavily weighted on cloudy days than on sunny days.** >> The weather had nothing to do with the applicants. >> It still changed what the people deciding paid the most attention to. || **Simonsohn (2009)** studied **prospective students visiting an academically highly rated university**. >> They **were more likely to enroll if they had visited the campus on a cloudy day**. >> The weather during one visit is unrelated to the quality of the school, yet it carried into the decision to enroll. >> In both of Simonsohn’s studies, nothing about the people or the schools changed with the weather, so the cloudy day itself is the incidental factor that moved the decision. || These findings matter because the emotions enter decisions without being part of them. >> A cloudy day is unrelated to an applicant’s record or to a university’s quality. >> Even so, cloudy weather went with weighting academic attributes more heavily and with enrolling after a visit.
6|**Lerner and colleagues (2004)** had participants watch one of three film clips. >> A clip of **a person dying** created the **sad** condition. >> A clip of **a person using a dirty toilet** created the **disgust** condition. >> A clip of **fish at the Great Barrier Reef** was the **neutral** condition. || Participants were then **given a set of highlighters** and **asked to determine the price**. >> One price was what **they would be willing to sell the highlighters** for. >> The other was what **they would be willing to pay to buy the highlighters**. >> The clips had nothing to do with highlighters, so any price difference came from the mood. || The prices changed with the mood. >> For selling, the neutral group asked about $4.50, the disgust group about $2.70 and the sad group about $3.00. >> For buying, the neutral group offered about $3.50, the disgust group about $3.00 and the sad group about $4.50. >> Disgust lowered both prices, while sadness lowered the selling price and raised the buying price. >> Because every group priced the same highlighters, the gaps between the groups can only come from the moods the clips created. || **A person’s mood can influence decision making.** >> These moods are incidental emotions, since they came from clips unrelated to the choice.
7|**Decisions can depend on the context within which they are made.** **Physicians were presented with a hypothetical test case involving a possible candidate for cesarean section.** >> A cesarean section is a surgical delivery of a baby. >> The test case was exactly the same for every physician. || The test case appeared in **three different contexts**. >> In the **control** context, **the test case was presented first**. >> In the **serious previous cases** context, **the test case was preceded by four other cases in which there were serious complications**. >> In the **not serious previous cases** context, **the test case was preceded by four other cases that were fairly routine**. || **Significantly more doctors recommended cesarean after reading about non-serious cases first.** >> The test case never changed, so the earlier cases changed the recommendation. >> The cases a person has just considered are part of the context of a decision. >> Reading four routine cases first made the same patient look different, so the physicians’ recommendation depended partly on what they had just read. || Physicians are experts, yet their judgment of the same case still shifted. >> The four earlier cases set a standard for judging the fifth. >> After four routine cases, the test case led to more recommendations for cesarean than in the other contexts.
8|**Decisions depend on how choices are presented.** >> In an **opt-in procedure**, a person takes an **active step to be an organ donor**, and **countries with opt-in procedures have less than a 28% consent rate**. >> In an **opt-out procedure**, a person is an **organ donor unless they request not to be**, and **countries with opt-out procedures have over a 99% consent rate**. || The **status quo bias** is **the tendency to do nothing when faced with a difficult decision**. >> Under opt-in, doing nothing means not becoming a donor. >> Under opt-out, doing nothing means becoming a donor. >> Because many people do nothing either way, the two consent rates end up far apart. >> Organ donation is the same choice in both kinds of country, and the gap between under 28% and over 99% comes from which outcome happens when people do nothing. || In the story **The International Community**, a chicken, a duck, a turkey, a pheasant, a quail and a partridge are summoned to a summit. >> The king’s chef tells them he called them there so they can say what sauce they would like to be eaten with. >> When one bird says it does not want to be eaten at all, the chef answers that this is out of the question. >> The only choice the birds are offered is which sauce, because the choices presented leave out not being eaten.
9|The **framing effect** is when **decisions are influenced by how a decision is stated**. >> The way a choice is stated **can highlight one aspect of the situation**, such as what might be gained or what might be lost. >> The outcomes can stay the same while the wording changes what people focus on. || **Tversky and Kahneman (1981)** studied how framing changes risk taking. >> **When situations are framed in terms of gains, people tend toward a risk-aversion strategy**, so they prefer the sure option. >> **When situations are framed in terms of losses, people tend toward a risk-taking strategy**, so they prefer the gamble. || The same people can switch strategies when only the wording changes. >> A gain frame makes keeping a certain gain feel safest. >> A loss frame makes gambling to avoid a certain loss more attractive. || Framing works by changing what the statement draws attention to. >> A statement about lives saved points to what can be gained. >> A statement about deaths points to what can be lost. >> The disease problem tests both frames with the same outcomes.

11|**A disease is expected to kill 600 people**, and **two alternative programs to combat the disease have been proposed**. The outcomes are stated as lives saved, which is the gain frame. >> With **Program A**, **200 people will be saved** for certain. >> With **Program B**, there is **a 1/3 probability that 600 people will be saved and a 2/3 probability that no people will be saved**. || **72% chose Program A**, and 28% chose Program B. >> Program A is the sure option, and Program B is the gamble. >> Choosing A locks in 200 lives instead of risking saving no one. >> Most people avoided the risk when the outcomes were described as lives saved, which is the risk-aversion strategy of a gain frame. >> Saving 200 for certain felt better to most people than a gamble that would most likely save no one, even though the gamble could save all 600. || The gamble in Program B can save everyone, but it is more likely to save no one. >> The 1/3 chance of saving all 600 sits beside a 2/3 chance of saving none. >> Most people preferred a guaranteed 200 over that gamble.
12|The same disease is expected to kill 600 people, but now the outcomes are stated as deaths, which is the loss frame. >> With **Program C**, **400 people will die** for certain. >> With **Program D**, there is **a 1/3 probability that no one will die and a 2/3 probability that 600 people will die**. || **78% chose Program D**, and 22% chose Program C. >> Program D is the gamble, so most people now accepted the risk. >> Choosing D means gambling to avoid a certain loss of 400 lives. >> Losing 400 for certain felt worse to most people than a gamble that might save everyone, so they took the risk they had avoided in the gain frame. || The two versions describe the same outcomes. >> Programs A and C are the same, since saving 200 of 600 people means that 400 die. >> Programs B and D are the same gamble. >> Only the wording changed, so the switch from the sure option to the gamble is the **framing effect**. || The shift between the two versions is large. >> In the gain frame, 72% chose the sure option. >> In the loss frame, only 22% chose the sure option. >> The same outcomes produced opposite majorities, depending only on how they were described.
13|**Neuroeconomics** **combines research from psychology, neuroscience, and economics**. >> Psychology contributes how people think and feel. >> Neuroscience contributes what happens in the brain. >> Economics contributes how choices about money and value are analyzed. || **One finding is that decisions are influenced by emotions, and those emotions are associated with activity in specific areas of the brain.** >> Emotion is part of the decision process. >> Its role can be traced to particular brain areas. >> A choice that looks purely economic, such as accepting an amount of money, can depend on how a person feels about it. || Neuroeconomics brings these fields together to study the same choice from several sides. >> Economics can describe what a rational choice would be. >> Psychology and neuroscience can test whether real choices follow that standard and what the brain is doing when they do not. >> The ultimatum game is one way to make that test.
14|**Sanfey and coworkers (2003)** used the **ultimatum game**, which has **two players**, a **proposer** and a **responder**. >> The **proposer gets $10**. >> The proposer **makes an offer to the responder as to how this money should be split**. || The **responder can accept or reject the offer**. >> **If the responder rejects, neither receives anything.** >> If the responder accepts, the money is split the way the proposer offered. >> A rejection therefore costs both players money. || **According to utility theory, what is the rational decision for the responder?** The rational decision is to **accept any offer**. >> Utility theory judges a choice by the value of its outcome. >> Any offer, even $1, is worth more than the nothing that comes from rejecting. >> So a responder following utility theory would never turn down money. || The game sets money against fairness. >> The proposer decides the split, but the responder decides whether anyone is paid. >> A very uneven offer leaves the responder choosing between a small amount and nothing, which is where fairness and money pull in different directions.
15|**In Sanfey’s experiment, participants played 20 separate games**, **10 with human partners and 10 with computer partners**. >> Offers of $5 and $5 were accepted about 100% of the time, and $7 and $3 about 95%, from both kinds of partner. >> An $8 and $2 offer was accepted about 53% of the time from a human and about 85% from a computer. >> A $9 and $1 offer was accepted about 37% of the time from a human and about 68% from a computer. || Participants did not follow utility theory, which says to accept every offer. >> They **often rejected low offers because they became angry that offers were unfair**. >> They were **less angry with an unfair computer**, so they accepted its low offers more often. >> The gap between human and computer partners grew as the offers became more uneven, which fits anger at unfairness from a person rather than a simple judgment about the money. || **More activation of the right anterior insula**, an area **connected with emotional states**, went with participants being **more likely to reject more offers**. >> Rejecting meant getting nothing, so this emotional activity led people to give up money. >> **Emotion is important in decision making.**
16|**Deductive reasoning** is **determining whether a conclusion logically follows from premises**. >> A premise is a statement that the reasoning starts from. >> Deductive reasoning asks only whether the conclusion follows from those premises. || A **syllogism** is the **basic form of deductive reasoning**, **introduced by Aristotle**. >> It has **two statements called premises**. >> It has a **third statement called the conclusion**. || A **categorical syllogism** **describes the relation between two categories using all, no, or some**. >> **Premise 1** is **All birds are animals**. >> **Premise 2** is **All animals eat food**. >> From these premises comes **Therefore, all birds eat food**. >> Birds fall inside the category of animals, and animals fall inside the category of things that eat food, so birds fall inside it too. || Deductive reasoning differs from judging whether something is likely. >> A deductive conclusion either follows from the premises or it does not. >> The bird syllogism follows, because the categories fit inside each other.
17|**A syllogism is valid if its conclusion follows logically from its two premises.** >> **If the two premises of a valid syllogism are true, the syllogism’s conclusion must be true.** >> Validity depends on the form of the reasoning, while truth depends on whether the statements match the world. || **Do not confuse validity with truth.** >> All birds are animals, all animals have four legs, so all birds have four legs. >> This syllogism is **valid**, because it has the **same form as the first example**. >> Its conclusion is false only because the second premise is false. || **Validity is about whether the conclusion logically follows from the premises.** >> **If it does, and the premises are true, then the conclusion must be true.** >> A false premise can lead a valid syllogism to a false conclusion. || The two bird syllogisms make validity and truth easy to separate. >> Both have the same form, so both are valid. >> The first has true premises and a true conclusion, while the second has a false premise and a false conclusion.
18|**The difference between validity and truth can make it difficult to judge whether reasoning is logical or not.** >> **Valid syllogisms can result in false conclusions.** >> **Syllogisms can be invalid even though each of the premises and the conclusion seem reasonable.** || All of the students are tired, some tired people are irritable, so some of the students are irritable. >> Each statement sounds reasonable. >> The syllogism is **not valid**, because the tired people who are irritable might not include any students. || **There are many errors in evaluation, because good reasoning and truth are not the same thing.** >> **Belief bias** is **the tendency to think that a syllogism is valid if its conclusions are believable**. >> **71% of participants judged invalid, believable conclusions as valid.** >> A believable conclusion leads most people to accept reasoning that does not hold. >> With 71% judging invalid but believable syllogisms as valid, belief in the conclusion overrode the check on whether the reasoning held for most participants. || The students syllogism fails because of the word some. >> Some tired people are irritable leaves open which tired people they are. >> All the students being tired does not place any of them among the irritable ones, so the conclusion does not follow.
19|**There is no easy method for determining whether a syllogism is valid or invalid.** >> People still need a way to work out what follows from a set of statements. || In the pool table problem, **a black ball is directly above the cue ball**, **the green ball is on the right side of the cue ball**, and **there is a red ball between them**. >> The speaker moves so that the red ball is between the speaker and the black ball. >> The question is on which side of the speaker’s line of sight the cue ball now lies. || The answer is that the cue ball is **to the left** of the line of sight. >> Placing the balls in the mind puts the black ball above the cue ball, with the red ball and then the green ball to the cue ball’s right. >> Standing so the red ball blocks the black ball means looking past the red ball toward the black ball, and from there the cue ball sits off to the left. || **Most people solve this by imagining the way the balls are arranged on the pool table.** >> **The idea that people can imagine situations is the basis of Johnson-Laird’s proposal that people use mental models to solve deductive reasoning.**
20|In the **mental model approach (Johnson-Laird, 1999)**, a **mental model** is **a specific situation represented in a person’s mind that can be used to help determine the validity of syllogisms in deductive reasoning**. >> The person reasons from an imagined situation that fits the premises. || The approach works in steps. >> The person **creates a model of a situation**. >> The person **generates tentative conclusions about the model**, which are conclusions that might hold. >> The person **looks for exceptions to falsify the model**, searching for a case where the premises are true but the conclusion turns out false. >> The person **determines the validity of the syllogism**. || **A conclusion is valid only if it cannot be refuted by any model of the premises.** >> To refute a conclusion is to prove that it can be false. >> A single model that fits the premises while contradicting the conclusion makes that conclusion invalid. || The search for exceptions is what turns a model into a test of validity. >> A tentative conclusion may fit the first model a person builds. >> The person then tries other ways the premises could be true. >> If none of them refutes the conclusion, that conclusion is valid.
21|**Conditional syllogisms have two premises and a conclusion.** >> **The first premise is of the form If p, then q.** >> The letter p stands for a condition, and q stands for what follows when the condition is met. || **There are four major types of conditional syllogism.** >> All four share the first premise. >> They differ in the second premise and the conclusion. >> Syllogism 1 goes from p to q, syllogism 2 from not q to not p, syllogism 3 from q to p, and syllogism 4 from not p to not q. || Two of the types are valid and two are not. >> Syllogisms 1 and 2 are valid, and people judged them correctly 97% and 60% of the time. >> Syllogisms 3 and 4 are not valid, and people judged them correctly 40% of the time each. || Comparing the four types in letters makes the pattern clear. >> The valid types either confirm p or deny q. >> The invalid types either confirm q or deny p.
22|**Conditional syllogism 1** is called **modus ponens**. >> If I study, I’ll get a good grade. >> I studied. >> Therefore, I’ll get a good grade. || In letters, the second premise is p and the conclusion reads therefore q. >> The condition is met, so what follows from it must follow too. >> With any content, If p, then q together with p leads to q. || This syllogism is **valid**, and **97%** of participants judged it correctly. >> Modus ponens is the easiest of the four types to judge. >> Its reasoning runs in the same direction as the first premise. >> Because 97% judged modus ponens correctly, nearly everyone handles reasoning that runs forward from a condition that was met. || Modus ponens confirms the condition and concludes the result. >> Knowing that studying happened, and that studying leads to a good grade, settles that the good grade follows. >> Nearly everyone accepts this form.
23|**Conditional syllogism 2** is called **modus tollens**. >> If I study, I’ll get a good grade. >> I didn’t get a good grade. >> Therefore, I didn’t study. || In letters, the second premise is not q and the conclusion reads therefore not p. >> If studying always leads to a good grade, then missing the good grade means the studying did not happen. >> With any content, If p, then q together with not q leads to not p. || This syllogism is **valid**, but only **60%** of participants judged it correctly. >> It is just as valid as modus ponens. >> It is much harder to judge, because it reasons from a result that did not happen back to a condition that did not happen. >> The drop from 97% for modus ponens to 60% for modus tollens comes from the direction of the reasoning, since both forms are equally valid. || Modus tollens denies the result and concludes that the condition did not happen. >> If the condition had happened, the result would have had to follow. >> Since the result did not happen, the condition could not have happened either.
24|**Conditional syllogism 3** reasons from the result back to the condition. >> If I study, I’ll get a good grade. >> I got a good grade. >> Therefore, I studied. || In letters, the second premise is q and the conclusion reads therefore p. >> This syllogism is **not valid**, and only **40%** of participants judged it correctly. >> The first premise says what happens when someone studies, but it never says that studying is the only way to get a good grade. >> Fewer than half judged this form correctly, so most people accepted a conclusion that does not follow. || The same form with other content makes the problem easier to see. >> If I live in Tucson, then I live in Arizona. I live in Arizona. Therefore, I live in Tucson. >> A person can live in Arizona without living in Tucson, so the conclusion does not follow. || The error in syllogism 3 is treating If p, then q as if it also meant If q, then p. >> The first premise runs only from studying to the good grade. >> It does not run from the good grade back to studying.
25|**Conditional syllogism 4** reasons from a missing condition to a missing result. >> If I study, I’ll get a good grade. >> I didn’t study. >> Therefore, I didn’t get a good grade. || In letters, the second premise is not p and the conclusion reads therefore not q. >> This syllogism is **not valid**, and only **40%** of participants judged it correctly. >> The first premise says nothing about what happens without studying. >> A good grade could come about some other way. >> Fewer than half judged this form correctly as well, so the two invalid forms tripped up most people to the same degree. || Across the four types, the valid forms were judged correctly more often than the invalid forms. >> Modus ponens reached 97% and modus tollens 60%. >> Both invalid forms reached only 40%. || Syllogism 4 makes the same kind of error from the other side. >> If p, then q tells what happens when p is true. >> It says nothing about what happens when p is false, so denying p settles nothing about q.
26|The **Wason four-card problem** tests the **effect of using real-world items in a conditional-reasoning problem**. >> The cards read **E**, **K**, **4** and **7**. >> The task is to **determine the minimum number of cards to turn over to test the rule if there is a vowel on one side, then there is an even number on the other side**. || **E needs to be turned over.** >> **46% indicated that 4 would need to be turned over**, which is an error. >> **Only 7% indicated that 7 needs to be turned over**, which is correct. >> Only 7% chose the 7, so almost no one looked for the card that could prove the rule false. || The correct answer is E and 7. >> E could have an odd number on its back, which would break the rule. >> A 7 with a vowel on its back would also break the rule. >> A 4 cannot break the rule whatever is on its back, and K is not a vowel, so neither of those tests the rule. || Most people chose the 4 because it matches the rule’s even number. >> Turning over the 4 can only find cases that fit the rule. >> It can never find a case that breaks it.
27|The **falsification principle** states that **to test a rule, you must look for situations that falsify the rule**. >> To falsify a rule is to prove it false. >> **Most participants fail to do this**, which is why so few turned over the 7. || **When the problem is stated in concrete everyday terms, correct responses greatly increase.** >> The rule is **If drinking beer, then 21 years old**. >> The cards read **beer**, **soda**, **16 years old** and **21 years old**. >> **73%** of participants answered this version correctly. >> Following the falsification principle, the cards to turn over are **beer** and **16 years old**, because someone drinking beer while under 21 is the only case that breaks the rule. >> Going from 7% choosing the 7 card in the abstract version to 73% correct in the concrete version is a very large change for problems with the same logical form. || The concrete rule has the same If p, then q form as the vowel and number rule. >> Only the content changed, from letters and numbers to drinking and age. >> That change alone raised correct responses far above the 7% who chose the 7 card.
28|A **permission schema** is the idea that **if A is satisfied, B can be carried out**. >> A schema is a familiar pattern of knowledge. >> In the beer rule, being 21 years old is the condition, and drinking beer is what can then be carried out. || **Permission schemas are used in the concrete versions.** >> **People are familiar with rules** that grant permission. >> That familiar pattern tells people to check whether the condition for the permission has been met. || **Context is important.** >> The same logical problem becomes easier in a familiar setting that calls up a permission schema. >> **Familiarity is not always important**, so familiarity alone does not explain the improvement. || A permission rule tells people what to check. >> If someone is drinking beer, their age has to be checked. >> If someone is 16, what they are drinking has to be checked. >> The abstract vowel and number rule does not call up this familiar pattern.
