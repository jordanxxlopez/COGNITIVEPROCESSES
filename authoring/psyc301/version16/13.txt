2|**Decisions** are **the process of making choices between alternatives**. **Reasoning** is **the process of drawing conclusions**. >> Choosing which class to take or which route to drive is a decision. >> Working out what follows from the evidence we have is reasoning. || **Decisions are based on the judgments we make, and applying these judgments can involve various reasoning processes**. >> A **judgment** is an assessment of how likely or how good something is. >> Reasoning produces a judgment about each option, and the decision then picks an option based on those judgments. >> A judgment that rain is likely leads to a decision to carry an umbrella. Errors in judgment carry forward into poor decisions, which is why the shortcuts used in judgment matter so much. || **One of the primary mechanisms involved in making judgments is inductive reasoning**. >> **Inductive reasoning** is **reasoning based on observations**. >> It means **reaching conclusions from evidence**, moving from specific cases to a general conclusion. It turns past observations into expectations about what will happen next. >> Reasoning and decisions therefore form a chain, from observations to judgments to choices.
3|**Inductive reasoning is the basis of scientific investigations**. **In science, observations are made, data are collected, and conclusions are drawn**. Scientists observe many cases and then propose a general rule that fits them. || **In inductive reasoning, the conclusions we reach are probably, but not definitely, true**. >> **The conclusions reached are suggested with various degrees of certainty**. >> A new observation could always overturn a conclusion based on earlier observations. || The **strength of an inductive argument** depends on three factors. >> **Representativeness of the observations**, meaning how well the observations stand for the whole category. Many observations from a narrow, unrepresentative group can still lead to a wrong conclusion. >> **Number of observations**, since more observations support a conclusion more strongly. >> **Quality of the observations**, since careful, accurate observations count for more. A few careful observations from a well-chosen sample can support a strong conclusion. >> **Sometimes one very compelling, high-quality observation can carry a lot of weight**. || That last point follows from the nature of induction. Inductive conclusions are general claims built from specific cases, so a single clear counterexample can show that the general claim is false, no matter how many supporting cases came before it.
4|**Inductive reasoning is used to make scientific discoveries**, by forming **hypotheses and general conclusions** from observations. || **Inductive reasoning is also used in everyday life**. >> We **make a prediction about what will happen based on observations of what has happened in the past**. >> **We make predictions and choices based on past experience, especially when predictions are based on familiar situations**. >> Familiar situations provide many past observations, which makes predictions based on them reliable. Unfamiliar situations provide few. || Inductive reasoning **provides a mechanism for using past experience to guide present behavior**. >> Everyday induction usually works well because the world is fairly regular, so patterns that held in the past often continue to hold. >> **Some types of inductive reasoning happen automatically**, without deliberate thought, so they shape our expectations constantly. >> Automatic induction saves time and effort. The same speed also makes it open to the systematic errors that heuristics produce. >> Scientists and ordinary people use the same process. A scientist proposes a general rule from many cases, and a person in daily life expects the future to follow the pattern of the past.
5|**When people use past experience to guide present behavior, they often use shortcuts to help them reach conclusions rapidly**. These shortcuts are called **heuristics**. >> **Heuristics** are **“rules of thumb” that are likely to provide the correct answer to a problem, but are not foolproof**. >> Most everyday judgments have to be made quickly, with limited information, so a fast rule of thumb is a practical tool. >> The answer is usually close to right, which is good enough for most purposes. || **Two commonly used heuristics are the availability heuristic and the representativeness heuristic**. >> The **availability heuristic** judges probability by how easily examples come to mind. >> The **representativeness heuristic** judges probability by how much something resembles a typical case. || Heuristics trade accuracy for speed. >> Each heuristic reaches a quick answer using a simple cue. >> Because each relies on a particular cue, it fails in a predictable way whenever that cue points away from the truth. Studying these systematic errors reveals how the heuristics work. >> Full analysis would require working through every possibility carefully, which is slow and often impossible.
6|The **availability heuristic** is the tendency for **events that are more easily remembered to be judged as more probable than events that are less easily remembered**. >> **Availability is used to judge the probability of events**. >> **We use availability by trying to call examples to mind**. >> **The more examples we can think of, the more probable we judge the event to be**. || The heuristic relies on a reasonable connection. >> Events that happen often usually leave many memories, so examples come to mind easily. >> Rare events are encountered seldom, so examples are hard to recall. Ease of recall therefore usually tracks frequency. || **The problem lies in the factors that make events stand out in memory**. >> **Distinctiveness**, since unusual events are easier to remember. >> **Emotional salience**, since events that stir strong emotions stick in memory. >> **Frequency of encoding and retrieval**, since events we hear about or think about often are easier to recall. >> These factors affect how easily events come to mind apart from how often they actually happen. When something other than frequency makes an event memorable, such as drama or heavy news coverage, a rare event can feel common.
7|The availability heuristic shapes judgments about words. **Which are more common in English, words that begin with the letter r or words in which r is the third letter?** >> **Seventy percent of people respond that more words begin with r**. >> **In reality, three times more words have r in the third position**. || Availability causes this error. >> The question asks about the true size of each category, but people answer by how easily they can retrieve examples. >> It is easy to call to mind words that begin with r, because memory is organized so that words can be searched by their first letter. >> No such search works for third letters, so those words are hard to retrieve even though they are far more numerous. || The words that come to mind easily seem more common, even though they are much less common. >> The error comes entirely from the way retrieval works. >> It does not reflect any lack of knowledge about English words. >> Seventy percent of people pick the wrong answer, even though words with r in the third position are about three times as common. >> The ease of thinking of r-words makes the category feel large, and that feeling becomes the judgment, even though the true count favors the third-position words.
8|The availability heuristic also shapes judgments about **causes of death**. People were asked which cause in each pair was more likely to kill someone in the United States in the next year. The table lists the **more likely** cause, how many times more likely it is, and the **percentage of people who picked the less likely cause**. >> **Homicide** is 20 times more likely than **appendicitis**, and 9% picked appendicitis. >> **Drowning** is 5 times more likely than an **auto–train collision**, and 34% picked the collision. >> **Asthma** is 920 times more likely than **botulism**, and 41% picked botulism. >> **Asthma** is 20 times more likely than a **tornado**, and 58% picked the tornado. >> **Appendicitis** is 2 times more likely than **pregnancy**, and 83% picked pregnancy. || **These errors are due to the availability of the causes**. >> **When people die of botulism, it is front-page news**. >> **Deaths from asthma go virtually unnoticed**. >> Dramatic, rare causes get heavy news coverage, so examples come to mind easily. Common, quiet causes get little attention, so they seem less likely than they are. || The pattern follows media attention. People judge likelihood by how easily examples come to mind, and news coverage shapes what comes to mind. A cause 920 times more likely can still lose to a rarer one that makes headlines.
9|The **representativeness heuristic** is the tendency for **people to make judgments based on how much one event resembles another event**. >> **The probability that A is a member of class B is judged by how well the properties of A resemble the properties normally associated with class B**. >> The more an individual resembles our image of a category, the more likely we think the individual belongs to it. || **America has about 2.2 million farms and 120,000 libraries**. >> **We randomly pick one male from the population of the US**. **That male, Robert, wears glasses, speaks quietly and reads a lot**. **Is it more likely that Robert is a librarian or a farmer?** || **More people predict that Robert is a librarian**. >> Robert’s description matches the common image of a librarian, and the match feels like strong evidence. >> In doing so, people **ignore the base rate of farmers and librarians in the US**. A **base rate** is how common a category is in the population. >> With about 18 farms for every library, there are far more farmers than male librarians. Even if Robert’s traits are somewhat more common among librarians, a randomly chosen man is much more likely to be a farmer.
10|People follow a pattern in how they use base rates. >> They **use base rate information if it is all that is available**. >> They **use descriptive information if it is available and disregard base rate information**. || When only base rates are given, people use them correctly. >> **In a group of 100 people, there are 70 lawyers and 30 engineers**. **What is the chance that a person picked from the group at random will be an engineer?** >> **People get this right**. They answer 30%. || When a description is added, people ignore the base rates. >> **Jack is a 45-year-old man**. **He is married and has four children**. **He is generally conservative, careful and ambitious**. **He shows no interest in political and social issues and spends most of his free time on his many hobbies, which include home carpentry, sailing and mathematical puzzles**. >> **People say Jack is an engineer**. >> Jack’s careful personality and mathematical puzzles fit a stereotype of an engineer. The fit overwhelms the base rate, even though the description could easily belong to one of the 70 lawyers. >> Without a description, people say there is a 30% chance of picking an engineer, which matches the base rate. >> Jack’s description contains nothing that rules out a lawyer, so the base rate should still matter. Representativeness makes the description feel decisive anyway.
11|The **conjunction rule** states that **the probability of two events together cannot be higher than the probability of either single event alone**. >> A **conjunction** is two things being true at the same time. >> Everything in the combined category also belongs to each single category, so the combined category can never be larger. || **Linda is 31 years old, single, outspoken and very bright**. **She majored in philosophy**. **As a student, she was deeply concerned with issues of discrimination and social justice, and also participated in antinuclear demonstrations**. **Which of the following is more probable?** >> **1. Linda is a bank teller**. >> **2. Linda is a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement**. || **Eighty-two percent pick option 2**. This violates the conjunction rule. >> The diagram has a large circle for **bank tellers**, with a smaller circle inside it for **feminist bank tellers**. >> Every feminist bank teller is also a bank teller, and a smaller group inside a larger group can never be more likely than the larger group. >> Linda’s description makes her sound like a feminist, so the feminist option feels more representative of her. This sense of fit overrides the logic, which is the representativeness heuristic producing an error. >> The choice of option 2 by 82% of people reveals how strongly resemblance can override a basic rule of probability.
12|**People also make errors in reasoning by ignoring the importance of the size of the sample on which observations are based**. >> **A certain town is served by two hospitals**. **In the larger hospital, about 45 babies are born each day, and in the smaller hospital, about 15 babies are born each day**. >> **About 50% of all babies are boys**. **However, the exact percentage varies from day to day**. **Sometimes it may be higher than 50%, sometimes lower**. >> **For a period of one year, each hospital recorded the days on which more than 60% of the babies born were boys**. **Which hospital recorded more such days?** || The choices were **1, the larger hospital**, **2, the smaller hospital**, and **3, about the same**. >> **Twenty-two percent chose the larger hospital**. >> **Twenty-two percent chose the smaller hospital**. >> **Fifty-six percent chose about the same**. || Most people judged that both hospitals would be equally likely to have unusual days. >> They focused on the 50% figure that applies to both hospitals. >> They overlooked how the number of births each day affects how often the daily percentage strays from that rate. >> The question asks about variability from day to day, which depends on how many babies are born each day, not only on the average rate of boys.
13|For the **hospital problem**, **the correct answer is 2, the smaller hospital**. Only 22% of people chose this answer. || The key is **sample size**, which controls how far a percentage can stray from the true value by chance. >> With only 15 births a day, chance swings in the proportion of boys are large. A day with 15 births can easily include 10 boys by chance, which is more than 60%. >> With 45 births a day, the proportion of boys stays closer to 50%, because random variations tend to even out across more births. A 45-birth day would need 28 or more boys to pass 60%, which happens much less often. >> The smaller hospital therefore records many more days with more than 60% boys. || **People make errors in reasoning by ignoring the importance of the size of the sample on which observations are based**. >> Small samples are much more likely than large samples to stray far from the true average. >> People who chose about the same were applying representativeness, expecting every sample to look like the population. >> The same principle applies to any judgment based on a small sample. Small groups produce more extreme percentages by chance than large groups do.
14|The **law of large numbers** states that **the larger the number of individuals randomly drawn from a population, the more representative the resulting group will be of the entire population**. >> A large random sample averages out chance variations, so it closely matches the population it comes from. >> A small random sample is easily pushed off by a few unusual cases, so it can differ from the population quite a lot. || **People often assume that representativeness holds for small samples, and this results in errors in reasoning**. >> In the hospital problem, people expected a sample of 15 births to look as balanced as a sample of 45. >> Small samples often look unrepresentative, and the law of large numbers explains why. || The law explains the hospital result directly. >> The larger hospital’s daily births form a bigger sample, so its percentage of boys stays closer to 50%. >> The smaller hospital’s daily births form a smaller sample, so its percentage of boys swings more widely and more often passes 60%. >> Expecting a small sample to look just like the population is a common error, and it comes from applying representativeness where sample size matters.
15|**Illusory correlations** occur when **a correlation appears to exist, but either does not exist or is much weaker than assumed**. A **correlation** is a relationship between two things, where one tends to go with the other. || **Illusory correlations can occur when we expect two things to be related, so we fool ourselves into thinking they are related even when they are not**. Expectations shape what people notice, and what they notice shapes what they believe. >> **Stereotypes** are **oversimplified generalizations about a group or class of people that often focus on the negative**. >> **A stereotype about a group may lead people to pay particular attention to behaviors associated with that stereotype**. >> **This attention creates an illusory correlation that reinforces the stereotype**. People notice and remember the cases that fit and overlook the many cases that do not. || Illusory correlation is **related to the availability heuristic**. >> **Selective attention to stereotypical behaviors makes these behaviors more “available”** in memory. >> Those easily recalled examples are then used as evidence that the stereotype is true, so it seems more accurate than it is.
16|**Confirmation bias** shapes how people evaluate evidence. **Lord and coworkers (1979)** studied this with capital punishment. >> **They had people in favor of capital punishment and people against it read the same article**. >> The article presented evidence on both sides. || Reactions split by prior belief. >> **People in favor found the article to support their view**. >> **People against found the article to support their view**. >> The same article with the same information strengthened opposite beliefs. Supporters focused on the parts favoring capital punishment, and opponents on the parts arguing against it. || This is the **“myside” bias**, the **tendency for people to generate and evaluate evidence and test their hypotheses in a way that is biased toward their own opinions and attitudes**. >> **Participants focused on information that agreed with their beliefs and disregarded information that did not**. >> The bias works by selective focus and selective weighting. People give more weight to agreeing evidence and find reasons to dismiss disagreeing evidence, so mixed evidence strengthens existing beliefs on both sides. >> Each side came away more convinced of the view it already held, so the evidence did not move the two groups closer together. >> Lord and coworkers gave both groups the same balanced evidence, so differences in their conclusions can come only from how each group evaluated it.
17|**The myside bias is a type of confirmation bias**. **Confirmation bias** is the **tendency to selectively look for information that conforms to our hypothesis and overlook information that argues against it**. || A classic study used a number-sequence task. >> Participants were told they **would be given three numbers that conform to a simple rule the experimenter had in mind**. Their **aim was to discover the rule by writing down sets of three numbers along with their reasons for choosing them**. >> **After each set, the experimenter said whether the numbers conformed to the rule or not**. **When participants felt highly confident that they had discovered the rule, they were to write it down**. >> The starting set was **2, 4, 6**. >> **Participants then generated their own sets of three numbers**, and **for each set, they received feedback only on whether their numbers fit the rule**. || The **most common hypothesis** was **increasing intervals of two**. >> The **actual rule** was **three numbers in increasing order of magnitude**, so any rising sequence fit. >> Participants kept testing sets that matched their own hypothesis. Every one also fit the real rule, so positive feedback made them confident in the wrong rule. >> Only a test that could fail, a set that breaks the participant’s hypothesis, would reveal that the real rule is broader. || **Confirmation bias acts like a pair of blinders**. **We see the world according to rules we think are correct and are never dissuaded from this view, because we seek out only evidence that confirms our rule**. >> Participants rarely chose sets that could produce a no, so the feedback never challenged their rule.
18|**Decision making** is **making judgments that involve choices between different courses of action**. || **Utility** refers to **outcomes that are desirable because they are in the person’s best interest**. An option with higher utility gives the person a better outcome. || **Expected utility theory** makes several claims. >> **People are rational**. >> **If they have all the relevant information, they will make the decision that results in the maximum expected utility**. **Expected utility** combines how good each outcome is with how likely it is. >> In its early form, utility was measured as **maximum monetary payoff**, so the best choice was the one that paid the most money on average. || The theory gives a clear rule for choosing. >> For each option, multiply the value of each possible outcome by its probability and add up the results. >> Choose the option with the highest total. >> The theory serves as a benchmark. Real decisions can be compared with what it predicts, and the gaps reveal the other factors that influence how people actually choose. >> When outcomes are measured in money, the best choice is the one with the highest expected monetary payoff.
19|The **utility approach** has an **advantage**. It offers **specific procedures for determining the “best choice.”** For any decision, utility theory gives a clear method for working out which option a perfectly rational decision-maker would pick. || The utility approach also has **problems**. >> Utility is **not necessarily money**. **People find value in other things**, such as safety, fairness or how they feel about the choice. >> **Many decisions do not maximize the probability of the best outcome**. >> **People are not utility maximization machines**. Real decisions also depend on emotions, values beyond money and the way the options are presented. || For example, **people gamble in casinos all the time, even though they know the odds are very much against them**. >> The expected monetary payoff of casino gambling is negative. >> People gamble anyway, because they value the excitement or the chance of a big win. >> The utility approach is therefore strong as a standard but weak as a description of what people actually choose. >> People also often fail to choose the option most likely to give the best outcome, as the jellybean and travel examples reveal.
20|In the **jellybean study**, **participants were given the opportunity to earn up to $7 by receiving $1 every time they drew a red jellybean**. **Each time, they were given a choice between two different bowls**. >> One bowl contained **1 red jellybean out of 10**, so **the odds were 1/10**. >> The other bowl contained **7 red jellybeans out of 100**, so **the odds were 7/100, or 0.7/10**. || The first bowl gives better odds of winning. >> One in 10 is 10%, while 7 in 100 is 7%. \[\frac{1}{10}=0.10,\qquad\frac{7}{100}=0.07\] >> Yet **most participants chose the 7/100 bowl**. || **Their choice was influenced by considerations other than probabilities**. >> The second bowl has more red jellybeans, seven instead of one, so it looks like it offers more chances to win, even though it has many more white ones too. >> People attended to the number of winning beans rather than the proportion, which lowered their actual chance of winning. Their choice was driven by appearance rather than by probability. >> Most participants chose the bowl with the lower chance of winning, a 7% bowl over a 10% bowl. >> Seven red beans look like more chances to win than one, even though the bowl with seven also holds 93 white beans, which makes each draw less likely to win.
21|**After 9/11, air travel decreased and driving increased**. >> After the attacks of September 11, 2001, many Americans chose to drive instead of fly. >> They did so **even though the probability of dying in a car accident is far greater than in flying**. || **The number of Americans who lost their lives on the road to avoid flying is higher than the total number of passengers killed on the four hijacked flights**. >> The decision to avoid flying felt safer. >> In fact, it exposed people to a greater risk, and more lives were lost as a result. || The shift reflects fear rather than calculation. >> The attacks were vivid, frightening and heavily reported, which made the risk of flying feel enormous. >> The everyday risk of driving felt familiar and controllable, so it received far less weight, even though it was much larger. >> Emotional judgments of risk led people to a choice that increased their danger. >> A frightening, heavily reported event makes its risk feel larger than its actual probability, which links this choice to the availability heuristic.
22|An **example dilemma** tests how people weigh lives. >> **A convoy of food trucks is on its way to a refugee camp during a famine**. **Airplanes cannot be used**. >> **You find that a second camp has even more refugees**. >> **If you tell the convoy to go to the second camp instead of the first, you will save 1,000 people from death, but 100 people in the first camp will die as a result**. >> **Would you send the convoy to the second camp?** || The utilities can be written out. >> **Not rerouting** saves the 100 people in the first camp but loses the 1,000 people in the second. \[U(\mathrm{not\ reroute})=100-1000=-900\] >> **Rerouting** saves the 1,000 people in the second camp but loses the 100 in the first. \[U(\mathrm{reroute})=1000-100=900\] >> Rerouting saves 900 more lives, so expected utility theory says to reroute. || **Ritov and Baron (1999)** found that **63% did not choose to send the convoy to the second camp**. >> Rerouting means actively causing the deaths in the first camp, and many people refused to do that, even to save many more lives. >> **Twelve percent responded that they would not change the route of the convoy even if only 1 person in the first camp would die**. >> For these people, causing harm through an action weighs more heavily than failing to prevent a much larger harm. >> Expected utility counts each life equally, so the 900-life difference should decide the choice. The refusal of many participants reveals that how a harm comes about matters to people as well as how many lives are at stake.
23|**Tetlock, Kristel, Elson, Green and Lerner (2000)** proposed the **sacred value protection model**. **People want to protect what they hold sacred**. >> A **sacred value** is a value treated as beyond price, such as human life, loyalty or justice. >> **Sacred values are kept in a separate compartment from secular values**. **Secular values** are ordinary values that can be traded, such as money and time, and they are routinely traded against each other. || **When the two domains are mixed together, people feel threatened and respond with moral outrage**. >> This is a **taboo** tradeoff, such as trading a human life for money. >> Putting a price on something sacred treats it as just another commodity, which people experience as a moral violation. || **In contrast, when two sacred values are compared with each other, people are less morally outraged**. >> This is a **tragic** tradeoff, such as choosing between two lives. >> Both sides are sacred, so the choice is painful and difficult, but it does not cross the line between the sacred and the secular. >> The model predicts different emotional reactions to different kinds of tradeoffs, which is what the Tetlock studies test.
24|**Tetlock and colleagues (2000)** used two scenarios to compare taboo and tragic tradeoffs. || In the **taboo tradeoff scenario**, **Robert can save the life of Johnny, a five-year-old who needs a liver transplant**. >> **The transplant procedure will cost the hospital $1,000,000 that could be spent in other ways, such as purchasing better equipment and raising salaries to recruit talented doctors to the hospital**. >> **Johnny is very ill and has been on the waiting list for a transplant, but because of the shortage of local organ donors, obtaining a liver will be expensive**. >> **Robert could save Johnny’s life, or he could use the $1,000,000 for other hospital needs**. >> The tradeoff sets a life, a sacred value, against money, a secular value. || In the **tragic tradeoff scenario**, **there are two children who need a liver transplant, but only one liver**. >> The tradeoff sets one life against another life. >> Both sides are sacred values. || The two scenarios hold one side constant. >> In both, Johnny’s life is at risk. >> What changes is what his life is weighed against, money in the taboo scenario and another child’s life in the tragic scenario. Any difference in reactions to Robert’s choice can be traced to the type of tradeoff.
25|The results of **Tetlock and colleagues (2000)** plot **moral outrage** toward Robert, from 0 to 6, for each **tradeoff type**, split by whether Robert **did not save Johnny** or **saved Johnny**. || In the **taboo** tradeoff, which set Johnny’s life against money, people reacted strongly to Robert’s choice. >> When Robert **did not save Johnny**, moral outrage was very high, about **5.4**. >> When Robert **saved Johnny**, moral outrage was much lower, about **2.5**. >> Choosing money over a child’s life treated a life as something that can be traded for money, which provoked strong condemnation. || In the **tragic** tradeoff, which set one child’s life against another, Robert’s choice mattered very little. >> Moral outrage was about **3.7** when Robert did not save Johnny and about **3.8** when he saved Johnny. >> Since both options involved saving a life, neither choice violated the separation between sacred and secular values. || Moral outrage depends on whether a sacred value is traded for a secular one. >> Trading a life for money is taboo and provokes outrage. >> Choosing between two lives is tragic, and people judge it less harshly. >> The outrage pattern matches the sacred value protection model. Mixing a sacred value with money provokes condemnation, while choosing between two sacred values does not.
26|**Ginges, Atran, Medin and Shikaki (2007)** **extended the earlier findings to a more realistic setting**. >> They **interviewed Israeli settlers**, with **601** participants. >> They interviewed **Palestinian refugees**, with **535** participants. >> They interviewed **Palestinian students affiliated with various political groups**, with **719** participants. || These groups were directly involved in the Israeli–Palestinian conflict. >> The issues under dispute, such as land and the status of Jerusalem, are deeply important to them. >> Testing sacred values with these participants shows whether laboratory findings hold up when the issues matter deeply to the people involved. || The researchers **offered three types of tradeoff deals as possible solutions to the conflict**. >> Each deal asked participants to give up something they valued in exchange for something else. >> The deals were realistic proposals that could be part of a peace agreement. >> The researchers measured how participants reacted to each deal, including their anger, disgust and support for violence. >> Large samples of 601, 535 and 719 participants gave the study a broad base across all three groups.
27|**Ginges and colleagues (2007)** used three kinds of deals. The examples are deals presented to Palestinian participants. || The **taboo** deal. **Palestinians would recognize the sacred and historic right of the Jewish people to Israel**. **There would be two states, a Jewish state of Israel and a Palestinian state in 99% of the West Bank and Gaza**. >> This deal asks Palestinians to give up a claim they may hold sacred. || The **taboo-plus** deal adds money to the taboo deal. **On their part, Israel will pay Palestine 1 billion US dollars a year for 10 years**. >> This adds a large material incentive to the same compromise. || The **symbolic** deal adds a symbolic concession instead. **On their part, Israel will recognize the historic and legitimate right of the Palestinians to their own state and would apologize for all of the wrongs done to the Palestinian people**. >> This offers recognition and an apology, which are symbolic rather than material. >> It addresses what the other side holds sacred, which makes it a tragic tradeoff rather than a taboo one. || Material and symbolic additions might both be expected to make a deal more attractive. Comparing the three deals reveals whether they have the same effect on people who hold the issues as sacred.
28|Ginges and colleagues used a **sacred value measure** to divide participants into two groups. || **Moral absolutists** **transformed the issues under dispute into sacred values**. >> **Among settlers, 46% of the sample** were moral absolutists. They **believed it was never permissible for the Jewish people to “give up” part of the “Land of Israel,” no matter how great the benefit**. >> **Among Palestinian students, 54%** were moral absolutists. They **treated both the principle of the “right of return” and Jerusalem as sacred values**. The **right of return** is the claim that Palestinian refugees and their descendants may return to the land they left. || **Non-absolutists** **had strong preferences against compromise over these issues but did not regard them as sacred values**. >> They opposed giving up these things, but for them the issues could in principle be traded. || Both groups opposed compromise, but for different reasons. >> Non-absolutists weighed costs and benefits and found the costs too high. >> Absolutists rejected the very idea of weighing the issue against benefits. >> This difference determined how each group responded when material benefits were offered.
29|The results of **Ginges and colleagues (2007)** compare the deals. || **Taboo versus taboo-plus** compares the same compromise with and without money. >> Panel (a) plots **anger and disgust**. For **non-absolutists**, anger and disgust **fell slightly** when money was added, to just below zero. For **moral absolutists**, anger and disgust **rose** when money was added, from about 0.1 to about 0.27. >> Panel (b) plots **percent support for violence**. For non-absolutists, support for violence **fell** with money, to about −5. For moral absolutists, support for violence **rose** with money, to about +7. >> Non-absolutists reacted as a cost-benefit view predicts. For moral absolutists, money turned a difficult compromise into an insulting trade of the sacred for cash. || **Taboo versus symbolic**, the tragic tradeoff, compares the compromise with and without a symbolic concession. >> Panel (a). **Anger or disgust** was about **0.20** for the taboo deal and dropped to about **0.12** for the symbolic deal. >> Panel (b). **Percent support for violence** was about **+3** for the taboo deal and dropped to about **−10** for the symbolic deal. || When a value is sacred, material incentives backfire. >> Money turns the deal into a taboo trade of the sacred for the secular. >> A symbolic concession addresses a sacred value with a sacred value. Recognition and an apology honored what the other side held sacred, which made compromise feel less like a betrayal. >> The same added benefit, money, moved the two groups in opposite directions, which reveals that moral absolutists judge a deal by what it says about their sacred values rather than by its material payoff.
30|Research on **sacred values in intergroup conflicts** has **investigated the impact of monetary incentives and disincentives in negotiations involving sacred values**. Two studies, **Dehghani, Atran and colleagues (2010)** and **Dehghani, Iliev and colleagues (2009)**, addressed two questions. >> **How material incentives and disincentives, such as economic sanctions, psychologically affect the targeted population and potentially influence behavior**. >> **Whether Iranians who treat the Iranian nuclear program as a sacred value would be open to material incentives or disincentives to compromise**. || An **incentive** is a reward offered for compromising, such as economic aid, meant to make a deal more attractive. >> A **disincentive** is a penalty for refusing, such as **economic sanctions**, meant to make refusal more costly. || For ordinary preferences, a large enough reward or penalty usually shifts a decision toward a deal. >> For sacred values, the same reward or penalty may be felt as an attempt to buy or force a compromise of something beyond price. >> Both may then backfire by turning a principled stand into what looks like a bribe or a threat. >> The nuclear studies therefore test the sacred value findings in the setting of international negotiations.
31|The **experiments** used **1,997 Iranian participants in an online study conducted in Farsi**. **Farsi** is the main language of Iran. >> The **dependent variables** were **people’s approval** of a deal, which measured personal support, **people’s anger**, which measured expected public reaction, and a **misleading factor**, meaning whether participants suspected the deal’s intentions. || The **sacred value measure** was **based on Baron and Spranca’s 1997 measure**. It asked **what participants thought about Iran giving up its nuclear energy or weapons program**. >> **a. I think this definitely needs to happen**. >> **b. I do not object to this**. >> **c. This should be done only if the benefits of stopping the program are great enough**. >> **d. This shouldn’t be done no matter how great the consequences**. || **Participants who answered “d” were considered to hold a sacred value**. >> This was **14% for nuclear energy** and **13% for nuclear weapons**. >> Answer “d” refuses any tradeoff, no matter what is offered, which is the defining feature of a sacred value. >> Classifying participants this way allows the study to compare reactions of people who hold a sacred value with reactions of people who do not.
32|The study used a **2 by 3 design**, which produced six versions of the deal. >> The first factor was the **nuclear program**, either **nuclear energy** or **nuclear weapons**. >> The second factor was the **type of deal**, either **taboo**, **taboo-plus** or **taboo-minus**. || The **scenarios** were as follows. >> **Taboo**. **Iran will give up its program for developing nuclear energy, or weapons, and surrender the current nuclear facilities to the UN**. **In return, Israel will give up its program for developing nuclear energy, or weapons, and surrender the existing nuclear facilities to the UN**. >> **Taboo-plus**. The taboo deal, plus **in return, the United Nations will give $40 billion in economic funds to Iran**. >> **Taboo-minus**. The taboo deal, plus **you know that if Iran does not accept this deal, the United Nations will impose additional sanctions on Iran**. || **Participants were asked three questions**. >> **Do you personally approve of this deal?** >> **If the Iranian government approves such a deal, do you think people will be angry?** >> **If such a deal is really proposed to Iran, do you find it misleading?** || Comparing the deals measures how incentives and disincentives change reactions, and comparing participants with and without sacred values tests whether those changes depend on how the program is held.
33|Approval and anger for the **nuclear energy** program compare participants who held a sacred value, **SV**, with those who did not, **noSV**, across the three deals. || The top graph plots **approval** on a scale from 1 to 6. >> For **noSV** participants, approval was high for all three deals, around **4.5 to 4.8**. >> For **SV** participants, approval was low. It was about **2.8** for the taboo deal and dropped to about **1.9** for taboo-plus and about **2.3** for taboo-minus. >> Adding money or sanctions **lowered** approval among those who held a sacred value. || The bottom graph plots **anger** on a scale from 1 to 6. >> For **noSV** participants, anger was low for all three deals, around **2.1**. >> For **SV** participants, anger was about **3.2** for the taboo deal, rose to about **4.6** for taboo-plus and was about **3.9** for taboo-minus. >> Adding money or sanctions **raised** anger among those who held a sacred value. || Material incentives and disincentives backfire when a value is sacred. >> For people who do not hold the value as sacred, the deal is acceptable either way. >> For people who do, offering money or threatening sanctions turns the deal into a taboo trade, which makes them less approving and more angry. When one side treats an issue as sacred, money or penalties can harden opposition instead of softening it. >> Participants without a sacred value treated the extra money or sanctions as ordinary costs and benefits, so their approval stayed high and their anger stayed low across all three deals.
