2|The **modal model** view of short-term memory turned out to be **too simple**. **Short-term memory is not just passive storage**. It also requires **“control processes,”** so a model is needed that includes them. >> **Control processes** are the active operations we perform on information, such as selecting, repeating and transforming it. >> The modal model treated short-term memory mainly as a holding place, with information sitting there until it was used or lost. || **Baddeley and Hitch (1974)** proposed **working memory**, a concept similar to short-term memory but broader. **Working memory** is a **limited-capacity system for the temporary storage and manipulation of information for complex tasks such as comprehension, learning and reasoning**. >> **Storage** keeps information available for a short time. >> **Manipulation** means actively working with that information, such as changing it, combining it or using it in a calculation. || Complex tasks need both functions at once. >> **Comprehension** requires holding the start of a sentence while relating it to the words that follow. >> **Learning** requires keeping new information active while connecting it to what we already know. >> **Reasoning** requires holding facts in mind while working toward a conclusion. || The phrase limited capacity matters because storage and manipulation share the same resources. >> When a task demands a lot of processing, less room remains for holding information. >> When a lot must be held, less room remains for processing, which is why complex mental tasks become harder as more information must be juggled.
3|**Multiplying 43 times 6 in your head** requires storage and active processing at the same time. \[43\times6=258\] The steps run as follows. >> **Step 1. Visualize 43 × 6**, holding the whole problem in mind so each step can refer back to it. >> **Step 2. Multiply 3 × 6**, which gives 18. \[3\times6=18\] >> **Step 3. Hold the 8 in memory** as the ones digit of the answer, **while carrying the 1 over to the 4**. >> **Step 4. Multiply 6 × 4 = 24, then add the carried 1 to get 25**. \[6\times4=24,\qquad24+1=25\] >> **Step 5. Combine the 25 with the 8** being held, which gives **258**. || This operation involves both **storage** and **active processes**. >> Holding the 8 in memory while the rest of the calculation continues is storage. >> Carrying the 1, multiplying and adding are active processes that change the information. >> The 8 has to survive several intervening steps, so the calculation depends on storage and processing working together. This combination is what working memory provides. || The calculation fails if any stored piece is lost. Forgetting the 8 while working on the tens, or forgetting to add the carried 1, produces a wrong answer, which makes mental arithmetic a clear test of working memory.
4|**Short-term memory**, in the model of **Atkinson and Shiffrin**, and **working memory**, in the model of **Baddeley and Hitch**, differ in their structure and purpose. || **Short-term memory** in the Atkinson and Shiffrin model has two key features. >> It is a **single component for all types of information**, so words, pictures and sounds all go into the same store. >> It is **mainly used for holding information for a short time**. || **Working memory** in the Baddeley and Hitch model has **three components**. >> The **central executive**, which directs and coordinates the system. >> The **visuospatial sketchpad**, which holds visual and spatial information. >> The **phonological loop**, which holds verbal and auditory information. >> A fourth component, the **episodic buffer**, was **added by Baddeley in 2000**. It gives the model a place to combine information from different sources. || Working memory is **used for the manipulation of information during complex cognition**, such as reasoning, comprehension and learning. >> A single store predicts that any two tasks held in memory at once should interfere with each other. >> Separate components predict that a verbal task and a visual task can be done together with little interference, since each uses its own store.
5|Each **component of working memory** has its own job. || The **phonological loop** handles verbal and auditory information. It has two parts. >> The **phonological store** **holds verbal and auditory information**, such as words and speech sounds. >> The **articulatory rehearsal process** is **responsible for rehearsal that keeps items in the phonological store from decaying**. **Articulatory** refers to the movements of speech, so this process works by silently saying the items over and over, much like an inner voice. >> Without rehearsal, items in the phonological store fade within a few seconds. || The **visuospatial sketchpad** **holds visual and spatial information**. >> Visual information concerns what things look like, such as shape and color. >> Spatial information concerns where things are and how they are arranged, such as the layout of a room. || The **central executive** **pulls information from long-term memory, coordinates the other components, and directs and maintains attention**. >> In the diagram of **Baddeley’s working memory model**, the central executive sits in the middle, overlapping with the phonological loop, which holds **verbal and auditory information**, on one side and the visuospatial sketchpad, which holds **visual and spatial information**, on the other. >> The executive does not store information itself. It decides what the storage components hold, brings in knowledge from long-term memory and keeps attention on the task.
6|The **working memory model of Baddeley and Hitch (1974)** traces how information flows through the system. >> **Input** from the environment enters **sensory memory**. >> From sensory memory, information passes to the **central executive**. >> The central executive exchanges information in both directions with **long-term knowledge**. || The central executive also exchanges information in both directions with the **phonological loop** and the **visuospatial sketchpad**. >> Arrows run up and down between the executive and each storage component. >> Verbal information, such as a spoken word, goes to the phonological loop, and visual information, such as a shape, goes to the visuospatial sketchpad. >> Long-term knowledge also connects directly with both storage components. || Working memory is a set of interacting parts within a larger memory system. >> The executive controls what each storage component holds and does. >> Working memory constantly draws on stored knowledge, such as word meanings and familiar images, and it can also send new information into long-term storage. This exchange lets working memory interpret new input in light of what we already know. >> Information moves through the system in both directions, so working memory both receives from and contributes to long-term knowledge.
7|Three effects reveal how the **phonological loop** works. || The **phonological similarity effect** is that **letters or words that sound similar are confused**. >> **Phonological** refers to the sounds of language. >> Items that sound alike, such as the letters B, D and G, are easy to mix up in memory because their sound codes overlap. || The **word-length effect** is that **memory for lists of words is better for short words than for long words**. >> **It takes longer to rehearse long words and to produce them during recall**. >> In the same amount of time, fewer long words can be rehearsed, so fewer stay active. || **Articulatory suppression** is when **speaking interferes with rehearsal, so memory is reduced**. >> Saying something out loud, such as repeating “the, the, the,” uses the articulatory rehearsal process. >> With rehearsal taken up, the items being remembered are not refreshed and fade from the phonological store. || Each effect would be hard to explain if verbal information were stored in a visual or meaning-based form. >> Verbal information is held as sounds in the phonological store and kept alive by an inner voice that repeats it. >> When the inner voice is slow, busy or confused by similar sounds, memory suffers.
8|The **phonological similarity effect** is that **words that sound similar are confused by people**. **Conrad (1964)** tested this with letters. || **Participants saw target letters that were quickly flashed**, and **then participants wrote them down**. >> **Half of the time the letters were similar, and half of the time they were not**. >> Because the letters were flashed so quickly, participants had to rely on memory when writing them. || **Mistakes were made**, and the errors followed a clear pattern. >> Participants were **unlikely to replace a letter with one that looked like the target**, such as writing **F** instead of **E**. E and F share many visual strokes but have different-sounding names. >> They were **likely to replace it with one that sounded like the target**, such as writing **B** instead of **E**. The names “E” and “B” share the same vowel sound. >> Lists of letters with similar-sounding names produced more errors than lists with distinct-sounding names. || Participants were remembering the letters by their sounds. >> The letters were seen, not heard. >> If letters were held as images, errors should involve letters that look alike. Instead, the errors were based on sound, so the letters were held in a sound-based code in the phonological loop. >> The design pits two possible codes against each other. A visual code predicts confusions between letters that look alike, while a sound code predicts confusions between letters whose names sound alike. The errors came out on the side of sound.
9|The **phonological similarity effect experiment** **demonstrated the phonological similarity effect**, in which **people confuse letters that sound similar**, **even though the information was presented visually**. >> The letters reached the participants through their eyes. >> They were still confused by sound, not by appearance. || The experiment **also showed that the phonological loop is necessary for the conversion**, **not just for holding information**. >> People do not have to decide to name letters silently. Reading a letter calls up its spoken name, and that name is what gets held. >> This automatic recoding turns a visual input into a phonological code. || The phonological loop is therefore involved at two points. >> It translates visual input, such as written letters and words, into sound. >> It stores and rehearses that sound code until recall, which is why sound-based confusions appear for visually presented items. || Recoding helps explain why written material behaves like spoken material in memory. >> Once letters are converted into their names, they are rehearsed by the same inner voice as heard words. >> Errors then follow sound rather than appearance, whichever way the letters arrived.
10|The **word-length effect** is that **when memorizing words, you can remember fewer words if the words are long**. >> **People remembered more of the short words than the long words**, according to **Baddeley and colleagues (1975, 1984)**. >> Long words take longer to say, so fewer can be rehearsed in the time before they fade. || **Welsh–English bilingual children** have a **shorter digit span in Welsh than in English**, according to **Ellis and Hennelly (1980)**. >> The reason is that **Welsh numbers take longer to pronounce**. >> The children know the same numbers in both languages. The only difference is how long the words take to say, which makes this an especially clean test. >> The very long Welsh place name **Llanfairpwllgwyngyllgogerychwyrndrobwllllantysiliogogogoch** is a famous example of how long Welsh words can be. || **The number of words you can say in 1.5 to 2.0 seconds is likely to be your digit span**. >> The phonological loop holds about as much as can be rehearsed in that window before its contents fade. >> Short words such as one-syllable nouns fit easily, while long multisyllable words fit less well. Memory span is tied to speaking time rather than to a fixed number of items. || The word-length effect therefore reveals how the phonological loop is limited. >> Its capacity is set by time, the roughly 1.5 to 2 seconds of speech it can refresh. >> More items fit when each one is quick to say, and fewer fit when each one takes longer.
11|**Articulatory suppression** is what happens **if you speak while memorizing**, which **keeps the phonological loop busy**. >> **You get worse at remembering**. >> **The word-length and phonological similarity effects are reduced**. || The **word-length effect is reduced** because rehearsal is blocked. >> Normally, short words are remembered better because they can be rehearsed faster. >> When speaking takes over the rehearsal process, neither short nor long words get rehearsed, so the advantage for short words shrinks. || The **phonological similarity effect is also reduced** because the conversion into sound is blocked. >> Normally, seen letters are converted into sound codes, which leads to sound-based confusions. >> When the phonological loop is busy, visually presented letters are not converted into sound, so sound similarity no longer matters as much. || These results **support the concept of a phonological loop**. >> Saying something irrelevant, such as “the, the, the,” occupies the speech system that rehearsal needs. >> One manipulation removes both effects at once, so rehearsal and recoding both depend on the same speech-based system. >> If the effects came from separate systems, one manipulation would not remove both of them together.
12|The **visuospatial sketchpad** **holds visual and spatial information**. >> **Visual** information is about appearance, such as shape and color. >> **Spatial** information is about location and arrangement, such as where things are and how they relate to one another. || The sketchpad is **involved in the process of visual imagery**. >> **Visual imagery** is creating or holding a picture in the mind without seeing it. >> Planning a route, imagining how furniture would fit in a room and mentally rotating an object all use the visuospatial sketchpad. || In the working memory model, the sketchpad connects with the **central executive** and with **long-term knowledge**. >> The executive directs what the sketchpad holds. >> Long-term knowledge supplies stored images and spatial layouts that can be brought into the sketchpad. || Because the sketchpad and the phonological loop are separate components, a visual task and a verbal task can be carried out together with little interference. >> Holding a picture in the sketchpad leaves the phonological loop free for words. >> Holding words in the phonological loop leaves the sketchpad free for images and locations. >> The sketchpad can also transform images, such as turning a mental picture to a new angle, which is active manipulation and not only storage.
13|The **sentence experiment** of **Brooks (1968)** tests the **phonological loop** and the **visuospatial sketchpad**. || Participants first **memorized a sentence**. >> They held it in mind for the rest of the task. >> They then went through the sentence word by word and **indicated whether each word was or was not a noun**. >> In **condition 1**, they **indicated by speaking**, saying “yes” or “no.” >> In **condition 2**, they **indicated by pointing** to yes or no on a sheet. >> The task was identical in both conditions. Only the response changed, which isolates the effect of response type. || For the participants, **pointing was easier than speaking**, because **the phonological loop was busy processing the sentence, but the sketchpad was free**. >> The sentence is verbal, so it was held in the phonological loop. >> Speaking the answers also uses the phonological loop, so the verbal response competed with the verbal sentence. >> Pointing uses the visuospatial sketchpad, which was free, so there was no competition. The difference in difficulty reflects competition for the same component of working memory. >> Holding a sentence and classifying its words uses the phonological loop, so a second verbal task interferes and a spatial task does not. >> Participants went through the sentence word by word, so they had to keep the sentence active in the phonological loop the whole time they were responding.
14|The **“F” demo** of **Brooks (1968)** tests the same two components, but with a picture instead of a sentence. || Participants **memorized a shape**, in this case a block **F**. >> They held the image in mind for the rest of the task. >> Starting from a corner marked with a star, they mentally traced around the F and **indicated whether each corner was an “inside corner” or an “outside corner.”** >> In **condition 1**, they **indicated by speaking**, saying “in” or “out.” >> In **condition 2**, they **indicated by pointing**, working down a list of corners numbered 1 to 10 and pointing to **OUT** or **IN** in each row. || This time **speaking was easier than pointing**, **the opposite of the sentence experiment**, because **the sketchpad was busy with the image, but the phonological loop was free**. >> The F is a visual image, so it was held in the visuospatial sketchpad. >> Pointing to locations on a sheet requires looking and moving the hand to locations, which uses the same spatial resources and competed with the image. >> Speaking uses the phonological loop, which was free, so there was no competition. || The reversal is the most important feature of Brooks’s design. Speaking was harder for the sentence and easier for the image, while pointing was easier for the sentence and harder for the image. A single store could not produce this crossover pattern. >> Tracing the corners of the F means moving around a mental image one corner at a time, which keeps the visuospatial sketchpad busy throughout the task.
15|**Tasks are easier when the information held in mind and the operation performed on it involve different types of short-term memory**. The two types are **verbal or phonological** and **visual or spatial**. >> In the sentence task, a verbal memory paired with a spatial response, pointing, was easy. >> In the F task, a visual memory paired with a verbal response, speaking, was easy. >> When the memory and the response used the same system, performance suffered. || **The two types of short-term memory are therefore somewhat independent**. >> **At the least, they have separate capacities**. >> Filling the phonological loop leaves room in the visuospatial sketchpad, and filling the sketchpad leaves room in the phonological loop. || Each component interferes with itself much more than with the other. >> Two verbal demands compete, and two visual-spatial demands compete. >> A verbal demand paired with a visual-spatial one leaves both running smoothly. Listening to directions while driving uses both systems, and the parts that overlap are where trouble arises. >> The crossover between the two Brooks tasks is the evidence. Each kind of memory is disrupted only by a response of its own kind.

17|**Mental rotation** is the ability to turn a mental image to a new orientation. **Shepard and Metzler (1971)** showed participants pairs of three-dimensional objects built from cubes and asked whether the two objects were the same object or mirror images. >> In **picture-plane rotation** pairs, one object is rotated flat within the plane of the page, like turning a clock face. >> In **depth rotation** pairs, one object is rotated in depth, as if turned around a vertical axis. >> In some pairs, the objects are mirror images that cannot be rotated into a match, so recognizing them depends on completing the rotation. || For the picture-plane pairs, reaction time, **RT**, in seconds is plotted against the **angle of rotation** in degrees, from 0 to 180. >> At **0 degrees**, the RT is about **1 second**. >> RT rises in a straight line as the angle grows. >> At **180 degrees**, the RT is about **4.5 seconds**. || Participants **mentally rotated** one object to compare it with the other. >> A bigger difference in angle requires a longer mental rotation. >> The straight-line relationship means each extra degree adds about the same amount of time, as if the image were turned at a steady speed through intermediate orientations, much as a real object would be. >> Depth rotation produced the same linear pattern, so the mental image is treated as a three-dimensional object. Mental rotation is a clear example of active manipulation in the visuospatial sketchpad. >> A rotation of 160 degrees takes longer than a rotation of 80 degrees, roughly in proportion to the angle, which is what we expect if the image turns at a steady rate.
18|The **central executive** **does the “work” of working memory**. Its jobs include the following. >> **Directing and maintaining attention**, which includes **focusing**, **dividing** and **switching** attention. Focusing keeps task-relevant information active, dividing splits resources between two tasks, and switching moves resources from one task to another. >> **Controlling suppression of irrelevant information**, so that distractions do not take up working memory. >> **Coordinating the sketchpad and the phonological loop**, so that both can contribute to a task. >> **Performing calculations**, as in mental arithmetic. || Today, the central executive is **often split into updating, shifting and inhibition**, following **Miyake and colleagues (2000)**. >> **Updating** is replacing old information in working memory with new, relevant information. >> **Shifting** is switching between tasks or mental sets. >> **Inhibition** is suppressing unwanted thoughts or responses. || **The idea of a “central executive” is often criticized as theorizing a “homunculus” in the brain**. >> A **homunculus** is a “little person” inside the head who makes all the decisions. >> Saying that a central executive controls everything describes the control without explaining how it works. >> Splitting the executive into updating, shifting and inhibition gives specific, measurable functions that can be studied separately and that differ between people.
19|The **episodic buffer** is **a fourth component, added to the working memory model by Baddeley in 2000**. >> It has a **limited capacity of about 4 “chunks.”** A **chunk** is a meaningful unit of information, which can contain several pieces. >> It **binds information from the phonological loop, the visuospatial sketchpad and long-term memory into integrated “episodes.”** **Binding** means linking different pieces of information into one combined memory. || In the updated diagram, the **central executive** sits at the top, connected to the **phonological loop**, the **episodic buffer** and the **visuospatial sketchpad** below it. >> The episodic buffer sits in the middle, connected to both the phonological loop and the visuospatial sketchpad. >> All three connect down to **long-term knowledge**. || Two findings showed **why the episodic buffer was needed**. >> **People can recall about 16 words of a meaningful sentence**, **far beyond their span for unrelated words**. Meaning and grammar bind the words into a few large chunks, which the original model had no place to hold. >> **Some amnesic patients can immediately recall a just-heard story**. **Amnesia** is a severe loss of long-term memory. These patients could not store the story in long-term memory, so the story must have been held as a meaningful episode in a working memory component large enough for it.
20|**Working memory and the brain** can be studied through **individual differences**, the ways people differ from one another. **Vogel and colleagues (2005)** first **determined participants’ working memory capacity** and divided them into a **high-capacity group** and a **low-capacity group**. They then **measured ERP responses**. >> An **ERP**, or **event-related potential**, is the brain’s electrical activity recorded from the scalp, time-locked to events in a task. || Participants were **shown either simple or complex stimuli**. Each trial had four phases. >> A **cue** arrow, shown for **200 milliseconds**, pointed left or right to indicate which side of the display to remember. >> A **memory display** of colored bars at different angles appeared for **100 milliseconds**. >> A blank **delay**, the retention interval, lasted **900 milliseconds**. ERPs during this delay tracked how much information each person was holding. >> A **test display** appeared for **2,000 milliseconds**, and participants judged whether the orientation of a red bar had changed. || In the **simple** condition, the cued side contained only **red bars**, the targets to remember. >> In the **complex** condition, the cued side contained red bars plus **blue bars**, which were **distractors** to be ignored. >> If a person filters out the blue bars, brain activity should reflect only the red bars. If the blue bars get in, activity should rise as if more items were being held. >> Using a cue that points to one side of the screen means the participants must attend to the cued side and hold only its bars, so the task measures what they let into working memory.
21|In **panel b** of the **Vogel and colleagues (2005)** results, brain activity during the task is plotted separately for **high-capacity** and **low-capacity** participants in three conditions. >> **Two items**, two red bars to remember. >> **Four items**, four red bars to remember. >> **Two items with two distractors**, two red bars plus two blue bars to ignore. || **Activity continues well into the retention interval**, after the memory display has disappeared. >> This activity reflects the items being held in visual working memory. >> More items held means more activity. || For **high-capacity** individuals, **activity is greater when there are four relevant items than when there are two relevant items**. >> **Adding irrelevant blue items has no effect**. Activity with two targets and two distractors looks like activity with two targets alone. >> High-capacity people keep the distractors out of working memory. || For **low-capacity** individuals, **adding irrelevant items increases activity**. >> Activity with two targets and two distractors rises to nearly the level of four targets. >> Low-capacity people let the distractors into working memory, where they take up space meant for the targets. || The difference between groups lies mainly in how well each group controls what enters working memory, rather than in raw storage space. >> A two-target display with distractors that produces four-target activity means the distractors are being stored as if they were targets.
22|In **panel c** of the **Vogel and colleagues (2005)** results, each participant’s **memory capacity** is plotted against their **filtering efficiency**. Each point is one person. >> **Filtering efficiency** is an **EEG** measure of how well a person keeps irrelevant items out of working memory. **EEG** records electrical activity of the brain from the scalp. >> Memory capacity runs from about 1 to 4 items, and filtering efficiency from 0 to 1. || **Capacity is correlated with the EEG measure of filtering efficiency**. >> The points rise from lower left to upper right, and a line drawn through them slopes upward. >> **People with higher capacity are also less distracted by the irrelevant items**. || This changes how differences in working memory capacity are understood. >> High capacity reflects how well a person controls what enters working memory, along with how much it can hold. >> People with high capacity filter out distractors, which leaves their limited space free for relevant information. >> People with low capacity let distractors in, which fills their working memory with items that are not needed. Improving attentional control could free up working memory for the information that matters. >> The upward slope means that individual differences in capacity and in filtering go together. People who store more items are the same people who keep irrelevant items out.
