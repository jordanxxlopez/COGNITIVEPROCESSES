2|**Problem solving** can be understood in several ways. >> **Problem solving as reorganization and insight**. In the Gestalt view, a solution comes from restructuring how the problem is represented in the mind, sometimes in a sudden flash of insight. >> **Problem solving as search**, a **search that occurs between the posing of the problem and its solution**. In the information-processing view of Newell and Simon, the solver moves step by step through a problem space from the initial state toward the goal state. || **There is more to problem solving than specifying the problem state**. **How a problem is stated can affect its difficulty**. >> The **mutilated checkerboard**, a checkerboard with two opposite corners removed and a domino beside it, is the standard case. >> Its difficulty depends on whether the solver notices that each domino must cover one square of each color. >> Presenting the board in ways that highlight the two kinds of squares makes the problem easier. || The two views of problem solving focus on different parts of the process. The Gestalt view focuses on how the problem is represented and how that representation changes. The information-processing view focuses on the steps taken to move from the start to the goal. >> A representation determines which paths seem possible. >> Search determines which path is actually followed. || Both views agree that the statement of a problem matters. The same problem can be easy or hard depending on whether its wording or presentation points the solver toward a helpful representation. This idea connects the earlier work on representation with the study of analogies, where the solution to one problem helps the solver represent another.
3|**Analogies** can help solve problems. **Using the solution to a similar problem guides the solution to a new problem**. || The **Russian marriage problem** runs as follows. >> **In a small Russian village, there were 32 bachelors and 32 unmarried women**. >> **Through tireless efforts, the village matchmaker succeeded in arranging 32 highly satisfactory marriages**, and **the village was proud and happy**. >> **Then one drunken night, two bachelors, in a test of strength, stuffed each other with pierogies and died**. >> **Can the matchmaker, through some quick arrangements, come up with 31 heterosexual marriages among the 62 survivors?** || The answer is **no**. >> After the deaths, there are **30 men and 32 women**, which makes 62 people. >> Each marriage in the problem needs **one man and one woman**. >> With only 30 men, at most **30 marriages** can be arranged, and two women are left without partners. >> The total of 62 people would allow 31 pairs only if any two people could be paired. The requirement of one from each group makes the smaller group the limit. || The Russian marriage problem is easy to solve because its structure is obvious. Everyone knows a marriage in this problem requires one man and one woman, so the shortage of men immediately limits the number of marriages. >> The story makes the two groups and the one-of-each pairing rule plain. >> Counting the groups after the two deaths gives the answer at once. || The same structure is hidden in the checkerboard problem. There, the two groups are the two colors of squares, and each domino is a pair that needs one of each. Because the marriage version makes this structure clear, it can serve as a source that helps people solve the checkerboard.
4|The **mutilated checkerboard** has the same structure as the Russian marriage problem. >> The board has two opposite corners removed, shown set off to either side, and a domino sits beside it. >> A **domino** covers **two neighboring squares**, which are always **one dark and one light**. || Removing two opposite corners removes two squares of the **same color**. >> The remaining 62 squares are **32 of one color and 30 of the other**. >> Thirty-one dominoes would need **31 squares of each color**. || A complete covering is therefore impossible. >> Each domino is like a marriage, pairing one member of each kind. >> The two missing corners are like the two missing bachelors. In both problems, an imbalance between the two kinds rules out the last pair, no matter how the pieces are arranged. || Seen through the lens of the marriage problem, the checkerboard becomes simple. Removing two corners of the same color creates the same kind of imbalance as removing two bachelors. >> Each domino covers one dark and one light square, just as each marriage joins one man and one woman. >> With 32 squares of one color and 30 of the other, at most 30 dominoes can be placed in matched pairs, and two squares of the same color are left over. || The problem cannot be solved by trying harder to arrange the dominoes. The count of the two colors rules out a complete covering before any domino is placed.
5|**Analogical problem solving** is **using the solution to a similar problem to guide the solution to a new problem**. >> The **Russian marriage problem** is the **source problem**, and the **mutilated-checkerboard problem** is the **target problem**. || **Analogical transfer** is **the transfer from one problem to another**, from the **source problem** to the **target problem**. >> The **target problem** is **the problem the person is trying to solve**. >> The **source problem** is **another problem that shares some similarities with the target problem**. || **Learning about the Russian marriage problem enhances the ability to solve the mutilated-checkerboard problem**. >> Men and women correspond to the two colors of squares. >> Marriages correspond to dominoes, which each pair one of each kind. >> The missing bachelors correspond to the missing corners. Once the solver sees these correspondences, the logic of the marriage problem solves the checkerboard. || Analogical transfer requires matching the parts of the source to the parts of the target. In the marriage and checkerboard problems, men map onto squares of one color, women map onto squares of the other color, and marriages map onto dominoes. >> Once this mapping is in place, the logic of the source applies directly to the target. >> The solver does not have to discover the color argument from scratch. || Learning the marriage problem first makes the checkerboard much easier to solve. The marriage story supplies the key idea, which is that pairing requires equal numbers of two kinds, and the solver carries that idea over to the board.
6|**Duncker’s radiation problem** is a classic test of analogical problem solving. >> **Suppose you are a doctor faced with a patient who has a malignant tumor in his stomach**. >> **It is impossible to operate on the patient, but unless the tumor is destroyed, the patient will die**. >> **There is a kind of ray that can be used to destroy the tumor**. **If the rays reach it all at once at a sufficiently high intensity, the tumor will be destroyed**. >> **Unfortunately, at this intensity, the healthy tissue the rays pass through on the way to the tumor will also be destroyed**. >> **At lower intensities, the rays are harmless to healthy tissue, but they will not affect the tumor either**. >> **What type of procedure might be used to destroy the tumor with the rays and at the same time avoid destroying the healthy tissue?** || The solution is to use **several low-intensity rays from different directions** that **converge on the tumor at the same time**. >> Each ray is weak enough to pass safely through healthy tissue. >> Where the rays meet at the tumor, their combined intensity is high enough to destroy it. || **Only 10% of participants arrive at the correct solution** on their own. >> The solution requires splitting one strong ray into many weak ones that combine at the target, which is an idea most people do not think of. || Duncker’s radiation problem pits two requirements against each other. The tumor needs a high intensity to be destroyed, while the healthy tissue needs a low intensity to be spared. A single ray cannot satisfy both requirements at once. >> A high-intensity ray destroys the tumor but also the tissue along its path. >> A low-intensity ray spares the tissue but leaves the tumor intact. || The convergence solution resolves the conflict by changing how the rays are delivered. Each ray along its own path is weak, but all the rays add up at the tumor. Only about 10% of people find this solution on their own, which makes the problem a good test of whether an analogy can help.
7|The **fortress problem** serves as a source problem for the radiation problem. >> **A small country was ruled from a strong fortress by a dictator**. **The fortress was in the middle of the country, surrounded by farms and villages**, and **many roads led to the fortress through the countryside**. >> **A rebel general vowed to capture the fortress**. **He knew that an attack by his entire army would capture it**, and **he gathered his army at the head of one of the roads, ready to launch a full-scale direct attack**. >> **However, the general then learned that the dictator had planted mines on each of the roads**. **The mines were set so that small bodies of men could pass over them safely**, **since the dictator needed to move his troops and workers to and from the fortress**. >> **However, any large force would detonate the mines**. **Not only would this blow up the road, it would also destroy many neighboring villages**. **It therefore seemed impossible to capture the fortress**. || The general’s solution is to **divide the army into small groups**, send them **along different roads** and have them **arrive at the fortress at the same time**. >> Each small group passes safely over the mines. >> Together, the groups make up the full army at the fortress, which is enough to capture it. || The fortress problem has two conflicting requirements. >> A large force is needed at the target. >> Only small forces can travel safely along any one route. Splitting and converging satisfies both. || The fortress story contains the same conflict as the radiation problem in a different setting. A large force is needed at the fortress, but only small forces can travel safely along the roads. >> The mines play the same role as the healthy tissue, limiting what can pass along each route. >> The fortress plays the same role as the tumor, requiring a large combined force. || The general’s solution is the convergence idea in military form. Small groups travel separately and arrive together, so each road carries only a small force while the fortress faces the full army.
8|The **fortress problem** and the **radiation problem** have the same structure. >> The fortress picture has many roads leading into a central fortress from all directions. >> The radiation picture has a person’s body with several rays coming from different directions and meeting at the tumor in the stomach. || The elements of the two problems correspond. >> The **fortress** corresponds to the **tumor**. >> The **small groups of soldiers** correspond to the **low-intensity rays**. >> The **roads** correspond to the **paths through healthy tissue**. >> The **mines that a large force would set off** correspond to the **healthy tissue that a strong ray would destroy**. || Both solutions work the same way. >> Weak forces travel safely along separate paths. >> They converge at the target, where their combined strength is enough to do the job. || The pictures of the fortress and the tumor make the shared structure visible. Roads lead into the fortress from many directions, and rays enter the tumor from many directions. >> In both, the paths approach a central target from all around. >> In both, what travels along each path is weak, while the combination at the center is strong. || The mapping between the two problems is complete. Each element of the fortress story has a counterpart in the radiation problem, and the solution in one translates directly into the solution in the other.
9|**After reading the fortress story, 30% of participants arrived at the correct solution** to the radiation problem. >> That is three times the 10% who solve it without the story. >> Even so, most participants did not use the analogy. || **Gick and Holyoak** described three steps in analogical problem solving. >> **Noticing an analogous relationship** between the source and the target. This is **crucial, and the most difficult step**. >> **Mapping the correspondence between source and target**. **Elements of the source need to be mapped onto elements of the target**, such as soldiers onto rays. >> **Applying the mapping** **to generate a parallel solution in the target**. || **Seventy percent of participants were not able to see the correspondences** on their own. >> **When participants were told to think about the story they had read, the success rate rose to 75%**. >> The hint did not supply the solution. It only told participants that the story was relevant, so noticing is the main barrier. || Gick and Holyoak’s three steps explain why many participants failed to use the fortress story. Noticing, mapping and applying must all happen, and failure at any step blocks the solution. >> Noticing is the hardest step because the fortress story looks unrelated to a medical problem. >> Mapping requires lining up soldiers with rays and roads with paths through tissue. >> Applying requires turning the mapped idea into a concrete procedure for the rays. || The hint result reveals where the difficulty lies. When told to use the story, three-quarters of participants succeeded, so most of them could map and apply the analogy. Without the hint, most of them never noticed that the story was relevant, so noticing is the main barrier.
10|There are **factors that make the noticing and mapping steps easier**. || **One reason noticing is difficult is that people often focus on surface features**. >> **Surface features** are the specific objects and details in a problem. >> The **fortress** and the **tumor** have completely different surface features. One is about armies and roads, and the other is about rays and medicine. >> Because the surface features differ so much, people do not think of the fortress story when facing the tumor. || This raises a question. **Can making the surface features similar make it easier to notice analogous relationships?** >> If the source problem looked more like the target, people might recognize the connection more easily. >> The **lightbulb problem** tests this. || Surface features dominate what people notice when they read a problem. A story about armies brings military knowledge to mind, while a problem about a tumor brings medical knowledge to mind. >> Memory retrieval is guided by surface similarity, so a source with different surface features is rarely recalled. >> Even a perfect structural match can go unnoticed when the surface features differ. || This suggests a way to make analogies easier. If the source problem shares surface features with the target, it may be more likely to come to mind, and the solver can then discover the deeper structural match.
11|The **lightbulb problem** has surface features much closer to the radiation problem. >> **In a physics lab at a major university, a very expensive lightbulb, which would emit precisely controlled quantities of light, was being used in some experiments**. >> **One morning, Ruth, the research assistant, came into the lab and found that the lightbulb no longer worked**. **She noticed that the filament inside the bulb had broken into two parts**. >> **The surrounding glass bulb was completely sealed, so there was no way to open it**. >> **Ruth knew that the lightbulb could be repaired if a brief, high-intensity laser beam could be used to fuse the two parts of the filament into one**. || **However, a high-intensity laser beam would also break the fragile glass surrounding the filament**. >> **At lower intensities, the laser would not break the glass, but neither would it fuse the filament**. >> **What type of procedure might be used to fuse the filament with the laser and at the same time avoid breaking the glass?** || The solution is to aim **several low-intensity lasers from different directions** so that they **converge on the filament**. >> Each beam is weak enough to pass through the glass without breaking it. >> Together, the beams fuse the filament. || The lightbulb problem was built to share surface features with the radiation problem. Both involve focused beams directed at a small target inside something fragile. >> The broken filament corresponds to the tumor. >> The fragile glass corresponds to the healthy tissue. >> The laser corresponds to the radiation. || The solution follows the same convergence idea. Several weak lasers aimed from different directions meet at the filament, combining to fuse it, while each point on the glass receives only a weak beam.
12|**Duncker’s radiation problem was used as the source** for the lightbulb problem. >> **Eighty-one percent of participants who knew about the radiation problem solved the lightbulb problem**. >> **Only 10% of participants in the control group were able to solve it**. || **Surface similarity helped in solving the problem**. >> The radiation problem and the lightbulb problem share many surface features. Both involve beams directed at a small target inside something that must not be damaged. >> The tumor corresponds to the filament, and the healthy tissue corresponds to the fragile glass. || Compared with the fortress story, which helped only 30% of participants, the radiation problem was a much more effective source. >> When the source and target look alike, people notice the connection far more easily. || The radiation problem was a very effective source for the lightbulb problem. Eighty-one percent of participants who knew the radiation problem solved the lightbulb problem, compared with only 10% of the control group. >> The control group had no source problem, so their 10% reflects how hard the problem is on its own. >> A well-matched source helps a great deal. || The surface similarity between the two problems made the radiation problem easy to notice as relevant. Rays and lasers, tumors and filaments, and tissue and glass are close enough that the connection comes to mind readily.
13|In the **lightbulb problem**, **high surface similarities aid analogical problem solving**. **Surface features** are **the specific elements of a given problem**, such as lasers, glass and filaments. || **Structural features** are a second kind of feature. They are **the underlying principle that governs the solution**. >> In the **radiation** problem, the structural feature is that **a strong ray destroys tissue**. >> In the **lightbulb** problem, the structural feature is that **a strong laser breaks the lightbulb**. >> In both, a strong beam would damage what surrounds the target, which is why the beam must be split. || To separate the two kinds of features, researchers **made two versions of the lightbulb problem**, **keeping the surface features the same and varying the structural features**. >> Both versions use the same lab, the same lightbulb and the same lasers. >> They differ in the reason why a single strong beam cannot be used. || Surface features and structural features play different roles in analogy. Surface features help a source come to mind, while structural features determine whether the source’s solution actually fits. >> A source with matching surface features but a different structure would come to mind but would mislead. >> A source with matching structure but different surface features would help but would rarely be noticed. || The two versions of the lightbulb problem separate these roles. Both versions keep the same surface features, so any difference in transfer must come from the structural difference between them.
14|**Source problem 1** is the **fragile-glass version**, in which **the source and target have similar structural features**. >> The **problem**. **A high-intensity laser beam would break the fragile glass surrounding the filament**. **At lower intensities, the laser would not break the glass, but neither would it fuse the filament**. >> This matches the radiation problem, where a strong ray would destroy healthy tissue. || **Ruth’s solution**. **Ruth placed several lasers in a circle around the lightbulb and administered low-intensity laser beams from several directions at once**. >> **The beams all converged on the filament, where their combined effect was enough to fuse it**. >> **Because each spot on the surrounding glass received only a low-intensity beam from each laser, the glass was left intact**. || **Sixty-nine percent of participants who read this solution were able to solve the radiation problem**. >> The fragile glass plays the same role as the healthy tissue. >> Because the reason for splitting the beam is the same in both problems, the solution transferred well. || In the fragile-glass version, the reason for using several weak beams is to protect the glass. This matches the radiation problem, where the reason for using several weak rays is to protect the healthy tissue. >> The structural feature is the same in both problems, namely that a strong beam would damage what surrounds the target. >> The solution therefore transfers directly, since the same reasoning applies. || A structurally matched source works well, with a 69% success rate. Readers of this version understood why convergence was needed, and that understanding carried over to the radiation problem.
15|**Source problem 2** is the **insufficient-intensity version**, in which **the source and target have different structural features**. >> The **problem**. **The laser generated only low-intensity beams that were not strong enough to fuse the filament**. **A much more intense laser beam was needed**. >> Here there is no danger of breaking the glass. The only obstacle is that each laser is too weak. || **Ruth’s solution** is the same as before. **Ruth placed several lasers in a circle around the lightbulb and administered low-intensity laser beams from several directions at once**. **The beams all converged on the filament, where their combined effect was enough to fuse it**. >> The procedure and the surface features match the fragile-glass version exactly. >> Only the reason for using several beams has changed. || **Only 33% of participants who read this solution were able to solve the radiation problem**. >> This is about half the 69% success after the fragile-glass version. >> The radiation problem’s key constraint is avoiding damage to surrounding tissue, and only the fragile-glass version shares that constraint. Matching structural features matters more than matching surface features. || In the insufficient-intensity version, the reason for using several beams is different. Each laser is simply too weak, so several are combined to reach enough intensity. >> This version has no risk of damaging the glass, so it lacks the radiation problem’s key structural feature. >> The procedure is the same, but the reason behind it differs. || Only 33% of readers solved the radiation problem, about half the success of the fragile-glass group. Knowing the procedure was not enough. Participants needed to understand the reason for convergence, and only the fragile-glass version supplied the reason that matched the radiation problem.
16|**Making structural features more obvious aids analogical problem solving**. **Structural features** are **the underlying principles that govern the solution to a problem**. >> When people see the principle behind a solution, they can apply it to problems that look very different. || **Analogical encoding** is **the process by which two problems are compared and the similarities between them are determined**. >> It is **an effective way to get participants to pay attention to the structural features that aid problem solving**. >> **Having people compare source stories may be an effective way to make them focus on structural features**. || Comparing two stories brings out what they share. >> Two convergence stories with different surface details, such as soldiers and lasers, both rely on weak forces combining at a target. >> Comparing them pulls out that common principle, which can then be applied to a new problem like the radiation problem. || Analogical encoding works by comparison. When people compare two source stories, the details unique to each story fade, and the structure they share stands out. >> Comparing a fortress story with a lightbulb story highlights the common idea of weak forces converging on a target. >> The surface details, armies in one and lasers in the other, are left behind. || The extracted structure is easier to apply to new problems. Because it is no longer tied to one setting, it can be recognized in a problem with completely different surface features, such as a tumor treated with rays.
17|The **analogical paradox** is that **it can be difficult to apply analogies in the laboratory, but people routinely use analogies in real-world settings**. >> **Scientists use about 3 to 15 analogies in a 1-hour lab meeting**. >> In everyday work, people draw on analogies constantly, even though they often fail to use them in experiments. || **In vivo problem-solving research** studies problem solving in natural settings. **People are observed to determine how they solve problems in the real world**. >> **In vivo** means “in the living,” referring to real-life situations as opposed to the laboratory. >> The **advantage** is the **naturalistic setting**, which reveals how people actually solve problems in their work. >> The **disadvantages** are that it is **time-consuming** and researchers **cannot isolate and control variables** the way they can in an experiment. || The analogical paradox has a likely explanation in how analogies are used outside the lab. In real-world settings, people generate their own analogies from knowledge they already understand well, rather than being handed an unfamiliar story to apply. >> Scientists in lab meetings draw on their deep knowledge of related experiments and systems. >> Their analogies often share both surface and structural features with the problem at hand. || In vivo research captures this natural use of analogy. Observing real groups reveals how often and how effectively analogies are used, though the method cannot isolate the specific causes the way a controlled experiment can.
18|An **expert** is **“a person who, by devoting a large amount of time to learning about a field and practicing and applying that learning, has become acknowledged as being extremely knowledgeable or skilled in that field.”** >> A **chess expert**, for example, is **someone who has spent 10,000 to 20,000 hours studying and playing chess**. >> Expertise comes from long, focused experience in one area. || **Experts solve problems in their field faster and with a higher success rate than beginners**. >> They reach correct solutions more often. >> They also reach them more quickly. || The question is **why experts are better**. >> The answers lie in how much experts know and how their knowledge is organized. || Expertise is built through long practice in one field. The 10,000 to 20,000 hours a chess expert spends studying and playing build a huge store of knowledge about positions, moves and strategies. >> This knowledge is specific to chess. >> It does not transfer to unrelated fields. || Experts are faster and more accurate within their field. The question of why they are better leads to two answers, which are that experts know more and that their knowledge is organized in a more useful way.
19|**Experts possess more knowledge about their fields**. **Chase and Simon (1973)** **compared how well a chess master and a beginner, with fewer than 100 hours of play, were able to reproduce the positions of pieces on a chessboard after looking at an arrangement for 5 seconds**. || The study had **two conditions**. >> **Pieces arranged in actual game positions**. >> **Pieces arranged randomly**. || **Experts did much better than non-experts in the first condition**. >> **They had stored these actual positions in their long-term memory**. >> **The positions were accessed in chunks**, which are groups of pieces that form familiar patterns from real games. >> **These chunks were not available in the random condition**, so the expert’s advantage largely disappeared when the pieces were placed randomly. || The expert’s advantage comes from knowledge. >> The master recognizes meaningful game patterns and stores each pattern as one chunk. >> Random placements match no stored patterns, so the master has no chunks to use. || Expert knowledge is stored as meaningful patterns. After only 5 seconds of viewing, the master could reproduce real game positions far better than the beginner. >> Real positions match patterns the master has seen many times, so each pattern is stored as a single chunk. >> The beginner, with fewer than 100 hours of experience, has few stored patterns, so each piece must be remembered separately. || Random arrangements remove the master’s advantage. The pieces no longer form familiar patterns, so the master has to remember each piece individually, just like the beginner. Expertise works by giving meaning to familiar arrangements.
20|**Experts’ knowledge is organized so it can be accessed when needed to work on a problem**. **Chi and colleagues (1982)** **presented 24 physics problems to a group of experts, who were physics professors, and a group of novices, who were students with one semester of physics**. >> **They asked participants to sort the problems into groups based on their similarities**. || The two groups sorted the problems very differently. >> **Novices sorted based on surface features**, **what the objects looked like**. Problems with similar pictures, such as blocks on slopes, went together. >> **Experts sorted based on structural features**, **the underlying principles involved**. Problems that needed the same physics principle went together, even if they looked different. || Experts organize their knowledge around principles. >> When an expert sees a problem, the principle needed to solve it comes to mind. >> A novice sees the objects in the problem, which may have nothing to do with how it is solved. || The sorting task in Chi’s study reveals how knowledge is organized. How people group problems reveals which features they consider important. >> Novices grouped problems by the objects they contained, such as inclined planes or springs. >> Experts grouped problems by the physics principles needed to solve them, such as conservation of energy. || The expert’s organization is more useful for solving. Grouping by principle means that, when an expert reads a new problem, the principle needed to solve it comes to mind. Grouping by surface objects gives the novice no such guidance.
21|Examples from the **Chi and colleagues** study reveal the difference between novices and experts. || The **novice** **grouped problems 23 and 24 together because they both involve similar objects, inclined planes**. >> **Problem 23** has a 2-pound block on an inclined plane, with a friction coefficient of 0.2 and an initial velocity of 4 feet per second up the slope. >> **Problem 24** has a block on an inclined plane, with friction and a marked length along the slope. >> Both problems show a block on a slope, so they look alike. || The **expert** **grouped problems 21 and 24 together because they both involve similar physics principles, conservation of energy**. >> **Problem 21** has a spring with a constant of 200 newtons per meter that pushes a block along a flat surface. The spring is compressed 0.15 meters and the block travels 0.6 meters. >> Problem 21 has no slope at all, so it looks nothing like problem 24. >> Both problems are solved using **conservation of energy**, which tracks how energy changes form while the total stays the same. || The same problem, problem 24, ends up in different groups. >> The novice links it by appearance. >> The expert links it by the principle that solves it. || The three problems in the figure make the contrast concrete. Problem 23 and problem 24 both show a block on an inclined plane, while problem 21 shows a spring pushing a block across a flat surface. >> The novice paired problems 23 and 24 because they look alike. >> The expert paired problems 21 and 24 because both are solved with conservation of energy, even though they look different. || The expert looks past the pictures to the principle. This deeper representation lets the expert choose the right solution method directly, without being misled by surface similarity.
22|**Experts spend more time analyzing a problem**. >> This gives them a **slower start**, **but this strategy usually pays off**. >> Time spent understanding the problem leads to a faster and more accurate solution overall. || **Experts are no better than novices when given problems outside their field**. >> Expertise is tied to a specific domain. >> A chess master has no special advantage in physics, and a physicist has no special advantage in chess. || **Being an expert is not always an advantage**. **Experts are less likely to be open to new ways of looking at problems**. >> **Being an expert may be a disadvantage when confronting a problem that requires flexible thinking**. >> These are **problems whose solution may involve rejecting the usual procedures in favor of other procedures that are less frequently used**. >> An expert’s well-practiced methods can act like a mental set, making it harder to see a new approach. || Expert analysis pays off because a good representation speeds everything that follows. By spending time understanding the problem first, experts choose the right method and avoid false starts. >> Novices often jump to calculations based on surface features. >> Experts first identify the principle, then apply it efficiently. || Expertise has limits. Outside their field, experts have no special knowledge to draw on. Within their field, strong habits can make it hard to see an unusual solution, because the usual method comes to mind first and blocks alternatives.
23|**Creativity** has several aspects. >> **Innovative thinking**. >> **Novel ideas**. >> **New connections between existing ideas**. >> **Divergent thinking**, which is **open-ended** and produces **a large number of potential “solutions.”** Divergent thinking generates many possible answers, rather than narrowing down to one. || Creative problem solving is a **process**. **Most creative problem solving includes far more than just getting an idea**. >> **It involves a lengthy period of trial and error**. || The process has **stages**. >> **Problem generation**, which includes **problem finding** and **fact finding**. >> **Problem formulation**, which includes **problem definition** and **idea finding**. >> **Problem solving**, which includes **evaluation** and **planning**. >> **Solution implementation**, which is **taking action**. || Each stage does a different job. >> The early stages identify and define the problem. >> The later stages test ideas and put the best one into practice. || Creativity involves producing ideas that are new and useful. Innovative thinking, novel ideas and new connections between existing ideas all describe this kind of thinking. >> Divergent thinking generates many possible answers to one open-ended question. >> Generating many options increases the chance that one of them will be a good solution. || Creative problem solving unfolds in stages. Problem generation identifies what needs solving, problem formulation defines it and produces ideas, problem solving evaluates and plans, and solution implementation puts the plan into action. Trial and error runs throughout, as ideas are tested and revised.
24|**Generating ideas is only one part of the creative process**. >> **Without ideas, the process stops**. >> Ideas are necessary, but they also have to be evaluated, planned and carried out. || **Too much knowledge can sometimes hinder creative problem solving**. >> **Problems often require flexible thinking and rejecting accepted procedures**. >> Someone with deep knowledge of the usual way of doing things may find it hard to set that way aside. || Creative problem solving balances knowledge with flexibility. >> Knowledge supplies the material for new ideas. >> Flexibility lets the solver set aside familiar procedures when a problem calls for a different approach. || An idea is necessary to start the creative process, but it is only one stage. Ideas must be evaluated, developed and carried out before they produce a solution. >> Without ideas, nothing moves forward. >> With ideas alone, nothing is completed. || Knowledge can both help and hinder creativity. It supplies the raw material for new ideas, but it also supplies accepted procedures that can block new approaches. Creative problem solving often requires setting aside the familiar method to find a better one.
