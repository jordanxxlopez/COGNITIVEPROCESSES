2|**Decisions** are **the process of making choices between alternatives**. **Reasoning** is **the process of drawing conclusions**. >> Choosing which class to take or which route to drive is a decision. >> Working out what follows from the evidence we have is reasoning. || **Decisions are based on the judgments we make, and applying these judgments can involve various reasoning processes**. >> A **judgment** is an assessment of how likely or how good something is. >> Before deciding, we judge the options, and those judgments rely on reasoning. || **One of the primary mechanisms involved in making judgments is inductive reasoning**. >> **Inductive reasoning** is **reasoning based on observations**. >> It means **reaching conclusions from evidence**, moving from specific cases to a general conclusion. || Decisions and reasoning work together in everyday life. >> Reasoning produces a conclusion, such as which option is likely to turn out best. >> A decision uses that conclusion to choose what to do. >> Inductive reasoning supplies many of the judgments that decisions rely on, because it turns past observations into expectations about what will happen next. || Judgments sit between reasoning and decisions. Reasoning produces a judgment about how likely or how good each option is, and the decision then picks an option based on those judgments. >> A judgment that rain is likely leads to a decision to carry an umbrella. >> Errors in judgment carry forward into poor decisions, which is why the shortcuts used in judgment matter so much.
3|**Inductive reasoning is the basis of scientific investigations**. **In science, observations are made, data are collected, and conclusions are drawn**. >> Scientists observe many cases and then propose a general rule that fits them. || **In inductive reasoning, the conclusions we reach are probably, but not definitely, true**. >> **The conclusions reached are suggested with various degrees of certainty**. >> A new observation could always overturn a conclusion based on earlier observations. || The **strength of an inductive argument** depends on three factors. >> **Representativeness of the observations**, meaning how well the observations stand for the whole category. >> **Number of observations**, since more observations support a conclusion more strongly. >> **Quality of the observations**, since careful, accurate observations count for more. >> **Sometimes one very compelling, high-quality observation can carry a lot of weight**. || A strong inductive argument rests on observations that are representative, numerous and of high quality. >> A conclusion drawn from a small or unusual sample is weak, because it may not reflect the wider group. >> A conclusion drawn from many careful, representative observations is strong, and we can hold it with more confidence. >> Even a strong inductive conclusion stays open to revision, since a single clear observation that contradicts it can overturn it. || The three factors that set the strength of an inductive argument work together. A conclusion is strongest when it rests on many careful observations drawn from a representative sample. >> Many observations from a narrow, unrepresentative group can still lead to a wrong conclusion. >> A few careful observations from a well-chosen sample can support a strong conclusion. || The point about one compelling observation follows from the nature of induction. Because inductive conclusions are general claims built from specific cases, a single clear counterexample can show that the general claim is false, no matter how many supporting cases came before it.
4|**Inductive reasoning is used to make scientific discoveries**, by forming **hypotheses and general conclusions** from observations. || **Inductive reasoning is also used in everyday life**. >> We **make a prediction about what will happen based on observations of what has happened in the past**. >> **We make predictions and choices based on past experience, especially when predictions are based on familiar situations**. || Inductive reasoning **provides a mechanism for using past experience to guide present behavior**. >> **Some types of inductive reasoning happen automatically**, without deliberate thought. >> Many of our everyday expectations come from this kind of quick, automatic induction. || Inductive reasoning links science and everyday life. >> A scientist observes many cases and proposes a general rule. >> A person in daily life does the same thing on a smaller scale, expecting the future to follow the pattern of the past. >> Because so much of this reasoning happens automatically, it shapes our expectations and choices constantly, often without our noticing. || Everyday induction usually works well because the world is fairly regular. Patterns that held in the past often continue to hold, so expecting them to continue is usually a good bet. >> Familiar situations provide many past observations, which makes predictions based on them reliable. >> Unfamiliar situations provide few observations, which makes predictions less reliable. || Automatic induction saves time and effort. We do not stop to reason through every expectation, because past experience shapes our predictions without conscious thought. The same speed that makes automatic induction useful also makes it open to the systematic errors that heuristics produce.
5|**When people use past experience to guide present behavior, they often use shortcuts to help them reach conclusions rapidly**. These shortcuts are called **heuristics**. >> **Heuristics** are **“rules of thumb” that are likely to provide the correct answer to a problem, but are not foolproof**. >> They save time and effort, and they usually work. >> Sometimes they lead to systematic errors in judgment. || **Two commonly used heuristics are the availability heuristic and the representativeness heuristic**. >> The **availability heuristic** judges probability by how easily examples come to mind. >> The **representativeness heuristic** judges probability by how much something resembles a typical case. || Heuristics trade accuracy for speed. >> Working through every possibility carefully would take a long time. >> A heuristic reaches a quick answer using a simple cue, such as how easily examples come to mind or how closely something resembles a category. >> The cost is that the cue sometimes points in the wrong direction, which produces predictable errors in judgment. || Heuristics are useful because full analysis is slow and often impossible. Most everyday judgments have to be made quickly, with limited information, so a fast rule of thumb is a practical tool. >> A heuristic reaches an answer in a fraction of the time careful reasoning would take. >> The answer is usually close to right, which is good enough for most purposes. || The cost of heuristics appears in systematic errors. Because each heuristic relies on a particular cue, it fails in a predictable way whenever that cue points away from the truth. Studying these failures reveals how the heuristics work.
6|The **availability heuristic** is the tendency for **events that are more easily remembered to be judged as more probable than events that are less easily remembered**. >> **Availability is used to judge the probability of events**. >> **We use availability by trying to call examples to mind**. >> **The more examples we can think of, the more probable we judge the event to be**. || **The problem lies in the factors that make events stand out in memory**. >> **Distinctiveness**, since unusual events are easier to remember. >> **Emotional salience**, since events that stir strong emotions stick in memory. >> **Frequency of encoding and retrieval**, since events we hear about or think about often are easier to recall. || These factors affect how easily events come to mind, apart from how often they actually happen. >> When ease of recall and actual frequency differ, the availability heuristic leads to errors. || The availability heuristic works well when the examples that come to mind reflect how often events really happen. >> Common events usually are easy to remember, because we encounter them often. >> The heuristic fails when something other than frequency makes an event memorable, such as drama, emotion or heavy news coverage. >> In those cases, a rare event can feel common simply because it is easy to recall. || The availability heuristic relies on a reasonable connection. Events that happen often usually leave many memories, so ease of recall usually tracks frequency. >> Frequent events are encountered often, so examples come to mind easily. >> Rare events are encountered seldom, so examples are hard to recall. || The connection breaks when memorability comes from something other than frequency. Distinctive, emotional or heavily reported events are easy to recall even when they are rare, so the availability heuristic makes them seem more common than they are.
7|The availability heuristic shapes judgments about words. **Which are more common in English, words that begin with the letter r or words in which r is the third letter?** >> **Seventy percent of people respond that more words begin with r**. >> **In reality, three times more words have r in the third position**. || The explanation is availability. >> It is easy to call to mind words that begin with r, because we can search memory by first letter. >> Words with r in the third position are much harder to retrieve that way. >> The words that come to mind easily seem more common, even though they are much less common. || The question asks about the true size of each category, but people answer by how easily they can retrieve examples. >> Memory is organized so that first letters make good retrieval cues. >> Third letters make poor retrieval cues, so examples of that category are hard to produce even though there are many more of them. || The availability heuristic produces a clear error in the letter-r question. People judge how common each kind of word is by how easily examples come to mind, but the ease of recall depends on how memory is searched. >> Memory is organized so that words can be searched by their first letter. >> No such search works for third letters, so those words are hard to retrieve even though they are far more numerous. || Seventy percent of people pick the wrong answer, even though words with r in the third position are about three times as common. The error comes entirely from the way retrieval works, not from any lack of knowledge about English words.

9|The **representativeness heuristic** is the tendency for **people to make judgments based on how much one event resembles another event**. >> **The probability that A is a member of class B is judged by how well the properties of A resemble the properties normally associated with class B**. || **America has about 2.2 million farms and 120,000 libraries**. >> **We randomly pick one male from the population of the US**. **That male, Robert, wears glasses, speaks quietly and reads a lot**. **Is it more likely that Robert is a librarian or a farmer?** || **More people predict that Robert is a librarian**. >> Robert’s description matches the common image of a librarian. >> In doing so, people **ignore the base rate of farmers and librarians in the US**. >> A **base rate** is how common a category is in the population. With about 18 farms for every library, a randomly chosen man is much more likely to be a farmer, even with Robert’s description. || The representativeness heuristic judges probability by similarity. The more an individual resembles our image of a category, the more likely we think the individual belongs to it. >> Robert’s glasses, quiet speech and love of reading match a common image of a librarian. >> The match feels like strong evidence, so people choose librarian. || The base rate tells a different story. With about 2.2 million farms and 120,000 libraries, there are far more farmers than male librarians. Even if Robert’s traits are somewhat more common among librarians, the much larger number of farmers makes it more likely that a randomly chosen man is a farmer.
10|People follow a pattern in how they use base rates. >> They **use base rate information if it is all that is available**. >> They **use descriptive information if it is available and disregard base rate information**. || When only base rates are given, people use them correctly. >> **In a group of 100 people, there are 70 lawyers and 30 engineers**. **What is the chance that a person picked from the group at random will be an engineer?** >> **People get this right**. They answer 30%. || When a description is added, people ignore the base rates. >> **Jack is a 45-year-old man**. **He is married and has four children**. **He is generally conservative, careful and ambitious**. **He shows no interest in political and social issues and spends most of his free time on his many hobbies, which include home carpentry, sailing and mathematical puzzles**. >> **People say Jack is an engineer**. >> The description fits the image of an engineer, so people judge by resemblance and set aside the fact that lawyers outnumber engineers 70 to 30. || The lawyer–engineer study shows when base rates are used and when they are ignored. With only base rate information, people answer correctly. Once a description is added, people rely on the description and set the base rate aside. >> Without a description, people say there is a 30% chance of picking an engineer. >> With Jack’s description, people say Jack is an engineer, ignoring that 70% of the group are lawyers. || Jack’s description fits a stereotype of an engineer, with his careful personality and mathematical puzzles. The fit overwhelms the base rate, even though the description could easily belong to one of the 70 lawyers.
11|The **conjunction rule** states that **the probability of two events together cannot be higher than the probability of either single event alone**. >> A **conjunction** is two things being true at the same time. >> Everything in the combined category also belongs to each single category, so the combined category can never be larger. || **Linda is 31 years old, single, outspoken and very bright**. **She majored in philosophy**. **As a student, she was deeply concerned with issues of discrimination and social justice, and also participated in antinuclear demonstrations**. **Which of the following is more probable?** >> **1. Linda is a bank teller**. >> **2. Linda is a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement**. || **Eighty-two percent pick option 2**. This violates the conjunction rule. >> The diagram has a large circle for **bank tellers**, with a smaller circle inside it for **feminist bank tellers**. >> Every feminist bank teller is also a bank teller, so option 2 can never be more likely than option 1. >> People choose option 2 because Linda’s description resembles a feminist, which is the representativeness heuristic at work. || The conjunction rule follows from simple logic. Every bank teller who is a feminist is also a bank teller, so the group of feminist bank tellers is contained within the group of all bank tellers. >> The diagram draws feminist bank tellers as a small circle inside the larger circle of bank tellers. >> A smaller group inside a larger group can never be more likely than the larger group. || Linda’s description makes her sound like a feminist, so the feminist option feels more representative of her. Eighty-two percent let this sense of fit override the logic of the conjunction rule, which is a clear case of the representativeness heuristic producing an error.
12|**People also make errors in reasoning by ignoring the importance of the size of the sample on which observations are based**. >> **A certain town is served by two hospitals**. **In the larger hospital, about 45 babies are born each day, and in the smaller hospital, about 15 babies are born each day**. >> **About 50% of all babies are boys**. **However, the exact percentage varies from day to day**. **Sometimes it may be higher than 50%, sometimes lower**. >> **For a period of one year, each hospital recorded the days on which more than 60% of the babies born were boys**. **Which hospital recorded more such days?** || The choices were **1, the larger hospital**, **2, the smaller hospital**, and **3, about the same**. >> **Twenty-two percent chose the larger hospital**. >> **Twenty-two percent chose the smaller hospital**. >> **Fifty-six percent chose about the same**. || Most people judged that both hospitals would be equally likely to have unusual days. >> They focused on the 50% figure that applies to both hospitals. >> They overlooked the difference in how many babies each hospital delivers per day. || The hospital problem tests understanding of how sample size affects variability. Both hospitals have the same underlying rate of boys, about 50%, but they differ greatly in how many babies are born each day. >> The larger hospital has about 45 births a day. >> The smaller hospital has about 15 births a day. >> Both recorded days on which more than 60% of the babies were boys. || More than half of people judged the two hospitals to be about the same. They focused on the shared 50% rate and overlooked how the number of births affects how often the daily percentage strays from that rate.
13|For the **hospital problem**, **the correct answer is 2, the smaller hospital**. >> Only 22% of people chose this answer. || The key is **sample size**. >> With only 15 births a day, chance swings in the proportion of boys are large. Just a couple of extra boys can push the day above 60%. >> With 45 births a day, the proportion of boys stays closer to 50%, because random variations tend to even out across more births. >> The smaller hospital therefore records many more days with more than 60% boys. || **People make errors in reasoning by ignoring the importance of the size of the sample on which observations are based**. >> Small samples are much more likely than large samples to stray far from the true average. || Small samples vary more than large samples. >> A day with 15 births can easily include 10 boys by chance, which is more than 60%. >> A day with 45 births would need 28 or more boys to pass 60%, which happens much less often by chance. >> The answer depends on how much daily percentages swing, and smaller samples swing more. || Sample size controls how far a percentage can stray from the true value by chance. With few births, a small run of boys moves the percentage a lot. With many births, the same run is diluted by all the other births. >> On a 15-birth day, a few extra boys push the percentage well past 60%. >> On a 45-birth day, many more extra boys are needed to cross 60%, which happens less often. || The smaller hospital therefore records more days above 60% boys. People who chose about the same were applying representativeness, expecting every sample to look like the population, without considering how small samples swing more widely.
14|The **law of large numbers** states that **the larger the number of individuals randomly drawn from a population, the more representative the resulting group will be of the entire population**. >> A large random sample closely matches the population it comes from. >> A small random sample can differ from the population quite a lot, just by chance. || **People often assume that representativeness holds for small samples, and this results in errors in reasoning**. >> In the hospital problem, people expected a sample of 15 births to look as balanced as a sample of 45. >> Small samples often look unrepresentative, and the law of large numbers explains why. || The law of large numbers explains the hospital problem. >> The larger hospital’s daily births form a bigger sample, so its percentage of boys stays closer to 50%. >> The smaller hospital’s daily births form a smaller sample, so its percentage of boys swings more widely and more often passes 60%. >> Expecting a small sample to look just like the population is a common error, and it comes from applying representativeness where sample size matters. || The law of large numbers describes how samples behave as they grow. Large random samples closely match the population, while small random samples can differ from it by a lot. >> A large sample averages out chance variations. >> A small sample is easily pushed off by a few unusual cases. || People often expect small samples to be representative. This expectation produces errors whenever small samples are used to judge a population, as in the hospital problem, where a 15-birth day was expected to look just like the overall rate.
15|**Illusory correlations** occur when **a correlation appears to exist, but either does not exist or is much weaker than assumed**. >> A **correlation** is a relationship between two things, where one tends to go with the other. || **Illusory correlations can occur when we expect two things to be related, so we fool ourselves into thinking they are related even when they are not**. >> **Stereotypes** are **oversimplified generalizations about a group or class of people that often focus on the negative**. >> **A stereotype about a group may lead people to pay particular attention to behaviors associated with that stereotype**. >> **This attention creates an illusory correlation that reinforces the stereotype**. People notice and remember the cases that fit the stereotype and overlook the many cases that do not. || Illusory correlation is **related to the availability heuristic**. >> **Selective attention to stereotypical behaviors makes these behaviors more “available”** in memory. >> Because these examples come to mind easily, the stereotype seems more accurate than it is. || An illusory correlation is a relationship that people see in their observations even though the observations do not support it. Expectations shape what people notice, and what they notice shapes what they believe. >> Expecting two things to go together makes the cases that fit stand out. >> Cases that do not fit are overlooked or forgotten. || Stereotypes create and sustain illusory correlations. A person who holds a stereotype pays special attention to behaviors that fit it, remembers those behaviors easily, and then uses those easily recalled examples as evidence that the stereotype is true. This connects illusory correlation to the availability heuristic.
16|**Confirmation bias** shapes how people evaluate evidence. **Lord and coworkers (1979)** studied this with capital punishment. >> **They had people in favor of capital punishment and people against it read the same article**. >> The article presented evidence on both sides. || The **results** split by prior belief. >> **People in favor found the article to support their view**. >> **People against found the article to support their view**. >> The same article strengthened opposite beliefs. || This is the **“myside” bias**, the **tendency for people to generate and evaluate evidence and test their hypotheses in a way that is biased toward their own opinions and attitudes**. >> **Participants focused on information that agreed with their beliefs and disregarded information that did not**. || The study reveals how people treat evidence. >> Each side read exactly the same article. >> Each side came away more convinced of the view it already held. >> The same evidence was interpreted in opposite ways, depending on the reader’s starting belief. || Beliefs shape the evaluation of evidence. Both groups read the same article with the same information, yet each group came away more convinced of its own view. >> Supporters focused on the parts of the article that favored capital punishment. >> Opponents focused on the parts that argued against it. || The myside bias works by selective focus and selective weighting. People give more attention and more weight to evidence that agrees with them, and they find reasons to dismiss evidence that disagrees. As a result, mixed evidence strengthens existing beliefs on both sides.
17|**The myside bias is a type of confirmation bias**. **Confirmation bias** is the **tendency to selectively look for information that conforms to our hypothesis and overlook information that argues against it**. || A classic study used a number-sequence task. >> Participants were told they **would be given three numbers that conform to a simple rule the experimenter had in mind**. Their **aim was to discover the rule by writing down sets of three numbers along with their reasons for choosing them**. >> **After each set, the experimenter said whether the numbers conformed to the rule or not**. **When participants felt highly confident that they had discovered the rule, they were to write it down**. >> The starting set was **2, 4, 6**. >> **Participants then generated their own sets of three numbers**, and **for each set, they received feedback only on whether their numbers fit the rule**. || The **most common hypothesis** was **increasing intervals of two**. >> The **actual rule** was **three numbers in increasing order of magnitude**, so any rising sequence fit. >> Participants kept testing sets that matched their own hypothesis. Every one fit the real rule, so they grew confident in the wrong rule. >> Testing a set that broke their hypothesis would have revealed that the real rule was broader. || **Confirmation bias acts like a pair of blinders**. **We see the world according to rules we think are correct and are never dissuaded from this view, because we seek out only evidence that confirms our rule**. || The 2, 4, 6 task shows confirmation bias in a hypothesis-testing setting. Participants formed a hypothesis and then tested it only with examples that fit it. >> The common hypothesis was increasing intervals of two. >> Every test set built on that hypothesis also fit the real rule of increasing numbers. >> Positive feedback on every test made participants confident in the wrong rule. || The task is designed so that confirmation alone cannot reveal the answer. Only a test that could fail, a set that breaks the participant’s hypothesis, would show that the real rule is broader. Confirmation bias steers people away from such tests.
18|**Decision making** is **making judgments that involve choices between different courses of action**. || **Utility** refers to **outcomes that are desirable because they are in the person’s best interest**. >> An option with higher utility gives the person a better outcome. || **Expected utility theory** makes several claims. >> **People are rational**. >> **If they have all the relevant information, they will make the decision that results in the maximum expected utility**. **Expected utility** combines how good each outcome is with how likely it is. >> In its early form, utility was measured as **maximum monetary payoff**, so the best choice was the one that paid the most money on average. || Expected utility theory gives a clear rule for choosing. >> For each option, multiply the value of each possible outcome by its probability and add up the results. >> Choose the option with the highest total. >> A rational decision-maker following this rule always picks the option with the greatest expected benefit. || Expected utility theory gives a clear standard for good decisions. It assumes that people are rational and have the relevant information, and it predicts that they will choose the option with the highest expected utility. >> Expected utility weighs each outcome by how desirable it is and how likely it is. >> When outcomes are measured in money, the best choice is the one with the highest expected monetary payoff. || The theory serves as a benchmark. Real decisions can be compared with what the theory predicts, and the gaps reveal the other factors that influence how people actually choose.
19|The **utility approach** has an **advantage**. It offers **specific procedures for determining the “best choice.”** >> For any decision, utility theory gives a clear method for working out which option is best. || The utility approach also has **problems**. >> Utility is **not necessarily money**. **People find value in other things**, such as safety, fairness or how they feel about the choice. >> **Many decisions do not maximize the probability of the best outcome**. >> **People are not utility maximization machines**. || For example, **people gamble in casinos all the time, even though they know the odds are very much against them**. >> The expected monetary payoff of casino gambling is negative. >> People gamble anyway, because they value the excitement or the chance of a big win. || The utility approach is useful as a standard for what a fully rational choice would be. >> It is less successful at describing what people actually choose. >> Real decisions also depend on emotions, values beyond money and the way the options are presented. || The utility approach is strong as a standard but weak as a description. It tells us what a perfectly rational decision-maker would do, but people often choose differently. >> People value things other than money, such as excitement, safety and how they feel about a choice. >> People often fail to choose the option most likely to give the best outcome. || Casino gambling makes the gap plain. Gamblers know the odds favor the casino, so the expected monetary payoff is negative, yet they keep playing. Their choices reflect values and feelings that a money-based utility calculation leaves out.
20|In the **jellybean study**, **participants were given the opportunity to earn up to $7 by receiving $1 every time they drew a red jellybean**. **Each time, they were given a choice between two different bowls**. >> One bowl contained **1 red jellybean out of 10**, so **the odds were 1/10**. >> The other bowl contained **7 red jellybeans out of 100**, so **the odds were 7/100, or 0.7/10**. || The first bowl gives better odds of winning. >> One in 10 is 10%, while 7 in 100 is 7%. \[\frac{1}{10}=0.10,\qquad\frac{7}{100}=0.07\] >> Yet **most participants chose the 7/100 bowl**. || **Their choice was influenced by considerations other than probabilities**. >> The second bowl has more red jellybeans, seven instead of one, so it feels like it offers more chances to win. >> People attended to the number of winning beans rather than the proportion, which lowered their actual chance of winning. || The result reveals that people do not simply compare probabilities. >> The bowl with more red jellybeans looks more promising, even though it has many more white ones too. >> Attending to the number of winners rather than the proportion of winners leads people to pick the worse bowl. || In the jellybean study, people made a choice that goes against the probabilities. Participants could win $1 for each red jellybean they drew, up to $7, and they chose between a bowl with 1 red bean in 10 and a bowl with 7 red beans in 100. >> The first bowl offers a 10% chance of winning on each draw. >> The second bowl offers a 7% chance, which is worse. >> Most participants still chose the second bowl. || The second bowl simply looks like it has more chances to win, because it has seven red beans instead of one. People responded to the number of winning beans rather than the proportion, so their choice was driven by appearance rather than by probability.
21|**After 9/11, air travel decreased and driving increased**. >> After the attacks of September 11, 2001, many Americans chose to drive instead of fly. >> They did so **even though the probability of dying in a car accident is far greater than in flying**. || **The number of Americans who lost their lives on the road to avoid flying is higher than the total number of passengers killed on the four hijacked flights**. >> The decision to avoid flying felt safer. >> In fact, it exposed people to a greater risk, and more lives were lost as a result. || This choice reflects factors beyond probability. >> The vivid, frightening images of the hijackings made flying feel dangerous. >> The everyday risk of driving felt familiar and controllable, even though it was much larger. || The choice to drive was driven by fear and vivid memories of the attacks. >> Those memories made flying seem much riskier than it was. >> The familiar, everyday risk of driving received far less weight, so people moved into a more dangerous form of travel. || The shift from flying to driving after 9/11 reflects fear rather than calculation. 22|An **example dilemma** tests how people weigh lives. >> **A convoy of food trucks is on its way to a refugee camp during a famine**. **Airplanes cannot be used**. >> **You find that a second camp has even more refugees**. >> **If you tell the convoy to go to the second camp instead of the first, you will save 1,000 people from death, but 100 people in the first camp will die as a result**. >> **Would you send the convoy to the second camp?** || The utilities can be written out. >> **Not rerouting** saves the 100 people in the first camp but loses the 1,000 people in the second. \[U(\mathrm{not\ reroute})=100-1000=-900\] >> **Rerouting** saves the 1,000 people in the second camp but loses the 100 in the first. \[U(\mathrm{reroute})=1000-100=900\] >> Rerouting saves 900 more lives, so expected utility theory says to reroute. || **Ritov and Baron (1999)** found that **63% did not choose to send the convoy to the second camp**. >> **Twelve percent responded that they would not change the route of the convoy even if only 1 person in the first camp would die**. >> Many people refused to actively cause any deaths, even to save many more lives. They treated causing harm as different from failing to prevent it. || The convoy dilemma sets a clear calculation against a strong moral intuition. Rerouting saves 900 more lives than staying the course, yet most people chose not to reroute. >> Utility theory favors rerouting, since the outcome is far better. >> Many people resisted, because rerouting means actively causing the deaths in the first camp. || This resistance can be very strong. Twelve percent would not reroute even to save 1,000 people at the cost of a single life. For these people, causing harm through an action weighs more heavily than failing to prevent a much larger harm.
23|**Tetlock, Kristel, Elson, Green and Lerner (2000)** proposed the **sacred value protection model**. **People want to protect what they hold sacred**. >> A **sacred value** is a value treated as beyond price, such as human life, loyalty or justice. >> **Sacred values are kept in a separate compartment from secular values**. **Secular values** are ordinary values that can be traded, such as money and time. || **When the two domains are mixed together, people feel threatened and respond with moral outrage**. >> This is a **taboo** tradeoff, such as trading a human life for money. || **In contrast, when two sacred values are compared with each other, people are less morally outraged**. >> This is a **tragic** tradeoff, such as choosing between two lives. >> Both sides are sacred, so the choice is painful but not an insult to what people hold sacred. || The model predicts different emotional reactions to different kinds of tradeoffs. >> Putting a price on something sacred feels like a moral violation, so it produces outrage. >> Weighing one sacred value against another feels sad and difficult, but it does not violate the sacred domain in the same way. || The sacred value protection model explains why some tradeoffs feel acceptable and others feel offensive. The key is which kinds of values are being traded. >> Secular values, such as money and time, are routinely traded against each other. >> Sacred values, such as life and moral principles, are kept apart from secular values. || Mixing the two kinds triggers moral outrage. Putting a price on a sacred value treats it as just another commodity, which people experience as a moral violation. Trading one sacred value against another is painful but does not cross that line, so it produces less outrage.
24|**Tetlock and colleagues (2000)** used two scenarios to compare taboo and tragic tradeoffs. || In the **taboo tradeoff scenario**, **Robert can save the life of Johnny, a five-year-old who needs a liver transplant**. >> **The transplant procedure will cost the hospital $1,000,000 that could be spent in other ways, such as purchasing better equipment and raising salaries to recruit talented doctors to the hospital**. >> **Johnny is very ill and has been on the waiting list for a transplant, but because of the shortage of local organ donors, obtaining a liver will be expensive**. >> **Robert could save Johnny’s life, or he could use the $1,000,000 for other hospital needs**. >> The tradeoff sets a life, a sacred value, against money, a secular value. || In the **tragic tradeoff scenario**, **there are two children who need a liver transplant, but only one liver**. >> The tradeoff sets one life against another life. >> Both sides are sacred values. || The two scenarios hold the stakes constant on one side. >> In both, Johnny’s life is at risk. >> What changes is what his life is weighed against, money in the taboo scenario and another child’s life in the tragic scenario. || The two scenarios differ in what Johnny’s life is weighed against. In the taboo scenario, it is weighed against $1,000,000 for hospital equipment and salaries. In the tragic scenario, it is weighed against another child’s life. >> The taboo scenario mixes a sacred value, a life, with a secular value, money. >> The tragic scenario compares two sacred values, two lives. || Holding everything else constant lets the study isolate the effect of the tradeoff type. Any difference in how people react to Robert’s choice can be traced to whether a life was being traded for money or for another life.
