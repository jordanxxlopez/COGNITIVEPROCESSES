2|Two views of problem solving have come up so far. The Gestalt view treats it as **reorganization and insight**, while **Newell and Simon (1956)** treat it as **search** through the steps between posing a problem and solving it. >> Both are useful, and each captures something the other misses. || There is also **more to problem solving than laying out the problem space**, because **how a problem is stated affects how hard it is**. The mutilated checkerboard is the reminder, since the same problem became much easier when its wording pointed toward the key idea. >> That raises the question of how one problem can help us solve another.
3|**Using the solution to a similar problem can guide the solution to a new one.** The Russian marriage problem tells of a village with equal numbers of bachelors and unmarried women, all paired off into marriages. Then two bachelors die, and the question is whether the remaining people can all still be paired into marriages between a man and a woman. >> The answer is no, because there are now more women than men, so someone is always left over. || This story has exactly the same structure as the mutilated checkerboard. Each marriage pairs one man with one woman, just as each domino covers one dark and one light square. >> Removing two of the same kind leaves an imbalance that no pairing can fix.
4|The **mutilated checkerboard** comes back here as the problem to be solved by analogy. Two corners of the same color are missing, and the question is whether dominoes can still cover the board. >> Read next to the marriage story, the answer becomes much easier to see. || The marriage problem points straight at what matters, which is that every pair needs one of each kind. Once people see that the board is missing two squares of the same color, they know it cannot be covered.
5|**Analogical problem solving** is using the solution to a similar problem to guide the solution to a new one. The Russian marriage problem is the **source problem**, and the mutilated checkerboard is the **target problem**. >> The **target problem** is the one a person is trying to solve, and the **source problem** is another problem that shares some similarities with it. || **Analogical transfer** is the carryover from one problem to another. Learning about the Russian marriage problem makes people better at solving the mutilated checkerboard. >> The solution transfers because both problems are really about pairing up two kinds of things.
6|**Duncker's radiation problem** asks how a doctor can destroy a stomach tumor without surgery. A ray can destroy the tumor, but a ray strong enough to do that would also destroy the healthy tissue it passes through, while a weak ray harms nothing but also does nothing to the tumor. >> The question is how to use rays to destroy the tumor and still spare the healthy tissue. || **Only about one person in ten** solves it without help. The answer is to aim several weak rays from different directions so they meet at the tumor, where together they are strong enough.
7|The **fortress story** is a source problem for the radiation problem. A general wants to capture a fortress in the middle of a country, but every road to it is mined so that a large force would set off the mines, though small groups can pass safely. >> A full attack down one road would fail. || The general's solution is to split his army into small groups and send them down many roads at once, so they meet at the fortress together. That is the same solution the radiation problem needs, many weak forces converging on one target.
8|The pictures of the fortress and the body lay the two problems side by side. Small groups of soldiers come in from every direction to meet at the fortress, and weak rays come in from every direction to meet at the tumor. >> Seen together, the match between the two solutions is obvious. || The surface details of the two stories are completely different, armies and roads in one case and rays and organs in the other. What they share is the structure of the solution, not the setting.
9|In an experiment by **Gick and Holyoak**, after reading the fortress story, **about three times as many people** solved the radiation problem as without it, though most people still did not. They described three steps in analogical problem solving. **Noticing** an analogous relationship is the crucial and most difficult step. >> **Mapping** means matching the elements of the source problem to the elements of the target. >> **Applying** the mapping means using it to generate a parallel solution in the target. || **Most people were not able to see the correspondences** on their own. But when they were told to think about the story they had just read, the success rate rose to **about three out of four**. >> So the hard part is noticing the analogy, not using it once it is pointed out.
10|Some factors make the noticing and mapping steps easier. One reason noticing is hard is that people often focus on **surface features**, such as a fortress versus a tumor, which look nothing alike. >> Different surface features hide a shared solution. || That raises a natural question, whether making the **surface features similar** would help people notice the analogy. A source problem that looks like the target might be easier to connect to it.
11|The **lightbulb problem** has surface features much closer to the radiation problem. A research assistant finds that the filament inside an expensive, sealed lightbulb has broken, and a strong laser could fuse it back together. >> But a strong laser would also break the fragile glass, while a weak laser would not break the glass and would not fuse the filament either. || The question is how to use the laser to fix the filament without breaking the glass. This problem shares with the radiation problem both the structure and the look of a powerful beam aimed at something delicate.
12|When **Duncker's radiation problem** was used as the source, **most people** solved the lightbulb problem, while only about **one in ten** people in the control group solved it. The source helped far more here than the fortress story did. >> The radiation problem and the lightbulb problem look alike, since both involve beams and something fragile. || So **surface similarity helped** people notice and use the analogy. When a source looks like the target, people are much more likely to connect the two.
13|The lightbulb problem reveals that **high surface similarity helps analogical problem solving**, where surface features are the specific elements of a problem. But what about **structural features**, the underlying principle that governs the solution? In the radiation problem, a strong ray destroys healthy tissue, and in the lightbulb problem a strong laser breaks the glass. >> That shared structure is what makes the converging solution work in both. || To test structure, researchers made two versions of the lightbulb story with the same surface features but different structural features. Comparing them reveals whether matching structure matters beyond matching appearance.
14|In the **fragile glass version**, the source and target share structural features. A strong laser would break the glass, and a weak laser would not fuse the filament, so the assistant aims several weak lasers from different directions to meet at the filament. >> Each spot on the glass gets only a weak beam, so the glass stays intact while the filament gets the combined strength. || **About two thirds of people** who read this solution then solved the radiation problem. When the reason for the solution matched, the analogy transferred well.
15|In the **insufficient intensity version**, the source and target have different structural features. The laser simply was not strong enough on its own, so the assistant again aimed several weak lasers to meet at the filament. >> The solution looks the same, but the reason for it is different, since nothing fragile needed protecting. || **Only about a third of people** who read this version solved the radiation problem, about half as many as after the fragile glass version. Matching the underlying reason, not just the steps, is what made the analogy useful.
16|**Making structural features more obvious helps analogical problem solving.** Structural features are the underlying principles that govern the solution to a problem. >> When people see why a solution works, they can carry it to a new problem. || **Analogical encoding** is the process of comparing two problems and working out what they have in common. Having people compare source stories is an effective way to get them to pay attention to structural features. >> Comparing two examples pushes people past the surface to the shared principle.
17|There is an **analogical paradox**. It can be hard to get people to use analogies in the laboratory, yet people use analogies all the time in real life. >> Scientists, for example, use several analogies in a single lab meeting. || **In vivo problem-solving research** observes people solving problems in the real world. Its advantage is a natural setting, while its disadvantages are that it is time-consuming and cannot isolate and control variables. >> Real-world observation explains why analogy feels so natural even though it is hard to produce in a laboratory.
18|An **expert** is a person who has spent a great deal of time learning about a field and practicing and applying that learning, and who is acknowledged as very knowledgeable or skilled in it. A chess expert, for example, has spent many thousands of hours studying and playing chess. >> Expertise is built over a long time, not acquired quickly. || **Experts solve problems in their field faster and more successfully than beginners.** The interesting question is why they are better.
19|**Experts have more knowledge about their fields.** **Chase and Simon (1973)** compared how well a chess master and a beginner could reproduce the positions of pieces on a board after seeing it for just a few seconds. There were two conditions, with pieces arranged as in **real games** or arranged **randomly**. >> Experts did much better than beginners with real game positions. || The masters had stored real game positions in long-term memory and recognized them in **chunks**. In the random condition, no such chunks were available, so the masters lost their advantage. >> Expertise comes from stored, meaningful knowledge, not from a better memory in general.
20|Experts' knowledge is also **organized so it can be found when it is needed**. **Chi and colleagues (1982)** gave a set of physics problems to experts, who were physics professors, and to novices, who were students with one semester of physics, and asked both groups to sort the problems by similarity. >> Novices sorted by **surface features**, such as what the objects looked like. >> Experts sorted by **structural features**, the underlying physics principles. || The way knowledge is organized shapes what a person notices first. Experts see the deep principle, while novices see the objects on the surface.
21|In the experiment by **Chi and colleagues (1982)**, the sorting results make the difference concrete. A novice grouped two problems together because both involved similar objects, blocks sliding on inclined planes. >> To a novice, problems that look alike seem to belong together. || An expert grouped two different-looking problems together because both involved the same principle, conservation of energy. To an expert, problems that work the same way belong together, however different they look.
22|**Experts spend more time analyzing a problem before solving it.** That makes for a slower start, but the strategy usually pays off. >> Understanding the problem well up front prevents wasted effort later. || Experts are **no better than novices on problems outside their field**, and being an expert is not always an advantage. Experts can be **less open to new ways of looking at problems**, which is a disadvantage when a problem needs flexible thinking. >> Some problems require rejecting the usual procedure in favor of one that is rarely used.
23|**Creativity** involves innovative thinking, novel ideas and new connections between existing ideas. **Divergent thinking** is open-ended thinking that produces a large number of possible solutions. >> Creative problem solving starts by generating many options rather than one answer. || Most creative problem solving involves far more than just having an idea, since it usually includes a long period of trial and error. It moves through stages of **problem generation**, **problem formulation**, **problem solving** and **solution implementation**. >> Those stages run from finding and defining the problem, through evaluating and planning, to finally taking action.
24|**Generating ideas is only one part of the creative process.** Without ideas, though, the process stops, so idea generation is still essential. >> Every later stage depends on having options to work with. || **Too much knowledge can sometimes get in the way of creative problem solving.** Many problems require flexible thinking and a willingness to reject accepted procedures. >> This is the same trap that can make experts less open to new approaches.
