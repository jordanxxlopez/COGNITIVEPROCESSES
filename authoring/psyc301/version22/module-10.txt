2|A **problem** is an **obstacle between a present state and a goal**, where **the solution is not immediately obvious**. If the way to the goal were obvious, there would be nothing to solve. >> Both parts matter, a gap between where we are and where we want to be, and no clear path across it. || The sliding tile puzzle is a classic example. The scrambled board on the left is the present state, the ordered board on the right is the goal, and the challenge is figuring out which moves get from one to the other. >> Every move changes the board, but most moves do not obviously bring it closer to the goal.
3|According to **Koffka (1935)**, **Gestalt psychologists** were interested not only in perception but also in learning, problem solving and even attitudes and beliefs. They took a **perceptual approach** to all of these, treating thinking a lot like seeing. >> For them, solving a problem was about how the problem looks in the mind. || Problem solving for the Gestalt psychologists was about **representing the problem in the mind** and then **restructuring** that representation. Kohler's **circle problem** asks for the length of a line inside a circle when only the radius is known. >> Different people represent this problem differently, and most people's mental picture does not match the drawing. >> Once the line is seen as the diagonal of a rectangle whose other diagonal is a radius, the answer is suddenly obvious.
4|Solutions often come from **seeing the problem first and then representing it in a different way**, which is called **restructuring** the problem's representation. Gestalt psychologists linked restructuring with **insight**, the sudden realization of a problem's solution. >> Insight often comes from discovering a crucial element that was there all along. >> They thought solutions come to people all at once, as an **aha** experience. || Other researchers do not necessarily agree with this view. A lot of our problem solving does not involve any aha experience at all. >> Many problems are solved step by step, with no sudden moment of understanding.
5|**Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987)** designed an experiment to tell **insight problems** apart from **non-insight problems**. Insight problems included the triangle problem and the chain problem, while non-insight problems were algebra problems. >> The question was whether people can sense their own progress on each kind. || The hypothesis was that people solving insight problems should **not** be good at predicting how close they are to a solution, while people solving non-insight problems should be. A non-insight problem is worked through methodically, so progress can be felt. >> To measure this, people gave **warmth ratings** every few seconds, where a hot rating meant they felt close to the answer.
6|In the experiment by **Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987)**, the **triangle problem** asks how to move three of the dots so the triangle points down instead of up. The answer comes from seeing which dots can stay put, which is hard to reach step by step. >> Most people stare at it for a while and then see the answer all at once. || The **chain problem** gives four short pieces of chain and asks for one closed loop, with a cost for opening and for closing each link and a tight budget. The obvious plan of opening one link on every piece costs too much. >> The trick is to take one piece apart completely and use its links to join the other three.
7|In the experiment by **Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987)**, the warmth ratings revealed two very different patterns. **Insight problems were solved suddenly**, with ratings staying cold and flat until a big jump just before the solution. **Non-insight problems were solved gradually**, with warmth rising steadily as people approached the answer. >> People working on algebra could feel themselves getting closer. >> People working on insight problems felt far away right up until the moment they solved it. || This supports the Gestalt idea that insight really is a sudden restructuring. At least for some problems, the answer is not built up piece by piece.
8|Gestalt psychologists claimed that one of the major obstacles to problem solving is **fixation**, which is focusing on one particular feature of a problem in a way that keeps people from reaching a solution. **Functional fixedness** is a kind of fixation in which we restrict how we use an object to its familiar function. >> We see a tool only as what it is normally for. || In the **candle problem**, people must mount a candle on a corkboard so it burns without dripping wax on the floor, using a candle, matches and a box of tacks. Seeing the matchbox as a container for tacks keeps people from seeing that it can be tacked to the wall as a shelf. >> People given the same items with the box already **empty** were about twice as likely to solve the problem, because an empty box no longer looks like just a container.
9|The **two-string problem** is another case of functional fixedness. Two strings hang from the ceiling too far apart to reach both at once, and the task is to tie them together. >> The solution is to tie a heavy object to one string and swing it like a pendulum. || The usual function of the **pliers** in the room gets in the way. People see pliers as a tool for gripping, not as a weight, so they miss that pliers can turn a string into a pendulum. >> Fixing on an object's normal job hides the other jobs it could do.
10|In the two-string problem, when a hint was given by **accidentally setting a string in motion**, most people were able to solve it. In Gestalt terms, the solution came when people **restructured their representation** of the problem. >> Seeing the string swing changed how they pictured the strings. || A **mental set** is a preconceived notion about how to approach a problem, based on a person's past experience with that problem or similar ones. The **water-jug problem**, where people must measure out a required amount of water using three jugs of different sizes, is the classic example. >> A strategy that worked before can blind us to an easier one.
11|Each water-jug problem gives the sizes of three jugs and a target amount, and the task is to use the jugs to measure out exactly that target. The early problems can all be solved the same way, by filling the large jug, pouring off one medium jug and then pouring off the small jug twice. >> After a few problems, people settle into that routine. || Later problems can be solved in a much simpler way, using just two jugs. Yet people who had been given the earlier problems kept using the long method, because the **mental set** they had built up kept them from seeing the simpler solution. >> Experience helped at first and then got in the way.
12|**Newell and Simon (1956)** created the **Logic Theorist**, a computer program, and described problem solving as **a process that involves search**. Instead of looking only at the starting structure and the final structure, they focused on what happens in between. >> Problem solving became a search for a way to reach a goal and a way around roadblocks. || They described the **problem space**, which has three parts. The **initial state** is the conditions at the start, the **intermediate states** are the steps along the way, and the **goal state** is the solution. >> Solving a problem means finding a path through this space from start to goal.
13|**Operators** are the rules that specify which moves are allowed and which are not, and each move takes the problem from one state to another. The **Tower of Hanoi** has a stack of disks on the first peg, and the goal is to move the whole stack to the third peg. >> Its operators are that disks are moved one at a time and only the top disk can move. >> A larger disk can never be placed on top of a smaller one. || These simple rules make the problem space concrete. Every legal move leads to a new state, and the solver has to find a sequence of legal moves that ends at the goal. >> The rules are easy, but the right sequence is not obvious.
14|Solving the Tower of Hanoi with three disks takes a long sequence of moves, laid out step by step from the starting stack to the finished stack. Each picture shows one state, and each step follows the rules exactly. >> Even with three disks, the path is longer than most people expect. || The number of moves explodes as disks are added. With a very large stack of disks, solving the puzzle would take an unimaginably long time even at one correct move every second. >> A problem space can grow far too big to search through one possibility at a time.
15|The sliding tile puzzle can also be described as a problem space. The scrambled board is the **initial state**, the ordered board is the **goal state**, and every slide of a tile into the empty square is an **operator** that leads to a new state. >> Thinking of it this way turns a confusing puzzle into a search for a path between two states. || Each board position along the way is an intermediate state. The solver's job is to find the series of moves that connects the start to the goal.
16|Mapping out the tile puzzle's problem space produces a large branching tree. The starting board is at the top, each possible move creates a new branch, and the branches multiply level after level until one path finally reaches the goal. >> Only one route through the whole tree ends at the solution. || Most branches lead nowhere useful. That is why searching blindly through every possible move is so slow, and why good strategies for choosing moves matter so much. >> People need a way to cut the tree down rather than explore every branch.
17|According to **Newell and Simon (1956)**, we solve problems by **searching the problem space**. One powerful strategy is **means-end analysis**, which works by **reducing the difference between the current state and the goal state**. >> The solver keeps asking what is still different from the goal and how to shrink that difference. || Means-end analysis often uses **subgoals**, which are intermediate states that are closer to the goal. Breaking a hard problem into smaller steps makes each step easier to see. >> For the Tower of Hanoi, means-end analysis steadily reduces the difference between the starting stack and the goal stack.
18|Means-end analysis in the Tower of Hanoi works through a series of subgoals. The first subgoal is to **free up the largest disk** by moving the smaller disks off it, and the second is to **free up the third peg** so the largest disk has somewhere to go. >> The third subgoal is to **move the largest disk onto the third peg**. || Once the largest disk is in place, the remaining smaller disks can be moved on top of it. Each subgoal is easier than the whole problem, and reaching them one by one gets the whole stack to the goal.
19|One of the main contributions of the approach developed by **Newell and Simon (1956)** was **a way to specify all the possible paths from the initial state to the goal state**. But there is **more to problem solving than specifying the problem space**. >> Two problems with the same structure can feel very different. || **How a problem is stated can affect how hard it is.** The **mutilated checkerboard problem** reveals this effect clearly. >> The way the information is presented can point people toward the solution or away from it.
20|A full checkerboard can be completely covered with dominoes, each one covering two squares side by side. The **mutilated checkerboard problem** removes two opposite corners and asks whether the remaining squares can still be covered with dominoes. >> Most people start trying to fit dominoes and quickly get lost. || Searching through every arrangement would take forever. The problem only becomes easy when it is represented in the right way. >> The key lies in noticing something about the two missing corners.
21|The key to the mutilated checkerboard is understanding that **each domino covers two squares** and that **those two squares must be different colors**. The two removed corners are the same color, so the board is left with more squares of one color than the other, and the dominoes cannot cover it. >> Once that is seen, no search is needed at all. || The conditions differed in how much information they gave about the squares. Some people got a **blank board**, some a **colored board**, some a board with the words **black** and **pink**, and some a board with the words **bread** and **butter**. >> The problem was **easier to solve when the information pointed toward the right representation**. >> The bread and butter version stressed that the squares come in pairs, and people solved it about twice as fast as the blank version.
22|The **think-aloud protocol** asks people to say aloud what they are thinking while solving a problem. They are told not to describe what they are doing, but to put new thoughts into words. >> The goal is to find out what information people are attending to while they work. || Think-aloud reports revealed a **shift in how people perceive the elements of a problem**. People solved the checkerboard problem after realizing that the words **bread** and **butter** were important, because those words made them think about pairs. >> This is very similar to the Gestalt idea of a sudden shift in representation.
