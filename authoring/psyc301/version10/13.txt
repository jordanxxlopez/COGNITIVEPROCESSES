2|**Decisions** are **the process of making choices between alternatives**. **Reasoning** is **the process of drawing conclusions**. >> Choosing which class to take or which route to drive is a decision. >> Working out what follows from the evidence we have is reasoning. || **Decisions are based on the judgments we make, and applying these judgments can involve various reasoning processes**. >> A **judgment** is an assessment of how likely or how good something is. >> Before deciding, we judge the options, and those judgments rely on reasoning. || **One of the primary mechanisms involved in making judgments is inductive reasoning**. >> **Inductive reasoning** is **reasoning based on observations**. >> It means **reaching conclusions from evidence**, moving from specific cases to a general conclusion.
3|**Inductive reasoning is the basis of scientific investigations**. **In science, observations are made, data are collected, and conclusions are drawn**. >> Scientists observe many cases and then propose a general rule that fits them. || **In inductive reasoning, the conclusions we reach are probably, but not definitely, true**. >> **The conclusions reached are suggested with various degrees of certainty**. >> A new observation could always overturn a conclusion based on earlier observations. || The **strength of an inductive argument** depends on three factors. >> **Representativeness of the observations**, meaning how well the observations stand for the whole category. >> **Number of observations**, since more observations support a conclusion more strongly. >> **Quality of the observations**, since careful, accurate observations count for more. >> **Sometimes one very compelling, high-quality observation can carry a lot of weight**.
4|**Inductive reasoning is used to make scientific discoveries**, by forming **hypotheses and general conclusions** from observations. || **Inductive reasoning is also used in everyday life**. >> We **make a prediction about what will happen based on observations of what has happened in the past**. >> **We make predictions and choices based on past experience, especially when predictions are based on familiar situations**. || Inductive reasoning **provides a mechanism for using past experience to guide present behavior**. >> **Some types of inductive reasoning happen automatically**, without deliberate thought. >> Many of our everyday expectations come from this kind of quick, automatic induction.
5|**When people use past experience to guide present behavior, they often use shortcuts to help them reach conclusions rapidly**. These shortcuts are called **heuristics**. >> **Heuristics** are **“rules of thumb” that are likely to provide the correct answer to a problem, but are not foolproof**. >> They save time and effort, and they usually work. >> Sometimes they lead to systematic errors in judgment. || **Two commonly used heuristics are the availability heuristic and the representativeness heuristic**. >> The **availability heuristic** judges probability by how easily examples come to mind. >> The **representativeness heuristic** judges probability by how much something resembles a typical case.
6|The **availability heuristic** is the tendency for **events that are more easily remembered to be judged as more probable than events that are less easily remembered**. >> **Availability is used to judge the probability of events**. >> **We use availability by trying to call examples to mind**. >> **The more examples we can think of, the more probable we judge the event to be**. || **The problem lies in the factors that make events stand out in memory**. >> **Distinctiveness**, since unusual events are easier to remember. >> **Emotional salience**, since events that stir strong emotions stick in memory. >> **Frequency of encoding and retrieval**, since events we hear about or think about often are easier to recall. || These factors affect how easily events come to mind, apart from how often they actually happen. >> When ease of recall and actual frequency differ, the availability heuristic leads to errors.
7|The availability heuristic shapes judgments about words. **Which are more common in English, words that begin with the letter r or words in which r is the third letter?** >> **Seventy percent of people respond that more words begin with r**. >> **In reality, three times more words have r in the third position**. || The explanation is availability. >> It is easy to call to mind words that begin with r, because we can search memory by first letter. >> Words with r in the third position are much harder to retrieve that way. >> The words that come to mind easily seem more common, even though they are much less common.
8|The availability heuristic also shapes judgments about **causes of death**. People were asked which cause in each pair was more likely to kill someone in the United States in the next year. The table lists the **more likely** cause, how many times more likely it is, and the **percentage of people who picked the less likely cause**. >> **Homicide** is 20 times more likely than **appendicitis**, and 9% picked appendicitis. >> **Drowning** is 5 times more likely than an **auto–train collision**, and 34% picked the collision. >> **Asthma** is 920 times more likely than **botulism**, and 41% picked botulism. >> **Asthma** is 20 times more likely than a **tornado**, and 58% picked the tornado. >> **Appendicitis** is 2 times more likely than **pregnancy**, and 83% picked pregnancy. || **These errors are due to the availability of the causes**. >> **When people die of botulism, it is front-page news**. >> **Deaths from asthma go virtually unnoticed**. >> Dramatic, rare causes get heavy news coverage, so examples come to mind easily. Common, quiet causes get little attention, so they seem less likely than they are.
9|The **representativeness heuristic** is the tendency for **people to make judgments based on how much one event resembles another event**. >> **The probability that A is a member of class B is judged by how well the properties of A resemble the properties normally associated with class B**. || **America has about 2.2 million farms and 120,000 libraries**. >> **We randomly pick one male from the population of the US**. **That male, Robert, wears glasses, speaks quietly and reads a lot**. **Is it more likely that Robert is a librarian or a farmer?** || **More people predict that Robert is a librarian**. >> Robert’s description matches the common image of a librarian. >> In doing so, people **ignore the base rate of farmers and librarians in the US**. >> A **base rate** is how common a category is in the population. With about 18 farms for every library, a randomly chosen man is much more likely to be a farmer, even with Robert’s description.
10|People follow a pattern in how they use base rates. >> They **use base rate information if it is all that is available**. >> They **use descriptive information if it is available and disregard base rate information**. || When only base rates are given, people use them correctly. >> **In a group of 100 people, there are 70 lawyers and 30 engineers**. **What is the chance that a person picked from the group at random will be an engineer?** >> **People get this right**. They answer 30%. || When a description is added, people ignore the base rates. >> **Jack is a 45-year-old man**. **He is married and has four children**. **He is generally conservative, careful and ambitious**. **He shows no interest in political and social issues and spends most of his free time on his many hobbies, which include home carpentry, sailing and mathematical puzzles**. >> **People say Jack is an engineer**. >> The description fits the image of an engineer, so people judge by resemblance and set aside the fact that lawyers outnumber engineers 70 to 30.
11|The **conjunction rule** states that **the probability of two events together cannot be higher than the probability of either single event alone**. >> A **conjunction** is two things being true at the same time. >> Everything in the combined category also belongs to each single category, so the combined category can never be larger. || **Linda is 31 years old, single, outspoken and very bright**. **She majored in philosophy**. **As a student, she was deeply concerned with issues of discrimination and social justice, and also participated in antinuclear demonstrations**. **Which of the following is more probable?** >> **1. Linda is a bank teller**. >> **2. Linda is a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement**. || **Eighty-two percent pick option 2**. This violates the conjunction rule. >> The diagram has a large circle for **bank tellers**, with a smaller circle inside it for **feminist bank tellers**. >> Every feminist bank teller is also a bank teller, so option 2 can never be more likely than option 1. >> People choose option 2 because Linda’s description resembles a feminist, which is the representativeness heuristic at work.
12|**People also make errors in reasoning by ignoring the importance of the size of the sample on which observations are based**. >> **A certain town is served by two hospitals**. **In the larger hospital, about 45 babies are born each day, and in the smaller hospital, about 15 babies are born each day**. >> **About 50% of all babies are boys**. **However, the exact percentage varies from day to day**. **Sometimes it may be higher than 50%, sometimes lower**. >> **For a period of one year, each hospital recorded the days on which more than 60% of the babies born were boys**. **Which hospital recorded more such days?** || The choices were **1, the larger hospital**, **2, the smaller hospital**, and **3, about the same**. >> **Twenty-two percent chose the larger hospital**. >> **Twenty-two percent chose the smaller hospital**. >> **Fifty-six percent chose about the same**. || Most people judged that both hospitals would be equally likely to have unusual days. >> They focused on the 50% figure that applies to both hospitals. >> They overlooked the difference in how many babies each hospital delivers per day.
13|For the **hospital problem**, **the correct answer is 2, the smaller hospital**. >> Only 22% of people chose this answer. || The key is **sample size**. >> With only 15 births a day, chance swings in the proportion of boys are large. Just a couple of extra boys can push the day above 60%. >> With 45 births a day, the proportion of boys stays closer to 50%, because random variations tend to even out across more births. >> The smaller hospital therefore records many more days with more than 60% boys. || **People make errors in reasoning by ignoring the importance of the size of the sample on which observations are based**. >> Small samples are much more likely than large samples to stray far from the true average.
14|The **law of large numbers** states that **the larger the number of individuals randomly drawn from a population, the more representative the resulting group will be of the entire population**. >> A large random sample closely matches the population it comes from. >> A small random sample can differ from the population quite a lot, just by chance. || **People often assume that representativeness holds for small samples, and this results in errors in reasoning**. >> In the hospital problem, people expected a sample of 15 births to look as balanced as a sample of 45. >> Small samples often look unrepresentative, and the law of large numbers explains why.
15|**Illusory correlations** occur when **a correlation appears to exist, but either does not exist or is much weaker than assumed**. >> A **correlation** is a relationship between two things, where one tends to go with the other. || **Illusory correlations can occur when we expect two things to be related, so we fool ourselves into thinking they are related even when they are not**. >> **Stereotypes** are **oversimplified generalizations about a group or class of people that often focus on the negative**. >> **A stereotype about a group may lead people to pay particular attention to behaviors associated with that stereotype**. >> **This attention creates an illusory correlation that reinforces the stereotype**. People notice and remember the cases that fit the stereotype and overlook the many cases that do not. || Illusory correlation is **related to the availability heuristic**. >> **Selective attention to stereotypical behaviors makes these behaviors more “available”** in memory. >> Because these examples come to mind easily, the stereotype seems more accurate than it is.
16|**Confirmation bias** shapes how people evaluate evidence. **Lord and coworkers (1979)** studied this with capital punishment. >> **They had people in favor of capital punishment and people against it read the same article**. >> The article presented evidence on both sides. || The **results** split by prior belief. >> **People in favor found the article to support their view**. >> **People against found the article to support their view**. >> The same article strengthened opposite beliefs. || This is the **“myside” bias**, the **tendency for people to generate and evaluate evidence and test their hypotheses in a way that is biased toward their own opinions and attitudes**. >> **Participants focused on information that agreed with their beliefs and disregarded information that did not**.
17|**The myside bias is a type of confirmation bias**. **Confirmation bias** is the **tendency to selectively look for information that conforms to our hypothesis and overlook information that argues against it**. || A classic study used a number-sequence task. >> Participants were told they **would be given three numbers that conform to a simple rule the experimenter had in mind**. Their **aim was to discover the rule by writing down sets of three numbers along with their reasons for choosing them**. >> **After each set, the experimenter said whether the numbers conformed to the rule or not**. **When participants felt highly confident that they had discovered the rule, they were to write it down**. >> The starting set was **2, 4, 6**. >> **Participants then generated their own sets of three numbers**, and **for each set, they received feedback only on whether their numbers fit the rule**. || The **most common hypothesis** was **increasing intervals of two**. >> The **actual rule** was **three numbers in increasing order of magnitude**, so any rising sequence fit. >> Participants kept testing sets that matched their own hypothesis. Every one fit the real rule, so they grew confident in the wrong rule. >> Testing a set that broke their hypothesis would have revealed that the real rule was broader. || **Confirmation bias acts like a pair of blinders**. **We see the world according to rules we think are correct and are never dissuaded from this view, because we seek out only evidence that confirms our rule**.
18|**Decision making** is **making judgments that involve choices between different courses of action**. || **Utility** refers to **outcomes that are desirable because they are in the person’s best interest**. >> An option with higher utility gives the person a better outcome. || **Expected utility theory** makes several claims. >> **People are rational**. >> **If they have all the relevant information, they will make the decision that results in the maximum expected utility**. **Expected utility** combines how good each outcome is with how likely it is. >> In its early form, utility was measured as **maximum monetary payoff**, so the best choice was the one that paid the most money on average.
19|The **utility approach** has an **advantage**. It offers **specific procedures for determining the “best choice.”** >> For any decision, utility theory gives a clear method for working out which option is best. || The utility approach also has **problems**. >> Utility is **not necessarily money**. **People find value in other things**, such as safety, fairness or how they feel about the choice. >> **Many decisions do not maximize the probability of the best outcome**. >> **People are not utility maximization machines**. || For example, **people gamble in casinos all the time, even though they know the odds are very much against them**. >> The expected monetary payoff of casino gambling is negative. >> People gamble anyway, because they value the excitement or the chance of a big win.
20|In the **jellybean study**, **participants were given the opportunity to earn up to $7 by receiving $1 every time they drew a red jellybean**. **Each time, they were given a choice between two different bowls**. >> One bowl contained **1 red jellybean out of 10**, so **the odds were 1/10**. >> The other bowl contained **7 red jellybeans out of 100**, so **the odds were 7/100, or 0.7/10**. || The first bowl gives better odds of winning. >> One in 10 is 10%, while 7 in 100 is 7%. \[\frac{1}{10}=0.10,\qquad\frac{7}{100}=0.07\] >> Yet **most participants chose the 7/100 bowl**. || **Their choice was influenced by considerations other than probabilities**. >> The second bowl has more red jellybeans, seven instead of one, so it feels like it offers more chances to win. >> People attended to the number of winning beans rather than the proportion, which lowered their actual chance of winning.
21|**After 9/11, air travel decreased and driving increased**. >> After the attacks of September 11, 2001, many Americans chose to drive instead of fly. >> They did so **even though the probability of dying in a car accident is far greater than in flying**. || **The number of Americans who lost their lives on the road to avoid flying is higher than the total number of passengers killed on the four hijacked flights**. >> The decision to avoid flying felt safer. >> In fact, it exposed people to a greater risk, and more lives were lost as a result. || This choice reflects factors beyond probability. >> The vivid, frightening images of the hijackings made flying feel dangerous. >> The everyday risk of driving felt familiar and controllable, even though it was much larger.
22|An **example dilemma** tests how people weigh lives. >> **A convoy of food trucks is on its way to a refugee camp during a famine**. **Airplanes cannot be used**. >> **You find that a second camp has even more refugees**. >> **If you tell the convoy to go to the second camp instead of the first, you will save 1,000 people from death, but 100 people in the first camp will die as a result**. >> **Would you send the convoy to the second camp?** || The utilities can be written out. >> **Not rerouting** saves the 100 people in the first camp but loses the 1,000 people in the second. \[U(\mathrm{not\ reroute})=100-1000=-900\] >> **Rerouting** saves the 1,000 people in the second camp but loses the 100 in the first. \[U(\mathrm{reroute})=1000-100=900\] >> Rerouting saves 900 more lives, so expected utility theory says to reroute. || **Ritov and Baron (1999)** found that **63% did not choose to send the convoy to the second camp**. >> **Twelve percent responded that they would not change the route of the convoy even if only 1 person in the first camp would die**. >> Many people refused to actively cause any deaths, even to save many more lives. They treated causing harm as different from failing to prevent it.
23|**Tetlock, Kristel, Elson, Green and Lerner (2000)** proposed the **sacred value protection model**. **People want to protect what they hold sacred**. >> A **sacred value** is a value treated as beyond price, such as human life, loyalty or justice. >> **Sacred values are kept in a separate compartment from secular values**. **Secular values** are ordinary values that can be traded, such as money and time. || **When the two domains are mixed together, people feel threatened and respond with moral outrage**. >> This is a **taboo** tradeoff, such as trading a human life for money. || **In contrast, when two sacred values are compared with each other, people are less morally outraged**. >> This is a **tragic** tradeoff, such as choosing between two lives. >> Both sides are sacred, so the choice is painful but not an insult to what people hold sacred.
24|**Tetlock and colleagues (2000)** used two scenarios to compare taboo and tragic tradeoffs. || In the **taboo tradeoff scenario**, **Robert can save the life of Johnny, a five-year-old who needs a liver transplant**. >> **The transplant procedure will cost the hospital $1,000,000 that could be spent in other ways, such as purchasing better equipment and raising salaries to recruit talented doctors to the hospital**. >> **Johnny is very ill and has been on the waiting list for a transplant, but because of the shortage of local organ donors, obtaining a liver will be expensive**. >> **Robert could save Johnny’s life, or he could use the $1,000,000 for other hospital needs**. >> The tradeoff sets a life, a sacred value, against money, a secular value. || In the **tragic tradeoff scenario**, **there are two children who need a liver transplant, but only one liver**. >> The tradeoff sets one life against another life. >> Both sides are sacred values.
25|The results of **Tetlock and colleagues (2000)** plot **moral outrage** toward Robert, from 0 to 6, for each **tradeoff type**, split by whether Robert **did not save Johnny** or **saved Johnny**. || In the **taboo** tradeoff, which set Johnny’s life against money, people reacted strongly to Robert’s choice. >> When Robert **did not save Johnny**, moral outrage was very high, about **5.4**. >> When Robert **saved Johnny**, moral outrage was much lower, about **2.5**. >> Choosing money over a child’s life provoked strong outrage. || In the **tragic** tradeoff, which set one child’s life against another, Robert’s choice mattered very little. >> Moral outrage was about **3.7** when Robert did not save Johnny and about **3.8** when he saved Johnny. >> Since both options involved saving a life, neither choice was seen as a violation of sacred values. || Moral outrage depends on whether a sacred value is traded for a secular one. >> Trading a life for money is taboo and provokes outrage. >> Choosing between two lives is tragic, and people judge it less harshly.
26|**Ginges, Atran, Medin and Shikaki (2007)** **extended the earlier findings to a more realistic setting**. >> They **interviewed Israeli settlers**, with **601** participants. >> They interviewed **Palestinian refugees**, with **535** participants. >> They interviewed **Palestinian students affiliated with various political groups**, with **719** participants. || These groups were directly involved in the Israeli–Palestinian conflict. >> The issues under dispute, such as land and the status of Jerusalem, are deeply important to them. || The researchers **offered three types of tradeoff deals as possible solutions to the conflict**. >> Each deal asked participants to give up something they valued in exchange for something else. >> Comparing reactions to the different deals tests how sacred values shape responses to compromise.
27|**Ginges and colleagues (2007)** used three kinds of deals. The examples are deals presented to Palestinian participants. || The **taboo** deal. **Palestinians would recognize the sacred and historic right of the Jewish people to Israel**. **There would be two states, a Jewish state of Israel and a Palestinian state in 99% of the West Bank and Gaza**. >> This deal asks Palestinians to give up a claim they may hold sacred. || The **taboo-plus** deal adds money to the taboo deal. **On their part, Israel will pay Palestine 1 billion US dollars a year for 10 years**. >> This adds a large material incentive to the same compromise. || The **symbolic** deal adds a symbolic concession instead. **On their part, Israel will recognize the historic and legitimate right of the Palestinians to their own state and would apologize for all of the wrongs done to the Palestinian people**. >> This offers recognition and an apology, which are symbolic rather than material. >> It treats the tradeoff as one sacred value against another, which makes it a tragic tradeoff rather than a taboo one.
28|Ginges and colleagues used a **sacred value measure** to divide participants into two groups. || **Moral absolutists** **transformed the issues under dispute into sacred values**. >> **Among settlers, 46% of the sample** were moral absolutists. They **believed it was never permissible for the Jewish people to “give up” part of the “Land of Israel,” no matter how great the benefit**. >> **Among Palestinian students, 54%** were moral absolutists. They **treated both the principle of the “right of return” and Jerusalem as sacred values**. The **right of return** is the claim that Palestinian refugees and their descendants may return to the land they left. || **Non-absolutists** **had strong preferences against compromise over these issues but did not regard them as sacred values**. >> They opposed giving up these things, but for them the issues could in principle be traded.
29|The results of **Ginges and colleagues (2007)** compare the deals. || **Taboo versus taboo-plus** compares the same compromise with and without money. >> Panel (a) plots **anger and disgust**. For **non-absolutists**, anger and disgust **fell slightly** when money was added, to just below zero. For **moral absolutists**, anger and disgust **rose** when money was added, from about 0.1 to about 0.27. >> Panel (b) plots **percent support for violence**. For non-absolutists, support for violence **fell** with money, to about −5. For moral absolutists, support for violence **rose** with money, to about +7. >> Offering money for a sacred value made moral absolutists angrier and more supportive of violence. || **Taboo versus symbolic**, the tragic tradeoff, compares the compromise with and without a symbolic concession. >> Panel (a). **Anger or disgust** was about **0.20** for the taboo deal and dropped to about **0.12** for the symbolic deal. >> Panel (b). **Percent support for violence** was about **+3** for the taboo deal and dropped to about **−10** for the symbolic deal. >> Symbolic recognition and an apology reduced anger and support for violence. || When a value is sacred, material incentives backfire. >> Money turns the deal into a taboo trade of the sacred for the secular. >> A symbolic concession treats the sacred value with respect, making compromise more acceptable.
30|Research on **sacred values in intergroup conflicts** has **investigated the impact of monetary incentives and disincentives in negotiations involving sacred values**. Two studies, **Dehghani, Atran and colleagues (2010)** and **Dehghani, Iliev and colleagues (2009)**, addressed two questions. >> **How material incentives and disincentives, such as economic sanctions, psychologically affect the targeted population and potentially influence behavior**. >> **Whether Iranians who treat the Iranian nuclear program as a sacred value would be open to material incentives or disincentives to compromise**. || An **incentive** is a reward offered for compromising, such as economic aid. >> A **disincentive** is a penalty for refusing, such as **economic sanctions**.
31|The **experiments** used **1,997 Iranian participants in an online study conducted in Farsi**. **Farsi** is the main language of Iran. >> The **dependent variables** were **people’s approval** of a deal, **people’s anger**, and a **misleading factor**, meaning whether participants found the deal misleading. || The **sacred value measure** was **based on Baron and Spranca’s 1997 measure**. It asked **what participants thought about Iran giving up its nuclear energy or weapons program**. >> **a. I think this definitely needs to happen**. >> **b. I do not object to this**. >> **c. This should be done only if the benefits of stopping the program are great enough**. >> **d. This shouldn’t be done no matter how great the consequences**. || **Participants who answered “d” were considered to hold a sacred value**. >> This was **14% for nuclear energy** and **13% for nuclear weapons**. >> Answer “d” refuses any tradeoff, no matter what is offered, which is the defining feature of a sacred value.
32|The study used a **2 by 3 design**. >> The first factor was the **nuclear program**, either **nuclear energy** or **nuclear weapons**. >> The second factor was the **type of deal**, either **taboo**, **taboo-plus** or **taboo-minus**. || The **scenarios** were as follows. >> **Taboo**. **Iran will give up its program for developing nuclear energy, or weapons, and surrender the current nuclear facilities to the UN**. **In return, Israel will give up its program for developing nuclear energy, or weapons, and surrender the existing nuclear facilities to the UN**. >> **Taboo-plus**. The taboo deal, plus **in return, the United Nations will give $40 billion in economic funds to Iran**. >> **Taboo-minus**. The taboo deal, plus **you know that if Iran does not accept this deal, the United Nations will impose additional sanctions on Iran**. || **Participants were asked three questions**. >> **Do you personally approve of this deal?** >> **If the Iranian government approves such a deal, do you think people will be angry?** >> **If such a deal is really proposed to Iran, do you find it misleading?**
33|The **results** for the **nuclear energy** program compare participants who held a sacred value, **SV**, with those who did not, **noSV**, across the three deals. || The top graph plots **approval** on a scale from 1 to 6. >> For **noSV** participants, approval was high for all three deals, around **4.5 to 4.8**. >> For **SV** participants, approval was low. It was about **2.8** for the taboo deal and dropped to about **1.9** for taboo-plus and about **2.3** for taboo-minus. >> Adding money or sanctions **lowered** approval among those who held a sacred value. || The bottom graph plots **anger** on a scale from 1 to 6. >> For **noSV** participants, anger was low for all three deals, around **2.1**. >> For **SV** participants, anger was about **3.2** for the taboo deal, rose to about **4.6** for taboo-plus and was about **3.9** for taboo-minus. >> Adding money or sanctions **raised** anger among those who held a sacred value. || Material incentives and disincentives backfire when a value is sacred. >> For people who do not hold the value as sacred, the deal is acceptable either way. >> For people who do, offering money or threatening sanctions turns the deal into a taboo trade and makes them less approving and more angry.
