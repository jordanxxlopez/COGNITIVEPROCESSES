2|**People with damage to an area of their prefrontal cortex, who suffer from flattened emotions, have impaired decision making**. Flattened emotions are emotions that are weakened, so the person feels much less than usual. >> Weaker emotion goes with worse decisions, not better ones. >> Emotion is part of how good decisions are made. || **Anxious people tend to avoid making decisions that could potentially lead to large negative consequences**. >> This pattern is **risk avoidance**. >> An option with the chance of a large loss feels too threatening, so the anxious person passes it up. || **Optimistic people are more likely to ignore negative information and focus on positive information**. >> **Too much optimism can lead to poor decision making**. >> Leaving out the negative information means deciding without the whole picture.
3|**Expected emotions** are **emotions that people predict that they will feel concerning an outcome**. >> Before a choice, a person imagines how a good result or a bad result would feel. >> The predicted feeling then guides the choice. || **Expected emotions are one of the determinants of risk aversion**, which is the **tendency to avoid taking risks**. >> Someone who expects to feel very bad after a loss is less willing to accept a chance of that loss. || **People inaccurately predict their emotions**. >> They show a **tendency to believe that a particular loss will have a greater impact than a gain of the same size**. >> That belief **increases risk aversion**, since a loss is expected to hurt more than an equal gain would help.
4|**Kermer and colleagues (2006)** **compared people’s expected emotions with their actual emotions**. >> Participants were **given $5**. >> They were **told that, based on a coin flip, they would either win an additional $5 or lose $3**. >> They **rated their happiness before the experiment started**, which set each person’s baseline. || Participants then **predicted how they would feel if they won or lost**. >> Before the flip, they expected losing $3 to lower happiness by about 4 points and winning $5 to raise it by about 2 points. >> After the flip, losing lowered happiness by only about 1 point, and winning raised it by about 1.2 points. || People **overestimated their negative feeling**. >> Losing hurt far less than predicted. >> **We usually have coping mechanisms that get activated, but we don’t consider them when predicting emotions**.
5|**Incidental emotions** are **emotions that are not specifically related to decision-making**. >> They **may be related to one’s general disposition or personality, some recent experience, or one’s general environment or surroundings**. >> These emotions have nothing to do with the choice, but they can still change it. || **Simonsohn (2007)** analyzed **university admissions decisions**. >> **Applicants’ academic attributes were more heavily weighted on cloudy days than on sunny days**. >> The weather changed what the decision makers emphasized. || **Simonsohn (2009)** studied **prospective students visiting an academically highly rated university**. >> They **were more likely to enroll if they had visited the campus on a cloudy day**. >> The weather during a visit is unrelated to the school, yet it carried into the decision to enroll.
6|**Lerner and colleagues (2004)** had participants view one of three clips. >> **A person dying** created the **sad condition**. >> **A person using a dirty toilet** created the **disgust condition**. >> **Fish at the Great Barrier Reef** was the **neutral** condition. || Next, participants were given a set of highlighters and **asked to determine the price**. >> One price was what **they would be willing to sell the highlighters** for. >> The other was what **they would be willing to buy the highlighters** for. || The prices differed by mood. >> For selling, the neutral group asked about $4.50, the disgust group about $2.70 and the sad group about $3.00. >> For buying, the neutral group offered about $3.50, the disgust group about $3.00 and the sad group about $4.50. >> Disgust lowered both prices, while sadness lowered the selling price and raised the buying price. || **A person’s mood can influence decision making**. >> The clips were unrelated to highlighters, so the differences came from incidental emotions.
7|**Decisions can depend on the context within which they are made**. **Physicians were presented with a hypothetical test case involving a possible candidate for cesarean section**, a surgical delivery of a baby. >> The test case was the same in all **three different contexts**. || The contexts differed in what came before the test case. >> In the **control** condition, **the test case was presented first**. >> In the **serious previous cases** condition, **the test case was preceded by four other cases in which there were serious complications**. >> In the **not serious previous cases** condition, **the test case was preceded by four other cases that were fairly routine**. || **Significantly more doctors recommended cesarean after reading about non-serious cases first**. >> The test case never changed, so the earlier cases changed the recommendation.
8|**Decisions depend on how choices are presented**. >> In an **opt-in procedure**, a person takes an **active step to be an organ donor**, and **countries with opt-in procedures have less than a 28% consent rate**. >> In an **opt-out procedure**, a person is an **organ donor unless they request not to be**, and **countries with opt-out procedures have over a 99% consent rate**. || The **status quo bias** is **the tendency to do nothing when faced with a difficult decision**. >> Under opt-in, doing nothing means not donating. >> Under opt-out, doing nothing means donating, so the consent rates end up far apart. || In the story **The International Community**, a chicken, a duck, a turkey, a pheasant, a quail and a partridge are summoned to a summit. >> The king’s chef asks what sauce they would like to be eaten with. >> When one bird says it does not want to be eaten at all, the chef says that is out of the question, so the only choice offered is the sauce.
9|The **framing effect** is when **decisions are influenced by how a decision is stated**. >> The statement **can highlight one aspect of the situation**, such as gains or losses. || **Tversky and Kahneman (1981)** studied how framing changes risk taking. >> **When situations are framed in terms of gains, people tend toward a risk-aversion strategy**, preferring the sure option. >> **When situations are framed in terms of losses, people tend toward a risk-taking strategy**, preferring the gamble. || The outcomes being chosen between can stay the same. >> Only the description changes, and that is enough to move people between avoiding risk and taking it.

11|**A disease is expected to kill 600 people**, and **two alternative programs to combat the disease have been proposed**. The outcomes are stated as lives saved, so this is the gain frame. >> **Program A** means **200 will be saved**. >> **Program B** means a **1/3 probability that 600 people will be saved, and a 2/3 probability that no people will be saved**. || **72% chose A**, and 28% chose B. >> Program A is the sure option, and Program B is the gamble. >> Most people avoided the risk when the outcomes were described as lives saved.
12|The same disease is expected to kill 600 people, but now the outcomes are stated as deaths, which makes this the loss frame. >> **Program C** means **400 people will die**. >> **Program D** means a **1/3 probability that no one will die, and a 2/3 probability that 600 people will die**. || **78% chose D**, and 22% chose C. >> Program D is the gamble, so most people now accepted the risk. || Programs A and C have the same outcome, since saving 200 of 600 means that 400 die. >> Programs B and D are the same gamble. >> Only the wording changed, so the switch in choices is the **framing effect**.
13|**Neuroeconomics** **combines research from psychology, neuroscience, and economics**. >> It studies decisions about value together with what happens in the brain. || **One finding is that decisions are influenced by emotions, and those emotions are associated with activity in specific areas of the brain**. >> Emotion is part of the decision process. >> Its role can be traced to particular brain areas.
14|**Sanfey and coworkers (2003)** used the **ultimatum game**. >> There are **two players**, a **proposer** and a **responder**. >> The **proposer gets $10** and **makes an offer to the responder as to how this money should be split**. || The **responder can accept or reject the offer**. >> **If the responder rejects, neither receives anything**. >> A rejection therefore costs both players. || According to **utility theory**, the rational decision for the responder is to **accept any offer**. >> Utility theory judges a choice by the value of its outcome. >> Any offer, even $1, is worth more than the nothing that comes from rejecting.
15|**In Sanfey’s experiment, participants played 20 separate games**, **10 with human partners and 10 with computer partners**. >> Offers of $5 and $5 were accepted about 100% of the time, and $7 and $3 about 95%, from both partners. >> An $8 and $2 offer was accepted about 53% of the time from a human and about 85% from a computer. >> A $9 and $1 offer was accepted about 37% from a human and about 68% from a computer. || Participants **often rejected low offers because they became angry that offers were unfair**. >> They were **less angry with an unfair computer**, so they accepted its low offers more often. || **More activation of the right anterior insula**, which is **connected with emotional states**, went with participants being **more likely to reject more offers**. >> **Emotion is important in decision-making**.
16|**Deductive reasoning** is **determining whether a conclusion logically follows from premises**. >> A premise is a statement the reasoning starts from. || A **syllogism** is the **basic form of deductive reasoning**. >> It was **introduced by Aristotle**. >> It has **two statements called premises** and a **third statement called the conclusion**. || A **categorical syllogism** **describes the relation between two categories using all, no, or some**. >> Premise 1 is **All birds are animals**. >> Premise 2 is **All animals eat food**. >> The conclusion is **Therefore, all birds eat food**.
17|**A syllogism is valid if its conclusion follows logically from its two premises**. **If two premises of a valid syllogism are true, the syllogism’s conclusion must be true**. >> Validity concerns the form of the reasoning. || **Do not confuse validity with truth**. >> All birds are animals, all animals have four legs, so all birds have four legs. >> This is **valid**, because it has the **same form as the first example**, even though the conclusion is false. || **Validity is about whether the conclusion logically follows from the premises**. >> **If it does, and the premises are true, then the conclusion must be true**. >> A false premise can lead a valid syllogism to a false conclusion.
18|**The difference between validity and truth can make it difficult to judge whether reasoning is logical or not**. >> **Valid syllogisms can result in false conclusions**. >> **Syllogisms can be invalid even though each of the premises and the conclusion seem reasonable**. || All of the students are tired, some tired people are irritable, so some of the students are irritable. >> Every statement sounds reasonable. >> The syllogism is **not valid**, because the conclusion does not follow. || **There are many errors in evaluation, because good reasoning and truth are not the same thing**. >> **Belief bias** is **the tendency to think that a syllogism is valid if its conclusions are believable**. >> **71% of participants judged invalid, believable conclusions as valid**.
19|**There is no easy method for determining whether a syllogism is valid or invalid**. >> People need some way to work out what follows from a set of statements. || In the pool table problem, a black ball is directly above the cue ball, the green ball is on the right side of the cue ball, and a red ball is between them. >> The speaker moves so that the red ball is between the speaker and the black ball. >> The task is to say on which side of the speaker’s line of sight the cue ball lies. || The answer is that the cue ball is to the **left** of the line of sight. >> Picturing the table puts the black ball above the cue ball, with the red ball and then the green ball to the cue ball’s right. >> Standing so the red ball blocks the black ball means looking past it toward the black ball, and from there the cue ball sits off to the left. || **Most people solve this by imagining the way the balls are arranged on the pool table**. >> **The idea that people can imagine situations is the basis of Johnson-Laird’s proposal that people use mental models to solve deductive reasoning**.
20|In the **mental model approach (Johnson-Laird, 1999)**, a **mental model** is **a specific situation represented in a person’s mind that can be used to help determine the validity of syllogisms in deductive reasoning**. >> The person reasons from a picture of what the premises describe. || The approach works through a series of steps. >> The person **creates a model of a situation**. >> The person **generates tentative conclusions about the model**. >> The person **looks for exceptions to falsify the model**, searching for a case where the premises are true and the conclusion is false. >> The person **determines the validity of the syllogism**. || **A conclusion is valid only if it cannot be refuted by any model of the premises**. >> One model that fits the premises but not the conclusion proves that the conclusion does not follow.
21|**Conditional syllogisms have two premises and a conclusion**. >> **The first premise is of the form if p, then q**. >> The letter p stands for a condition, and q stands for what follows when the condition is met. || **There are four major types of conditional syllogism**. >> They share the first premise and differ in the second premise and the conclusion. >> Syllogism 1 goes from p to q, syllogism 2 from not q to not p, syllogism 3 from q to p, and syllogism 4 from not p to not q.
22|**Conditional syllogism 1** is **modus ponens**. >> If I study, I’ll get a good grade. >> I studied. >> Therefore, I’ll get a good grade. || In letters, the second premise is p and the conclusion is therefore q. >> The condition is met, so what follows from it must follow. || This syllogism is **valid**. >> **97%** of participants judged it correctly, the highest of the four types.
23|**Conditional syllogism 2** is **modus tollens**. >> If I study, I’ll get a good grade. >> I didn’t get a good grade. >> Therefore, I didn’t study. || In letters, the second premise is not q and the conclusion is therefore not p. >> If studying always leads to a good grade, missing the good grade means the studying did not happen. || This syllogism is **valid**. >> Only **60%** of participants judged it correctly, even though it is as valid as modus ponens.
24|**Conditional syllogism 3** goes from the result back to the condition. >> If I study, I’ll get a good grade. >> I got a good grade. >> Therefore, I studied. || In letters, the second premise is q and the conclusion is therefore p. >> This syllogism is **not valid**, and only **40%** of participants judged it correctly. >> The first premise does not say that studying is the only way to get a good grade. || The same form appears with other content. >> If I live in Tucson, then I live in Arizona. I live in Arizona. Therefore, I live in Tucson. >> A person can live in Arizona without living in Tucson, so the conclusion does not follow.
25|**Conditional syllogism 4** goes from a missing condition to a missing result. >> If I study, I’ll get a good grade. >> I didn’t study. >> Therefore, I didn’t get a good grade. || In letters, the second premise is not p and the conclusion is therefore not q. >> This syllogism is **not valid**, and only **40%** of participants judged it correctly. >> The first premise says nothing about what happens without studying, and a good grade could come some other way. || Across the four types, the valid forms were judged correctly at 97% and 60%. >> The invalid forms were judged correctly at 40% each.
26|The **Wason four-card problem** tests the **effect of using real-world items in a conditional-reasoning problem**. >> The cards read **E, K, 4 and 7**. >> The task is to **determine the minimum number of cards to turn over to test if there is a vowel on one side, then there is an even number on the other side**. || **E needs to be turned over**. >> **46% indicated that 4 would need to be turned over**, which is an error. >> **Only 7% indicated that 7 needs to be turned over**, which is correct. || A 7 with a vowel on its back would break the rule. >> A 4 cannot break the rule whatever is on its back. >> The answer is therefore to turn over the E and the 7.
27|The **falsification principle** is that **to test a rule, you must look for situations that falsify the rule**. >> To falsify a rule is to prove it false. >> **Most participants fail to do this**. || **When the problem is stated in concrete everyday terms, correct responses greatly increase**. >> The rule is **if drinking beer, then 21 years old**. >> The cards read **beer**, **soda**, **16 years old** and **21 years old**. >> **73%** of participants answered this version correctly. >> Following the falsification principle, the cards to turn over are **beer** and **16 years old**, because someone drinking beer while under 21 is the only case that breaks the rule.
28|A **permission schema** is **if A is satisfied, B can be carried out**. >> Being 21 years old is the condition, and drinking beer is what can then be carried out. || Permission schemas are **used in the concrete versions**. >> **People are familiar with rules** like this one. >> A rule that grants permission is easier to reason about than an abstract rule about letters and numbers. || **Context is important**. >> The same logical problem becomes easier in a familiar setting. >> **Familiarity is not always important**, so familiarity alone does not explain the improvement.
