2|A **problem** is **an obstacle between a present state and a goal**, where **the solution is not immediately obvious**. >> The **present state** is how things are right now. >> The **goal** is how we want things to be. >> The **obstacle** is whatever keeps us from getting directly from one to the other. || The **sliding-tile puzzle** captures this structure. >> The starting board has eight numbered tiles and one empty space, arranged as **8, blank, 6** in the top row, **5, 4, 7** in the middle row and **2, 3, 1** in the bottom row. >> The goal board has the empty space in the top left, followed by **1, 2** in the top row, **3, 4, 5** in the middle row and **6, 7, 8** in the bottom row. >> Both boards have exactly the same pieces. Only their positions differ. || Knowing the goal is different from knowing how to reach it. >> The finished arrangement is easy to describe. >> The difficulty is finding the sequence of tile moves that turns the starting board into the goal board, which is what makes it a problem.
3|**Gestalt psychologists** were interested in **learning, problem solving, and even attitudes and beliefs**, along with **perception**, according to **Koffka (1935)**. >> They **took a perceptual approach** to all of these. >> In the Gestalt view, solving a problem works much like seeing a figure. It depends on how the parts are organized into a whole. || **Problem solving for Gestalt psychologists** had two parts. >> **Representing the problem in the mind**, which is how the solver mentally organizes the problem’s elements. >> **Restructuring**, which is **reorganizing the problem’s representation** to see it in a new way. || **Köhler’s “circle” problem** asks this question. **If the length of the circle’s radius is r, what is the length of line x?** >> The figure has a circle with a horizontal and a vertical radius, and a rectangle drawn in one quarter of the circle. Line **x** is a diagonal of the rectangle running from the center to a side of the rectangle. >> **Different people might represent this problem differently**, and **people’s representations are likely to differ from the picture**. || The solution comes from **restructuring**. >> The rectangle has a second diagonal, running from the center of the circle to the corner of the rectangle that touches the circle. >> That second diagonal is a radius, so its length is **r**. >> The two diagonals of a rectangle are always equal, so line x is also equal to the radius. \[x=r\]
4|**Solutions are often obtained by first perceiving the object and then representing it in a different way**. This is the **restructuring of the problem’s representation**. >> The elements of the problem stay the same. >> The way the solver organizes them changes. || **Gestalt psychologists related restructuring to insight**. **Insight** has several features. >> It is a **sudden realization of a problem’s solution**. >> It involves **discovering a crucial element** of the problem that was missed before. >> Gestalt psychologists **thought solutions come to people all of a sudden**. >> This sudden realization is the **“Aha” experience**. || **Other researchers disagree that all problem solving works this way**. >> **Much of our problem solving does not require “Aha” experiences**. >> Many problems are solved step by step, with steady progress toward the answer. >> Insight is one way of solving problems, and methodical step-by-step solving is another.
5|**Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987)** **designed an experiment to distinguish between insight and noninsight problems**. >> **Insight problems** included the **triangle problem** and the **chain problem**. >> **Noninsight problems** were **algebra** problems. || The **hypothesis** made opposite predictions for the two kinds of problems. >> **In insight problems, participants should not be very good at predicting how near they are to a solution**. If insight arrives suddenly, the solver should feel far from the answer right up until it appears. >> **Noninsight problems involve a more methodical process**, so **participants should know how close they are to the solution**. Each completed step brings the answer visibly closer. || The measure was **warmth judgments every 15 seconds**. >> Participants rated how close they felt to the solution, from cold to hot. >> **“Hot” ratings indicated that they were getting close to the solution**. >> Tracking these ratings over time reveals whether the feeling of progress grows gradually or appears all at once.
6|Two **insight problems** used by **Metcalfe and Wiebe** reveal the kind of restructuring insight requires. || The **triangle problem** asks how to **move three of the blue dots to make the triangle point to the bottom of the page**. >> The triangle has ten dots, with one dot at the top, then rows of two, three and four dots. >> Seven dots stay where they are, and only three move. >> Move the top dot to below the middle of the bottom row. Move the two end dots of the bottom row up to the ends of the two-dot row. >> The rows now hold four, three, two and one dots from top to bottom, so the triangle points down. || The **chain problem** asks how to **join four short pieces of chain into a single closed loop**. Each piece has three links. **Opening a link costs 2 cents, closing a link costs 3 cents, and there are only 15 cents**. >> The obvious approach opens one link on each piece and joins them in a ring, which takes four openings and four closings and costs 20 cents, more than the budget. >> The solution is to open all three links of one piece, which costs 6 cents. >> Those three open links then join the other three pieces into a loop, and closing them costs 9 cents. \[3(2+3)=15\] >> The total is exactly 15 cents. The insight is treating one piece as a set of connectors rather than as part of the chain.
7|**Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987)** compared **“warmer/colder” ratings** over time for insight and noninsight problems. The graph plots **median warmth rating**, from **cold, at 0**, to **solved, at 7**, against **time before the solution**, from 60 seconds before to the moment of solution. || For **algebra**, the noninsight problems, warmth showed a **gradual increase**. >> Ratings sat at about **3** at 60 and 45 seconds before the solution. >> They rose to about **4** at 30 seconds, **5** at 15 seconds and **7** at the solution. >> **Noninsight problems were solved gradually**, with participants feeling closer and closer to the answer. || For **insight** problems, warmth showed a **big jump from 3 to 7**. >> Ratings stayed at about **2** from 60 to 30 seconds before the solution. >> They rose only to about **3** at 15 seconds before the solution, then jumped to **7** at the solution. >> **Insight problems were solved suddenly**. Participants felt far from the answer until it arrived. || The results confirm the hypothesis. >> Insight and noninsight problems feel different from the inside. >> Insight comes as a sudden leap, while methodical problems produce a steady sense of progress.
8|**Gestalt psychologists claimed that one of the major obstacles to problem solving is fixation**. **Fixation** is the **tendency to focus on a specific characteristic of the problem that keeps people from arriving at a solution**. >> **Functional fixedness** is a kind of fixation. It is **restricting the use of an object to its familiar functions**. >> We see an object as having one purpose and miss other ways it could be used. || The **candle problem** tests functional fixedness. >> A person is in a room with a **vertical corkboard mounted on the wall** and is given a **candle**, a **box of matches** and a **box of tacks**. >> The task is to **mount the candle on the corkboard so it will burn without dripping wax on the floor**. >> The solution is to empty the matchbox, tack it to the corkboard and stand the candle in it as a shelf. || **Seeing the matchboxes as containers inhibited using them as supports**. >> When the boxes held matches and tacks, people thought of them only as containers. >> **Participants who were given empty matchboxes were twice as likely to solve the problem**. >> An empty box is easier to see as an object that can serve other functions, such as a platform.
9|**Fixation** and **functional fixedness** also block the **two-string problem**. The task is to **tie together two strings** hanging from the ceiling. >> The strings are too far apart to reach at the same time. >> Holding one string, the person cannot reach the other. >> A few objects, including a pair of **pliers**, are available in the room. || The solution is to **use the pliers to create a pendulum**. >> Tie the pliers to the end of one string, which turns the string into a **pendulum**, a weight that swings back and forth. >> Set it swinging, take hold of the other string and catch the swinging one when it comes close. || **The usual function of pliers gets in the way of seeing them as a weight**. >> People think of pliers as a tool for gripping. >> That familiar function makes it hard to notice that pliers are also heavy, which is the property the solution needs.
10|In the **two-string problem**, **once a hint was provided by “accidentally” setting a string in motion, most participants were able to solve it**. >> The experimenter brushed against one string so that it swung. >> Seeing the string swing suggested the idea of a pendulum. >> **In Gestalt terms, the solution occurred when participants restructured their representation**. The swinging string reorganized how they saw the problem. || A **mental set** is another obstacle. >> It is a **preconceived notion about how to approach a problem**. >> It is **based on a person’s past experiences with the problem or similar problems**. >> A method that worked before gets applied again, even when a better method is available. || The **water-jug problem** reveals mental set. >> The task is to **figure out how to obtain a required volume of water, given three empty jars for measuring**. >> Solving several problems the same way builds a mental set that shapes how later problems are approached. >> In the water-jug problem, a person who has solved several problems with one formula keeps reaching for that formula on new problems, even when a simpler method would work.
11|Each **water-jug problem** **specifies the capacities of jugs A, B and C and a final desired quantity**. **The task is to figure out how to use the jugs with these capacities to measure out the desired quantity**. The table lists eight problems, with capacities in quarts. >> Problem 1 has jugs of **21, 127 and 3** and a desired quantity of **100**. >> Problem 2 has **14, 163 and 25**, with a target of **99**. Problem 3 has **18, 43 and 10**, with a target of **5**. Problem 4 has **9, 42 and 6**, with a target of **21**. >> Problem 5 has **20, 59 and 4**, with a target of **31**. Problem 6 has **20, 50 and 3**, with a target of **24**. >> Problem 7 has **15, 39 and 3**, with a target of **18**. Problem 8 has **28, 59 and 3**, with a target of **25**. || For problem 1, the steps run as follows. >> Fill jug B with **127** quarts. >> Pour from B into A until A is full. That removes 21, leaving **106**, which is B minus A. >> Pour from B into C until C is full. That removes 3, leaving **103**. >> Pour from B into C a second time, leaving **100**. \[B-A-2C=127-21-2(3)=100\] || The same formula, B minus A minus 2C, solves the early problems. Participants who used it repeatedly developed a **mental set**. >> Problem 7 can be solved the long way, but it also has a much simpler solution. \[A+C=15+3=18\] >> Problem 8 also has a simple solution. \[A-C=28-3=25\] >> **The mental set inhibited participants from using the simpler solutions**. They kept using the familiar long formula, even when two jugs were enough.
12|**Newell and Simon (1956)** created the **Logic Theorist**, an early computer program that solved problems. They took an **information-processing approach** to problem solving. || They **described problem solving as a process that involves search**. >> Gestalt psychologists focused on **the initial structure of a problem and the new structure achieved when it is solved**. >> **Newell and Simon described problem solving as a search that occurs between the posing of the problem and its solution**. >> It is **“searching for a way to reach a goal”** and **“getting around road-blocks.”** || A **problem space** has three parts. >> The **initial state** is **the conditions at the beginning of the problem**. >> **Intermediate states** are the conditions reached along the way, after some steps but before the solution. >> The **goal state** is **the solution of the problem**. || Solving a problem means moving through the problem space. >> The solver starts at the initial state. >> Each step leads to a new intermediate state. >> The search ends when the goal state is reached.
13|**Operators** are **rules that specify which moves are allowed and which are not**. Operators **take the problem from one state to another**. >> Every legal move applies an operator. >> Each operator changes the current state into a new one. || The **Tower of Hanoi** is a classic problem with clear operators. >> In the **initial state**, three disks are stacked on the first of three pegs, with the largest at the bottom and the smallest at the top. >> In the **goal state**, the same stack sits on the third peg, in the same order. || Three rules define the legal moves. >> **Rule 1. Disks are moved one at a time** from one peg to another. >> **Rule 2. A disk can be moved only when there are no disks on top of it**. >> **Rule 3. A larger disk cannot be placed on top of a smaller disk**. || Following these rules means the stack cannot be moved all at once. >> The smaller disks have to be moved out of the way first. >> The middle peg serves as a temporary holding place.
14|The full **Tower of Hanoi** solution for a larger stack takes many steps. The sequence of moves for a stack of disks shows the disks shifting back and forth among the three pegs many times. >> Smaller disks move repeatedly to clear the way for larger ones. >> Each larger disk can only move after every smaller disk above it has been moved elsewhere. >> The pattern repeats at every level of the stack. || The number of moves grows enormously as disks are added. >> **With 64 disks, solving it would take a trillion years**, **even if each move were made in one second and every move were correct**. >> The rules stay the same, but the number of required moves explodes. || This makes the size of the problem space clear. >> The goal state is easy to describe. >> The path to it, through many intermediate states, can be extremely long.
15|The **sliding-tile puzzle** has an **initial state** and a **goal state**. >> In the initial state, the top row is **8, blank, 6**, the middle row is **5, 4, 7** and the bottom row is **2, 3, 1**. >> In the goal state, the top row is **blank, 1, 2**, the middle row is **3, 4, 5** and the bottom row is **6, 7, 8**. || The **operators** in this puzzle are tile moves. >> A tile next to the empty space can move into it. >> Each move shifts the empty space to where the tile was, which produces a new state. || Every arrangement of tiles is a different state in the problem space. >> The same eight tiles appear in every state. >> What changes is where each tile sits, and the solver has to find a sequence of moves that leads from the initial arrangement to the goal arrangement.
16|A **search tree** maps out the problem space of a sliding-tile puzzle. >> At the top, numbered **1**, is the starting state, with the tiles arranged as **2, 8, 3** in the top row, **1, 6, 4** in the middle row and **7, blank, 5** in the bottom row. >> Each line leading down from a state is a legal move, a tile sliding into the empty space. || The start has **three possible moves**, which lead to states **2, 3 and 4**. >> In state 2, the 7 moves right. In state 3, the 6 moves down. In state 4, the 5 moves left. >> Each of these states allows further moves, which branch into states **5 through 9**, and those branch further. || The tree spreads out quickly. >> By the bottom levels, there are dozens of states, numbered up to **46**. >> Only one path leads to the state marked **goal**. >> Searching a problem space means picking which branches to follow so that the goal is reached without trying every possibility.
17|**According to Newell and Simon, we have to search the problem space to find a solution**. A useful search strategy is **means-end analysis**. >> **Means-end analysis** aims to **reduce the differences between the initial and goal states**. >> At each point, the solver asks what is different from the goal and which move would reduce that difference. || Means-end analysis works through **subgoals**. **Subgoals create intermediate states closer to the goal**. >> A **subgoal** is a smaller goal that has to be reached before the main goal can be. >> Reaching each subgoal brings the solver one step closer to the final solution. || **For the Tower of Hanoi, applying means-end analysis reduces the size of the difference between the initial and goal states**. >> The big difference is that the whole stack is on the wrong peg. >> Breaking this into subgoals, such as moving the largest disk to the goal peg, makes the problem manageable.
18|**Means-end analysis** solves the **Tower of Hanoi** through a series of subgoals. || **Subgoal 1 is to free up the large disk**. >> In step (a), the smallest disk moves from the first peg to the third peg. >> In step (b), the middle disk moves from the first peg to the middle peg. >> The large disk on the first peg is now uncovered. || **Subgoal 2 is to free up the third peg**. >> In step (c), the smallest disk moves from the third peg onto the middle disk on the middle peg. >> The third peg is now empty, and the smaller disks are stacked legally on the middle peg. || **Subgoal 3 is to move the large disk onto the third peg**. >> In step (d), the large disk moves from the first peg to the third peg. >> The large disk is now in its final position, and the two smaller disks can be moved onto it. || Each subgoal removes one obstacle. >> Freeing the large disk lets it move. >> Freeing the third peg gives it somewhere to go. Together, they reduce the difference between the current state and the goal. >> The three subgoals together move the largest disk to the goal peg, after which the same strategy can be repeated to move the smaller disks onto it.
19|**One of the main contributions of Newell and Simon’s approach** is that it **provided a way to specify the possible pathways from the initial state to the goal state**. >> Defining states and operators maps out every route through a problem. >> This made problem solving something that could be described precisely and even programmed into a computer. || **There is more to problem solving than specifying the problem state**. **How a problem is stated can affect its difficulty**. >> Two versions of the same problem can have identical states and operators. >> One version can still be much easier to solve, because its wording or presentation helps the solver see the key relationship. || The **mutilated-checkerboard problem** is a clear example. >> Its difficulty depends heavily on how the board is presented.
20|A **checkerboard** has **64 squares** and **can be completely covered by placing 32 dominoes on the board**, **with each domino covering 2 squares**. >> Each domino covers two neighboring squares, either side by side or one above the other. >> Thirty-two dominoes times two squares each gives exactly 64. || The **mutilated-checkerboard problem** asks this question. **If two opposite corners are eliminated, can the remaining 62 squares be covered with 31 dominoes?** >> The area seems to match, since 31 dominoes cover 62 squares. >> The answer is **no**. || The key is the colors of the squares. >> Every domino covers **one dark square and one light square**, because neighboring squares always have different colors. >> The two opposite corners have the **same color**, so removing them leaves **32 squares of one color and 30 of the other**. >> Thirty-one dominoes would need to cover 31 squares of each color. With 32 of one color and 30 of the other, a complete covering is impossible.
21|**The key to solving the mutilated-checkerboard problem** is **understanding two things**. >> **Each domino covers two squares**. >> **These squares must be of different colors**. || **Conditions differed in how much information was provided about the squares**. >> **Condition 1** used a **blank board**, with no colors at all. >> **Condition 2** used a **colored board**, with dark and light squares. >> **Condition 3** put the **words “black” and “pink”** on the squares. >> **Condition 4** put the **words “bread” and “butter”** on the squares. || The problem was **easier to solve when the information provided pointed toward the correct representation of the problem**. >> The **“bread and butter” condition emphasized the difference between the squares**. Bread and butter naturally go together in pairs, one of each. >> **Participants solved this condition twice as fast as the blank condition**. >> The labels pointed solvers toward the idea that each domino must pair one square of each kind.
22|The **think-aloud protocol** is a method for studying problem solving. Participants **say aloud what they are thinking while solving a problem**. >> They are **instructed not to describe what they are doing, but to verbalize new thoughts** as they occur. >> The **goal** is to find out **what information the participant is attending to while solving the problem**. || In the **mutilated-checkerboard problem**, think-aloud protocols reveal a **shift in how the solver perceives the elements of the problem**. >> **Participants get the problem after realizing that the words “bread” and “butter” are important**. >> At that moment, they stop seeing the squares as just places for dominoes and start seeing them as two kinds that each domino must pair. || This shift is **similar to the Gestalt idea of a representational shift**. >> The board and the rules stay the same. >> The solver’s mental representation reorganizes, which is restructuring, and the solution follows.
