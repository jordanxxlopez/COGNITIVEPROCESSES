2|The **modal model** view of short-term memory turned out to be **too simple**. **Short-term memory is not just passive storage**. It also requires **“control processes,”** so a model is needed that includes them. >> **Control processes** are the active operations we perform on information, such as selecting, repeating and transforming it. >> The modal model treated short-term memory mainly as a holding place, with information sitting there until it was used or lost. || **Baddeley and Hitch (1974)** proposed **working memory**, a concept similar to short-term memory but broader. **Working memory** is a **limited-capacity system for the temporary storage and manipulation of information for complex tasks such as comprehension, learning and reasoning**. >> **Storage** keeps information available for a short time. >> **Manipulation** means actively working with that information, such as changing it, combining it or using it in a calculation. || Complex tasks need both functions at once. >> **Comprehension** requires holding the start of a sentence while relating it to the words that follow. >> **Learning** requires keeping new information active while connecting it to what we already know. >> **Reasoning** requires holding facts in mind while working toward a conclusion.
3|**Multiplying 43 times 6 in your head** requires storage and active processing at the same time. \[43\times6=258\] The steps run as follows. >> **Step 1. Visualize 43 × 6**, holding the whole problem in mind. >> **Step 2. Multiply 3 × 6**, which gives 18. \[3\times6=18\] >> **Step 3. Hold the 8 in memory** as the ones digit of the answer, **while carrying the 1 over to the 4**. >> **Step 4. Multiply 6 × 4 = 24, then add the carried 1 to get 25**. \[6\times4=24,\qquad24+1=25\] >> **Step 5. Combine the 25 with the 8** being held, which gives **258**. || This operation involves both **storage** and **active processes**. >> Holding the 8 in memory while the rest of the calculation continues is storage. >> Carrying the 1, multiplying and adding are active processes that change the information. >> The 8 has to survive while the other operations are carried out, so the calculation depends on storage and processing working together. This combination is what working memory provides.
4|**Short-term memory**, in the model of **Atkinson and Shiffrin**, and **working memory**, in the model of **Baddeley and Hitch**, differ in their structure and purpose. || **Short-term memory** in the Atkinson and Shiffrin model has two key features. >> It is a **single component for all types of information**, so words, pictures and sounds all go into the same store. >> It is **mainly used for holding information for a short time**. || **Working memory** in the Baddeley and Hitch model has **three components**. >> The **central executive**, which directs and coordinates the system. >> The **visuospatial sketchpad**, which holds visual and spatial information. >> The **phonological loop**, which holds verbal and auditory information. >> A fourth component, the **episodic buffer**, was **added by Baddeley in 2000**. || Working memory is **used for the manipulation of information during complex cognition**. >> Short-term memory emphasizes holding information. >> Working memory emphasizes using it, with separate parts for different kinds of information and a control system that manages them.
5|Each **component of working memory** has its own job. || The **phonological loop** handles verbal and auditory information. It has two parts. >> The **phonological store** **holds verbal and auditory information**, such as words and speech sounds. >> The **articulatory rehearsal process** is **responsible for rehearsal that keeps items in the phonological store from decaying**. **Articulatory** refers to the movements of speech, so this process works by silently saying the items over and over. >> Without rehearsal, items in the phonological store fade within a few seconds. || The **visuospatial sketchpad** **holds visual and spatial information**. >> Visual information concerns what things look like, such as shape and color. >> Spatial information concerns where things are and how they are arranged. || The **central executive** **pulls information from long-term memory, coordinates the other components, and directs and maintains attention**. >> In the diagram of **Baddeley’s working memory model**, the central executive sits in the middle, overlapping with the phonological loop, which holds **verbal and auditory information**, on one side and the visuospatial sketchpad, which holds **visual and spatial information**, on the other. >> The central executive is the control center that manages the two storage components.
6|The **working memory model of Baddeley and Hitch (1974)** traces how information flows through the system. >> **Input** from the environment enters **sensory memory**. >> From sensory memory, information passes to the **central executive**. >> The central executive exchanges information in both directions with **long-term knowledge**. || The central executive also exchanges information in both directions with the **phonological loop** and the **visuospatial sketchpad**. >> Arrows run up and down between the executive and each storage component. >> Long-term knowledge also connects directly with both the phonological loop and the visuospatial sketchpad. || Working memory is a set of interacting parts. >> The executive controls what each storage component holds and does. >> The storage components can draw on long-term knowledge, so stored facts, word meanings and familiar images contribute to what is held and processed.
7|Three effects reveal how the **phonological loop** works. || The **phonological similarity effect** is that **letters or words that sound similar are confused**. >> **Phonological** refers to the sounds of language. >> Items that sound alike, such as the letters B, D and G, are easy to mix up in memory because their sound codes overlap. || The **word-length effect** is that **memory for lists of words is better for short words than for long words**. >> **It takes longer to rehearse long words and to produce them during recall**. >> In the same amount of time, fewer long words can be rehearsed, so fewer stay active. || **Articulatory suppression** is when **speaking interferes with rehearsal, so memory is reduced**. >> Saying something out loud, such as repeating “the, the, the,” uses the articulatory rehearsal process. >> With rehearsal taken up, the items being remembered are not refreshed and fade from the phonological store.
8|The **phonological similarity effect** is that **words that sound similar are confused by people**. **Conrad (1964)** tested this with letters. || The **setup** worked as follows. >> **Participants saw target letters that were quickly flashed**. >> **Half of the time the letters were similar, and half of the time they were not**. >> **Then participants wrote them down**. || The **observation** was in the errors. **Mistakes were made**, and they followed a clear pattern. >> Participants were **unlikely to replace a letter with one that looked like the target**, such as writing **F** instead of **E**. >> They were **likely to replace it with one that sounded like the target**, such as writing **B** instead of **E**. The names “E” and “B” share the same vowel sound. || The **interpretation** is that participants remembered the letters by their sounds. >> The letters were seen, not heard. >> Even so, the errors were based on sound, so the letters were held in a sound-based code in the phonological loop.
9|The **phonological similarity effect experiment** **demonstrated the phonological similarity effect**, in which **people confuse letters that sound similar**, **even though the information was presented visually**. >> The letters reached the participants through their eyes. >> They were still confused by sound, not by appearance. || The experiment **also showed that the phonological loop is necessary for the conversion**, **not just for holding information**. >> Seen letters are converted into their spoken names. >> The phonological loop carries out this conversion and then holds the sound-based code. || The phonological loop has two roles. >> It translates visual input, such as written letters and words, into sound. >> It stores and rehearses that sound code, which is why sound-based confusions appear for visually presented items.
10|The **word-length effect** is that **when memorizing words, you can remember fewer words if the words are long**. >> **People remembered more of the short words than the long words**, according to **Baddeley and colleagues (1975, 1984)**. >> Long words take longer to say, so fewer can be rehearsed in the time before they fade. || **Welsh–English bilingual children** have a **shorter digit span in Welsh than in English**, according to **Ellis and Hennelly (1980)**. >> The reason is that **Welsh numbers take longer to pronounce**. >> The children know the same numbers in both languages. The only difference is how long the words take to say. >> The very long Welsh place name **Llanfairpwllgwyngyllgogerychwyrndrobwllllantysiliogogogoch** is a famous example of how long Welsh words can be. || **The number of words you can say in 1.5 to 2.0 seconds is likely to be your digit span**. >> The phonological loop holds about as much as can be rehearsed in that window. >> Faster speech means more items fit, which ties memory span to speaking time rather than to a fixed number of items.
11|**Articulatory suppression** is what happens **if you speak while memorizing**, which **keeps the phonological loop busy**. >> **You get worse at remembering**. >> **The word-length and phonological similarity effects are reduced**. || The **word-length effect is reduced** because rehearsal is blocked. >> Normally, short words are remembered better because they can be rehearsed faster. >> When speaking takes over the rehearsal process, neither short nor long words get rehearsed, so the advantage for short words shrinks. || The **phonological similarity effect is also reduced** because the conversion into sound is blocked. >> Normally, seen letters are converted into sound codes, which leads to sound-based confusions. >> When the phonological loop is busy, visually presented letters are not converted into sound, so sound similarity no longer matters as much. || These results **support the concept of a phonological loop**. >> One specific activity, speaking, removes both effects at once. >> Both effects therefore depend on the same speech-based system, which is the phonological loop.
12|The **visuospatial sketchpad** **holds visual and spatial information**. >> **Visual** information is about appearance, such as shape and color. >> **Spatial** information is about location and arrangement. || The sketchpad is **involved in the process of visual imagery**. >> **Visual imagery** is creating or holding a picture in the mind without seeing it. >> Imagining the layout of a room, or picturing a shape and rotating it, uses the visuospatial sketchpad. || In the working memory model, the sketchpad connects with the **central executive** and with **long-term knowledge**. >> The executive directs what the sketchpad holds. >> Long-term knowledge supplies stored images and spatial layouts that can be brought into the sketchpad. || Because the sketchpad and the phonological loop are separate components, a visual task and a verbal task can be carried out together with little interference. >> Holding a picture in the sketchpad leaves the phonological loop free for words. >> Holding words in the phonological loop leaves the sketchpad free for images and locations.
13|The **sentence experiment** of **Brooks (1968)** tests the **phonological loop** and the **visuospatial sketchpad**. || The **setup** worked as follows. >> Participants **memorized a sentence**. >> They then went through the sentence word by word and **indicated whether each word was or was not a noun**. >> In **condition 1**, they **indicated by speaking**, saying “yes” or “no.” >> In **condition 2**, they **indicated by pointing** to yes or no on a sheet. || The **result** was that **pointing was easier than speaking for the participants**. || The **explanation** is that **the phonological loop was busy processing the sentence, but the sketchpad was free**. >> The sentence is verbal, so it was held in the phonological loop. >> Speaking the answers also uses the phonological loop, so it competed with the sentence. >> Pointing uses the visuospatial sketchpad, which was free, so there was no competition.
14|The **“F” demo** of **Brooks (1968)** tests the same two components, but with a picture instead of a sentence. || The **setup** worked as follows. >> Participants **memorized a shape**, in this case a block **F**. >> Starting from a corner marked with a star, they mentally traced around the F and **indicated whether each corner was an “inside corner” or an “outside corner.”** >> In **condition 1**, they **indicated by speaking**, saying “in” or “out.” >> In **condition 2**, they **indicated by pointing**, working down a list of corners numbered 1 to 10 and pointing to **OUT** or **IN** in each row. || The **result** was that **speaking was easier than pointing**, **the opposite of the sentence experiment**. || The **explanation** is that **the sketchpad was busy with the image, but the phonological loop was free**. >> The F is a visual image, so it was held in the visuospatial sketchpad. >> Pointing to locations on a sheet also uses the sketchpad, so it competed with the image. >> Speaking uses the phonological loop, which was free, so there was no competition.
15|**Tasks are easier when the information held in mind and the operation performed on it involve different types of short-term memory**. The two types are **verbal or phonological** and **visual or spatial**. >> In the sentence task, a verbal memory paired with a spatial response, pointing, was easy. >> In the F task, a visual memory paired with a verbal response, speaking, was easy. >> When the memory and the response used the same system, performance suffered. || **The two types of short-term memory are therefore somewhat independent**. >> **At the least, they have separate capacities**. >> Filling the phonological loop leaves room in the visuospatial sketchpad, and filling the sketchpad leaves room in the phonological loop. || Each component interferes with itself much more than with the other. >> Two verbal demands compete, and two visual-spatial demands compete. >> A verbal demand paired with a visual-spatial one leaves both running smoothly, which supports treating the phonological loop and the visuospatial sketchpad as separate components of working memory.

17|**Mental rotation** is the ability to turn a mental image to a new orientation. **Shepard and Metzler (1971)** showed participants pairs of three-dimensional objects built from cubes and asked whether the two objects were the same object or mirror images. >> In **picture-plane rotation** pairs, one object is rotated flat within the plane of the page, like turning a clock face. >> In **depth rotation** pairs, one object is rotated in depth, as if turned around a vertical axis. >> In some pairs, the objects are mirror images that cannot be rotated into a match. || The **results** for the picture-plane pairs plot reaction time, **RT**, in seconds against the **angle of rotation** in degrees, from 0 to 180. >> At **0 degrees**, the RT is about **1 second**. >> RT rises in a straight line as the angle grows. >> At **180 degrees**, the RT is about **4.5 seconds**. || The **interpretation** is that participants **mentally rotated** one object to compare it with the other. >> A bigger difference in angle requires a longer mental rotation. >> The straight-line relationship means each extra degree of rotation adds about the same amount of time, as if the image were turned at a steady speed. >> Mental rotation is a clear example of active manipulation in the visuospatial sketchpad.
18|The **central executive** **does the “work” of working memory**. Its jobs include the following. >> **Directing and maintaining attention**, which includes **focusing**, **dividing** and **switching** attention. >> **Controlling suppression of irrelevant information**, so that distractions do not take up working memory. >> **Coordinating the sketchpad and the phonological loop**, so that both can contribute to a task. >> **Performing calculations**, as in mental arithmetic. || Today, the central executive is **often split into updating, shifting and inhibition**, following **Miyake and colleagues (2000)**. >> **Updating** is replacing old information in working memory with new, relevant information. >> **Shifting** is switching between tasks or mental sets. >> **Inhibition** is suppressing unwanted thoughts or responses. || **The idea of a “central executive” is often criticized as theorizing a “homunculus” in the brain**. >> A **homunculus** is a “little person” inside the head who makes all the decisions. >> Saying that a central executive controls everything describes the control without explaining how it works. >> Splitting the executive into updating, shifting and inhibition gives specific, testable functions in place of a single controller.
19|The **episodic buffer** is **a fourth component, added to the working memory model by Baddeley in 2000**. >> It has a **limited capacity of about 4 “chunks.”** A **chunk** is a meaningful unit of information, which can contain several pieces. >> It **binds information from the phonological loop, the visuospatial sketchpad and long-term memory into integrated “episodes.”** **Binding** means linking different pieces of information into one combined memory. || In the updated diagram, the **central executive** sits at the top, connected to the **phonological loop**, the **episodic buffer** and the **visuospatial sketchpad** below it. >> The episodic buffer sits in the middle, connected to both the phonological loop and the visuospatial sketchpad. >> All three connect down to **long-term knowledge**. || Two findings showed **why the episodic buffer was needed**. >> **People can recall about 16 words of a meaningful sentence**, **far beyond their span for unrelated words**. Meaning and grammar bind the words into larger chunks, which the original model had no place to hold. >> **Some amnesic patients can immediately recall a just-heard story**. **Amnesia** is a severe loss of long-term memory. These patients could not store the story in long-term memory, so the story must have been held in a working memory component large enough for meaningful material.
20|**Working memory and the brain** can be studied through **individual differences**, the ways people differ from one another. **Vogel and colleagues (2005)** first **determined participants’ working memory capacity** and divided them into a **high-capacity group** and a **low-capacity group**. They then **measured ERP responses**. >> An **ERP**, or **event-related potential**, is the brain’s electrical activity recorded from the scalp, time-locked to events in a task. || Participants were **shown either simple or complex stimuli**. Each trial had four phases. >> A **cue** arrow, shown for **200 milliseconds**, pointed left or right to indicate which side of the display to remember. >> A **memory display** of colored bars at different angles appeared for **100 milliseconds**. >> A blank **delay**, the retention interval, lasted **900 milliseconds**. >> A **test display** appeared for **2,000 milliseconds**, and participants judged whether the orientation of a red bar had changed. || In the **simple** condition, the cued side contained only **red bars**, the targets to remember. >> In the **complex** condition, the cued side contained red bars plus **blue bars**, which were **distractors** to be ignored. >> Comparing the conditions tests how well people keep irrelevant items out of working memory.
21|In **panel b** of the **Vogel and colleagues (2005)** results, brain activity during the task is plotted separately for **high-capacity** and **low-capacity** participants in three conditions. >> **Two items**, two red bars to remember. >> **Four items**, four red bars to remember. >> **Two items with two distractors**, two red bars plus two blue bars to ignore. || **Activity continues well into the retention interval**, after the memory display has disappeared. >> This activity reflects the items being held in visual working memory. >> More items held means more activity. || For **high-capacity** individuals, **activity is greater when there are four relevant items than when there are two relevant items**. >> **Adding irrelevant blue items has no effect**. Activity with two targets and two distractors looks like activity with two targets alone. >> High-capacity people keep the distractors out of working memory. || For **low-capacity** individuals, **adding irrelevant items increases activity**. >> Activity with two targets and two distractors rises to nearly the level of four targets. >> Low-capacity people let the distractors into working memory, where they take up space meant for the targets.
22|In **panel c** of the **Vogel and colleagues (2005)** results, each participant’s **memory capacity** is plotted against their **filtering efficiency**. >> **Filtering efficiency** is an **EEG** measure of how well a person keeps irrelevant items out of working memory. **EEG** records electrical activity of the brain from the scalp. >> Memory capacity runs from about 1 to 4 items, and filtering efficiency from 0 to 1. || **Capacity is correlated with the EEG measure of filtering efficiency**. >> The points rise from lower left to upper right, and a line drawn through them slopes upward. >> **People with higher capacity are also less distracted by the irrelevant items**. || This changes how differences in working memory capacity are understood. >> High capacity reflects how well a person controls what enters working memory, along with how much it can hold. >> People with high capacity filter out distractors, which leaves their limited space free for relevant information. >> People with low capacity let distractors in, which fills their working memory with items that are not needed.
