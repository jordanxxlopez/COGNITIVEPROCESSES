2|**Problem solving** can be understood in several ways. >> **Problem solving as reorganization and insight**. In the Gestalt view, a solution comes from restructuring how the problem is represented in the mind, sometimes in a sudden flash of insight. >> **Problem solving as search**, a **search that occurs between the posing of the problem and its solution**. In the information-processing view of Newell and Simon, the solver moves step by step through a problem space from the initial state toward the goal state. || **There is more to problem solving than specifying the problem state**. **How a problem is stated can affect its difficulty**. >> The **mutilated checkerboard**, a checkerboard with two opposite corners removed and a domino beside it, is the standard case. >> Its difficulty depends on whether the solver notices that each domino must cover one square of each color. >> Presenting the board in ways that highlight the two kinds of squares makes the problem easier.
3|**Analogies** can help solve problems. **Using the solution to a similar problem guides the solution to a new problem**. || The **Russian marriage problem** runs as follows. >> **In a small Russian village, there were 32 bachelors and 32 unmarried women**. >> **Through tireless efforts, the village matchmaker succeeded in arranging 32 highly satisfactory marriages**, and **the village was proud and happy**. >> **Then one drunken night, two bachelors, in a test of strength, stuffed each other with pierogies and died**. >> **Can the matchmaker, through some quick arrangements, come up with 31 heterosexual marriages among the 62 survivors?** || The answer is **no**. >> After the deaths, there are **30 men and 32 women**, which makes 62 people. >> Each marriage in the problem needs **one man and one woman**. >> With only 30 men, at most **30 marriages** can be arranged, and two women are left without partners. >> The total of 62 people would allow 31 pairs only if any two people could be paired. The requirement of one from each group makes the smaller group the limit.
4|The **mutilated checkerboard** has the same structure as the Russian marriage problem. >> The board has two opposite corners removed, shown set off to either side, and a domino sits beside it. >> A **domino** covers **two neighboring squares**, which are always **one dark and one light**. || Removing two opposite corners removes two squares of the **same color**. >> The remaining 62 squares are **32 of one color and 30 of the other**. >> Thirty-one dominoes would need **31 squares of each color**. || A complete covering is therefore impossible. >> Each domino is like a marriage, pairing one member of each kind. >> The two missing corners are like the two missing bachelors. In both problems, an imbalance between the two kinds rules out the last pair, no matter how the pieces are arranged.
5|**Analogical problem solving** is **using the solution to a similar problem to guide the solution to a new problem**. >> The **Russian marriage problem** is the **source problem**, and the **mutilated-checkerboard problem** is the **target problem**. || **Analogical transfer** is **the transfer from one problem to another**, from the **source problem** to the **target problem**. >> The **target problem** is **the problem the person is trying to solve**. >> The **source problem** is **another problem that shares some similarities with the target problem**. || **Learning about the Russian marriage problem enhances the ability to solve the mutilated-checkerboard problem**. >> Men and women correspond to the two colors of squares. >> Marriages correspond to dominoes, which each pair one of each kind. >> The missing bachelors correspond to the missing corners. Once the solver sees these correspondences, the logic of the marriage problem solves the checkerboard.
6|**Duncker’s radiation problem** is a classic test of analogical problem solving. >> **Suppose you are a doctor faced with a patient who has a malignant tumor in his stomach**. >> **It is impossible to operate on the patient, but unless the tumor is destroyed, the patient will die**. >> **There is a kind of ray that can be used to destroy the tumor**. **If the rays reach it all at once at a sufficiently high intensity, the tumor will be destroyed**. >> **Unfortunately, at this intensity, the healthy tissue the rays pass through on the way to the tumor will also be destroyed**. >> **At lower intensities, the rays are harmless to healthy tissue, but they will not affect the tumor either**. >> **What type of procedure might be used to destroy the tumor with the rays and at the same time avoid destroying the healthy tissue?** || The solution is to use **several low-intensity rays from different directions** that **converge on the tumor at the same time**. >> Each ray is weak enough to pass safely through healthy tissue. >> Where the rays meet at the tumor, their combined intensity is high enough to destroy it. || **Only 10% of participants arrive at the correct solution** on their own. >> The solution requires splitting one strong ray into many weak ones that combine at the target, which is an idea most people do not think of.
7|The **fortress problem** serves as a source problem for the radiation problem. >> **A small country was ruled from a strong fortress by a dictator**. **The fortress was in the middle of the country, surrounded by farms and villages**, and **many roads led to the fortress through the countryside**. >> **A rebel general vowed to capture the fortress**. **He knew that an attack by his entire army would capture it**, and **he gathered his army at the head of one of the roads, ready to launch a full-scale direct attack**. >> **However, the general then learned that the dictator had planted mines on each of the roads**. **The mines were set so that small bodies of men could pass over them safely**, **since the dictator needed to move his troops and workers to and from the fortress**. >> **However, any large force would detonate the mines**. **Not only would this blow up the road, it would also destroy many neighboring villages**. **It therefore seemed impossible to capture the fortress**. || The general’s solution is to **divide the army into small groups**, send them **along different roads** and have them **arrive at the fortress at the same time**. >> Each small group passes safely over the mines. >> Together, the groups make up the full army at the fortress, which is enough to capture it. || The fortress problem has two conflicting requirements. >> A large force is needed at the target. >> Only small forces can travel safely along any one route. Splitting and converging satisfies both.
8|The **fortress problem** and the **radiation problem** have the same structure. >> The fortress picture has many roads leading into a central fortress from all directions. >> The radiation picture has a person’s body with several rays coming from different directions and meeting at the tumor in the stomach. || The elements of the two problems correspond. >> The **fortress** corresponds to the **tumor**. >> The **small groups of soldiers** correspond to the **low-intensity rays**. >> The **roads** correspond to the **paths through healthy tissue**. >> The **mines that a large force would set off** correspond to the **healthy tissue that a strong ray would destroy**. || Both solutions work the same way. >> Weak forces travel safely along separate paths. >> They converge at the target, where their combined strength is enough to do the job.
9|**After reading the fortress story, 30% of participants arrived at the correct solution** to the radiation problem. >> That is three times the 10% who solve it without the story. >> Even so, most participants did not use the analogy. || **Gick and Holyoak** described three steps in analogical problem solving. >> **Noticing an analogous relationship** between the source and the target. This is **crucial, and the most difficult step**. >> **Mapping the correspondence between source and target**. **Elements of the source need to be mapped onto elements of the target**, such as soldiers onto rays. >> **Applying the mapping** **to generate a parallel solution in the target**. || **Seventy percent of participants were not able to see the correspondences** on their own. >> **When participants were told to think about the story they had read, the success rate rose to 75%**. >> The hint did not supply the solution. It only told participants that the story was relevant, so noticing is the main barrier.
10|There are **factors that make the noticing and mapping steps easier**. || **One reason noticing is difficult is that people often focus on surface features**. >> **Surface features** are the specific objects and details in a problem. >> The **fortress** and the **tumor** have completely different surface features. One is about armies and roads, and the other is about rays and medicine. >> Because the surface features differ so much, people do not think of the fortress story when facing the tumor. || This raises a question. **Can making the surface features similar make it easier to notice analogous relationships?** >> If the source problem looked more like the target, people might recognize the connection more easily. >> The **lightbulb problem** tests this.
11|The **lightbulb problem** has surface features much closer to the radiation problem. >> **In a physics lab at a major university, a very expensive lightbulb, which would emit precisely controlled quantities of light, was being used in some experiments**. >> **One morning, Ruth, the research assistant, came into the lab and found that the lightbulb no longer worked**. **She noticed that the filament inside the bulb had broken into two parts**. >> **The surrounding glass bulb was completely sealed, so there was no way to open it**. >> **Ruth knew that the lightbulb could be repaired if a brief, high-intensity laser beam could be used to fuse the two parts of the filament into one**. || **However, a high-intensity laser beam would also break the fragile glass surrounding the filament**. >> **At lower intensities, the laser would not break the glass, but neither would it fuse the filament**. >> **What type of procedure might be used to fuse the filament with the laser and at the same time avoid breaking the glass?** || The solution is to aim **several low-intensity lasers from different directions** so that they **converge on the filament**. >> Each beam is weak enough to pass through the glass without breaking it. >> Together, the beams fuse the filament.
12|**Duncker’s radiation problem was used as the source** for the lightbulb problem. >> **Eighty-one percent of participants who knew about the radiation problem solved the lightbulb problem**. >> **Only 10% of participants in the control group were able to solve it**. || **Surface similarity helped in solving the problem**. >> The radiation problem and the lightbulb problem share many surface features. Both involve beams directed at a small target inside something that must not be damaged. >> The tumor corresponds to the filament, and the healthy tissue corresponds to the fragile glass. || Compared with the fortress story, which helped only 30% of participants, the radiation problem was a much more effective source. >> When the source and target look alike, people notice the connection far more easily.
13|In the **lightbulb problem**, **high surface similarities aid analogical problem solving**. **Surface features** are **the specific elements of a given problem**, such as lasers, glass and filaments. || **Structural features** are a second kind of feature. They are **the underlying principle that governs the solution**. >> In the **radiation** problem, the structural feature is that **a strong ray destroys tissue**. >> In the **lightbulb** problem, the structural feature is that **a strong laser breaks the lightbulb**. >> In both, a strong beam would damage what surrounds the target, which is why the beam must be split. || To separate the two kinds of features, researchers **made two versions of the lightbulb problem**, **keeping the surface features the same and varying the structural features**. >> Both versions use the same lab, the same lightbulb and the same lasers. >> They differ in the reason why a single strong beam cannot be used.
14|**Source problem 1** is the **fragile-glass version**, in which **the source and target have similar structural features**. >> The **problem**. **A high-intensity laser beam would break the fragile glass surrounding the filament**. **At lower intensities, the laser would not break the glass, but neither would it fuse the filament**. >> This matches the radiation problem, where a strong ray would destroy healthy tissue. || **Ruth’s solution**. **Ruth placed several lasers in a circle around the lightbulb and administered low-intensity laser beams from several directions at once**. >> **The beams all converged on the filament, where their combined effect was enough to fuse it**. >> **Because each spot on the surrounding glass received only a low-intensity beam from each laser, the glass was left intact**. || **Sixty-nine percent of participants who read this solution were able to solve the radiation problem**. >> The fragile glass plays the same role as the healthy tissue. >> Because the reason for splitting the beam is the same in both problems, the solution transferred well.
15|**Source problem 2** is the **insufficient-intensity version**, in which **the source and target have different structural features**. >> The **problem**. **The laser generated only low-intensity beams that were not strong enough to fuse the filament**. **A much more intense laser beam was needed**. >> Here there is no danger of breaking the glass. The only obstacle is that each laser is too weak. || **Ruth’s solution** is the same as before. **Ruth placed several lasers in a circle around the lightbulb and administered low-intensity laser beams from several directions at once**. **The beams all converged on the filament, where their combined effect was enough to fuse it**. >> The procedure and the surface features match the fragile-glass version exactly. >> Only the reason for using several beams has changed. || **Only 33% of participants who read this solution were able to solve the radiation problem**. >> This is about half the 69% success after the fragile-glass version. >> The radiation problem’s key constraint is avoiding damage to surrounding tissue, and only the fragile-glass version shares that constraint. Matching structural features matters more than matching surface features.
16|**Making structural features more obvious aids analogical problem solving**. **Structural features** are **the underlying principles that govern the solution to a problem**. >> When people see the principle behind a solution, they can apply it to problems that look very different. || **Analogical encoding** is **the process by which two problems are compared and the similarities between them are determined**. >> It is **an effective way to get participants to pay attention to the structural features that aid problem solving**. >> **Having people compare source stories may be an effective way to make them focus on structural features**. || Comparing two stories brings out what they share. >> Two convergence stories with different surface details, such as soldiers and lasers, both rely on weak forces combining at a target. >> Comparing them pulls out that common principle, which can then be applied to a new problem like the radiation problem.
17|The **analogical paradox** is that **it can be difficult to apply analogies in the laboratory, but people routinely use analogies in real-world settings**. >> **Scientists use about 3 to 15 analogies in a 1-hour lab meeting**. >> In everyday work, people draw on analogies constantly, even though they often fail to use them in experiments. || **In vivo problem-solving research** studies problem solving in natural settings. **People are observed to determine how they solve problems in the real world**. >> **In vivo** means “in the living,” referring to real-life situations as opposed to the laboratory. >> The **advantage** is the **naturalistic setting**, which shows how people actually solve problems in their work. >> The **disadvantages** are that it is **time-consuming** and researchers **cannot isolate and control variables** the way they can in an experiment.
18|An **expert** is **“a person who, by devoting a large amount of time to learning about a field and practicing and applying that learning, has become acknowledged as being extremely knowledgeable or skilled in that field.”** >> A **chess expert**, for example, is **someone who has spent 10,000 to 20,000 hours studying and playing chess**. >> Expertise comes from long, focused experience in one area. || **Experts solve problems in their field faster and with a higher success rate than beginners**. >> They reach correct solutions more often. >> They also reach them more quickly. || The question is **why experts are better**. >> The answers lie in how much experts know and how their knowledge is organized.
19|**Experts possess more knowledge about their fields**. **Chase and Simon (1973)** **compared how well a chess master and a beginner, with fewer than 100 hours of play, were able to reproduce the positions of pieces on a chessboard after looking at an arrangement for 5 seconds**. || The study had **two conditions**. >> **Pieces arranged in actual game positions**. >> **Pieces arranged randomly**. || **Experts did much better than non-experts in the first condition**. >> **They had stored these actual positions in their long-term memory**. >> **The positions were accessed in chunks**, which are groups of pieces that form familiar patterns from real games. >> **These chunks were not available in the random condition**, so the expert’s advantage largely disappeared when the pieces were placed randomly. || The expert’s advantage comes from knowledge. >> The master recognizes meaningful game patterns and stores each pattern as one chunk. >> Random placements match no stored patterns, so the master has no chunks to use.
20|**Experts’ knowledge is organized so it can be accessed when needed to work on a problem**. **Chi and colleagues (1982)** **presented 24 physics problems to a group of experts, who were physics professors, and a group of novices, who were students with one semester of physics**. >> **They asked participants to sort the problems into groups based on their similarities**. || The two groups sorted the problems very differently. >> **Novices sorted based on surface features**, **what the objects looked like**. Problems with similar pictures, such as blocks on slopes, went together. >> **Experts sorted based on structural features**, **the underlying principles involved**. Problems that needed the same physics principle went together, even if they looked different. || Experts organize their knowledge around principles. >> When an expert sees a problem, the principle needed to solve it comes to mind. >> A novice sees the objects in the problem, which may have nothing to do with how it is solved.
21|Examples from the **Chi and colleagues** study reveal the difference between novices and experts. || The **novice** **grouped problems 23 and 24 together because they both involve similar objects, inclined planes**. >> **Problem 23** has a 2-pound block on an inclined plane, with a friction coefficient of 0.2 and an initial velocity of 4 feet per second up the slope. >> **Problem 24** has a block on an inclined plane, with friction and a marked length along the slope. >> Both problems show a block on a slope, so they look alike. || The **expert** **grouped problems 21 and 24 together because they both involve similar physics principles, conservation of energy**. >> **Problem 21** has a spring with a constant of 200 newtons per meter that pushes a block along a flat surface. The spring is compressed 0.15 meters and the block travels 0.6 meters. >> Problem 21 has no slope at all, so it looks nothing like problem 24. >> Both problems are solved using **conservation of energy**, which tracks how energy changes form while the total stays the same. || The same problem, problem 24, ends up in different groups. >> The novice links it by appearance. >> The expert links it by the principle that solves it.
22|**Experts spend more time analyzing a problem**. >> This gives them a **slower start**, **but this strategy usually pays off**. >> Time spent understanding the problem leads to a faster and more accurate solution overall. || **Experts are no better than novices when given problems outside their field**. >> Expertise is tied to a specific domain. >> A chess master has no special advantage in physics, and a physicist has no special advantage in chess. || **Being an expert is not always an advantage**. **Experts are less likely to be open to new ways of looking at problems**. >> **Being an expert may be a disadvantage when confronting a problem that requires flexible thinking**. >> These are **problems whose solution may involve rejecting the usual procedures in favor of other procedures that are less frequently used**. >> An expert’s well-practiced methods can act like a mental set, making it harder to see a new approach.
23|**Creativity** has several aspects. >> **Innovative thinking**. >> **Novel ideas**. >> **New connections between existing ideas**. >> **Divergent thinking**, which is **open-ended** and produces **a large number of potential “solutions.”** Divergent thinking generates many possible answers, rather than narrowing down to one. || Creative problem solving is a **process**. **Most creative problem solving includes far more than just getting an idea**. >> **It involves a lengthy period of trial and error**. || The process has **stages**. >> **Problem generation**, which includes **problem finding** and **fact finding**. >> **Problem formulation**, which includes **problem definition** and **idea finding**. >> **Problem solving**, which includes **evaluation** and **planning**. >> **Solution implementation**, which is **taking action**. || Each stage does a different job. >> The early stages identify and define the problem. >> The later stages test ideas and put the best one into practice.
24|**Generating ideas is only one part of the creative process**. >> **Without ideas, the process stops**. >> Ideas are necessary, but they also have to be evaluated, planned and carried out. || **Too much knowledge can sometimes hinder creative problem solving**. >> **Problems often require flexible thinking and rejecting accepted procedures**. >> Someone with deep knowledge of the usual way of doing things may find it hard to set that way aside. || Creative problem solving balances knowledge with flexibility. >> Knowledge supplies the material for new ideas. >> Flexibility lets the solver set aside familiar procedures when a problem calls for a different approach.
