=== 9:2
Working Memory
• Modal model view of STM turned out to be too simple
– STM is not just passive storage, but also requires “control 
processes,” so we need a model that includes those 
somehow…
• Baddeley and Hitch (1974): Working memory - Similar 
concept to short-term memory
• Working memory: limited capacity system for temporary 
storage and manipulation of information for complex tasks 
such as comprehension, learning, and reasoning

=== 9:3
Working Memory
• “multiply 43 times 6 in your head”
1. Visualize: 43 * 6
2. Multiply 3 * 6 = 18
3. Hold 8 in memory, while carrying the 1 over the 4
4. Multiply 6 * 4 = 24, then add the carried 1 = 25
5. Combine 25 and the 8 you were holding = 258
This operation involves both storage (holding 8 in memory) & active 
processes (carrying the 1…)

=== 9:4
Comparing Memory Models
• Short-term Memory (Atkinson & Shiffrin)
– Single component for all types of info
– Mainly used for holding information for a short time
• Working Memory (Baddeley & Hitch)
– Three components:
• Central Executive
• Visuospatial Sketchpad
• Phonological Loop
• (+ Episodic Buffer, added by Baddeley in 2000)
– Used for manipulation of information during complex cognition

=== 9:5
Components of Working Memory
• Phonological Loop
– Phonological store: Holds verbal and auditory information
– Articulatory rehearsal process: responsible for rehearsal that can keep items in the 
phonological store from decaying
• Visuospatial Sketch Pad
– Holds visual and spatial information
• Central Executive
– Pulls info from long-term memory, coordinates other components, directs and maintains 
attention…

=== 9:7
Phonological Loop
• Phonological similarity effect
– Letters or words that sound similar are confused
• Word-length effect
– Memory for lists of words is better for short words than for long words
– Takes longer to rehearse long words and to produce them during recall
• Articulatory suppression
– Speaking interferes with rehearsal, therefore memory is reduced

=== 9:8
Phonological Similarity Effect
• The basic effect: words that sound similar are confused by people
• Conrad (1964)
– Participants saw target letters (quickly flashed)
– Half of the time the letters were similar and half of the time they weren’t
– Then they wrote them down
– Mistakes were made
• Not likely to replace with something that looked like the target (E for F)
• Likely to replace with something that SOUNDED like the target (E for B)

=== 9:9
Phonological Similarity Effect
• The Phonological Similarity Effect experiment:
– Demonstrated the phonological similarity effect (people confuse letters that 
sound similar)
• even though information was presented visually
– As we’ll see later, it also showed that the phonological loop is necessary for the 
conversion (not just holding info)

=== 9:10
Word-Length Effect
• The basic effect: When memorizing words, you can remember fewer words if the 
words are long
– People remembered more of the short words than long words (Baddeley et al., 
1975, 1984)
– Welsh–English bilingual children have a shorter digit span in Welsh than in English 
(Ellis & Hennelly, 1980)
• Because Welsh numbers take longer to pronounce!
– The number of words you can say in 1.5-2.0 seconds is likely to be your digit span

=== 9:11
Articulatory Suppression
• The basic finding: if you speak while memorizing (which keeps the phonological loop 
busy):
– you get worse at remembering,
– the word length and phonological similarity effects are reduced
• What is going on in these situations? Why does this support the concept of a 
phonological loop?

=== 9:12
Visuospatial Sketch Pad

=== 9:13
Visuospatial Sketch Pad
• Brooks (1968) – the sentence experiment
– Memorize a sentence
– Indicate whether each word is / is not a noun
• Condition 1: indicate by speaking
• Condition 2: indicate by pointing
– Results: pointing was easier than speaking for the participants
• Explanation: Phonological loop was busy processing the sentence, but sketch pad 
was free

=== 9:14
Visuospatial Sketch Pad
• Brooks (1968) – the “F” demo
– Memorize a shape (in this case an F)
– Indicate whether each corner is an “inside corner” 
or an “outside corner”
• Condition 1: Indicate by speaking
• Condition 2: Indicate by pointing
– Results: Speaking is easier than pointing (the 
OPPOSITE of what happened before)
• Explanation: Sketch Pad was busy with image, but 
phonological loop was free

=== 9:15
Visuospatial Sketch Pad
• Tasks are easier when the information being held in mind and the operation being 
performed on it involve different types of short-term memory
– Verbal / Phonological
– Visual / Spatial
• That means that the two types of short-term memory are somewhat independent
– At the least, separate capacities

=== 9:18
The Central Executive
• The Central Executive does the “work” of working 
memory
– Directing and maintaining attention
• Focus, divide, switch attention
– Controls suppression of irrelevant information
– Coordinating sketchpad and phonological loop
– Performing calculations
– Today often split into updating, shifting, and 
inhibition (Miyake et al., 2000)
The idea of a “central executive” is 
often criticized as theorizing a 
“homunculus” in the brain.

=== 9:19
The Episodic Buffer (Baddeley, 2000)
• A fourth component, added to the model in 2000
– Limited capacity (about 4 “chunks”)
– Binds information from the loop, sketch pad, and long-term memory into integrated 
“episodes”
• Why was it needed?
– People can recall ~16 words of a meaningful sentence, far beyond span for unrelated words
– Some amnesic patients can immediately recall a just-heard story
