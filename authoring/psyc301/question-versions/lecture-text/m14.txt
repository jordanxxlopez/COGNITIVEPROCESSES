=== 14:1
Phonological Similarity Effect. Conrad (1964) found that items that sound alike are harder to recall as a list than items that sound different, the phonological similarity effect; sound is what matters, so acoustic similarity effect is arguably the better name. It holds even for silent reading, so no sound has to be presented. The best reading is that we convert what we see into a speech code. With a purely visual code the mix-ups would be between look-alikes such as E and F. Baddeley (1986) built the phonological loop of working memory on this logic. The loop pairs a phonological store for short-lived speech traces with an articulatory control process that recodes visual input and refreshes traces. Murray (1968) kept that process busy with articulatory suppression to block recoding; with visually presented lists the sound-alike cost goes away.

=== 14:2
Neighborhood Size Effect. Roodenrys et al. (2002) showed that long-term memory factors affect short-term memory: a word's neighborhood, the words one letter (orthographic) or one sound (phonological) away, changes how well it is recalled. Cat has many neighbors (large neighborhood); hippopotamus has none (small neighborhood). In their short-term memory task, large neighborhood words were recalled better than small neighborhood words. Stored knowledge about words is boosting short-term performance. Therefore memory span cannot be a clean measure of short-term capacity, because long-term memory contributes to it. 

=== 14:3
Brown-Peterson Task. In the Brown-Peterson task people studied a few letters and then did a distractor task, counting backwards, that stopped rehearsal; Peterson and Peterson (1959) found that the longer the distraction, the worse the recall. Counting is so unlike remembering letters that they judged interference an unlikely cause. Their conclusion was that short-term memory (STM) holds information only briefly and loses it through decay unless it is rehearsed. This went against the view that followed McGeoch's (1932) critique, that forgetting is due to interference. Decay is the original researchers' conclusion, not the only reading: Keppel and Underwood (1962) are cited as offering an alternative explanation. 

=== 14:4
Modality Effect. The modality effect is a memory advantage for the last one or two items of a heard list over a silently read one, and it also shows up with lip-read or mouthed lists.  It strongly shaped thinking about sensory memory, a back-up store holding raw, unanalyzed sensory input that can help recall.  Neath and Surprenant (2003) give the favored explanation: hearing adds a further kind of information, how the list sounds, and this helps the final items. 

=== 14:5
Partial Report. Sperling (1960) challenged the idea that perception caps how much we take in from a brief glance. The cap, he argued, is on recall.  His partial-report method cued observers toward one part of the display as it vanished, and whichever part was cued came out nearly intact. That implies a sensory (iconic) store briefly held almost the whole display. A delayed cue lost the advantage, so the store fades quickly, and full report is limited because items disappear while being reported. 

=== 14:6
Position Error Effect. Order memory shows a clear pattern: each item most often comes back in its own slot.  When it does not, the item usually lands one place away, so the third item of a list is more likely to appear second or fourth than first or last. The errors are systematic, not random.  That means forgetting a position is not the same as losing the item. Percent correct alone makes it look like lost information, but people retain approximate position information. 

=== 14:7
Mental Rotation. Self-descriptions of mental images are unreliable, so imagery is studied with tasks that force it, as in Shepard and Metzler (1971). People judged whether two block shapes were the same or mirror images when one was turned, and said they mentally rotated one shape to match the other. The bigger the turn, the longer the judgment, and the extra time rose at a constant rate. Everyday tasks such as counting the windows in your home seem to rely on a mental image, and these results suggest such images are treated much like real images. Rotating an image takes time, as with a real object. Shepard and Metzler's (1971) findings shaped theories of mental representation. 

=== 14:8
Typical Reasoning. Tversky and Kahneman (1983) showed that probability judgments run on heuristics: fast shortcuts that give up some accuracy. They work day to day but can produce wrong judgments.  The conjunction fallacy shows it. People say that A and B together is more likely than A alone or B alone, even though a combination is never more likely than either part.  Shafir, Smith, and Osherson (1990) had people read a short description of a person and rate how likely certain professions or activities were. They sometimes rated the combination above a single part, which shows reliance on heuristics, not probability rules. 

=== 14:9
Wason Selection. The Wason Selection Task asks which cards to turn over to check "If a card has a D on one side, it has a 3 on the other." Only D and 7 could falsify it. A common choice is D and 3, yet the 3 can only confirm the rule, while the 7 goes unchecked. A familiar rule with identical logic, "If you borrow my car, you must fill up the tank," is far easier, since the borrowed car and the empty tank are the obvious cards to check.  Wason and Shapiro (1971) compared abstract with thematic rules and concluded that relevant experience lets people test a rule, whereas abstract rules leave only logic. 

=== 14:10
Decision Making. How a choice is framed shapes the decision, not just the options themselves. Kahneman and Tversky (1982) summarized many of these framing effects.  What defines a framing effect is that the wording or context changes the choice even though the situations are otherwise equivalent.  Tversky and Kahneman's classic framing studies showed the same choice in two different ways, and gain wording typically led to the sure option while loss wording led to the gamble. 

=== 14:11
Monty Hall. The Monty Hall 3 Door problem is a test of how people handle combinations of events. You pick a door, the host who always plays fair opens an empty one, and the best move is to switch. Always switching wins about two-thirds of the time, always staying about one-third. Why: the first pick holds a 1 in 3 chance, so the other two doors together hold 2 in 3. The reveal does not change your door's chance, so all 2 in 3 now sits behind the one remaining door.  Most people say the two doors are equally likely and that it makes no difference. That is wrong, because the host's choice of which door to open is not random. 

=== 14:12
Risky Decisions. Kahneman and Tversky (1982) summarized many framing effects, in which the same situation described differently draws a different choice, and risky decisions are a prime case.  Researchers set a guaranteed outcome against a gamble with stated chances of winning and losing, and make the guaranteed outcome either a gain or a loss to see what that does to the choice.  Kahneman and Tversky's prospect-theory work gives the typical result: with gains people play it safe and take the sure thing, while with losses they gamble. How an outcome is perceived drives the decision, not only the odds. 

=== 14:13
German & Defeyter (2000): Immunity to functional fixedness in young children. German and Defeyter (2000) asked whether children share the functional fixedness that Duncker (1945) found with the candle problem and Adamson (1952) found in adults. Because problem solving improves with age, the general expectation was that younger children would do worse. Their box task had a toy bear reach a toy on a high shelf by using a box as a support, after the box was or was not shown as a container. Children of 6 and 7 were slowed by the container demonstration, but 5-year-olds were immune and solved faster than the older children in that condition. Without the demonstration, children improved with age. German and Johnson (1997) proposed that young children have a more fluid idea of function, and German and Defeyter predicted the immunity from it. Older children hold function rigidly and must restructure the container use to see a support.

=== 14:14
Leist et al. (2022): Irrelevant speech impairs serial recall of verbal but not spatial items. Salamé and Baddeley (1982), Jones and colleagues, and Cowan (1995) offered competing explanations of why ignored speech disrupts serial recall: phonological traces, order rehearsal, and attention capture. Changing-state speech does more damage than steady-state speech. Leist and colleagues (2022) showed that speech did not disrupt spatial recall in either children or adults, only verbal recall, unlike the equal effects reported by Jones and colleagues (1995). Children were more impaired than adults relative to silence, but the added cost of changing-state speech was about the same in both groups. Leist and colleagues concluded the effect is specific to verbal material, which challenges the attention-capture and changing-state accounts and fits similarity-based interference with phonological codes. 

=== 14:15
Morrison & Richmond (2020): Offloading items from memory. Risko and Gilbert (2016) defined offloading as using actions to reduce a task's mental demands. Morrison and Richmond (2020) tested whether people with weaker working memory offload more, as Risko and Dunn (2015) had reported. Morrison and Richmond (2020) found that offloading helped most at high loads and rose with load, matching Risko and Dunn (2015).  They did not replicate Risko and Dunn's link between memory ability and offloading, and ability did not predict the benefit, so offloading may be a compensatory strategy across abilities. 

=== 14:16
Hoover & Healy (2019): The Bat-and-Ball Problem. Frederick (2005) used the bat-and-ball problem: 10 cents feels right, but 5 cents is correct because the bat must cost a dollar more.  Kahneman and Frederick (2002) proposed attribute substitution, and De Neys, Rossi and Houdé (2013) showed wrong reasoners were less confident than on a control version, a substitution sensitivity.  Hoover and Healy (2019) found wrong reasoners mostly remembered 'more than' but apparently did not use its meaning, evidence for a conscious error process. Because some lost the phrase and many stayed confident, substitution may still occur for some reasoners. Hoover and Healy prefer error sensitivity because they cannot be sure the error was substitution.

=== 14:17
Giuliani et al. (2023): The joint effect of framing and defaults on choice behavior. Giuliani and colleagues (2023) joined the framing effect of Tversky and Kahneman (1981) to the default effect shown in organ donation by Johnson and Goldstein (2003).  They found a classic framing effect and more risk taking with lives than money (as in Fagley and Miller, 1997), though lives were another country's and money was the participant's own. A risky default raised risk taking while a sure default did not raise risk aversion overall, so in some circumstances people are nudged toward risk more easily than toward certainty. Framing and default effects were additive. In Giuliani and colleagues' (2023) exploratory question after the choice, belief in the default as most rational went with following it most, expert advice with indifference, and previous respondents or chance with rejection. Beliefs may partly be justification of a choice already made.

=== 14:18
Chein et al. (2011) and Albert, Chein & Steinberg (2013): Peers and the adolescent brain. Albert, Chein and Steinberg (2013) point to the peer context, citing Gardner and Steinberg (2005), where adolescents, but not adults, took more risks with peers than alone. Their model is a maturational gap between a peer-primed reward system and a slowly maturing cognitive-control system. Chein, Albert, O'Brien, Uckert and Steinberg (2011) scanned a driving game played alone or watched by friends, and found adolescents took significantly more risks when watched. Reward regions (ventral striatum, orbitofrontal cortex) were more active in adolescents when peers watched, going with risky decisions. Chein and colleagues (2011) found cognitive-control regions did not change with peers, so they concluded the effect works mainly through reward circuitry; Albert and colleagues (2013) note the two systems interact. O'Brien et al. (2011) and Weigard et al. (2011) add that peers raise preference for immediate rewards, even when only believed present.
