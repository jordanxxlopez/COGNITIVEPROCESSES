=== 13:7
Context
•
Decisions can depend on the context within 
which they are made
•
Physicians were presented with a hypothetical 
test case involving a possible candidate for 
cesarean section. Decisions were made under 
three different context:
–
Control: the test case was presented first
–
Serious previous cases: test case was preceded by four 
other cases in which there were serious complications
–
Not serious previous cases: Test case was preceded by 
four other cases that were fairly routine 
–
➔ significantly more doctors recommended cesarean 
after reading about non-serious cases first

=== 13:16
Deductive Reasoning
•
Deductive Reasoning: Determining whether a conclusion logically 
follows from premises
•
Syllogism: basic form of deductive reasoning
– Introduced by Aristotle 
– Two statements called premises
– Third statement called conclusion
•
Categorical syllogism
– Describe relation between two categories using all, no, or some
•
Premise 1: All birds are animals
•
Premise 2: All animals eat food
•
Conclusion: Therefore, all birds eat food

=== 13:17
Deductive Reasoning
•
Syllogism is valid if conclusion follows logically from its two premises
•
If two premises of a valid syllogism are true, the syllogism’s 
conclusion must be true
– Do not confuse “validity” with “truth”
• All birds are animals
• All animals have four legs
• All birds have four legs
• Valid! Same form as the first example
– Validity is about whether the conclusion logically follows from the 
premises
– If it does, and the premises are true, then the conclusions must be 
true

=== 13:18
How Well Can People Judge Validity?
•
The difference between validity and truth can make it difficult to 
judge whether reasoning is “logical” or not
–
Valid syllogisms can result in false conclusions
–
Syllogisms can be invalid even though each of the premises and the conclusion 
seem reasonable
•
All of the students are tired
•
Some tired people are irritable
•
Some of the students are irritable
•
Not valid!
•
Many errors in evaluation: good reasoning and truth are not the same 
thing
– Belief bias: The tendency to think that a syllogism is valid if its 
conclusions are believable
– 71% of S judge invalid, believable conclusions as valid

=== 13:20
Mental Model Approach
•
Mental Model approach (Johnson-Laird, 1999): A specific situation 
represented in a person’s mind that can be used to help determine the 
validity of syllogisms in deductive reasoning
– Create a model of a situation
– Generate tentative conclusions about model
– Look for exceptions to falsify model
– Determine validity of syllogism
– ➔ A conclusions is valid only if it cannot be refuted by any model of 
the premises
