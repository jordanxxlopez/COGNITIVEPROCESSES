=== 12:2
Decisions and Reasoning
•
Decisions: The process of making choices between alternatives
•
Reasoning: The process of drawing conclusions
•
Decisions are based on judgments we make, and applying these 
judgments can involve various reasoning processes
•
One of the primary mechanism involved in making judgments in 
inductive reasoning
–
Reasoning based on observations
–
Reaching conclusions from evidence

=== 12:3
Inductive Reasoning
•
Inductive reasoning is the basis of scientific investigations
•
In science: observations are made, data are collected, and conclusions are drawn
–
In inductive reasoning conclusions we reach are probably, but not
definitely, true
•
Conclusions reached are suggested with various degrees of certainty
–
Strength of argument
•
Representativeness of observations
•
Number of observations
•
Quality of observations
Sometimes one very compelling high quality
observation can carry a lot of weight.

=== 12:4
Inductive Reasoning
•
Used to make scientific discoveries
– Hypotheses and general conclusions
•
Used in everyday life
– Make a prediction about what will happen based on observation 
about what has happened in the past
– We make predictions and choices based on past experience, 
especially when predictions are based on familiar situations
• Provides a mechanism for using past experience to guide present behavior
– Some types of inductive reasoning happen automatically

=== 12:5
Heuristics
•
When people use past experience to guide present behavior, they 
often use shortcuts to help them reach conclusions rapidly
•
“Rules of thumb” that are likely to provide the correct answer to a 
problem, but are not foolproof
•
Two more commonly used heuristics include the availability heuristic 
and the representativeness heuristic

=== 12:6
•
Availability heuristic: events more easily remembered are judged as being 
more probable than those less easily remembered
•
Problem lies in factors that make events stand out in memory (e.g., 
distinctiveness, emotional salience, frequency of encoding / retrieval)
•
Availability is used to judge the probability of events
•
You use availability by trying to call to mind examples
•
The more examples you think of, the more probable you judge the 
event to be
Heuristics

=== 12:14
Heuristics
•
Law of large numbers: the larger the number of individuals 
randomly drawn from a population, the more representative the 
resulting group will be of the entire population
–
People often assume that representativeness holds for small samples, and this 
results in errors in reasoning

=== 12:15
Heuristics
•
Illusory correlations: correlation appears to exist, but either does not 
exist or is much weaker than assumed
•
They can occur when we expect two things to be related, so we fool 
ourselves into thinking they are related even when they are not
– Stereotypes: Oversimplified generalizations about a group or class 
of people that often focuses on the negative
• A stereotype about a group may lead people to pay particular attention to 
behaviors associated with that stereotype
• This attention creates an illusory correlation that reinforces the stereotype
• Related to the availability heuristic because selective attention to the 
stereotypical behaviors makes these behaviors more “available”

=== 12:18
Decision-Making
•
Decision making: making judgments that involve choices between 
different courses of action
•
Utility: outcomes that are desirable because they are in the person’s 
best interest
•
Expected utility theory:
– People are rational
– If they have all relevant information, they will make a decision 
that results in the maximum expected utility
– Maximum monetary payoff

=== 12:19
Decision-Making
•
Advantages for utility approach
– Specific procedures to determine the “best choice”
•
Problems for utility approach
– Not necessarily money, people find value in other things
– Many decisions do not maximize the probability of the best 
outcome
• ➔people are not utility maximization machines
• e.g. people gamble in casinos all the time, even though they know that the 
odds are very much against them

=== 12:22
An Example Dilemma
•
A convoy of food trucks is on its way to a refugee camp during a 
famine. (Airplanes cannot be used.) You find that a second camp 
has even more refugees. If you tell the convoy to go to the second 
camp instead of the first, you will save 1000 people from death, but 
100 people in the first camp will die as a result.
– Would you send the convoy to the second camp?
• U(notReRoute)=100-1000
• U(Reroute)=1000-100
– Ritov and Baron (1999): 63% did not choose to send the convoy 
to the second camp
• 12% responded that they would not change the route of the convoy 
even if only 1 person in the first camp would die

=== 12:23
Tetlock, Kristel, Elson, Green, Lerner (2000)
Sacred Value Protection Model
•
People want to protect what they hold sacred
– Sacred values are compartmentalized separately from secular values.
– When the two domains are intermingled, people feel threatened and 
they respond with moral outrage (Taboo).
– In contrast, when two sacred values are compared with each other, 
people are less morally outraged (Tragic).

=== 12:24
Tetlock, Kristel, Elson, Green, Lerner (2000)
•
Taboo Tradeoff Scenario
Robert can save the life of Johnny, a five year old who 
needs a liver transplant, but the transplant procedure 
will cost the hospital $1,000,000 that could be spent in 
other ways, such as purchasing better equipment and 
enhancing salaries to recruit talented doctors to the 
hospital. Johnny is very ill and has been on the waiting list 
for a transplant but because of the shortage of local 
organ donors, obtaining a liver will be expensive. Robert 
could save Johnny's life, or he could use the $1,000,000 
for other hospital needs.
•
Tragic Trade-off Scenario
There are two children who need a liver transplant, but 
only one liver.
