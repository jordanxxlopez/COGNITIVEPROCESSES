2|Emotions help us make decisions. **People with damage to an area of their prefrontal cortex, who suffer from flattened emotions, have impaired decision making.** >> Flattened emotions are weakened emotions, so the person feels much less than usual. >> When emotion drops, decisions get worse, not better. || **Anxious people tend to avoid making decisions that could potentially lead to large negative consequences.** This is called **risk avoidance**. >> A choice that could end in a big loss feels too threatening. >> So the anxious person skips it, even if it might work out. || **Optimistic people are more likely to ignore negative information and focus on positive information.** >> **Too much optimism can lead to poor decision making.** >> Ignoring the bad news means deciding without the whole picture. || Each emotional state changes which information a person weighs. >> Too little emotion impairs the decision itself. >> Anxiety pulls attention toward possible large losses. >> Too much optimism pulls attention away from negative information.
3|**Expected emotions** are **emotions that people predict they will feel concerning an outcome**. >> Before we choose, we imagine how a good or bad result would feel. >> That guess about our feelings then guides the choice. || **Expected emotions are one of the determinants of risk aversion**, the **tendency to avoid taking risks**. >> If we expect a loss to feel terrible, we are less willing to risk it. >> The predicted feeling pushes us toward the safer option. || **People inaccurately predict their emotions.** >> We tend to believe that **a loss will have a greater impact than a gain of the same size**. >> That belief **increases risk aversion**, so we turn down risks that would be reasonable if gains and losses counted equally. || The link between expected emotions and risk works through prediction. >> A person cannot feel an outcome before it happens, so the choice rests on a guess. >> When the guess makes losses feel bigger than equal gains, the person avoids risks more than the real feelings would justify.
4|**Kermer and colleagues (2006)** **compared people’s expected emotions with their actual emotions**. >> Participants were **given $5**. >> A coin flip would decide whether they **won another $5 or lost $3**. >> They **rated their happiness before the experiment started**, which set their baseline. || Participants then **predicted how they would feel if they won or lost**. >> They expected losing $3 to drop their happiness by about 4 points. >> They expected winning $5 to raise it by only about 2 points. >> So the smaller amount of money was expected to matter about twice as much. || After the flip, the real feelings were much milder. >> Losing lowered happiness by only about 1 point, and winning raised it by about 1.2 points. >> People **overestimated their negative feeling**. >> **We have coping mechanisms that kick in after a loss, but we don’t consider them when we predict our emotions.**
5|**Incidental emotions** are **emotions that are not specifically related to decision making**. >> They can come from our **general disposition or personality**, our usual emotional tendency. >> They can come from **some recent experience** that left a mood behind. >> They can come from our **general environment or surroundings**, such as the weather. || **Simonsohn (2007)** studied **university admissions decisions**. >> **Applicants’ academic attributes were weighted more heavily on cloudy days than on sunny days.** >> The weather had nothing to do with the applicants, yet it changed what the decision makers focused on. || **Simonsohn (2009)** studied **students visiting an academically highly rated university**. >> They **were more likely to enroll if they had visited on a cloudy day**. >> A cloudy day says nothing about the school, yet it carried into a big decision. || These findings matter because the emotions enter decisions without being part of them. >> A cloudy day is unrelated to an applicant’s record or to a university’s quality. >> Even so, cloudy weather went with weighting academic attributes more heavily and with enrolling after a visit.
6|**Lerner and colleagues (2004)** had participants watch one of three film clips. >> **A person dying** set up the **sad** condition. >> **A person using a dirty toilet** set up the **disgust** condition. >> **Fish at the Great Barrier Reef** set up the **neutral** condition. || Next, participants got **a set of highlighters** and **set two prices**. >> One was the price **they would sell the highlighters** for. >> The other was the price **they would pay to buy them**. || The prices changed with the mood. >> Selling prices were about $4.50 for neutral, $2.70 for disgust and $3.00 for sad. >> Buying prices were about $3.50 for neutral, $3.00 for disgust and $4.50 for sad. >> Disgust lowered both prices, while sadness lowered the selling price and raised the buying price. || **A person’s mood can influence decision making.** >> The clips had nothing to do with highlighters, so these are incidental emotions at work.
7|**Decisions can depend on the context they are made in.** **Physicians were given a hypothetical test case of a possible candidate for cesarean section**, a surgical delivery of a baby. >> Every physician saw the very same test case. || The test case came in **three different contexts**. >> In the **control** context, **it came first**. >> In the **serious previous cases** context, **it came after four cases with serious complications**. >> In the **not serious previous cases** context, **it came after four fairly routine cases**. || **Significantly more doctors recommended cesarean after reading about non-serious cases first.** >> The test case never changed, so the earlier cases must have changed the decision. >> Even experts are swayed by what they have just seen. || Physicians are experts, yet their judgment of the same case still shifted. >> The four earlier cases set a standard for judging the fifth. >> After four routine cases, the test case led to more recommendations for cesarean than in the other contexts.
8|**Decisions depend on how choices are presented.** >> In an **opt-in procedure**, you must take an **active step to become an organ donor**, and those countries have **less than a 28% consent rate**. >> In an **opt-out procedure**, you are an **organ donor unless you ask not to be**, and those countries have **over a 99% consent rate**. || The **status quo bias** is **the tendency to do nothing when faced with a difficult decision**. >> Under opt-in, doing nothing means not donating. >> Under opt-out, doing nothing means donating. >> Since many people do nothing, the default decides the outcome. || In the story **The International Community**, six birds are called to a summit. >> The king’s chef asks what sauce they would like to be eaten with. >> When one bird says it does not want to be eaten at all, the chef says that is out of the question. >> The only choice offered is the sauce, so not being eaten was never on the menu.
9|The **framing effect** means **decisions are influenced by how a decision is stated**. >> The wording **can highlight one aspect of the situation**, like what we might gain or lose. >> The outcomes can stay the same while the focus changes. || **Tversky and Kahneman (1981)** studied how framing changes risk taking. >> **When choices are framed as gains, people tend toward risk aversion** and take the sure thing. >> **When choices are framed as losses, people tend toward risk taking** and take the gamble. >> The same people switch strategies when only the wording changes. || Framing works by changing what the statement draws attention to. >> A statement about lives saved points to what can be gained. >> A statement about deaths points to what can be lost. >> The disease problem tests both frames with the same outcomes.

11|**A disease is expected to kill 600 people**, and **two programs have been proposed to fight it**. Here the outcomes are stated as lives saved, which is a gain frame. >> With **Program A**, **200 people will be saved** for sure. >> With **Program B**, there is **a 1/3 chance that all 600 are saved and a 2/3 chance that no one is saved**. || **72% chose Program A**, and 28% chose Program B. >> Program A is the sure thing, and Program B is the gamble. >> Most people locked in 200 lives instead of risking saving no one. >> That is the risk-averse choice a gain frame encourages. || The gamble in Program B can save everyone, but it is more likely to save no one. >> The 1/3 chance of saving all 600 sits beside a 2/3 chance of saving none. >> Most people preferred a guaranteed 200 over that gamble.
12|Now the same disease is expected to kill 600 people, but the outcomes are stated as deaths, which is a loss frame. >> With **Program C**, **400 people will die** for sure. >> With **Program D**, there is **a 1/3 chance that no one dies and a 2/3 chance that all 600 die**. || **78% chose Program D**, and 22% chose Program C. >> Program D is the gamble, so most people now took the risk. || The two versions describe the same outcomes. >> Saving 200 of 600 people is the same as 400 dying, so A and C match. >> B and D are the same gamble. >> Only the wording changed, and that switch in choices is the **framing effect**. || The shift between the two versions is large. >> In the gain frame, 72% chose the sure option. >> In the loss frame, only 22% chose the sure option. >> The same outcomes produced opposite majorities, depending only on how they were described.
13|**Neuroeconomics** **combines research from psychology, neuroscience, and economics**. >> Psychology looks at how people think and feel. >> Neuroscience looks at what the brain is doing. >> Economics looks at choices about money and value. || **One finding is that decisions are influenced by emotions, and those emotions are linked to activity in specific areas of the brain.** >> Emotion is part of the decision process. >> We can trace its role to particular brain areas. >> Even a choice about money can depend on how we feel about it. || Neuroeconomics brings these fields together to study the same choice from several sides. >> Economics can describe what a rational choice would be. >> Psychology and neuroscience can test whether real choices follow that standard and what the brain is doing when they do not. >> The ultimatum game is one way to make that test.
14|**Sanfey and coworkers (2003)** used the **ultimatum game**, which has **two players**, a **proposer** and a **responder**. >> The **proposer gets $10**. >> The proposer **offers the responder a way to split the money**. || The **responder can accept or reject the offer**. >> **If the responder rejects, neither player gets anything.** >> So a rejection costs both players money. || **According to utility theory, what is the rational decision for the responder?** The answer is to **accept any offer**. >> Utility theory judges a choice by the value of its outcome. >> Even $1 is worth more than the nothing you get by rejecting. >> So a responder following utility theory would never turn down money. || The game sets money against fairness. >> The proposer decides the split, but the responder decides whether anyone is paid. >> A very uneven offer leaves the responder choosing between a small amount and nothing, which is where fairness and money pull in different directions.
15|**In Sanfey’s experiment, participants played 20 separate games**, **10 with human partners and 10 with computer partners**. >> Even $5 and $5 offers were accepted about 100% of the time, and $7 and $3 offers about 95%, from either partner. >> An $8 and $2 offer was accepted about 53% of the time from a human and about 85% from a computer. >> A $9 and $1 offer was accepted about 37% of the time from a human and about 68% from a computer. || People did not follow utility theory, which says to accept every offer. >> They **often rejected low offers because they became angry that the offers were unfair**. >> They were **less angry with an unfair computer**, so they accepted its low offers more often. || **More activation of the right anterior insula**, an area **connected with emotional states**, went with **rejecting more offers**. >> Rejecting meant walking away with nothing, so emotion led people to give up money. >> **Emotion is important in decision making.**
16|**Deductive reasoning** is **determining whether a conclusion logically follows from premises**. >> A premise is a statement we start reasoning from. >> The only question is whether the conclusion follows from those premises. || A **syllogism** is the **basic form of deductive reasoning**, **introduced by Aristotle**. >> It has **two statements called premises**. >> It has a **third statement called the conclusion**. || A **categorical syllogism** **describes how two categories relate using all, no, or some**. >> **All birds are animals.** >> **All animals eat food.** >> So, **all birds eat food**, because birds sit inside animals, and animals sit inside things that eat food. || Deductive reasoning differs from judging whether something is likely. >> A deductive conclusion either follows from the premises or it does not. >> The bird syllogism follows, because the categories fit inside each other.
17|**A syllogism is valid if its conclusion follows logically from its two premises.** >> **If the two premises of a valid syllogism are true, its conclusion must be true.** >> Validity is about the form of the reasoning, while truth is about whether the statements match the world. || **Do not confuse validity with truth.** >> All birds are animals, all animals have four legs, so all birds have four legs. >> This is **valid**, because it has the **same form as the first example**. >> Its conclusion is false only because the second premise is false. || **Validity is about whether the conclusion logically follows from the premises.** >> **If it does and the premises are true, the conclusion must be true.** >> A false premise can still lead a valid syllogism to a false conclusion. || The two bird syllogisms make validity and truth easy to separate. >> Both have the same form, so both are valid. >> The first has true premises and a true conclusion, while the second has a false premise and a false conclusion.
18|**The difference between validity and truth makes it hard to judge whether reasoning is logical.** >> **Valid syllogisms can lead to false conclusions.** >> **Syllogisms can be invalid even when every premise and the conclusion sound reasonable.** || All of the students are tired, some tired people are irritable, so some of the students are irritable. >> Each statement sounds fine. >> The syllogism is **not valid**, because the irritable tired people might not include any students. || **People make many errors here, because good reasoning and truth are not the same thing.** >> **Belief bias** is **the tendency to think a syllogism is valid if its conclusion is believable**. >> **71% of participants judged invalid syllogisms with believable conclusions as valid.** || The students syllogism fails because of the word some. >> Some tired people are irritable leaves open which tired people they are. >> All the students being tired does not place any of them among the irritable ones, so the conclusion does not follow.
19|**There is no easy method for telling whether a syllogism is valid or invalid.** >> We still need some way to work out what follows from a set of statements. || In the pool table problem, **a black ball is directly above the cue ball**, **the green ball is on the right side of the cue ball**, and **there is a red ball between them**. >> You move so that the red ball is between you and the black ball. >> Which side of your line of sight is the cue ball on now? || The answer is **the left**. >> Picture the black ball above the cue ball, with the red ball and then the green ball off to the cue ball’s right. >> Line yourself up so the red ball blocks the black ball, and the cue ball sits off to your left. || **Most people solve this by imagining how the balls are arranged on the table.** >> **This ability to imagine situations is the basis of Johnson-Laird’s proposal that people use mental models in deductive reasoning.**
20|In the **mental model approach (Johnson-Laird, 1999)**, a **mental model** is **a specific situation represented in a person’s mind that can help determine whether a syllogism is valid**. >> We reason from an imagined situation that fits the premises. || The approach works in steps. >> **Create a model of the situation.** >> **Generate tentative conclusions about the model**, which are conclusions that might hold. >> **Look for exceptions that falsify the model**, cases where the premises are true but the conclusion turns out false. >> **Determine whether the syllogism is valid.** || **A conclusion is valid only if no model of the premises can refute it.** >> To refute a conclusion is to prove it can be false. >> Finding even one exception makes the conclusion invalid. || The search for exceptions is what turns a model into a test of validity. >> A tentative conclusion may fit the first model a person builds. >> The person then tries other ways the premises could be true. >> If none of them refutes the conclusion, that conclusion is valid.
21|**Conditional syllogisms have two premises and a conclusion.** >> **The first premise has the form If p, then q.** >> The letter p stands for a condition, and q stands for what follows when it is met. || **There are four major types of conditional syllogism.** >> All four share the first premise. >> They differ in the second premise and the conclusion. >> Syllogism 1 goes from p to q, syllogism 2 from not q to not p, syllogism 3 from q to p, and syllogism 4 from not p to not q. || Two types are valid and two are not. >> Syllogisms 1 and 2 are valid, and people got them right 97% and 60% of the time. >> Syllogisms 3 and 4 are not valid, and people got each right only 40% of the time. || Comparing the four types in letters makes the pattern clear. >> The valid types either confirm p or deny q. >> The invalid types either confirm q or deny p.
22|**Conditional syllogism 1** is called **modus ponens**. >> If I study, I’ll get a good grade. >> I studied. >> Therefore, I’ll get a good grade. || In letters, the second premise is p and the conclusion reads therefore q. >> The condition happened, so what follows from it must happen too. >> That works no matter what p and q stand for. || This syllogism is **valid**, and **97%** of participants judged it correctly. >> It is the easiest of the four types. >> The reasoning moves in the same direction as the rule. || Modus ponens confirms the condition and concludes the result. >> Knowing that studying happened, and that studying leads to a good grade, settles that the good grade follows. >> Nearly everyone accepts this form.
23|**Conditional syllogism 2** is called **modus tollens**. >> If I study, I’ll get a good grade. >> I didn’t get a good grade. >> Therefore, I didn’t study. || In letters, the second premise is not q and the conclusion reads therefore not p. >> If studying always brings a good grade, then no good grade means no studying. >> That works no matter what p and q stand for. || This syllogism is **valid**, but only **60%** of participants judged it correctly. >> It is just as valid as modus ponens. >> It feels harder because it reasons backward from a result that did not happen. || Modus tollens denies the result and concludes that the condition did not happen. >> If the condition had happened, the result would have had to follow. >> Since the result did not happen, the condition could not have happened either.
24|**Conditional syllogism 3** reasons from the result back to the condition. >> If I study, I’ll get a good grade. >> I got a good grade. >> Therefore, I studied. || In letters, the second premise is q and the conclusion reads therefore p. >> This syllogism is **not valid**, and only **40%** of participants judged it correctly. >> The rule never says studying is the only way to get a good grade. || Other content makes the mistake easy to see. >> If I live in Tucson, then I live in Arizona. I live in Arizona. Therefore, I live in Tucson. >> Plenty of people live in Arizona without living in Tucson. || The error in syllogism 3 is treating If p, then q as if it also meant If q, then p. >> The first premise runs only from studying to the good grade. >> It does not run from the good grade back to studying.
25|**Conditional syllogism 4** reasons from a missing condition to a missing result. >> If I study, I’ll get a good grade. >> I didn’t study. >> Therefore, I didn’t get a good grade. || In letters, the second premise is not p and the conclusion reads therefore not q. >> This syllogism is **not valid**, and only **40%** of participants judged it correctly. >> The rule says nothing about what happens without studying, and a good grade could come some other way. || Across all four types, the valid ones were judged correctly more often. >> Modus ponens reached 97% and modus tollens 60%. >> Both invalid forms reached only 40%. || Syllogism 4 makes the same kind of error from the other side. >> If p, then q tells what happens when p is true. >> It says nothing about what happens when p is false, so denying p settles nothing about q.
26|The **Wason four-card problem** tests the **effect of using real-world items in a conditional reasoning problem**. >> The cards read **E**, **K**, **4** and **7**. >> The task is to **find the fewest cards to turn over to test the rule that if one side has a vowel, the other side has an even number**. || **E needs to be turned over.** >> **46% said the 4 needed to be turned over**, which is a mistake. >> **Only 7% said the 7 needed to be turned over**, which is correct. || The right answer is E and 7. >> An odd number behind the E would break the rule. >> A vowel behind the 7 would also break it. >> The 4 can never break the rule, and the K is not a vowel, so neither one tests it.
27|The **falsification principle** says that **to test a rule, you must look for situations that could falsify it**. >> To falsify a rule is to prove it false. >> **Most participants fail to do this**, which is why so few picked the 7. || **When the problem is stated in concrete everyday terms, correct answers greatly increase.** >> The rule becomes **If drinking beer, then 21 years old**. >> The cards read **beer**, **soda**, **16 years old** and **21 years old**. >> **73%** of participants got this version right. >> Using the falsification principle, the cards to check are **beer** and **16 years old**, because a beer drinker under 21 is the only case that breaks the rule. || The logic is the same as the vowel and number rule. >> Only the content changed, from letters to drinking and age. >> That change alone lifted performance far above the 7% who chose the 7 card.
28|A **permission schema** is the idea that **if A is satisfied, B can be carried out**. >> A schema is a familiar pattern of knowledge. >> In the beer rule, being 21 is the condition, and drinking beer is what it allows. || **Permission schemas are used in the concrete versions**, since **people are familiar with rules** like this. >> If someone is drinking beer, we know to check their age. >> If someone is 16, we know to check what they are drinking. || **Context is important**, because the same logic gets easier in a familiar setting. >> **Familiarity is not always important**, so familiarity alone does not explain the improvement.
