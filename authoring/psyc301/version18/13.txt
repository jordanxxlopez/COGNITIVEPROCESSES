2|**Decisions** are **the process of making choices between alternatives**, and **reasoning** is **the process of drawing conclusions**. >> Picking a class or a route home is a decision. >> Working out what the evidence implies is reasoning. || **Decisions are based on the judgments we make, and applying these judgments can involve various reasoning processes**. >> A **judgment** is an estimate of how likely or how good something is. >> Reasoning yields a judgment about each option, and the decision picks an option based on those judgments. >> Judging that rain is likely leads to the decision to bring an umbrella. Errors in judgment pass straight into poor decisions, so the shortcuts behind our judgments matter a great deal. || **One of the primary mechanisms involved in making judgments is inductive reasoning**. >> **Inductive reasoning** is **reasoning based on observations**. >> It means **reaching conclusions from evidence**, going from particular cases to a general conclusion. >> It turns what we have seen before into expectations about what comes next, so observations lead to judgments and judgments lead to choices.
3|**Inductive reasoning is the basis of scientific investigations**. **In science, observations are made, data are collected, and conclusions are drawn**, and scientists propose a general rule that fits the many cases they have observed. || **In inductive reasoning, the conclusions we reach are probably, but not definitely, true**. >> **The conclusions reached are suggested with various degrees of certainty**. >> A new observation can always overturn a conclusion drawn from earlier ones. || The **strength of an inductive argument** rests on three factors. >> **Representativeness of the observations**, meaning how well they stand for the whole category. Many observations from a narrow group can still lead to a wrong conclusion. >> **Number of observations**, since more observations give stronger support. >> **Quality of the observations**, since careful, accurate observations count for more. >> **Sometimes one very compelling, high-quality observation can carry a lot of weight**. || That last factor follows from what induction is. An inductive conclusion is a general claim built from particular cases, so one clear counterexample can prove it false, however many cases supported it before.
4|**Inductive reasoning is used to make scientific discoveries**, by building **hypotheses and general conclusions** from observations. || **Inductive reasoning is also used in everyday life**. >> We **make a prediction about what will happen based on observations of what has happened in the past**. >> **We make predictions and choices based on past experience, especially when predictions are based on familiar situations**. >> Familiar situations supply many past observations, which makes predictions about them dependable, while unfamiliar situations supply few. || Inductive reasoning **provides a mechanism for using past experience to guide present behavior**. >> It usually works because the world is fairly regular, so past patterns tend to continue. >> **Some types of inductive reasoning happen automatically**, without deliberate thought, so they shape our expectations all the time. >> Automatic induction saves time and effort. That same speed leaves it open to the systematic errors that heuristics produce.
5|**When people use past experience to guide present behavior, they often use shortcuts to help them reach conclusions rapidly**. These shortcuts are **heuristics**. >> **Heuristics** are **“rules of thumb” that are likely to provide the correct answer to a problem, but are not foolproof**. >> Most everyday judgments must be made fast and with limited information, so a quick rule of thumb is practical. >> Its answer is usually close enough for everyday purposes. || **Two commonly used heuristics are the availability heuristic and the representativeness heuristic**. >> The **availability heuristic** judges probability by how easily examples come to mind. >> The **representativeness heuristic** judges probability by how closely something resembles a typical case. || Heuristics give up some accuracy in exchange for speed. >> Each reaches a fast answer from one simple cue. >> Because each depends on a particular cue, it fails in a predictable way whenever that cue points away from the truth. >> Those systematic errors reveal how each heuristic works.
6|The **availability heuristic** is the tendency for **events that are more easily remembered to be judged as more probable than events that are less easily remembered**. >> **Availability is used to judge the probability of events**. >> **We use availability by trying to call examples to mind**. >> **The more examples we can think of, the more probable we judge the event to be**. || The heuristic rests on a sensible link. >> Frequent events leave many memories, so examples come easily. >> Rare events are met seldom, so examples are hard to find. Ease of recall usually tracks frequency. || **The problem lies in the factors that make events stand out in memory**. >> **Distinctiveness**, since unusual events are easier to remember. >> **Emotional salience**, since emotionally powerful events stick. >> **Frequency of encoding and retrieval**, since events we hear or think about often are easier to recall. >> These factors make events easy to recall regardless of how often they truly happen. When drama or heavy news coverage makes an event memorable, a rare event can feel common.
7|The availability heuristic affects judgments about words. **Which are more common in English, words that begin with the letter r or words in which r is the third letter?** >> **Seventy percent of people respond that more words begin with r**. >> **In reality, three times more words have r in the third position**. || Availability produces this error. >> The question is about the true size of each category, but people answer by how easily examples come to mind. >> Words starting with r are easy to retrieve, because memory lets us search words by their first letter. >> No such search works for the third letter, so those words are hard to retrieve even though they are far more numerous. || The easily retrieved words feel more common even though they are much rarer. >> The error comes from how retrieval works. >> It does not reflect any gap in knowledge about English words.
8|The availability heuristic also affects judgments about **causes of death**. People chose which cause in each pair was more likely to kill someone in the United States in the coming year. >> For several pairs, many people picked the less likely cause. >> **Asthma** is 920 times more likely than **botulism**, yet 41% picked botulism. >> **Asthma** is also much more likely than a **tornado**, yet most people picked the tornado. || **These errors are due to the availability of the causes**. >> **When people die of botulism, it is front-page news**. >> **Deaths from asthma go virtually unnoticed**. >> Dramatic, rare causes get heavy coverage, so examples come to mind easily. Common, quiet causes get little notice, so they seem less likely than they are. || The errors follow media attention. People judge likelihood by ease of recall, and news coverage decides what is easy to recall. A far more common cause can still lose to a rare one that makes headlines.
9|The **representativeness heuristic** is the tendency for **people to make judgments based on how much one event resembles another event**. >> **The probability that A is a member of class B is judged by how well the properties of A resemble the properties normally associated with class B**. >> The closer a person matches our image of a category, the likelier we think they belong to it. || **America has about 2.2 million farms and 120,000 libraries**. >> **We randomly pick one male from the population of the US**. **That male, Robert, wears glasses, speaks quietly and reads a lot**. **Is it more likely that Robert is a librarian or a farmer?** || **More people predict that Robert is a librarian**. >> His description fits the usual image of a librarian, and the fit feels like strong evidence. >> People **ignore the base rate of farmers and librarians in the US**. A **base rate** is how common a category is in the population. >> Farms far outnumber libraries, so farmers far outnumber male librarians. Even if Robert’s traits are somewhat more common among librarians, a randomly picked man is much more likely to be a farmer.
10|People use base rates in a consistent pattern. >> They **use base rate information if it is all that is available**. >> They **use descriptive information if it is available and disregard base rate information**. || With base rates alone, people reason correctly. >> **In a group of 100 people, there are 70 lawyers and 30 engineers**. **What is the chance that a person picked from the group at random will be an engineer?** >> **People get this right** and answer 30%. || Once a description is added, people drop the base rates. >> **Jack is a 45-year-old man**. **He is married and has four children**. **He is generally conservative, careful and ambitious**. **He shows no interest in political and social issues and spends most of his free time on his many hobbies, which include home carpentry, sailing and mathematical puzzles**. >> **People say Jack is an engineer**. >> His careful nature and love of puzzles fit the engineer stereotype, and that fit outweighs the base rate. >> Nothing in the description rules out a lawyer, so the base rate should still count. Representativeness makes the description feel decisive anyway.
11|The **conjunction rule** says that **the probability of two events together cannot be higher than the probability of either single event alone**. >> A **conjunction** means two things being true at once. >> Anything in the combined category also belongs to each single category, so the combined category can never be bigger. || **Linda is 31 years old, single, outspoken and very bright**. **She majored in philosophy**. **As a student, she was deeply concerned with issues of discrimination and social justice, and also participated in antinuclear demonstrations**. **Which of the following is more probable?** >> **1. Linda is a bank teller**. >> **2. Linda is a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement**. || **Eighty-two percent pick option 2**, which breaks the conjunction rule. >> Feminist bank tellers are a smaller group inside the group of all bank tellers. >> A group contained inside a larger one can never be more probable than the larger one. >> Linda sounds like a feminist, so the feminist option feels more like her. That sense of fit overrides the logic, which is the representativeness heuristic producing an error.
12|**People also make errors in reasoning by ignoring the importance of the size of the sample on which observations are based**. >> **A certain town is served by two hospitals**. **In the larger hospital, about 45 babies are born each day, and in the smaller hospital, about 15 babies are born each day**. >> **About 50% of all babies are boys**. **However, the exact percentage varies from day to day**. **Sometimes it may be higher than 50%, sometimes lower**. >> **For a period of one year, each hospital recorded the days on which more than 60% of the babies born were boys**. **Which hospital recorded more such days?** || The choices were **1, the larger hospital**, **2, the smaller hospital**, and **3, about the same**. >> The larger hospital and the smaller hospital were each chosen by **22%**. >> **Fifty-six percent chose about the same**. || Most people expected both hospitals to have unusual days equally often. >> They fixed on the 50% figure that applies to both. >> They overlooked how the number of births per day changes how often the daily percentage strays from that figure. >> The question is about day-to-day variation, which depends on how many babies are born each day as well as on the average rate of boys.
13|For the **hospital problem**, **the correct answer is 2, the smaller hospital**, which only 22% of people chose. || The key is **sample size**, which controls how far a percentage can drift from the true value by chance. >> With about 15 births a day, chance swings in the share of boys are large, and a day with mostly boys happens fairly often. >> With about 45 births a day, the share stays nearer 50%, because chance variations even out over more births. >> The smaller hospital therefore records many more days with over 60% boys. || **People make errors in reasoning by ignoring the importance of the size of the sample on which observations are based**. >> Small samples stray far from the true average much more often than large ones. >> People who answered about the same were using representativeness, expecting every sample to look like the population. >> The principle applies to any judgment from a small sample, since small groups produce extreme percentages by chance far more often than large groups do.
14|The **law of large numbers** says that **the larger the number of individuals randomly drawn from a population, the more representative the resulting group will be of the entire population**. >> A large random sample evens out chance variation, so it closely matches its population. >> A small random sample can be thrown off by a few unusual cases, so it may differ a lot from the population. || **People often assume that representativeness holds for small samples, and this results in errors in reasoning**. >> In the hospital problem, people expected a small day of births to look as balanced as a large one. >> Small samples often look unrepresentative, and the law of large numbers explains why. || The law accounts for the hospital result. >> The larger hospital’s daily births form a bigger sample, so its share of boys stays near 50%. >> The smaller hospital’s daily births form a smaller sample, so its share swings more widely and passes 60% more often. >> Expecting a small sample to mirror the population is a common error that comes from applying representativeness where sample size matters.
15|**Illusory correlations** occur when **a correlation appears to exist, but either does not exist or is much weaker than assumed**. A **correlation** is a relationship in which one thing tends to go along with another. || **Illusory correlations can occur when we expect two things to be related, so we fool ourselves into thinking they are related even when they are not**. What we expect guides what we notice, and what we notice guides what we believe. >> **Stereotypes** are **oversimplified generalizations about a group or class of people that often focus on the negative**. >> **A stereotype about a group may lead people to pay particular attention to behaviors associated with that stereotype**. >> **This attention creates an illusory correlation that reinforces the stereotype**. People notice and remember the cases that fit and miss the many that do not. || Illusory correlation is **related to the availability heuristic**. >> **Selective attention to stereotypical behaviors makes these behaviors more “available”** in memory. >> Those easily recalled examples are then taken as proof of the stereotype, so it seems more accurate than it is.
16|**Confirmation bias** shapes how people judge evidence. **Lord and coworkers (1979)** examined it using capital punishment. >> **They had people in favor of capital punishment and people against it read the same article**. >> The article presented evidence for both sides. || Each group read it differently. >> **People in favor found the article to support their view**. >> **People against found the article to support their view**. >> The same information strengthened opposite beliefs, because supporters dwelled on the parts favoring capital punishment and opponents on the parts against it. || This is the **“myside” bias**, the **tendency for people to generate and evaluate evidence and test their hypotheses in a way that is biased toward their own opinions and attitudes**. >> **Participants focused on information that agreed with their beliefs and disregarded information that did not**. >> People give extra weight to agreeing evidence and find reasons to dismiss the rest. >> Both groups received identical, balanced evidence, so the opposite conclusions came entirely from how each group judged it, and each side left more certain than before.
17|**The myside bias is a type of confirmation bias**. **Confirmation bias** is the **tendency to selectively look for information that conforms to our hypothesis and overlook information that argues against it**. || A classic study used a number-sequence task. >> Participants were told they **would be given three numbers that conform to a simple rule the experimenter had in mind**. Their **aim was to discover the rule by writing down sets of three numbers along with their reasons for choosing them**. >> **After each set, the experimenter said whether the numbers conformed to the rule or not**. **When participants felt highly confident that they had discovered the rule, they were to write it down**. >> The first set was **2, 4, 6**. >> **Participants then generated their own sets of three numbers**, and **for each set, they received feedback only on whether their numbers fit the rule**. || The **most common hypothesis** was **increasing intervals of two**. >> The **actual rule** was **three numbers in increasing order of magnitude**, so any rising sequence fit. >> People kept trying sets that matched their own guess. Every such set also fit the real rule, so the yes answers made them confident in the wrong rule. >> Only a set that breaks their own guess could reveal that the real rule is broader. || **Confirmation bias acts like a pair of blinders**. **We see the world according to rules we think are correct and are never dissuaded from this view, because we seek out only evidence that confirms our rule**.
18|**Decision making** means **making judgments that involve choices between different courses of action**. || **Utility** refers to **outcomes that are desirable because they are in the person’s best interest**, so an option with higher utility leads to a better outcome for the person. || **Expected utility theory** makes several claims. >> **People are rational**. >> **If they have all the relevant information, they will make the decision that results in the maximum expected utility**. **Expected utility** combines how good each outcome is with how likely it is. >> In its early form, utility meant **maximum monetary payoff**, so the best choice paid the most money on average. || The theory supplies a clear rule. >> For each option, multiply the value of every possible outcome by its probability and add the products. >> Pick the option with the largest total. >> The theory works as a benchmark. Comparing real choices with its predictions exposes the other factors that shape how people actually decide.
19|The **utility approach** has an **advantage**, since it offers **specific procedures for determining the “best choice.”** For any decision, it spells out which option a fully rational decision-maker would choose. || It also has **problems**. >> Utility is **not necessarily money**. **People find value in other things**, such as safety, fairness or how they feel about a choice. >> **Many decisions do not maximize the probability of the best outcome**. >> **People are not utility maximization machines**. Real choices also depend on emotion, values beyond money and how the options are framed. || For example, **people gamble in casinos all the time, even though they know the odds are very much against them**. >> On average, casino gambling loses money. >> People gamble anyway, because they value the thrill or the chance at a big win. >> The utility approach is a strong standard but a weak description of how people really choose.
20|In the **jellybean study**, **participants were given the opportunity to earn up to $7 by receiving $1 every time they drew a red jellybean**. **Each time, they were given a choice between two different bowls**. >> One bowl held **1 red jellybean out of 10**, so **the odds were 1/10**. >> The other held **7 red jellybeans out of 100**, so **the odds were 7/100, or 0.7/10**. || The first bowl offers better odds. >> One in 10 is 10%, and 7 in 100 is 7%. \[\frac{1}{10}=0.10,\qquad\frac{7}{100}=0.07\] >> Even so, **most participants chose the 7/100 bowl**. || **Their choice was influenced by considerations other than probabilities**. >> The second bowl has more red beans, seven instead of one, so it seems to offer more chances, even though it also holds many more white beans. >> People paid attention to the number of winning beans instead of their proportion. >> Choosing by appearance instead of probability lowered their real chance of winning.
21|**After 9/11, air travel decreased and driving increased**. >> After the attacks of September 11, 2001, many Americans drove instead of flying. >> They did so **even though the probability of dying in a car accident is far greater than in flying**. || **The number of Americans who lost their lives on the road to avoid flying is higher than the total number of passengers killed on the four hijacked flights**. >> Avoiding planes felt safer. >> In reality it exposed people to more risk, and more lives were lost. || The shift came from fear, not calculation. >> The attacks were vivid, terrifying and heavily reported, which made flying feel extremely dangerous. >> Driving felt familiar and under control, so its risk got far less weight even though it was much larger. >> A frightening, widely covered event makes its risk feel bigger than its real probability, which ties this choice to the availability heuristic.
22|An **example dilemma** tests how people weigh lives. >> **A convoy of food trucks is on its way to a refugee camp during a famine**. **Airplanes cannot be used**. >> **You find that a second camp has even more refugees**. >> **If you tell the convoy to go to the second camp instead of the first, you will save 1,000 people from death, but 100 people in the first camp will die as a result**. >> **Would you send the convoy to the second camp?** || The utilities can be written out. >> **Not rerouting** saves the 100 people in the first camp and loses the 1,000 in the second. \[U(\mathrm{not\ reroute})=100-1000=-900\] >> **Rerouting** saves the 1,000 in the second camp and loses the 100 in the first. \[U(\mathrm{reroute})=1000-100=900\] >> Rerouting saves 900 more lives, so expected utility theory says to reroute. || **Ritov and Baron (1999)** found that **63% did not choose to send the convoy to the second camp**. >> Rerouting means actively causing the deaths in the first camp, which many people would not do even to save far more lives. >> **Twelve percent responded that they would not change the route of the convoy even if only 1 person in the first camp would die**. >> For these people, causing harm by acting weighs more than failing to prevent a much larger harm. How a harm comes about matters to people, as well as how many lives are at stake.
23|**Tetlock, Kristel, Elson, Green and Lerner (2000)** proposed the **sacred value protection model**, in which **people want to protect what they hold sacred**. >> A **sacred value** is treated as beyond price, such as human life, loyalty or justice. >> **Sacred values are kept in a separate compartment from secular values**. **Secular values** are ordinary values such as money and time, which people trade against each other all the time. || **When the two domains are mixed together, people feel threatened and respond with moral outrage**. >> This is a **taboo** tradeoff, such as trading a human life for money. >> Putting a price on something sacred treats it as a mere commodity, which people feel as a moral violation. || **In contrast, when two sacred values are compared with each other, people are less morally outraged**. >> This is a **tragic** tradeoff, such as choosing between two lives. >> Since both sides are sacred, the choice is painful but does not cross the line between sacred and secular. >> The model predicts different emotional reactions to the two kinds of tradeoff.
24|**Tetlock and colleagues (2000)** compared taboo and tragic tradeoffs with two scenarios. || In the **taboo tradeoff scenario**, **Robert can save the life of Johnny, a five-year-old who needs a liver transplant**. >> **The transplant procedure will cost the hospital $1,000,000 that could be spent in other ways, such as purchasing better equipment and raising salaries to recruit talented doctors to the hospital**. >> **Johnny is very ill and has been on the waiting list for a transplant, but because of the shortage of local organ donors, obtaining a liver will be expensive**. >> **Robert could save Johnny’s life, or he could use the $1,000,000 for other hospital needs**. >> A life, which is sacred, is set against money, which is secular. || In the **tragic tradeoff scenario**, **there are two children who need a liver transplant, but only one liver**. >> One life is set against another. >> Both sides are sacred. || Johnny’s life is at stake in both scenarios. Only what it is weighed against changes, money in one case and another child in the other, so any difference in how people judge Robert comes from the type of tradeoff.
25|**Tetlock and colleagues (2000)** measured **moral outrage** toward Robert for each **tradeoff type**, depending on whether he **did not save Johnny** or **saved Johnny**. || In the **taboo** tradeoff, Johnny’s life against money, Robert’s choice mattered a great deal. >> When Robert **did not save Johnny**, outrage was very high, about **5.4** on a scale with a top of 6. >> When he **saved Johnny**, outrage was much lower, about **2.5**. >> Picking money over a child’s life treats a life as tradable for cash, which drew strong condemnation. || In the **tragic** tradeoff, one child’s life against another’s, Robert’s choice made almost no difference. >> Outrage was moderate and nearly the same whether or not he saved Johnny. >> Either way he saved a life, so neither option broke the line between sacred and secular. || Moral outrage depends on whether a sacred value is traded for a secular one. >> Trading a life for money is taboo and provokes outrage. >> Choosing between two lives is tragic, and people judge it more gently, as the sacred value protection model predicts.
26|**Ginges, Atran, Medin and Shikaki (2007)** **extended the earlier findings to a more realistic setting**. >> They **interviewed Israeli settlers**, **601** in all. >> They interviewed **Palestinian refugees**, **535** in all. >> They interviewed **Palestinian students affiliated with various political groups**, **719** in all. || These groups were directly involved in the Israeli–Palestinian conflict. >> The disputed issues, such as land and the status of Jerusalem, matter deeply to them. >> Studying sacred values in these groups tests whether lab findings hold when the stakes are real. || The researchers **offered three types of tradeoff deals as possible solutions to the conflict**. >> Each deal asked participants to give up something they valued in return for something else. >> The deals were realistic proposals that could form part of a peace agreement. >> Participants’ reactions to each deal were measured, including anger, disgust and support for violence.
27|**Ginges and colleagues (2007)** used three kinds of deals, given here in the form presented to Palestinian participants. || The **taboo** deal. **Palestinians would recognize the sacred and historic right of the Jewish people to Israel**. **There would be two states, a Jewish state of Israel and a Palestinian state in 99% of the West Bank and Gaza**. >> It asks Palestinians to give up a claim they may hold sacred. || The **taboo-plus** deal adds money. **On their part, Israel will pay Palestine 1 billion US dollars a year for 10 years**. >> It attaches a large material reward to the same compromise. || The **symbolic** deal adds a symbolic concession instead. **On their part, Israel will recognize the historic and legitimate right of the Palestinians to their own state and would apologize for all of the wrongs done to the Palestinian people**. >> Recognition and an apology are symbolic, not material. >> They address what the other side holds sacred, which makes the deal a tragic tradeoff instead of a taboo one. || Both money and symbols might be expected to make a deal more appealing. The three deals test whether they have the same effect on people who treat the issues as sacred.
28|Ginges and colleagues used a **sacred value measure** to sort participants into two groups. || **Moral absolutists** **transformed the issues under dispute into sacred values**. >> **Among settlers, 46% of the sample** were moral absolutists. They **believed it was never permissible for the Jewish people to “give up” part of the “Land of Israel,” no matter how great the benefit**. >> **Among Palestinian students, 54%** were moral absolutists. They **treated both the principle of the “right of return” and Jerusalem as sacred values**. The **right of return** is the claim that Palestinian refugees and their descendants may return to the land they left. || **Non-absolutists** **had strong preferences against compromise over these issues but did not regard them as sacred values**. >> They opposed giving these things up, but in principle the issues could be traded. || Both groups opposed compromise, for different reasons. >> Non-absolutists weighed costs against benefits and found the costs too high. >> Absolutists rejected the idea of weighing the issue against benefits at all. >> That difference decided how each group reacted when material benefits were offered.
29|**Ginges and colleagues (2007)** compared reactions across the deals. || **Taboo versus taboo-plus** compares the same compromise with and without money. >> For **non-absolutists**, adding money **slightly lowered** anger and disgust and **lowered** support for violence, as a cost-benefit view predicts. >> For **moral absolutists**, adding money **raised** anger and disgust and **raised** support for violence. >> For them, money turned a hard compromise into an insulting trade of the sacred for cash. || **Taboo versus symbolic** compares the compromise with and without a symbolic concession, which makes it a tragic tradeoff. >> Adding recognition and an apology **lowered** anger and disgust. >> It also **lowered** support for violence. || When a value is sacred, material incentives backfire. >> Money makes the deal a taboo trade of the sacred for the secular. >> A symbolic concession answers a sacred value with a sacred value, so compromise feels less like betrayal. >> The same added money pushed the two groups in opposite directions. Moral absolutists judge a deal by what it says about their sacred values, not by its material payoff.
30|Research on **sacred values in intergroup conflicts** has **investigated the impact of monetary incentives and disincentives in negotiations involving sacred values**. Two studies, **Dehghani, Atran and colleagues (2010)** and **Dehghani, Iliev and colleagues (2009)**, asked two questions. >> **How material incentives and disincentives, such as economic sanctions, psychologically affect the targeted population and potentially influence behavior**. >> **Whether Iranians who treat the Iranian nuclear program as a sacred value would be open to material incentives or disincentives to compromise**. || An **incentive** is a reward for compromising, such as economic aid, meant to make a deal more attractive. >> A **disincentive** is a penalty for refusing, such as **economic sanctions**, meant to make refusal costly. || For ordinary preferences, a big enough reward or penalty usually pushes a decision toward a deal. >> For sacred values, the same reward or penalty can feel like an attempt to buy or force a compromise of something beyond price. >> Both can then backfire, turning a principled stand into what looks like a response to a bribe or a threat.
31|The **experiments** included **1,997 Iranian participants in an online study conducted in Farsi**, the main language of Iran. >> The **dependent variables** were **people’s approval** of the deal, a measure of personal support, **people’s anger**, a measure of expected public reaction, and a **misleading factor**, meaning whether participants distrusted the deal’s intentions. || The **sacred value measure** was **based on Baron and Spranca’s 1997 measure**. It asked **what participants thought about Iran giving up its nuclear energy or weapons program**. >> **a. I think this definitely needs to happen**. >> **b. I do not object to this**. >> **c. This should be done only if the benefits of stopping the program are great enough**. >> **d. This shouldn’t be done no matter how great the consequences**. || **Participants who answered “d” were considered to hold a sacred value**. >> That was **14% for nuclear energy** and **13% for nuclear weapons**. >> Answer “d” refuses every tradeoff, whatever is offered, which is the mark of a sacred value. >> Sorting participants this way lets the study compare people who hold a sacred value with people who do not.
32|The study used a **2 by 3 design**, giving six versions of the deal. >> The first factor was the **nuclear program**, either **nuclear energy** or **nuclear weapons**. >> The second factor was the **type of deal**, either **taboo**, **taboo-plus** or **taboo-minus**. || The **scenarios** read as follows. >> **Taboo**. **Iran will give up its program for developing nuclear energy, or weapons, and surrender the current nuclear facilities to the UN**. **In return, Israel will give up its program for developing nuclear energy, or weapons, and surrender the existing nuclear facilities to the UN**. >> **Taboo-plus**. The taboo deal, plus **in return, the United Nations will give $40 billion in economic funds to Iran**. >> **Taboo-minus**. The taboo deal, plus **you know that if Iran does not accept this deal, the United Nations will impose additional sanctions on Iran**. || **Participants were asked three questions**. >> **Do you personally approve of this deal?** >> **If the Iranian government approves such a deal, do you think people will be angry?** >> **If such a deal is really proposed to Iran, do you find it misleading?** || Comparing deals reveals how incentives and penalties change reactions, and comparing people with and without a sacred value tests whether those changes depend on how the program is held.
33|For the **nuclear energy** program, approval and anger were compared between participants who held a sacred value, **SV**, and those who did not, **noSV**, across the three deals. || **Approval** split sharply between the groups. >> **noSV** participants approved of all three deals at a high level. >> **SV** participants approved much less, and adding money or sanctions **lowered** their approval further. || **Anger** split the same way. >> **noSV** participants expected little anger for any of the deals. >> **SV** participants expected more anger, and adding money or sanctions **raised** it. || Material incentives and penalties backfire when a value is sacred. >> People who do not hold the value as sacred treat money and sanctions as ordinary costs and benefits, so the deal stays acceptable. >> People who do hold it as sacred see money or threats as turning the deal into a taboo trade, which makes them less approving and angrier. >> When one side treats an issue as sacred, rewards or penalties can harden opposition instead of softening it.
