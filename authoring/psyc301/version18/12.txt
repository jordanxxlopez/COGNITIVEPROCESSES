2|**Problem solving** can be described in two main ways. >> **Problem solving as reorganization and insight**. In the Gestalt view, the answer comes from restructuring the mental representation of the problem, sometimes in a sudden flash of insight. The focus is on how the representation changes. >> **Problem solving as search**, a **search that occurs between the posing of the problem and its solution**. In the information-processing view of Newell and Simon, the solver moves step by step through a problem space from the initial state toward the goal. The focus is on the steps taken. >> The representation decides which paths seem possible, and the search decides which path is followed. || **There is more to problem solving than specifying the problem state**. **How a problem is stated can affect its difficulty**. >> The **mutilated checkerboard**, a board with two opposite corners removed, is the standard case. >> Its difficulty hinges on noticing that every domino has to cover one square of each color. >> Presentations that highlight the two kinds of squares make it easier. In an analogy, the answer to one problem can lead the solver to represent another problem in the same helpful way.
3|**Analogies** can help solve problems, because **using the solution to a similar problem guides the solution to a new problem**. || The **Russian marriage problem** goes like this. >> **In a small Russian village, there were 32 bachelors and 32 unmarried women**. >> **Through tireless efforts, the village matchmaker succeeded in arranging 32 highly satisfactory marriages**, and **the village was proud and happy**. >> **Then one drunken night, two bachelors, in a test of strength, stuffed each other with pierogies and died**. >> **Can the matchmaker, through some quick arrangements, come up with 31 heterosexual marriages among the 62 survivors?** || The answer is **no**. >> After the deaths there are **30 men and 32 women**. >> Every marriage in the problem pairs **one man and one woman**. >> With only 30 men, no more than **30 marriages** are possible, and two women are left without partners. >> Sixty-two people would make 31 pairs only if anyone could pair with anyone. Because each pair needs one from each group, the smaller group sets the limit. || This problem feels easy because its structure is plain. The story makes the two groups and the one-of-each rule obvious, so counting settles it. The checkerboard hides the same structure, which makes the marriage story a useful source.
4|The **mutilated checkerboard** has the same structure as the Russian marriage problem. >> A **domino** covers **two neighboring squares**, always **one dark and one light**, just as each marriage joins one man and one woman. || Taking away two opposite corners takes away two squares of the **same color**. >> The 62 squares left are **32 of one color and 30 of the other**. >> Thirty-one dominoes would need **31 squares of each color**. || So the board cannot be fully covered. >> The two missing corners play the part of the two missing bachelors. >> No more than 30 dominoes can be placed, and two squares of one color are left over. || Arranging the dominoes more cleverly cannot fix this. In both problems, an imbalance between the two kinds rules out the last pair, so counting the colors settles the question before any domino is placed. The marriage story makes the pairing rule obvious, which can help a solver find the same rule in the board.
5|**Analogical problem solving** means **using the solution to a similar problem to guide the solution to a new problem**. >> Here the **Russian marriage problem** is the **source problem**, and the **mutilated-checkerboard problem** is the **target problem**. || **Analogical transfer** is **the transfer from one problem to another**, going from the **source problem** to the **target problem**. >> The **target problem** is **the problem the person is trying to solve**. >> The **source problem** is **another problem that shares some similarities with the target problem**. || **Learning about the Russian marriage problem enhances the ability to solve the mutilated-checkerboard problem**. Transfer works by matching parts of the source to parts of the target. >> Men and women match the two colors of squares. >> Marriages match dominoes, since each pairs one of each kind. >> The missing bachelors match the missing corners. || With this mapping in place, the reasoning from the source carries straight over to the target. The marriage story provides the key idea that pairing needs equal numbers of two kinds, so the solver does not have to discover the color argument alone.
6|**Duncker’s radiation problem** is a classic test of analogical problem solving. >> **Suppose you are a doctor faced with a patient who has a malignant tumor in his stomach**. >> **It is impossible to operate on the patient, but unless the tumor is destroyed, the patient will die**. >> **There is a kind of ray that can be used to destroy the tumor**. **If the rays reach it all at once at a sufficiently high intensity, the tumor will be destroyed**. >> **Unfortunately, at this intensity, the healthy tissue the rays pass through on the way to the tumor will also be destroyed**. >> **At lower intensities, the rays are harmless to healthy tissue, but they will not affect the tumor either**. >> **What type of procedure might be used to destroy the tumor with the rays and at the same time avoid destroying the healthy tissue?** || Two demands pull against each other. The tumor needs high intensity and the healthy tissue needs low intensity, so one ray cannot meet both. || The answer is to send **several low-intensity rays from different directions** that **converge on the tumor at the same time**. >> Each ray is weak enough to pass through healthy tissue safely. >> Where they meet at the tumor, their combined strength destroys it. || **Only 10% of participants arrive at the correct solution** without help. >> Splitting one strong ray into many weak ones that combine at the target is an idea few people reach. >> That low rate makes the problem a good test of whether an analogy helps.
7|The **fortress problem** acts as a source problem for the radiation problem. >> **A small country was ruled from a strong fortress by a dictator**. **The fortress was in the middle of the country, surrounded by farms and villages**, and **many roads led to the fortress through the countryside**. >> **A rebel general vowed to capture the fortress**. **He knew that an attack by his entire army would capture it**, and **he gathered his army at the head of one of the roads, ready to launch a full-scale direct attack**. >> **However, the general then learned that the dictator had planted mines on each of the roads**. **The mines were set so that small bodies of men could pass over them safely**, **since the dictator needed to move his troops and workers to and from the fortress**. >> **However, any large force would detonate the mines**. **Not only would this blow up the road, it would also destroy many neighboring villages**. **It therefore seemed impossible to capture the fortress**. || The general **divides the army into small groups**, sends them **along different roads** and has them **arrive at the fortress at the same time**. >> Each small group crosses the mines safely. >> Together they form the whole army at the fortress, which is enough to take it. || The fortress story holds the same conflict as the radiation problem in a new setting. >> A large force is needed at the target, but only small forces can travel any single route safely. >> The mines play the role of the healthy tissue, and splitting and converging meets both demands.
8|The **fortress problem** and the **radiation problem** share one structure. Both involve many paths leading from all around to a single central target. || The parts of the two problems line up. >> The **fortress** matches the **tumor**. >> The **small groups of soldiers** match the **low-intensity rays**. >> The **roads** match the **paths through healthy tissue**. >> The **mines that a large force would set off** match the **healthy tissue that a strong ray would destroy**. || The two solutions work the same way. >> Weak forces travel safely along separate paths toward a central target. >> They meet at the target, where their combined strength does the job. >> Since the mapping is complete, the answer in one problem translates directly into the answer in the other. || In each case, whatever travels along a single path is weak, and the combination at the center is strong.
9|**After reading the fortress story, 30% of participants arrived at the correct solution** to the radiation problem. >> That is three times the 10% who solve it without the story. >> Still, most participants never used the analogy. || **Gick and Holyoak** described three steps in analogical problem solving, and failing at any one blocks the answer. >> **Noticing an analogous relationship** between source and target. This is **crucial, and the most difficult step**, since a military story seems unrelated to a medical problem. >> **Mapping the correspondence between source and target**. **Elements of the source need to be mapped onto elements of the target**, such as soldiers onto rays and roads onto paths through tissue. >> **Applying the mapping** **to generate a parallel solution in the target**, which turns the mapped idea into a concrete plan for the rays. || **Seventy percent of participants were not able to see the correspondences** by themselves. >> **When participants were told to think about the story they had read, the success rate rose to 75%**. >> The hint gave no answer and said only that the story was relevant. >> Once told to use it, three-quarters could map and apply the analogy, so noticing is the main barrier.
10|Some **factors make the noticing and mapping steps easier**. || **One reason noticing is difficult is that people often focus on surface features**. >> **Surface features** are the particular objects and details in a problem. >> The **fortress** and the **tumor** share almost no surface features, since one involves armies and roads and the other involves rays and medicine. >> An army story calls up military knowledge and a tumor calls up medical knowledge. Memory retrieval follows surface similarity, so a source with different surface features rarely comes to mind, even when its structure matches perfectly. || That leads to a question. **Can making the surface features similar make it easier to notice analogous relationships?** >> A source that looks more like the target might come to mind more readily. >> The solver could then find the deeper structural match. >> The **lightbulb problem** tests this idea.
11|The **lightbulb problem** has surface features much closer to the radiation problem. >> **In a physics lab at a major university, a very expensive lightbulb, which would emit precisely controlled quantities of light, was being used in some experiments**. >> **One morning, Ruth, the research assistant, came into the lab and found that the lightbulb no longer worked**. **She noticed that the filament inside the bulb had broken into two parts**. >> **The surrounding glass bulb was completely sealed, so there was no way to open it**. >> **Ruth knew that the lightbulb could be repaired if a brief, high-intensity laser beam could be used to fuse the two parts of the filament into one**. || **However, a high-intensity laser beam would also break the fragile glass surrounding the filament**. >> **At lower intensities, the laser would not break the glass, but neither would it fuse the filament**. >> **What type of procedure might be used to fuse the filament with the laser and at the same time avoid breaking the glass?** || Both problems aim beams at a small target inside something fragile. >> The broken filament matches the tumor, the glass matches the healthy tissue and the laser matches the radiation. >> The answer is to aim **several low-intensity lasers from different directions** so they **converge on the filament**. Each spot on the glass gets only a weak beam, while together the beams fuse the filament.
12|**Duncker’s radiation problem was used as the source** for the lightbulb problem. >> **Eighty-one percent of participants who knew about the radiation problem solved the lightbulb problem**. >> **Only 10% of participants in the control group were able to solve it**. The control group had no source, so their rate reflects how hard the problem is by itself. || **Surface similarity helped in solving the problem**. >> Both problems involve beams aimed at a small target inside something that must not be harmed. >> Rays and lasers, tumors and filaments, tissue and glass are close enough that the link comes to mind easily. || The radiation problem worked far better as a source than the fortress story, which helped only 30% of participants. >> When source and target look alike, people notice the link much more easily. >> This source shares both the structure and the surface features of the target, so it is easy to notice and easy to map, and transfer is high.
13|In the **lightbulb problem**, **high surface similarities aid analogical problem solving**. **Surface features** are **the specific elements of a given problem**, such as lasers, glass and filaments, and they help a source come to mind. || **Structural features** are **the underlying principle that governs the solution**, and they decide whether the source’s answer truly fits. >> In the **radiation** problem, the structural feature is that **a strong ray destroys tissue**. >> In the **lightbulb** problem, the structural feature is that **a strong laser breaks the lightbulb**. >> In both, a strong beam would harm what surrounds the target, which is why the beam must be split. || A source with matching surface features but a different structure would come to mind and then mislead. A source with matching structure but different surface features would help but would rarely be noticed. || To pull the two apart, researchers **made two versions of the lightbulb problem**, **keeping the surface features the same and varying the structural features**. >> Both versions have the same lab, bulb and lasers. >> They differ only in why one strong beam cannot be used, so any difference in transfer comes from structure.
14|**Source problem 1** is the **fragile-glass version**, where **the source and target have similar structural features**. >> Here the obstacle is that **a high-intensity laser beam would break the fragile glass surrounding the filament**. **At lower intensities, the laser would not break the glass, but neither would it fuse the filament**. >> This matches the radiation problem, where a strong ray would destroy healthy tissue. || In **Ruth’s solution**, **Ruth placed several lasers in a circle around the lightbulb and administered low-intensity laser beams from several directions at once**. >> **The beams all converged on the filament, where their combined effect was enough to fuse it**. >> **Because each spot on the surrounding glass received only a low-intensity beam from each laser, the glass was left intact**. || **Sixty-nine percent of participants who read this solution were able to solve the radiation problem**. >> The fragile glass plays the same role as the healthy tissue. >> In both problems, the reason for several weak beams is to protect what surrounds the target. >> Readers grasped why convergence was needed, and that understanding carried over, with glass mapping onto tissue, filament onto tumor and lasers onto rays.
15|**Source problem 2** is the **insufficient-intensity version**, where **the source and target have different structural features**. >> Here **the laser generated only low-intensity beams that were not strong enough to fuse the filament**. **A much more intense laser beam was needed**. >> The glass is in no danger. The only obstacle is that each laser is too weak, so several are combined to reach enough strength. || **Ruth’s solution** is unchanged. **Ruth placed several lasers in a circle around the lightbulb and administered low-intensity laser beams from several directions at once**. **The beams all converged on the filament, where their combined effect was enough to fuse it**. >> The procedure and the surface features are identical to the fragile-glass version. >> Only the reason for using several beams is different. || **Only 33% of participants who read this solution were able to solve the radiation problem**, about half the success after the fragile-glass version. >> The radiation problem’s key demand is protecting the surrounding tissue, and only the fragile-glass version shares it. >> Knowing the steps of the solution was not enough. Readers had a method without the principle that tells them when to use it. >> Matching structural features therefore matters more than matching surface features.
16|**Making structural features more obvious aids analogical problem solving**. **Structural features** are **the underlying principles that govern the solution to a problem**. People who grasp the principle behind an answer can use it on problems that look very different. || **Analogical encoding** is **the process by which two problems are compared and the similarities between them are determined**. >> It is **an effective way to get participants to pay attention to the structural features that aid problem solving**. >> **Having people compare source stories may be an effective way to make them focus on structural features**. || Comparing two stories brings out what they have in common. >> Two convergence stories with different details, such as soldiers and lasers, both depend on weak forces combining at a target. >> The details that belong to only one story fade, and the shared structure stands out. >> Once the principle is free of any single setting, it can be recognized in a problem with entirely different surface features, such as a tumor treated with rays.
17|The **analogical paradox** is that **it can be difficult to apply analogies in the laboratory, but people routinely use analogies in real-world settings**. >> **Scientists use about 3 to 15 analogies in a 1-hour lab meeting**. >> People lean on analogies all the time at work, even though they often miss them in experiments. || The difference likely lies in how analogies are used outside the lab. >> In real settings, people come up with their own analogies from knowledge they understand well, instead of being given an unfamiliar story. >> Scientists in lab meetings draw on deep knowledge of related experiments, so their analogies tend to share both surface and structural features with the problem. || **In vivo problem-solving research** studies problem solving where it naturally happens. **People are observed to determine how they solve problems in the real world**. >> **In vivo** means “in the living,” referring to real life as opposed to the lab. >> Its **advantage** is the **naturalistic setting**, which reveals how people really solve problems at work. >> Its **disadvantages** are that it is **time-consuming** and researchers **cannot isolate and control variables** as they can in an experiment.
18|An **expert** is **“a person who, by devoting a large amount of time to learning about a field and practicing and applying that learning, has become acknowledged as being extremely knowledgeable or skilled in that field.”** >> A **chess expert** is **someone who has spent 10,000 to 20,000 hours studying and playing chess**. >> All those hours build a vast store of knowledge about positions, moves and strategies, and that knowledge belongs to chess alone. || **Experts solve problems in their field faster and with a higher success rate than beginners**. >> They get correct answers more often. >> They get them more quickly too. || The question is **why experts are better**. >> One possibility is that experts simply know more. >> Another is that their knowledge is arranged in a more useful way. >> Both possibilities are about long-term memory, namely how much it holds and how it is organized.
19|**Experts possess more knowledge about their fields**. **Chase and Simon (1973)** **compared how well a chess master and a beginner, with fewer than 100 hours of play, were able to reproduce the positions of pieces on a chessboard after looking at an arrangement for 5 seconds**. || There were **two conditions**. >> **Pieces arranged in actual game positions**. >> **Pieces arranged randomly**. || **Experts did much better than non-experts in the first condition**. >> **They had stored these actual positions in their long-term memory**. >> **The positions were accessed in chunks**, groups of pieces that form familiar patterns from real games. >> The beginner has few stored patterns and must remember each piece on its own. || **These chunks were not available in the random condition**, so the master’s advantage mostly vanished with random boards. >> Random placements match nothing in memory, so the master has to remember piece by piece, just like the beginner. >> The master’s short-term memory is no bigger. The advantage comes from long-term knowledge of real game patterns. >> Five seconds is too short to memorize pieces one by one, so strong recall depends on recognizing whole patterns at a glance.
20|**Experts’ knowledge is organized so it can be accessed when needed to work on a problem**. **Chi and colleagues (1982)** **presented 24 physics problems to a group of experts, who were physics professors, and a group of novices, who were students with one semester of physics**. >> **They asked participants to sort the problems into groups based on their similarities**. >> The way people group problems reveals which features they treat as important. || The two groups sorted in very different ways. >> **Novices sorted based on surface features**, **what the objects looked like**. Problems with blocks on slopes went together, and so did problems with springs. >> **Experts sorted based on structural features**, **the underlying principles involved**. Problems that needed the same principle, such as conservation of energy, went together even when they looked different. || Experts arrange their knowledge around principles. >> When an expert reads a new problem, the principle needed to solve it comes to mind. >> A novice notices the objects, which may have nothing to do with how the problem is solved. >> Because experts file problems under principles, the principle is what a new problem retrieves.
21|Examples from the **Chi and colleagues** study make the novice–expert difference concrete. || The **novice** **grouped problems 23 and 24 together because they both involve similar objects, inclined planes**. >> Each of these problems has a block on a slope with friction. >> They look alike on the surface. || The **expert** **grouped problems 21 and 24 together because they both involve similar physics principles, conservation of energy**. >> **Problem 21** has a compressed spring that pushes a block along a flat surface, with no slope at all. >> It looks nothing like problem 24. >> Both are solved with **conservation of energy**, which follows how energy changes form while the total stays the same. || Problem 24 lands in a different group depending on who sorts it. >> The novice links it by appearance. >> The expert looks past the objects to the principle that solves it. That deeper representation lets the expert pick the right method directly, without being misled by surface likeness.
22|**Experts spend more time analyzing a problem**. >> That gives them a **slower start**, **but this strategy usually pays off**. >> Novices often rush into calculations based on surface features. Experts first find the principle, choose the right method and avoid false starts, so they end up faster and more accurate overall. || **Experts are no better than novices when given problems outside their field**. >> Expertise belongs to a particular domain. >> A chess master has no special edge in physics, and a physicist has none in chess. || **Being an expert is not always an advantage**. **Experts are less likely to be open to new ways of looking at problems**. >> **Being an expert may be a disadvantage when confronting a problem that requires flexible thinking**. >> These are **problems whose solution may involve rejecting the usual procedures in favor of other procedures that are less frequently used**. >> Well-practiced methods can work like a mental set, where the usual method comes to mind first and crowds out others. >> The same deep knowledge that makes experts fast can make them rigid when a problem needs an unusual approach.
23|**Creativity** has several sides. >> **Innovative thinking**. >> **Novel ideas**. >> **New connections between existing ideas**. >> **Divergent thinking**, which is **open-ended** and produces **a large number of potential “solutions.”** It generates many possible answers to one question instead of narrowing to one, which raises the odds that one will be good. || Creative problem solving is a **process**. **Most creative problem solving includes far more than just getting an idea**. >> **It involves a lengthy period of trial and error**, in which ideas are tested and revised. || The process unfolds in **stages**. >> **Problem generation**, which includes **problem finding** and **fact finding**. >> **Problem formulation**, which includes **problem definition** and **idea finding**. >> **Problem solving**, which includes **evaluation** and **planning**. >> **Solution implementation**, which means **taking action**. || The stages do different jobs. >> Early stages identify and define the problem and produce ideas. >> Later stages test the ideas and put the best one into action. >> Trial and error runs through all of them, so creative work cycles through generating, testing and revising.
24|**Generating ideas is only one part of the creative process**. >> **Without ideas, the process stops**. >> Ideas are necessary, but they still have to be judged, developed and carried out before they become a solution. || **Too much knowledge can sometimes hinder creative problem solving**. >> **Problems often require flexible thinking and rejecting accepted procedures**. >> A person who knows the usual approach very well may struggle to set it aside. || Creative problem solving balances knowledge against flexibility. >> Knowledge provides the raw material for new ideas. >> It also provides accepted procedures that can block new approaches, so flexibility is needed to drop the familiar method when a better one is called for. || Producing ideas and judging them call for different kinds of thinking. >> Producing ideas relies on divergent thinking, which opens up many possibilities. >> Judging them narrows those possibilities to one that can actually be carried out.
