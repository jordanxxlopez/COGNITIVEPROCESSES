2|A **problem** is **an obstacle between a present state and a goal**, where **the solution is not immediately obvious**. >> The **present state** is the way things are now. >> The **goal** is the way we want them to be. >> The **obstacle** is whatever stops us from going straight from one to the other. || The **sliding-tile puzzle** has this structure. >> The starting board holds eight numbered tiles and one empty space in a scrambled order, with **8, blank, 6** across the top. >> The goal board holds the same pieces in order, with the empty space in the top left, then **1** through **8**. >> The two boards differ only in where the pieces sit. || Knowing the goal is not the same as knowing how to get there. >> The finished board is easy to describe. >> The obstacle is the rule that a tile can only move into the empty space, so reaching the goal takes many moves in the right order. || The words **not immediately obvious** separate a problem from a routine task. A problem means working out the path, so problem solving calls for thinking and not just following instructions.
3|**Gestalt psychologists** studied **learning, problem solving, and even attitudes and beliefs**, as well as **perception**, according to **Koffka (1935)**. >> They **took a perceptual approach** to every one of these. >> To them, solving a problem resembles seeing a figure. The same elements lead to a solution or a dead end depending on how they are organized into a whole. || For Gestalt psychologists, **problem solving** had two parts. >> **Representing the problem in the mind**, meaning how the solver mentally arranges its elements. >> **Restructuring**, which means **reorganizing the problem’s representation** to see it a new way. || **Köhler’s “circle” problem** asks, **if the length of the circle’s radius is r, what is the length of line x?** >> A rectangle sits in one quarter of the circle, and line **x** is one of the rectangle’s diagonals. >> **Different people might represent this problem differently**, and **people’s representations are likely to differ from the picture**. Seen as a lone line, x seems hard to measure. || The answer comes from **restructuring**. >> The rectangle’s other diagonal runs from the circle’s center to the corner that touches the circle, so it is a radius with length **r**. >> A rectangle’s two diagonals are always equal, so x equals the radius too. \[x=r\]
4|**Solutions are often obtained by first perceiving the object and then representing it in a different way**. This is called **restructuring of the problem’s representation**. >> The elements of the problem do not change. >> The way the solver arranges them does. || **Gestalt psychologists related restructuring to insight**, which has several features. >> **Insight** is a **sudden realization of a problem’s solution**. >> It involves **discovering a crucial element** that had been missed. >> Gestalt psychologists **thought solutions come to people all of a sudden**. The solver sees the problem the same way until something sets off a new arrangement. >> That moment is the **“Aha” experience**, the feeling of the right representation clicking into place. || **Other researchers disagree that all problem solving works this way**. >> **Much of our problem solving does not require “Aha” experiences**. >> Plenty of problems are solved one step at a time, each step bringing the solver a bit closer. >> Insight is one route to a solution and methodical work is another, which raises the question of how to tell them apart.
5|**Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987)** **designed an experiment to distinguish between insight and noninsight problems**. >> The **insight problems** included the **triangle problem** and the **chain problem**. >> The **noninsight problems** were **algebra** problems. || They expected opposite patterns for the two kinds. >> **In insight problems, participants should not be very good at predicting how near they are to a solution**. If insight comes suddenly, the solver should feel far away right until the answer arrives. >> **Noninsight problems involve a more methodical process**, so **participants should know how close they are to the solution**. Every finished step brings the answer closer. || Participants gave **warmth judgments every 15 seconds**, rating how close they felt to the answer from cold to hot. >> **“Hot” ratings indicated that they were getting close to the solution**. >> Checking only whether people solved the problem would not reveal whether the answer came suddenly or gradually. Warmth ratings track the feeling over time. >> Steadily rising warmth means gradual progress, and low warmth followed by a sudden jump means insight.
6|Two **insight problems** from **Metcalfe and Wiebe** reveal what kind of restructuring insight takes. || The **triangle problem** asks how to **move three of the blue dots to make the triangle point to the bottom of the page**. >> The triangle has ten dots in rows of one, two, three and four. >> Seven dots stay put and three move. >> The top dot goes below the middle of the bottom row, and the two ends of the bottom row go up beside the two-dot row. >> The rows now run four, three, two and one, so the triangle points down. || The **chain problem** asks how to **join four short pieces of chain into a single closed loop**, each with three links. **Opening a link costs 2 cents, closing a link costs 3 cents, and there are only 15 cents**. >> The obvious plan opens one link on each piece, which costs 20 cents and goes over budget. >> The answer is to open all three links of one piece. >> Those three open links join the other three pieces into a loop, and the total comes to exactly 15 cents. \[3(2+3)=15\] || Neither answer comes from gradual improvement. Each one requires leaving the obvious approach and seeing the parts differently, such as treating one piece of chain as a set of connectors.
7|**Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987)** compared **“warmer/colder” ratings** for insight and noninsight problems as the moment of solution approached. || For **algebra**, the noninsight problems, warmth showed a **gradual increase**. >> Ratings climbed steadily over the last minute and reached the top at the solution. >> **Noninsight problems were solved gradually**. Each finished step is visible progress, so warmth builds as steps add up. || For **insight** problems, warmth showed a **big jump from 3 to 7**. >> Ratings stayed low until just before the answer, then leapt to the top at the solution. >> **Insight problems were solved suddenly**. No step-by-step progress can be felt, so warmth stays low until the restructuring happens. || The results confirm the prediction. >> Insight and noninsight problems feel different from the inside. >> Participants felt cold only seconds before solving insight problems, so insight does arrive suddenly, as Gestalt psychologists proposed. >> Their sense of being close was a poor guide to how close they really were.
8|**Gestalt psychologists claimed that one of the major obstacles to problem solving is fixation**. **Fixation** is the **tendency to focus on a specific characteristic of the problem that keeps people from arriving at a solution**. >> **Functional fixedness** is one kind of fixation. It means **restricting the use of an object to its familiar functions**. >> We see an object as having one purpose and overlook other uses. || The **candle problem** tests functional fixedness. >> A person faces a **vertical corkboard mounted on the wall** and receives a **candle**, a **box of matches** and a **box of tacks**. >> The task is to **mount the candle on the corkboard so it will burn without dripping wax on the floor**. >> The answer is to empty the matchbox, tack it to the board and set the candle in it like a shelf. || **Seeing the matchboxes as containers inhibited using them as supports**. >> Full boxes looked only like containers. >> **Participants who were given empty matchboxes were twice as likely to solve the problem**. >> An empty box no longer looks like a container in use, so its flat bottom and shape are easier to notice as useful. >> Such a small change doubling the success rate reveals how strongly the familiar function held people back.
9|**Fixation** and **functional fixedness** also get in the way of the **two-string problem**, where the task is to **tie together two strings** hanging from the ceiling. >> The strings hang too far apart to reach both at once. >> Holding one, a person cannot reach the other. >> A few objects are in the room, including a pair of **pliers**. || The answer is to **use the pliers to create a pendulum**. >> Tying the pliers to one string turns it into a **pendulum**, a weight that swings back and forth. >> The person sets it swinging, grabs the other string and catches the first one as it swings near. || **The usual function of pliers gets in the way of seeing them as a weight**. >> People think of pliers as a gripping tool. >> That familiar use hides the fact that pliers are heavy, which is the property the solution needs. >> The solver also has to imagine motion, picturing a hanging string as something that can swing toward them. || The pliers are in plain view the whole time. The difficulty lies in how they are represented, as a gripping tool instead of a heavy object that can swing.
10|In the **two-string problem**, **once a hint was provided by “accidentally” setting a string in motion, most participants were able to solve it**. >> The experimenter bumped one string so it swung. >> Seeing it swing suggested a pendulum without giving the answer away. >> **In Gestalt terms, the solution occurred when participants restructured their representation**. The hint changed how the problem was represented, not what was available. || A **mental set** is a broader form of fixation. >> It is a **preconceived notion about how to approach a problem**. >> It is **based on a person’s past experiences with the problem or similar problems**. >> A method that worked before gets used again, even when a better one is available. || The **water-jug problem** reveals mental set. >> The task is to **figure out how to obtain a required volume of water, given three empty jars for measuring**. >> After solving several problems with one formula, people keep using it on new problems even when a simpler method would work.
11|Each **water-jug problem** **specifies the capacities of jugs A, B and C and a final desired quantity**. **The task is to figure out how to use the jugs with these capacities to measure out the desired quantity**, in quarts. || In problem 1, the jugs hold **21, 127 and 3**, and the target is **100**. >> Fill jug B with 127 quarts. >> Pour from B into A until A is full, which leaves 106 in B. >> Pour from B into C until C is full, then empty C and fill it once more. Exactly 100 quarts remain in B. \[B-A-2C=127-21-2(3)=100\] || The same formula, B minus A minus 2C, solves each of the early problems. Participants started applying it automatically, which is a **mental set**. || The later problems test that habit. >> Problem 7 has jugs of **15, 39 and 3** and a target of **18**. The long formula works, but simply adding A and C is much easier. \[A+C=15+3=18\] >> Problem 8 has jugs of **28, 59 and 3** and a target of **25**, which A minus C solves directly. \[A-C=28-3=25\] >> **The mental set inhibited participants from using the simpler solutions**. People who had not worked through the earlier problems found the short methods far more often. || A mental set speeds solving when problems are alike, but it hides easier paths once problems change.
12|**Newell and Simon (1956)** wrote the **Logic Theorist**, an early computer program that proved theorems in logic by hunting for a chain of valid steps. Their approach to problem solving was an **information-processing approach**. || In this approach, **problem solving** is **a process that involves search**. >> The Gestalt tradition looked at **the initial structure of a problem and the new structure achieved when it is solved**. >> Newell and Simon looked instead at what happens in between, a **search that occurs between the posing of the problem and its solution**. >> They called it **“searching for a way to reach a goal”** and **“getting around road-blocks.”** >> A program that solves problems by searching suggests that human solving can be described in the same step-by-step terms. || Every **problem space** contains three kinds of states. >> The **initial state** holds **the conditions at the beginning of the problem**. >> **Intermediate states** hold the conditions partway through, after some moves but before the answer. >> The **goal state** is **the solution of the problem**. || To solve a problem is to travel through its space. Starting from the initial state, each move reaches another intermediate state, until the search arrives at the goal.
13|**Operators** are **rules that specify which moves are allowed and which are not**. Operators **take the problem from one state to another**. >> Every legal move applies an operator. >> Each operator turns the current state into a new one. >> Without such rules, a problem would have no structure, because anything could happen at any step. || The **Tower of Hanoi** has especially clear operators. >> In the **initial state**, three disks sit on the first of three pegs, largest at the bottom and smallest at the top. >> In the **goal state**, the same stack sits on the third peg in the same order. || Three rules set the legal moves. >> **Rule 1. Disks are moved one at a time** from one peg to another. >> **Rule 2. A disk can be moved only when there are no disks on top of it**. >> **Rule 3. A larger disk cannot be placed on top of a smaller disk**. || These rules are what make the problem hard. >> The goal is easy to picture, but the stack cannot move all at once. >> Smaller disks keep moving out of the way, and the middle peg serves as a temporary parking spot. The solver has to plan steps in between instead of heading straight for the goal.
14|Solving the **Tower of Hanoi** with a larger stack takes a long series of moves. >> Small disks move again and again to clear the way for larger ones. >> A larger disk can move only once every smaller disk above it has gone elsewhere. >> The same pattern repeats at every level of the stack. || The number of moves grows enormously as disks are added. >> **With 64 disks, solving it would take a trillion years**, **even if each move were made in one second and every move were correct**. >> The rules never change, yet the number of moves explodes. || A problem space can become huge even when its rules stay simple. >> The goal state is easy to describe. >> The route to it, through many intermediate states, can be extremely long. >> Each larger disk has to wait until the pegs are arranged so its move is legal, while the smaller disks are stacked and restacked many times.
15|The **sliding-tile puzzle** has an **initial state** and a **goal state**. >> In the initial state, the eight tiles and the empty space are scrambled, starting with **8, blank, 6** across the top. >> In the goal state, the empty space is in the top left and the tiles run in order from **1** to **8**. || The **operators** are tile moves. >> A tile beside the empty space can move into it. >> Each such move shifts the empty space to where the tile was, creating a new state. || Every arrangement of the eight tiles and the empty space is a separate state in the problem space. >> The same tiles appear in every state, and just one arrangement is the goal. >> A solver cannot check every state, so the search has to be guided. >> Strategies that pick promising moves at each step are needed to reach the goal without wandering through the entire space.
16|A **search tree** lays out the problem space of a sliding-tile puzzle. Each state is a point in the tree, and each legal move links one state to the next. || The starting board allows **three possible moves**, so the tree splits three ways after the first step. >> Each of those new states allows further moves, and the tree splits again. >> The branching continues at every level. || The tree grows wider with every step, because each state opens several new ones. >> After just a few levels there are dozens of states. >> Only one path leads to the goal. || Trying every branch would mean facing an enormous number of possibilities. A good strategy follows the branches that bring the board closer to the goal and leaves the rest unexplored.
17|**According to Newell and Simon, we have to search the problem space to find a solution**. One useful strategy is **means-end analysis**. >> **Means-end analysis** aims to **reduce the differences between the initial and goal states**. >> At each point, the solver asks how the current state differs from the goal and which move would shrink that difference. || Means-end analysis relies on **subgoals**. **Subgoals create intermediate states closer to the goal**. >> A **subgoal** is a smaller goal that must be met before the main goal can be. >> When no move can close the main difference directly, the solver sets a subgoal that removes the obstacle. >> Subgoals break one hard problem into a series of easier ones. || **For the Tower of Hanoi, applying means-end analysis reduces the size of the difference between the initial and goal states**. >> The main difference is that the whole stack is on the wrong peg. >> The largest disk cannot move until the disks above it are cleared, so clearing them becomes the first subgoal. >> Means-end analysis steers the search toward the goal, so the solver does not have to try every possible move.
18|**Means-end analysis** solves the **Tower of Hanoi** through a chain of subgoals, each removing one obstacle to moving the largest disk. || **Subgoal 1 is to free up the large disk**. >> The smallest disk moves from the first peg to the third. >> The middle disk moves from the first peg to the middle peg. >> The large disk is now uncovered and free to move. || **Subgoal 2 is to free up the third peg**. >> The smallest disk moves from the third peg onto the middle disk. >> The third peg is empty, so the large disk has a place to go, and the two smaller disks sit legally on the middle peg. || **Subgoal 3 is to move the large disk onto the third peg**. >> The large disk moves from the first peg to the third. >> It is now in its final place, and the same strategy can move the two smaller disks onto it. || Some subgoal moves seem to go away from the goal. >> Putting the smallest disk on the third peg and then taking it off again looks wasteful. >> That detour is what frees the space the large disk needs, and means-end analysis accepts such detours when they remove an obstacle.
19|**One of the main contributions of Newell and Simon’s approach** is that it **provided a way to specify the possible pathways from the initial state to the goal state**. >> Spelling out the states and operators maps every route through a problem. >> Problem solving could then be described precisely and even programmed into a computer. >> That precision allowed researchers to compare strategies and predict where people would get stuck. || **There is more to problem solving than specifying the problem state**. **How a problem is stated can affect its difficulty**. >> Two versions of a problem can share the same states and operators. >> One version can still be much easier, because its wording or presentation leads the solver to a representation that exposes the key relationship. || The **mutilated-checkerboard problem** is a clear case. How hard it is depends heavily on how the board is presented. >> Presentation shapes the representation the solver adopts, and the representation decides how hard the search becomes.
20|A **checkerboard** has **64 squares** and **can be completely covered by placing 32 dominoes on the board**, **with each domino covering 2 squares**. >> Each domino covers two neighboring squares, either side by side or stacked. >> Thirty-two dominoes at two squares each cover all 64. || The **mutilated-checkerboard problem** asks, **if two opposite corners are eliminated, can the remaining 62 squares be covered with 31 dominoes?** >> The numbers seem to fit, since 31 dominoes cover 62 squares. >> The answer is **no**. || The colors of the squares hold the key. >> Every domino covers **one dark square and one light square**, because neighboring squares are always different colors. >> Opposite corners share the **same color**, so removing them leaves **32 squares of one color and 30 of the other**. >> Thirty-one dominoes would need 31 squares of each color, so a full covering is impossible. || Placing dominoes one at a time would take a very long time and could never prove a covering impossible. Once the board is represented by its colors, a single count settles the question, which a pure search approach would struggle to do.
21|**The key to solving the mutilated-checkerboard problem** is **understanding two things**. >> **Each domino covers two squares**. >> **These squares must be of different colors**. || **Conditions differed in how much information was provided about the squares**. >> **Condition 1** used a **blank board** with no colors, which gave no hint. >> **Condition 2** used a **colored board** of dark and light squares, a visual hint. >> **Condition 3** wrote the **words “black” and “pink”** on the squares, drawing attention to the two colors. >> **Condition 4** wrote the **words “bread” and “butter”** on the squares. || The problem was **easier to solve when the information provided pointed toward the correct representation of the problem**. >> The **“bread and butter” condition emphasized the difference between the squares**, since bread and butter naturally come in pairs, one of each. >> **Participants solved this condition twice as fast as the blank condition**. || The board and dominoes were identical in all four conditions. The labels changed only how easily solvers came to see each domino as pairing one square of each kind.
22|The **think-aloud protocol** is a method for studying problem solving in which participants **say aloud what they are thinking while solving a problem**. >> They are **instructed not to describe what they are doing, but to verbalize new thoughts** as they come. >> The **goal** is to discover **what information the participant is attending to while solving the problem**. >> Researchers can follow what the solver notices and when, instead of seeing only the final answer. || With the **mutilated-checkerboard problem**, think-aloud protocols reveal a **shift in how the solver perceives the elements of the problem**. >> **Participants get the problem after realizing that the words “bread” and “butter” are important**. >> Before that moment they talk about laying down dominoes, and afterward they talk about pairing the two kinds of squares. || This shift is **similar to the Gestalt idea of a representational shift**. >> The board and the rules do not change. >> The solver’s mental representation reorganizes, which is restructuring, and the answer follows. >> Think-aloud protocols capture restructuring while it happens, not just its outcome.
