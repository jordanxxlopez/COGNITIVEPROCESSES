2|The **modal model** of short-term memory proved **too simple**. **Short-term memory is not just passive storage**, because it also depends on **“control processes,”** so a fuller model has to include them. >> **Control processes** are things we actively do with information, such as choosing it, repeating it and changing it. >> In the modal model, short-term memory was mostly a waiting room where information stayed until it was used or lost. || **Baddeley and Hitch (1974)** proposed **working memory**, which resembles short-term memory but covers more. **Working memory** is a **limited-capacity system for the temporary storage and manipulation of information for complex tasks such as comprehension, learning and reasoning**. >> **Storage** keeps information on hand for a short time. >> **Manipulation** means working on that information, for example by changing it, combining it or calculating with it. || Complex tasks call for both at the same moment. >> To **comprehend** a sentence, we hold its opening while linking it to the words that come next. >> To **learn**, we keep new material active while tying it to what we already know. >> To **reason**, we keep facts in mind while working toward a conclusion. || Storage and manipulation draw on one shared, **limited capacity**. >> Heavy processing leaves less room for holding information. >> Holding a lot leaves less room for processing, so a mental task gets harder as more has to be juggled.
3|**Multiplying 43 times 6 in your head** needs storage and active processing at once. \[43\times6=258\] The calculation runs in five steps. >> **Step 1. Visualize 43 × 6**, keeping the whole problem in mind so later steps can return to it. >> **Step 2. Multiply 3 × 6**, which gives 18. \[3\times6=18\] >> **Step 3. Hold the 8 in memory** as the ones digit **while carrying the 1 over to the 4**. >> **Step 4. Multiply 6 × 4 = 24, then add the carried 1 to get 25**. \[6\times4=24,\qquad24+1=25\] >> **Step 5. Combine the 25 with the 8** that has been held, giving **258**. || The problem uses both **storage** and **active processes**. >> Keeping the 8 while the work continues is storage. >> Carrying, multiplying and adding are active processes that change the information. >> The 8 must last through several steps, so the answer depends on storage and processing working together, which is the job of working memory. || Losing any stored piece breaks the answer. If the 8 slips away during the tens, or the carried 1 is never added, the result is wrong. Mental arithmetic is therefore a direct test of working memory.
4|**Short-term memory** in the **Atkinson and Shiffrin** model and **working memory** in the **Baddeley and Hitch** model differ in how they are built and what they are for. || In the Atkinson and Shiffrin model, short-term memory has two main features. >> It is a **single component for all types of information**, so words, pictures and sounds share one store. >> It is **mainly used for holding information for a short time**. || Working memory in the Baddeley and Hitch model has **three components**. >> The **central executive** directs and coordinates the system. >> The **visuospatial sketchpad** holds visual and spatial information. >> The **phonological loop** holds verbal and auditory information. >> Baddeley **added** a fourth part, the **episodic buffer**, **in 2000**, as a place where information from different sources can be combined. || Working memory is **used for the manipulation of information during complex cognition**, including reasoning, comprehension and learning. >> If there were one store, any two tasks held at once should get in each other’s way. >> With separate components, a verbal task and a visual task can run together with little interference, since each has its own store.
5|Every **component of working memory** has a specific job. || The **phonological loop** deals with verbal and auditory information and has two parts. >> The **phonological store** **holds verbal and auditory information**, such as words and speech sounds. >> The **articulatory rehearsal process** is **responsible for rehearsal that keeps items in the phonological store from decaying**. **Articulatory** refers to speech movements, so this process silently repeats the items like an inner voice. >> Without that rehearsal, items in the store fade within a few seconds. || The **visuospatial sketchpad** **holds visual and spatial information**. >> Visual information is about appearance, such as shape and color. >> Spatial information is about position and layout, such as how a room is arranged. || The **central executive** **pulls information from long-term memory, coordinates the other components, and directs and maintains attention**. >> In **Baddeley’s working memory model**, the executive is linked to the phonological loop, which holds **verbal and auditory information**, and to the sketchpad, which holds **visual and spatial information**. >> The executive stores nothing itself. It decides what the two stores hold, brings in long-term knowledge and keeps attention on the task.
6|The **working memory model of Baddeley and Hitch (1974)** describes how information moves through the system. >> **Input** from the world enters **sensory memory**. >> From there it passes to the **central executive**. >> The executive trades information back and forth with **long-term knowledge**. || The executive also trades information in both directions with the **phonological loop** and the **visuospatial sketchpad**. >> A spoken word goes to the phonological loop, and a shape goes to the sketchpad. >> Long-term knowledge links directly to both of these stores as well. || Working memory is a group of interacting parts inside a larger memory system. >> The executive controls what each store holds and does. >> Working memory keeps drawing on stored knowledge, such as word meanings and familiar images, and it can send new information into long-term storage. >> Because information flows both ways, working memory can interpret new input using what we already know, and it also adds to that knowledge.
7|Three effects reveal how the **phonological loop** operates. || In the **phonological similarity effect**, **letters or words that sound similar are confused**. >> **Phonological** means having to do with the sounds of language. >> Letters such as B, D and G sound alike, so their sound codes overlap and they get mixed up. || In the **word-length effect**, **memory for lists of words is better for short words than for long words**. >> **It takes longer to rehearse long words and to produce them during recall**. >> In a fixed amount of time, fewer long words can be rehearsed, so fewer stay active. || In **articulatory suppression**, **speaking interferes with rehearsal, so memory is reduced**. >> Saying something aloud, such as “the, the, the,” takes over the rehearsal process. >> The items to be remembered are no longer refreshed, so they fade from the store. || All three effects point the same way. >> If verbal information were stored as images or as meanings, these effects would be hard to explain. >> Verbal information is held as sound and kept alive by an inner voice. When that voice is slow, busy or confused by similar sounds, memory suffers.
8|In the **phonological similarity effect**, **words that sound similar are confused by people**. **Conrad (1964)** tested it using letters. || **Participants saw target letters that were quickly flashed**, and **then participants wrote them down**. >> **Half of the time the letters were similar, and half of the time they were not**. >> The letters disappeared so fast that writing them depended on memory. || **Mistakes were made**, and they followed a clear pattern. >> Participants were **unlikely to replace a letter with one that looked like the target**, such as **F** for **E**. These two letters share strokes but have different-sounding names. >> They were **likely to replace it with one that sounded like the target**, such as **B** for **E**. The names “E” and “B” end in the same vowel sound. >> Lists of similar-sounding letters led to more errors than lists of distinct-sounding letters. || The participants were holding the letters by sound. >> They only ever saw the letters and never heard them. >> A visual code would produce confusions between letters that look alike. A sound code would produce confusions between letters whose names sound alike. >> The errors followed sound, so the letters were held as sound codes in the phonological loop.
9|The **phonological similarity effect experiment** **demonstrated the phonological similarity effect**, in which **people confuse letters that sound similar**, **even though the information was presented visually**. >> The letters came in through the eyes. >> The confusions still came from how the letters sound, not how they look. || It **also showed that the phonological loop is necessary for the conversion**, **not just for holding information**. >> No one has to decide to name a letter silently. Seeing it brings up its spoken name, and the name is what memory keeps. >> This automatic **recoding** turns a visual input into a phonological code. || The phonological loop therefore does two jobs here. >> It converts written letters and words into sound. >> It stores and rehearses the sound code until recall, which is why seen items produce sound-based errors. || Recoding explains why written material behaves like spoken material in memory. Once letters become names, the same inner voice that rehearses heard words rehearses them too, and errors follow sound however the letters arrived.
10|In the **word-length effect**, **when memorizing words, you can remember fewer words if the words are long**. >> **Baddeley and colleagues (1975, 1984)** found that **people remembered more of the short words than the long words**. >> Long words take more time to say, so fewer can be rehearsed before they fade. || **Ellis and Hennelly (1980)** found that **Welsh–English bilingual children** have a **shorter digit span in Welsh than in English**. >> The cause is that **Welsh numbers take longer to pronounce**. >> The children know the same numbers in both languages, and only the speaking time differs, which makes the test especially clean. >> The Welsh place name **Llanfairpwllgwyngyllgogerychwyrndrobwllllantysiliogogogoch** is a well-known example of a very long Welsh word. || **The number of words you can say in 1.5 to 2.0 seconds is likely to be your digit span**. >> The phonological loop holds roughly what can be rehearsed in that window before it fades. >> Short words fit easily and long words fit less well, so span depends on speaking time rather than a fixed number of items. || The limit of the phonological loop is therefore a limit of time. More items fit when each is quick to say, and fewer fit when each takes longer.
11|**Articulatory suppression** happens **if you speak while memorizing**, which **keeps the phonological loop busy**. >> **You get worse at remembering**. >> **The word-length and phonological similarity effects are reduced**. || The **word-length effect** shrinks because rehearsal is blocked. >> Short words normally win because they can be rehearsed faster. >> When speaking takes over rehearsal, neither short nor long words get rehearsed, so the short-word advantage fades. || The **phonological similarity effect** shrinks because the conversion into sound is blocked. >> Seen letters are normally turned into sound codes, which causes sound-based confusions. >> With the loop busy, visually presented letters are not turned into sound, so sound similarity matters less. || These findings **support the concept of a phonological loop**. >> Repeating “the, the, the” fills the speech system that rehearsal needs. >> A single manipulation removes both effects, so rehearsal and recoding rely on the same speech-based system. If the two effects came from separate systems, one manipulation would not remove both.
12|The **visuospatial sketchpad** **holds visual and spatial information**. >> **Visual** information concerns appearance, such as shape and color. >> **Spatial** information concerns location and arrangement, such as where objects are relative to one another. || The sketchpad is **involved in the process of visual imagery**. >> **Visual imagery** means forming or holding a picture in the mind without seeing it. >> Planning a route, picturing how furniture fits in a room and mentally turning an object all rely on the sketchpad. || The sketchpad is connected to the **central executive** and to **long-term knowledge**. >> The executive decides what the sketchpad holds. >> Long-term knowledge supplies stored images and layouts that can be brought into it. || The sketchpad and the phonological loop are separate parts, so a visual task and a verbal task can run together with little interference. >> A picture in the sketchpad leaves the loop free for words. >> Words in the loop leave the sketchpad free for images and places. >> The sketchpad can also transform images, such as turning a mental picture to a new angle, so it manipulates as well as stores.
13|The **sentence experiment** of **Brooks (1968)** puts the **phonological loop** and the **visuospatial sketchpad** in competition. || Participants **memorized a sentence** and kept it in mind. >> They then stepped through it word by word and **indicated whether each word was or was not a noun**. >> In **condition 1**, they **indicated by speaking**, saying “yes” or “no.” >> In **condition 2**, they **indicated by pointing** to yes or no on a sheet. >> The task stayed the same and only the way of responding changed. || For the participants, **pointing was easier than speaking**, because **the phonological loop was busy processing the sentence, but the sketchpad was free**. >> A sentence is verbal, so it sat in the phonological loop. >> Spoken answers also need the loop, so they competed with the sentence. >> Pointing relies on the sketchpad, which had nothing else to do, so there was no competition. || Holding a sentence and sorting its words keeps the phonological loop busy the whole time. A second verbal demand interferes, while a spatial response does not.
14|The **“F” demo** of **Brooks (1968)** tests the same two parts with a picture instead of a sentence. || Participants **memorized a shape**, a block **F**, and kept the image in mind. >> Starting at a corner marked with a star, they mentally moved around the F and **indicated whether each corner was an “inside corner” or an “outside corner.”** >> In **condition 1**, they **indicated by speaking**, saying “in” or “out.” >> In **condition 2**, they **indicated by pointing**, going down a numbered list of corners and pointing to **OUT** or **IN** in each row. || Here **speaking was easier than pointing**, **the opposite of the sentence experiment**, because **the sketchpad was busy with the image, but the phonological loop was free**. >> The F is a visual image, so it sat in the sketchpad. >> Pointing means looking and moving the hand to places on a sheet, which needs the same spatial resources as the image. >> Speaking uses the phonological loop, which was free. || The two Brooks tasks produce a reversal. Speaking was harder for the sentence and easier for the image, and pointing was the reverse. A single store could not produce this crossover.
15|**Tasks are easier when the information held in mind and the operation performed on it involve different types of short-term memory**. One type is **verbal or phonological**, and the other is **visual or spatial**. >> A verbal memory with a spatial response, as in pointing during the sentence task, was easy. >> A visual memory with a verbal response, as in speaking during the F task, was easy. >> When memory and response used the same system, performance dropped. || **The two types of short-term memory are therefore somewhat independent**. >> **At the least, they have separate capacities**. >> Filling the phonological loop leaves the sketchpad with room to spare, and filling the sketchpad leaves the loop with room to spare. || Each part interferes with itself far more than with the other. >> Two verbal demands compete, and so do two visual-spatial demands. >> A verbal demand paired with a visual-spatial one lets both run well. >> Listening to directions while driving uses both systems, and trouble comes where their demands overlap.

17|**Mental rotation** means turning a mental image to a new orientation. **Shepard and Metzler (1971)** showed pairs of three-dimensional objects built from cubes, and participants judged whether the two were the same object or mirror images. >> In **picture-plane rotation** pairs, one object is turned flat within the page, like a clock hand. >> In **depth rotation** pairs, one object is turned as if spun around a vertical axis. >> Mirror-image pairs can never be rotated into a match, so rejecting them depends on finishing the rotation. || Reaction time, **RT**, grew steadily with the **angle of rotation**. >> Pairs at **0 degrees** took about **1 second**. >> Pairs at **180 degrees** took about **4.5 seconds**. >> RT rose in a straight line in between. || Participants **mentally rotated** one object to line it up with the other. >> A larger difference in angle needs a longer rotation. >> Each extra degree adds about the same time, as if the image turned at a steady speed through every in-between position, the way a real object would. >> Depth rotations gave the same straight-line pattern, so the mental image is handled as a three-dimensional object. Mental rotation is active manipulation in the visuospatial sketchpad.
18|The **central executive** **does the “work” of working memory**. Its jobs include the following. >> **Directing and maintaining attention**, which covers **focusing**, **dividing** and **switching**. Focusing keeps relevant information active, dividing spreads resources over two tasks, and switching moves resources from one task to another. >> **Controlling suppression of irrelevant information**, so distractions do not use up working memory. >> **Coordinating the sketchpad and the phonological loop**, so both can serve a task. >> **Performing calculations**, as in mental arithmetic. || The executive is **often split into updating, shifting and inhibition**, following **Miyake and colleagues (2000)**. >> **Updating** replaces old contents of working memory with new, relevant information. >> **Shifting** means moving between tasks or mental sets. >> **Inhibition** means holding back unwanted thoughts or responses. || **The idea of a “central executive” is often criticized as theorizing a “homunculus” in the brain**. >> A **homunculus** is a “little person” in the head who makes every decision. >> Saying an executive controls everything names the control without explaining it. >> Dividing the executive into updating, shifting and inhibition gives specific functions that can be measured separately and that vary between people.
19|The **episodic buffer** is **a fourth component, added to the working memory model by Baddeley in 2000**. >> It has a **limited capacity of about 4 “chunks.”** A **chunk** is a meaningful unit that may contain several pieces. >> It **binds information from the phonological loop, the visuospatial sketchpad and long-term memory into integrated “episodes.”** **Binding** means joining separate pieces into one combined memory. || In the updated model, the **central executive** connects to the **phonological loop**, the **episodic buffer** and the **visuospatial sketchpad**. >> The buffer links with both the loop and the sketchpad. >> All three connect to **long-term knowledge**. || Two findings explain **why the episodic buffer was needed**. >> **People can recall about 16 words of a meaningful sentence**, **far beyond their span for unrelated words**. Meaning and grammar join the words into a few large chunks, and the original model had nowhere to keep them. >> **Some amnesic patients can immediately recall a just-heard story**. **Amnesia** is a severe loss of long-term memory. Since these patients could not store the story long-term, it must have been held as a meaningful episode in a working memory component large enough to contain it.
20|**Working memory and the brain** can be studied through **individual differences**, meaning the ways people differ from each other. **Vogel and colleagues (2005)** first **determined participants’ working memory capacity**, sorted them into a **high-capacity group** and a **low-capacity group**, and then **measured ERP responses**. >> An **ERP**, or **event-related potential**, is electrical brain activity recorded from the scalp and timed to events in the task. || Participants were **shown either simple or complex stimuli** in trials with four phases. >> A **cue** arrow pointed left or right to mark which side to remember. >> A brief **memory display** of colored bars at different angles appeared. >> A blank **delay** followed, and ERPs during this delay tracked how much each person was holding. >> A **test display** then asked whether the angle of a red bar had changed. || In the **simple** condition, the cued side had only **red bars**, the targets. >> In the **complex** condition, it had red bars plus **blue bars**, which were **distractors** to ignore. >> If a person screens out the blue bars, brain activity should reflect only the red ones. If the blue bars get in, activity should rise as though more items were being held.
21|**Vogel and colleagues (2005)** compared brain activity in **high-capacity** and **low-capacity** participants across three conditions. >> **Two items**, meaning two red bars. >> **Four items**, meaning four red bars. >> **Two items with two distractors**, meaning two red bars and two blue bars to ignore. || **Activity continues well into the retention interval**, after the memory display is gone. >> This activity reflects the items held in visual working memory. >> The more items held, the more activity. || In **high-capacity** individuals, **activity is greater when there are four relevant items than when there are two relevant items**. >> **Adding irrelevant blue items has no effect**, so two targets with two distractors look like two targets alone. >> These people keep the distractors out. || In **low-capacity** individuals, **adding irrelevant items increases activity**. >> Two targets with two distractors produce activity close to four targets. >> These people let the distractors in, where they take space meant for targets. || The groups differ mainly in how well they control what enters working memory, not in how much raw space they have.
22|**Vogel and colleagues (2005)** also related each participant’s **memory capacity** to their **filtering efficiency**. >> **Filtering efficiency** is an **EEG** measure of how well someone keeps irrelevant items out of working memory. **EEG** records the brain’s electrical activity from the scalp. || **Capacity is correlated with the EEG measure of filtering efficiency**. >> **People with higher capacity are also less distracted by the irrelevant items**. >> Those who hold more items are the same people who keep unneeded items out. || This changes what a difference in capacity means. >> High capacity depends on control over what gets in, as well as on how much can be held. >> People with high capacity screen out distractors, leaving their limited space for relevant information. >> People with low capacity admit distractors, which fill working memory with items they do not need. || Better attentional control could therefore free working memory for the information that matters.
