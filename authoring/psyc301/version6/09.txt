2|**Memory** includes retaining, retrieving, and using information after the original experience is no longer present. The retained material can concern a stimulus, image, event, idea, or skill. What was encountered earlier continues to affect behavior even when its source has disappeared. || Memory is active whenever a past experience changes how someone thinks or acts in the present or future. Remembering is therefore broader than deliberately recalling a list. Using knowledge or carrying out a learned skill also depends on the continuing effects of earlier experience. || **Episodic memory** concerns events a person has experienced. **Endel Tulving** described it as **“mental time travel”** because remembering involves mentally returning to an earlier event. The event is over, but information about it becomes available within current thought.
3|**Memory is an active system** that stores, organizes, alters, and retrieves information. It does not simply preserve an untouched copy of every experience. Organizing material changes how its parts relate, and retrieving it makes those retained relationships available again. || **Encoding** converts incoming information into a form that can be stored. This is the formation of a usable memory representation when material is encountered. The information must be represented in some way before it can be retained beyond that encounter. || **Storage** holds the information, while **retrieval** brings it back into use. These operations answer different questions. Encoding concerns how the representation is formed, storage concerns its retention, and retrieval concerns accessing what has been retained.
4|Memory includes **sensory, short-term, and long-term systems**. Sensory memory briefly retains the effects of stimulation. Short-term memory holds a limited amount for immediate use, while long-term memory preserves information beyond that brief period. The systems differ in how long and how much they retain. || **Long-term memory** includes **declarative** and **procedural memory**. Declarative memory concerns information about facts and events. Procedural memory concerns learned skills, distinguishing retained knowledge about something from retained knowledge of how to do something. || **Episodic and semantic memory** are forms of declarative memory. Episodic memory concerns experienced events, while semantic memory concerns facts and knowledge. They are different kinds of retained information within long-term memory, rather than separate stages that every memory must pass through in sequence.
5|**Atkinson and Shiffrin’s modal model (1968)** distinguishes three stores. **Sensory memory** briefly registers incoming information for seconds or fractions of a second. Its broad initial contents do not all remain available for continued thought. Selection determines which material receives further processing. || **Short-term memory** holds roughly **five to seven items for 15–20 seconds** in this description. The information currently held can guide a response. **Long-term memory** has a much larger capacity and can preserve information for years or decades. || **Rehearsal** is a **control process**, meaning an activity performed on memory contents rather than another store. Repeating material keeps it active in short-term memory. Information can also enter long-term storage and later be retrieved into short-term memory, allowing present thought to use both new input and retained knowledge.
6|Looking at a directory supplies visual information about many entries at once. **Sensory memory** briefly registers that incoming scene. The initial input therefore contains more than the one phone number needed for the current call. || This broad registration does not preserve the entire directory indefinitely. The information is available only briefly while initial processing occurs. Material not selected can disappear even though it reached the visual system. || **Attention** selects the relevant entry for further use. Registering a display and choosing one part of it are different operations. The directory supplies many possible inputs, while the immediate task requires a particular number to become available beyond that initial sensory moment.
7|Attending to **555-5100** selects one number from the directory. That information becomes available in **short-term memory**, where it can guide the call. The relevant digits are now the focus of the task rather than merely one entry among many visible entries. || Selection reduces the amount currently being used. The original visual input contains the wider display, but the short-term representation concerns the number required for dialing. Remembering every directory entry is unnecessary for completing the call. || **Sensory registration and short-term retention serve different functions**. The first makes incoming visual information briefly available. Attention then gives the selected number a role in deliberate, immediate behavior. The same digits are involved, but their availability now depends on the material chosen for continued processing.
8|Repeating **555-5100** helps keep the digits available while the call is made. This is **maintenance rehearsal**, the repeated activation of information being held temporarily. The person continues working with the number after looking away from its printed entry. || The digits are the contents of **short-term memory**, while rehearsal is the process maintaining them. A store and an operation are therefore different parts of the account. The rehearsal loop represents activity on the contents rather than a separate place containing another copy. || The repeated number can guide the sequence of dialing responses. Maintaining it bridges the interval between reading the directory and completing the call. Immediate use therefore involves both retaining the digits and carrying out the action for which they were selected.
9|A memorized phone number can enter **long-term memory**. It can then remain available beyond the short interval required for the original call. Retention no longer depends on keeping the directory in view or continuously repeating the number throughout the time between calls. || **Short-term and long-term storage** refer to different roles, even when the remembered content is the same. Short-term storage supports current use. Long-term storage preserves the number across separate occasions. || Information encountered during one activity can therefore become knowledge used later. The original call ends, but its number may still be retained. That lasting representation connects an earlier encounter with a future opportunity to retrieve and use the information.
10|**Retrieval from long-term memory** makes a previously learned number available again. The person does not have to inspect the directory anew. The stored number returns to **short-term memory**, where its digits can guide dialing. || Retaining the number between calls and actively using it during a call are different functions. Long-term memory provides the lasting representation. Short-term memory supports the information currently available for the response. || Information therefore does not move only from incoming stimulation toward long-term storage. Older knowledge can return to current awareness and affect immediate behavior. Retrieval links something no longer present in the environment with what the person is doing now.
11|**Sensory memory** briefly retains the effects of stimulation after the input ends. As an initial memory stage, it is described as preserving an **exact copy of incoming information for less than a second**. The representation remains momentarily available even though the original source is gone. || An **icon** is a fleeting visual image or representation. It preserves what was seen briefly after the visual event. An **echo** is a brief continuation of a sound within the auditory system after that sound has been heard. || The difference concerns **sensory modality**, meaning the kind of input retained. An icon is visual, while an echo is auditory. These are short continuations of sensory information, rather than a person deliberately repeating a list to maintain it for a longer task.
12|**Persistence of vision** is the brief continuation of perceived light. A moving sparkler can leave a perceived trail because information from its recent positions remains available momentarily. The light has changed position, but the preceding stimulation continues to affect perception. || Successive **film frames** also arrive separately. Brief visual persistence contributes to continuity by retaining information as the next presentation arrives. Sensory memory can therefore fill small gaps rather than leaving every moment of input completely isolated. || Its important combination is **large capacity with rapid decay**. Many details can be registered and held for initial processing, but most do not remain accessible for long. Holding a broad scene briefly is different from preserving that whole scene over an extended interval.
13|**Sperling (1960)** studied the capacity and duration of **visual sensory memory**. Participants saw an array of letters flashed briefly on a screen. They reported letters afterward, when they could no longer continue inspecting the display. || Brief presentation limits the opportunity to study each item individually. The response therefore depends on information retained from the exposure. Naming letters also takes time, during which some initially available information can fade. || **Capacity** concerns how much information was available, while **duration** concerns how quickly it disappears. A short final report can reflect either a small initial store or loss while the person responds. Separating these possibilities requires changing how much must be reported and when the relevant letters are selected.
14|In **whole report**, participants name as many letters as possible after the array disappears. They must produce a relatively long response while the sensory representation is fading. Some letters can be lost before they reach that part of the answer. || In **partial report**, a tone presented **after the display** identifies one row to report. Participants do not know the relevant row while viewing the letters. Successful reporting of whichever row is requested therefore depends on broader initial information than one row selected in advance. || **Delayed partial report** adds a waiting interval before the tone. The array and requested response remain comparable, but selection occurs later. Comparing immediate and delayed cues tests how much of that broad sensory representation survives until the participant learns which letters matter.
15|The display contains **three rows of four letters**, including **X M L T** in the top row. The participant initially encounters the entire array. The row to report is selected only after the display has ended, so attention cannot be restricted to the requested row in advance. || In **delayed partial report**, the person waits before receiving the cue. If the top row is requested but only **M** is reported, one relevant letter remains accessible while the others have been lost. The answer is shorter than the information originally presented. || The crucial delay occurs before selection from memory. The participant must preserve the array long enough for the tone to identify the target row. Having initially registered a letter does not guarantee being able to retrieve it after the sensory representation has faded.
16|In **whole report**, participants recalled about **4.5 of 12 letters**. In **immediate partial report**, they recalled about **3.3 of the four letters** in the requested row. They could do this for any row, even though its identity was supplied only after viewing. || That advantage indicates broad initial availability. Participants were not simply encoding one known row more carefully. A short response allowed them to recover much of whichever part of the display the cue selected before the rest disappeared. || **Delayed partial report** fell to roughly **one letter out of four**. Waiting before the cue reduced how much was still available for selection. The important contrast is the strong immediate partial-report performance and its decline with delay, linking large initial capacity with rapid loss.
17|**Visual sensory memory has a large capacity but decays rapidly**. A whole-report answer of four or five letters does not mean that only those letters were initially encoded. More can be available at the beginning than can be named before the representation fades. || Immediate partial report allows successful recall of whichever row is requested. Because that row is unpredictable until after the display, information must initially extend across the scene. The cue selects from a representation broader than the final short answer. || Within about **one second**, much of that representation disappears. **Attention** can move selected material into short-term memory while it remains available. Broad initial registration therefore coexists with the later loss of information that was not selected for continued use.
18|**Partial-report accuracy declines as the row cue is delayed**. A tone immediately after the array permits recall from a relatively strong sensory representation. A later tone arrives after more information has faded, leaving less to select. || The manipulated interval is the time between the display and the cue. It does not give participants a longer view of the letters. The comparison concerns retention after exposure rather than additional opportunity to study the array. || By approximately **one second**, the partial-report advantage approaches whole-report performance. A shorter required answer helps most when its cue arrives promptly. Once the broad sensory representation has largely disappeared, knowing which row to report cannot restore its missing letters.
19|**Short-term memory, or STM**, briefly retains a limited amount of information for immediate use. Its contents are sensitive to **interruption and interference**. Another activity or competing information can disrupt what was available for the current task. || **Memory span** is the amount a person can hold and recall under the task’s conditions. The traditional estimate is **seven plus or minus two units**. This describes a range around seven rather than an exact universal count. || Capacity and duration are separate properties. Capacity concerns how much is available together, while duration concerns how long it remains available. Interruption can shorten access even when the amount originally retained was within the person’s span.
20|**Recoding** reorganizes or modifies information in short-term memory. The person changes how its elements are represented rather than leaving them as unrelated incoming pieces. Their organization can affect how easily they are maintained and used. || **Maintenance rehearsal** silently repeats the material to prolong its availability. It refreshes the information currently held. **Elaborative rehearsal** instead connects new material with memories and knowledge already available in **long-term memory**. || Repeating a label keeps that label active, while relating it to existing knowledge builds meaningful connections. Those connections support retention beyond immediate repetition. Both are active operations on memory, but they contribute through different kinds of processing.
21|**Short-term memory** contains selected new information and information retrieved from **long-term memory**. Current thought therefore draws on both recent input and older knowledge. The small amount presently active is different from the much larger amount retained outside awareness. || **Understanding sentences and doing arithmetic** require holding material while working with it. Words must remain available as their relationships are interpreted, and numbers must remain available during an operation. Temporary retention supports processing across successive parts of a task. || **Dialing, navigating, and knowing what one is doing** similarly depend on current access to relevant information. STM is described as a **window on the present** because its active contents guide immediate thought and behavior. Long-term knowledge contributes when it is retrieved into that present activity.

23|**Duration** concerns how long information stays available, while **capacity** concerns how much can be held at once. These properties are influenced by what the person does with the material. A measure of performance is therefore also affected by strategies used during the task. || **Rehearsal** can extend duration by keeping the information active. **Chunking** can increase effective capacity by combining smaller elements into larger meaningful units. One changes continued maintenance, while the other changes how the information is organized. || These are **control processes**. A person rehearsing throughout a delay is not doing the same thing as a person whose repetition is blocked. Measuring temporary memory requires considering both the store and the operations used to support its contents.
24|**Brown (1958) and Peterson and Peterson (1959)** presented a **trigram**, a set of three letters, followed by a number. For **F Z L, 45**, the letters are the target and 45 starts a competing task. The participant must remember the letters while doing something else. || The competing task is **counting backward by threes**, such as 42, 39, 36. This occupies the retention interval and interferes with silently repeating the trigram. The delay is filled with activity rather than ordinary unrestricted rehearsal. || After **3–18 seconds**, the participant recalls the letters. Varying that interval changes how long the same kind of information must survive under the competing demand. Letter recall measures retention, while counting is the method used to limit maintenance rehearsal.
25|On the **first Brown–Peterson trial**, recall remains relatively high after both the short and long delays. Extending the interval from **three to eighteen seconds** produces only a modest reduction in the depicted pattern. Much of the trigram remains available during this first filled delay. || Participants are counting backward, so the retention occurs while ordinary repetition is disrupted. The long-delay performance cannot simply be described as unrestricted rehearsal of the letters. The target must survive the competing activity. || The first trial also lacks earlier trigrams from this task. There are no preceding letter sets in the series to compete with the current one. Its memory context therefore contains less similar prior material than a trial occurring after repeated presentations of other trigrams.
26|On the **third trial**, recall after **eighteen seconds** is much poorer than recall after **three seconds**. The long filled delay now produces a more substantial cost. The difference concerns performance within this later position in the task. || Two earlier trigrams have already been encountered. Those sets resemble the current target in form and in the kind of response required. Their memories can compete when the person tries to recover the latest letters. || **Proactive interference** is disruption of newer information by older information. The third-trial pattern therefore involves both the current delay and preceding learning. The same waiting interval occurs in a changed memory context, containing more similar material than at the beginning of the task.
27|After **many trials**, recall at the long delay is very low compared with recall at the short delay. The **eighteen-second** interval is now associated with substantial forgetting. It occurs after repeated attempts to retain and report similar letter sets. || Each preceding trigram supplies another possible competitor. The person must recover the current letters rather than letters from an earlier trial. Similarity among the sets increases the opportunity for older material to interfere. || **Accumulated proactive interference** therefore contributes to the difficult long-delay condition. Rehearsal is blocked by counting while competing memories have built up across the series. The effect is not defined only by how much time passes after the latest trigram.
28|**Averaging across Brown–Peterson trials** produces better recall after the short delay than after the long one. This overall decline combines performance from the entire series. It includes initial trials and later trials with a larger history of similar material. || The trials entering that average do not all have the same amount of prior interference. Early in the series, few older trigrams compete with the target. Later, the current set must be distinguished from many preceding sets. || The mean therefore describes a delay effect across different memory histories. Longer intervals predict poorer average recall, while trial position helps explain how that pattern develops. Keeping the average and its contributing conditions distinct preserves both aspects of the result.
29|**Longer delays reduce letter recall**, but the size of the reduction depends on **trial position**. The filled interval limits rehearsal, while the series introduces similar information over time. Both factors affect the conditions under which the latest trigram is recovered. || An **interaction** means that the effect of one factor changes with another. Here, long delays become especially harmful as more trials have occurred. The result cannot be described fully by giving one identical time-related loss for every point in the series. || Earlier letter sets provide competing memories. The person’s task remains recalling the most recent set, but the amount of older material increases. Long-delay recall therefore reflects how long the target has been held together with the interference accumulated before it.
30|**Decay** is loss of a memory trace associated with the passage of time. Brown and Peterson and Peterson investigated whether short-term information disappears when rehearsal is prevented. Counting fills the delay during which the letters must remain available. || **Proactive interference** occurs when older information disrupts newer information. Previous trigrams can compete with the current one. The change across trial positions therefore identifies an influence beyond waiting through the latest interval. || Short-term forgetting involves **both decay and interference** in this account. One concerns the persistence of a representation over time, while the other concerns competition among memories. Its effective duration is about **15–20 seconds**, with rehearsal and competing information influencing what remains accessible under the task conditions.
31|**Interference theories** explain forgetting through competition among memories. One memory can disrupt or replace another, making the target difficult to recover. The difficulty is produced by the relationship between stored information rather than the mere passage of time. || **Retroactive interference** occurs when newer information disrupts recall of older information. The target was learned first, and the competitor came afterward. **Proactive interference** reverses that relationship, with older material disrupting a newer target. || The labels therefore depend on the order of learning and on which information is being requested. Both involve memories competing with memories. Distinguishing them requires identifying the target first rather than simply noting that two experiences are involved.
32|**Retroactive interference** occurs when a **new memory interferes with an old one**. The information being requested was learned before the competing material. The later learning makes recovery of that earlier target more difficult. || A **new phone number** can interfere when the person tries to remember the **old number**. The newer sequence is available and may come to mind in response to the same general request. It competes with the number learned earlier. || The target determines the label. The problem is retroactive because the person wants the old information and the later memory disrupts it. Merely knowing that an old and new number coexist is insufficient without specifying which one must be recalled.
33|**Proactive interference** occurs when an **old memory interferes with new information**. Earlier learning competes with the target that was acquired more recently. The person may retrieve something familiar that belongs to the wrong occasion. || Memories of parking locations from **the past week** can interfere with finding the car **today**. Today’s location is the target, while earlier locations supply competitors. The direction runs from older events toward the newer memory. || Both proactive and retroactive interference are strongest for **routine events**. Similar repeated experiences overlap and are easier to confuse. An unusual event is more distinctive, reducing its competition with the routine pattern. The direction of interference still depends on whether the requested event is older or newer than its competitor.
34|**STM has a capacity limit** as well as a duration limit. Information can be lost even over a brief interval if too much must be held together. Increasing the list length increases the demand on retaining a complete set. || **Digit span** measures how many digits a person can reproduce in their original order. Short lists such as **2 1 4** are followed by progressively longer ones. Correct recall requires retaining which digits occurred and where each belonged in the sequence. || **Capacity varies between people**. The task estimates what a particular person can maintain under its conditions. Its increasing list lengths identify where the demand exceeds successful retention of the whole sequence, rather than measuring how many digits can be recognized individually.
35|**Digit span** is the length of the longest row recalled **completely and correctly**. The rows become progressively longer, increasing the amount that must remain available. The score concerns successful reproduction of an entire ordered sequence. || Remembering the right digits in a different order does not reproduce the row. For example, **2 1 4 9** specifies both identities and positions. The arrangement is part of the information the task requires. || If a seven-digit row is recalled correctly but longer rows are not, the demonstrated span is **seven**. A partly correct ten-digit response does not make the span ten. The scoring rule therefore connects the capacity estimate with the longest complete success.
36|**George A. Miller’s “The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two”** concerns limits on information-processing capacity. The estimate describes a typical range around seven rather than exactly seven for every person. Different tasks and individuals can vary within that broad description. || A capacity statement also requires identifying the unit being counted. An item can contain a simple element or a larger organized combination. The same numerical span can therefore represent different amounts of underlying material. || The proposal concerns how much can be processed together. Counting remembered units and specifying what those units contain are related but separate parts of explaining limited capacity. A number alone does not establish that every remembered item carries the same information.
37|**Miller’s (1956) estimate of seven plus or minus two** has been applied to digits, words, multidigit numbers, and phrases. Those units differ in how much they contain. One phrase can include several words, while one multidigit number includes several digits. || An **item-count account** treats each counted unit as an item. An **information-based account** considers how much detail must be retained within it. Equal numbers of objects can therefore impose unequal memory demands. || **Alvarez and Cavanagh (2004)** used simple colored squares and more complex objects in **change detection**. Participants remembered a display and judged whether a later one differed. Comparing complexity tests whether counting each object as one equivalent unit adequately explains the amount successfully retained.
38|**Visual short-term memory, or VSTM**, temporarily preserves visual information after it disappears. In **change detection**, a later display must be compared with the remembered one. The response depends on retained appearance, not only on what is currently visible. || Capacity measured in this task may be smaller than capacity for digit or unrelated-word lists. Different materials and responses require different representations. Remembering visual properties for comparison is not identical to reproducing a spoken sequence. || Temporary memory may therefore include **modality-specific or domain-specific forms**. Modality refers to the type of sensory information, while domain refers to a kind of content. The number and specificity of such forms remain debated, so the proposal is not that one agreed division already explains every short-term task.
39|The initial display, **S1**, briefly presents colored squares for **100 milliseconds**. Participants must take in their colors and arrangement before the array disappears. The initial exposure supplies the information that must be retained for the comparison. || Colors can recur at different positions. Remembering that a color appeared somewhere is therefore different from remembering the color of a particular square. The relevant information includes the relationship between an object’s property and its location. || **VSTM** preserves enough of the first array to support a later same–different judgment. The first display is no longer available for direct inspection. Detecting a change depends on what remains represented from that earlier exposure.
40|A yellow rectangle, gray rectangle, and blue oval differ along several visual properties. The rectangles also differ in orientation. Retaining the display therefore requires more than remembering that three objects were present. || **Color, shape, orientation, and location** distinguish the items. A useful representation keeps the properties associated with the appropriate object. Knowing only that yellow occurred and a rectangle occurred would leave their particular combination unspecified. || A **same–different judgment** tests whether those retained properties match the comparison. The demand concerns preserving enough detail to identify a changed object. An object count alone cannot supply the visual information required for that decision.
41|The comparison display, **S2**, appears for **2,000 milliseconds**. Participants judge whether it matches the earlier array. The current input supplies S2, while memory supplies the earlier appearance against which it is compared. || One lower-right square is now **white** rather than **green**. Detecting the change requires remembering its earlier color at that location. Knowing that both colors occurred somewhere does not identify whether this particular object changed. || The task therefore links present vision with **visual short-term memory**. The first array cannot simply be reread alongside the second. Accuracy depends on retaining the relevant information through the interval separating them.
42|Change detection presents an initial array for **100 milliseconds**, a **900-millisecond gap**, and a comparison for **2,000 milliseconds**. The gap requires memory for the first array. Participants cannot solve the task solely by examining two simultaneously available displays. || **Set size** is the number of objects to remember. Accuracy decreases markedly above about four items, supporting an estimate of **three to four objects in visual working memory**. Larger arrays make it harder to preserve all relevant information. || Other comparisons test **orientation, color, and their conjunction**. A conjunction means retaining properties together as belonging to the same object. A blue bar can change orientation while retaining its color, so detecting the change requires the remembered feature relevant to that condition.
43|**Stimulus complexity affects change-detection performance**. Colored squares, forms labeled Chinese characters, random polygons, and shaded cubes all count as objects. They differ, however, in the visual detail needed to distinguish one from another. || Capacity is highest for **colored squares** and lower for the more complex categories. The detailed written forms, outlines, and shaded structures impose different demands from simple color differences. Counting every object as one does not make their representations equally demanding. || The result connects capacity with the **amount of information represented**. A fixed object count can contain different levels of complexity. The same–different response requires preserving whichever details distinguish the members of the tested category, not merely remembering that those objects occurred.
44|The forms labeled **Chinese characters** are identified in the annotation as **radicals**, components used in Chinese characters. One is also identified as incorrectly written. The label used for the stimulus category therefore does not precisely describe every displayed form. || Participants still must retain the actual shapes presented. Their stroke arrangements distinguish the written forms, just as outlines distinguish polygons and shading distinguishes cubes. The required representation depends on those visual properties rather than on the category name alone. || The comparison again relates **greater complexity to lower estimated capacity**. Simple colored squares are easier to maintain in larger numbers than the more detailed forms. What must be represented within an item contributes to how many items can be compared successfully after the delay.
45|**Chunking** combines small elements into larger meaningful units. A chunk can contain several digits or related objects. Organizing them this way increases the amount of material handled without treating every element as an unrelated unit. || **Ericsson and colleagues (1980)** trained a student who reached a **digit span of 79**. His experience with running allowed sequences to be grouped into meaningful running times. Existing long-term knowledge supplied a structure for retaining the digits together. || **Chase and Simon (1973)** found related organization in chess. Familiar relationships within a position let players retain groups of pieces as units. In both cases, expertise changes the organization of the material held temporarily, linking short-term performance with meaningful knowledge.
46|**Chase and Simon (1973)** compared chess masters and beginners on recall of piece placements. Masters recalled more from **meaningful arrangements**. Their expertise made familiar relationships within a game position available for organizing the individual pieces. || With **random arrangements**, the large advantage was greatly reduced. The objects were still chess pieces, but their configuration no longer provided the same game-based structure. Both groups recalled relatively few placements. || Expert performance therefore depends on **meaningful chunking** rather than a uniformly larger ability to remember any chessboard. Randomization removes relationships that connect with stored game knowledge. The difference between conditions identifies the contribution of how the material is organized.
47|**Control processes** are activities a person can use to influence memory. They describe what is done with information rather than where it is stored. Selecting, repeating, and organizing material can change its availability and usefulness. || **Maintenance rehearsal** repeats a stimulus, while **attentional strategies** keep processing directed toward selected material. Other strategies make the material more memorable. A **memory palace** uses a remembered spatial organization to support placement and recovery of information. || **Working memory** concerns actively using and managing temporarily available information. Rehearsal, attention, and memory strategies all operate alongside storage. Temporary retention therefore includes controlled activity on the contents relevant to the current task, rather than only a passive holding space.
