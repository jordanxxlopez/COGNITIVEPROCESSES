2|**Working memory** temporarily holds information while it is being used. A model of short-term memory as passive storage leaves out **control processes**, the activities that select, maintain, and work with those contents. Remembering an intermediate result while continuing a calculation requires more than keeping an unchanged item available. || **Baddeley and Hitch (1974)** proposed a **limited-capacity system for temporary storage and manipulation**. Storage keeps information accessible. Manipulation changes it or performs an operation with it while the task continues. || **Comprehension, learning, and reasoning** can require both functions together. Understanding a sentence involves keeping its parts available while relating them. Reasoning requires retaining relevant information while working toward an answer. Working memory includes these active demands rather than treating them as unrelated to temporary retention.
3|Calculating **43 times 6 mentally** requires maintaining the problem while carrying out its operations. \[43\times6=258\] The answer depends on both remembering intermediate information and changing other information during the calculation. || Multiplying the ones gives \[3\times6=18\] The **8** must remain available as the answer’s ones digit, while the **1** is carried to the tens calculation. Holding 8 is storage. Carrying 1 is an operation using the intermediate result. || The tens calculation gives \[6\times4=24,\qquad24+1=25\] That 25 represents the tens portion of the answer. Combining it with the retained 8 produces **258**. The stored digit must survive while multiplication and addition continue, so successful performance requires coordinated storage and processing.
4|In **Atkinson and Shiffrin’s short-term memory model**, one component temporarily holds different kinds of information. Its main role is brief storage. The model does not divide that store into separate verbal and visual systems. || **Baddeley and Hitch’s working-memory model** distinguishes a **central executive**, **phonological loop**, and **visuospatial sketchpad**. The loop supports verbal and auditory contents, while the sketchpad supports visual and spatial contents. The executive coordinates their use during a task. || This division includes the manipulation required by complex cognition as well as retention. **Baddeley added the episodic buffer in 2000** as a fourth component. It belongs to the expanded account, providing integration alongside the original temporary stores and control system.
5|The **phonological loop** maintains verbal and auditory information. Its **phonological store** holds the material, while an **articulatory rehearsal process** refreshes it. Articulatory refers to speech production, so rehearsal involves repeating the sounds, including internally, rather than merely looking again at their written forms. || The **visuospatial sketchpad** holds visual appearance and spatial arrangement. Shape concerns what something looks like, while spatial information concerns where its parts are and how they relate. These demands differ from keeping a sequence of speech sounds available. || The **central executive** retrieves relevant long-term information, coordinates the stores, and directs attention. It determines how their contents contribute to the current activity. It is a control component rather than another store devoted to one sensory kind of information.
6|**Baddeley and Hitch’s (1974) model** includes interacting components rather than one undifferentiated short-term store. Incoming information becomes available for the task, and the central executive coordinates how it is used. Temporary memory therefore includes both representations and the control of operations on them. || The **phonological loop** maintains verbal and auditory material. The **visuospatial sketchpad** maintains visual and spatial material. A task can use both, such as keeping a verbal intermediate result while also representing the layout of written digits. || **Long-term knowledge** connects with this system. Previously learned information can be retrieved and combined with the material currently held. The account therefore allows active processing to draw on existing knowledge rather than requiring every useful piece of information to enter anew through the senses.
7|The **phonological similarity effect** is increased confusion among items that sound alike. Phonological refers to language sounds. Overlapping sound patterns can make letters or words harder to keep distinct even when their visible forms are different. || The **word-length effect** is better memory for short than long words. Longer words take more time to rehearse and to produce during recall. A fixed period of repetition can therefore refresh fewer long items than short ones. || **Articulatory suppression** occurs when speaking interferes with rehearsal of the material being remembered. The competing speech occupies the process normally supporting verbal maintenance. These effects distinguish confusion between stored sound patterns, time required for repetition, and interference with the repetition process itself.
8|**Conrad (1964)** briefly flashed letters for participants to see, then asked them to write the letters down. Similar and dissimilar materials were compared. The required response depended on retaining identities after visual presentation ended. || Errors followed **sound similarity** more than visual similarity. A target **E** was likely to be replaced by **B**, whose letter name sounds similar. A substitution based mainly on appearance, such as **F for E**, was less likely. || The letters entered through vision, but the errors reflected their spoken names. Participants could therefore convert what they saw into a **phonological code**, a sound-based representation. The pattern of confusion identifies how the information was maintained, rather than simply repeating the format in which it was presented.
9|A **visually presented letter** can be remembered through the sound of its name. Seeing the written form and maintaining its pronunciation are separate operations. The input’s sensory format does not fix the form of the memory representation. || **Phonological similarity** therefore affects recall even without someone reading the targets aloud. Letters with similar names become harder to distinguish in memory. Their shared pronunciation supplies the overlap responsible for the confusion. || The **phonological loop** contributes to converting seen information into sounds as well as maintaining those sounds. It is not merely a storage location for material that arrived through hearing. This conversion connects visual presentation with the sound-based errors observed during later recall.
10|**Baddeley and colleagues (1975, 1984)** found better recall for lists of short words than long words. The **word-length effect** concerns pronunciation and rehearsal time. Longer verbal forms take more of the available interval to repeat, so fewer items can be refreshed together. || **Ellis and Hennelly (1980)** found a shorter digit span in **Welsh than English** among bilingual children. The number values were not different, but their Welsh names took longer to pronounce. The memory difference followed the verbal forms used to maintain them. || Span is related to how much material can be said in about **1.5–2 seconds**. Faster repetition allows more items to remain active within that period. The result connects capacity in a verbal task with the time required by the maintenance process, rather than only a fixed count independent of the items’ length.
11|**Articulatory suppression** means speaking while trying to memorize other material. The competing speech keeps the **phonological loop** busy and reduces the opportunity to rehearse the target items. Recall becomes worse because maintenance is interrupted by another demand on speech-related processing. || The **word-length effect is reduced**. Short words ordinarily benefit from faster repetition, but that advantage becomes smaller when rehearsal itself is occupied. Both short and long lists lose access to the usual maintenance process. || The **phonological similarity effect is also reduced under these conditions**. Suppression disrupts verbal maintenance and conversion of visually presented items into sound. Sound overlap then contributes less to performance. The combined changes support a component involved in rehearsal and recoding, rather than attributing every memory difference simply to visual input.
12|The **visuospatial sketchpad** keeps visual and spatial information temporarily available. Visual information concerns appearance, including shape. Spatial information concerns arrangement and relative location, including where parts belong in relation to one another. || **Visual imagery** involves maintaining such a representation without continuing to inspect the object. The remembered shape or arrangement can be used during an ongoing task. Its availability depends on temporary visual-spatial processing rather than a currently visible display alone. || This differs from the **phonological loop’s** maintenance of sounds and words. Holding an image and rehearsing a sentence impose different representational demands. The distinction allows tasks to interfere differently depending on whether they compete for verbal or visual-spatial processing.
13|**Brooks (1968)** asked participants to memorize a sentence and classify each word as a **noun or not a noun**. A noun names an entity such as a person, place, or thing. The sentence had to remain available while the decisions were made word by word. || Participants either **spoke** their judgments or **pointed** to response alternatives. The verbal material being remembered stayed the same, while the way of answering changed. **Pointing was easier than speaking**. || Maintaining and processing the sentence occupied the **phonological loop**. Speaking added a competing verbal operation. Pointing used a different response demand while the visuospatial system was available, reducing overlap between remembering the sentence and producing the answer.
14|In another **Brooks (1968)** task, participants memorized the outline of an **F**. They mentally followed its corners and classified each as **inside or outside**. An inside corner belongs to an indentation, while an outside corner turns around the projecting boundary. || The marked corner supplied a starting point for following the remembered shape. Participants answered by **speaking** or **pointing** to IN and OUT alternatives. These alternatives were response locations, not a list already supplying the correct classifications. || **Speaking was easier than pointing**. Maintaining the F used the **visuospatial sketchpad**, and pointing added a competing spatial demand. Spoken answers could use the available phonological loop. The reversal from the sentence task depends on the type of information being maintained, rather than one response method being universally easier.
15|Two simultaneous demands interfere more when they use the **same type of temporary representation**. Holding verbal information while producing verbal answers makes both activities compete for related processing. Holding a shape while responding spatially creates a different overlap. || In Brooks’s **sentence task**, pointing was easier because speaking competed with verbal maintenance. In the **F task**, speaking was easier because pointing competed with visual-spatial maintenance. Changing the remembered material reversed the advantage. || These opposite patterns support **partly independent verbal and visual-spatial capacities**. They do not require the components to operate without any interaction. They indicate that the amount of interference depends on the particular combination of memory material and response demand.

17|**Shepard and Metzler (1971)** presented block-built objects in different orientations. Participants judged whether the objects matched despite the rotation. Recognizing a match required comparing the arrangement of parts, rather than treating any difference in tilt as a different object. || **Picture-plane rotation** changes orientation within the viewing plane. **Depth rotation** changes orientation in three-dimensional space. A mental representation can be transformed to bring the parts into alignment for comparison. || For the **picture-plane pairs**, reaction time increased approximately linearly with angular difference. Greater rotation took longer to judge. Similar increases in angle added roughly similar amounts of response time. This pattern is consistent with **mental rotation**, an active spatial transformation whose extent affects processing time, rather than only passive retention of two unchanged pictures.
18|The **central executive** directs and maintains attention during working-memory tasks. It supports focusing, dividing attention, and switching between demands. These are control operations determining what receives processing, rather than another type of sensory content. || Its functions include suppressing irrelevant information, coordinating the loop and sketchpad, and managing calculations. **Miyake and colleagues (2000)** distinguish **updating**, changing maintained information, **shifting**, changing the task or operation, and **inhibition**, reducing interference from inappropriate information or responses. || The broad executive idea is criticized as a **homunculus**, a little internal agent assigned responsibility for controlling everything. Naming such an agent does not explain how control works. Specifying separate operations makes the account more precise by identifying which kind of control the task requires.
19|**Baddeley (2000)** added an **episodic buffer** with a capacity of about **four chunks**. A chunk is an organized unit containing related information. Four chunks therefore does not necessarily mean four isolated words. || The buffer **binds information from the phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, and long-term memory**. Binding keeps these contributions together as one integrated episode. Different representational sources can therefore contribute to a connected temporary memory. || People can immediately recall about **sixteen words from a meaningful sentence**, beyond their span for unrelated words. Some **amnesic patients**, whose memory is impaired, can also immediately recall a just-heard story. These findings motivated an integrated temporary representation supported by meaning and existing knowledge, even when longer-term retention is impaired.
20|**Vogel and colleagues (2005)** measured working-memory capacity and compared higher- and lower-capacity groups. Participants then performed a visual memory task during measurement of **event-related potentials, or ERPs**. These are electrical brain responses recorded in relation to task events. || A cue selected one side of a display. Participants remembered the orientations of **red bars** through a retention interval and judged whether a relevant orientation changed. More complex displays added **blue distractor bars**, which were visible but irrelevant to the answer. || The memory display lasted **100 milliseconds**, followed by a **900-millisecond retention interval**. Brain activity during the interval could therefore be compared when relevant information was maintained without the display. Adding distractors tested selection, because success required preserving the red targets while excluding extra visible items.
21|Memory-related electrical activity continued into the **retention interval**, after the bars disappeared. The conditions compared **two targets**, **four targets**, and **two targets with two distractors**. The number of visible items and the number relevant to the task could therefore be separated. || In **high-capacity participants**, four targets produced a larger response than two. The measured component has negative polarity, so greater activity means a stronger negative deflection. Adding blue distractors to two targets produced little additional response. || In **low-capacity participants**, distractors increased the response toward the four-item condition. More irrelevant material entered their memory processing. The difference concerns which items occupy the limited system, not simply whether the display contains more objects. Effective capacity is related to successful filtering as well as maintaining the targets.
22|**Working-memory capacity correlates positively with filtering efficiency**. Participants with higher measured capacity tend to have a stronger EEG-based filtering score. The relationship connects behavioral retention with how the brain responds to irrelevant material. **EEG** records electrical brain activity, which supplies the physiological filtering measure. || **Filtering efficiency** concerns keeping distractors out of working-memory processing. Excluding the blue bars leaves more of the limited system available for the relevant red bars. Including those distractors consumes processing without contributing information required for the answer. || A lower memory score can therefore involve difficulty controlling entry into memory. Individual differences concern selection and resistance to distraction alongside retention itself. The correlation relates these measures without making every difference in working memory equivalent to a different number of storage spaces.
