2|**Decisions** are choices between alternatives, while **reasoning** involves drawing conclusions. A person can reason about what the evidence supports and then choose a course of action. The conclusion and the choice are related, but they describe different activities. || Decisions depend on **judgments** about the available alternatives and their circumstances. A judgment supplies an assessment that can guide action. Applying it can require reasoning about what follows from the information available. || **Inductive reasoning** reaches conclusions from observations. It uses evidence about particular experiences to support a judgment extending beyond them. The information observed provides a basis for the inference rather than guaranteeing an answer independently of experience. || A conclusion expresses what the observations support, while a decision identifies what to do. This distinction allows reasoning to contribute to a choice without treating every judgment as an action. The two processes can work together during the same activity.
3|**Inductive reasoning** supports scientific investigation through observations, data, and conclusions. Its conclusions are **probably, but not definitely, true**. Evidence can support a broader claim without establishing every possible case covered by that claim. || **Representativeness** concerns how well the observations reflect the group being described. A conclusion becomes less secure when the sample leaves out relevant variation. **Number of observations** matters because repeated evidence can provide a stronger basis than very limited experience. || **Quality** also matters. Many weak observations and one highly compelling observation do not necessarily contribute equally. Their value depends on what each establishes about the claim, not merely how many examples can be counted. || Observing many **white swans** supports an expectation, but a clearly observed **black swan** contradicts the claim that all swans are white. One observation can carry substantial weight because it rules out that universal claim. Inductive strength therefore depends on the relationship between the evidence and the conclusion.
4|**Scientific induction** connects particular observations with hypotheses and general conclusions. The observed cases supply evidence, while the hypothesis extends beyond those cases. Scientific reasoning therefore uses what has been encountered to propose a broader pattern. || **Everyday prediction** also draws on earlier experience. People anticipate what will happen by relating a familiar situation to what happened previously. The remembered pattern guides an expectation about the present circumstances. || Predictions and choices are especially likely to use this process when the situation is **familiar**. Familiarity makes relevant experience available without requiring the person to reconstruct an entire argument. The past influences current behavior through the expectation it supports. || Some induction occurs **automatically**. A rapid response can still depend on learning from observations, even without consciously listing them. The distinction concerns awareness of the reasoning, not whether earlier experience contributes to the judgment.
5|**Heuristics** are shortcuts used to reach conclusions rapidly. They are **rules of thumb** that often provide a useful answer without guaranteeing correctness. They reduce the amount of analysis required before responding to a familiar kind of situation. || A person can use an accessible cue instead of examining every relevant possibility. That cue may track the desired property reasonably well in ordinary circumstances. Errors arise when the cue is influenced by something different from the property being judged. || The **availability heuristic** relies on how easily examples come to mind. The **representativeness heuristic** relies on how closely something resembles a category. Both provide rapid judgments, but one uses memory accessibility while the other uses similarity. || Their usefulness does not make them foolproof. A memorable event can be uncommon, and a good descriptive match can belong to an uncommon category. The shortcut becomes misleading when the cue substitutes for information needed to judge the particular question accurately.
6|The **availability heuristic** makes easily remembered events seem more probable. A person tries to recall examples and uses the accessible instances to estimate how often something occurs. More examples coming readily to mind can therefore produce a higher probability judgment. || Accessibility is affected by **distinctiveness**, how strongly an event stands out, and **emotional salience**, its emotional impact. It also depends on repeated **encoding and retrieval**, meaning storing the event and bringing it back to mind. These influences change memory without necessarily changing the event’s actual frequency. || A dramatic event can therefore seem common because it is easy to remember. Repeated exposure can make its examples readily available even when the underlying occurrence remains rare. Ease of retrieval and frequency are different properties. || Availability can be useful when remembered examples reflect what happens often. The error occurs when that relationship is assumed despite other causes of memorability. The judgment then follows accessibility rather than the probability it is intended to estimate.
7|The letter question compares English words **beginning with r** with words having **r in the third position**. The required judgment concerns which set is larger. It does not ask which set is easier to search in memory. || Words beginning with a specified letter are relatively easy to retrieve. Searching for a letter inside a word is harder, so fewer examples may come readily to mind. That difference can make the first-letter category feel more frequent. || About **seventy percent** choose initial r, although third-position r occurs in about **three times as many words**. The popular answer reverses the actual frequency relationship. The search procedure favors the less frequent category. || The **availability heuristic** explains how retrieval ease enters the probability judgment. Producing many examples of one kind can feel like evidence that it dominates the language. Here, the way memory is searched supplies an advantage unrelated to the true size of the set.
8|Judgments about **causes of death** compare actual likelihoods rather than vividness or fear. The paired causes include homicide and appendicitis, drowning and auto–train collisions, and asthma compared with botulism or tornadoes. The task asks which is more likely for a randomly selected person. || In these comparisons, **asthma is more common than botulism or tornado deaths**. Respondents can nevertheless favor the dramatic cause. The accessible memory of an unusual event can outweigh quieter experience with the more frequent one. || A death from **botulism** can receive front-page coverage, while deaths from **asthma** attract little notice. News exposure increases opportunities to encode and retrieve the unusual cause. It does not make that cause occur as often as the more common alternative. || **Availability** therefore changes the evidence that comes to mind during the choice. The judgment confuses an event’s memorability with its probability. Relative risk requires considering how often the causes occur, not only how easily a striking example can be recalled.
9|The **representativeness heuristic** judges category membership through resemblance. A person considers how well an individual’s properties fit expectations about a category. The best descriptive match can then be treated as the most probable answer. || **Robert wears glasses, speaks quietly, and reads a lot**. Many respondents choose librarian over farmer because the description fits a familiar librarian stereotype. The resemblance is psychologically persuasive even without settling the probability. || The background comparison includes about **2.2 million farms and 120,000 libraries**. Such prevalence information supplies a **base rate**, a background frequency relevant to judging the alternatives. The descriptive details do not erase that information. || **Base-rate neglect** occurs when the fit of the description displaces the background frequency. Robert can resemble a librarian without resemblance alone establishing the answer. A probability judgment requires considering the category information as well as the individual description.
10|A group contains **seventy lawyers and thirty engineers**. With no other information, a randomly selected member has a **thirty-in-one-hundred** chance of being an engineer. Respondents generally answer this base-rate question correctly. || **Jack** is then described as careful, conservative, ambitious, married, and interested in carpentry, sailing, and mathematical puzzles. These details make him resemble an engineer stereotype. Respondents tend to identify him as an engineer. || The description does not change the composition of the group. It changes which category feels like a better match. People who correctly used the base rate without a description can therefore disregard it when vivid personal information becomes available. || The contrast concerns **how evidence is weighted**. Numerical category information is used when it stands alone, while descriptive resemblance can dominate when both are present. Knowing how to answer the simple ratio question does not guarantee integrating that ratio into the richer judgment.
11|The **conjunction rule** states that two events occurring together cannot be more probable than either event alone. Every case satisfying both conditions also satisfies each individual condition. Adding a requirement narrows the set of possible cases. || **Linda** is described as outspoken, bright, concerned with social justice, and involved in antinuclear demonstrations. The choices are **bank teller** or **bank teller and active in the feminist movement**. The second option sounds like a closer descriptive match. || About **eighty-two percent** select the conjunction. Representativeness makes the detailed description feel more probable, despite the probability relationship. Every feminist bank teller is already included among bank tellers. || The error is not that feminist activity conflicts with Linda’s description. It is that a subset is ranked above the larger set containing it. The added detail can improve a story’s fit while making the combined condition no more probable than its simpler constituent.
12|The hospital problem compares **forty-five daily births** in a large hospital with **fifteen** in a small one. Roughly half of births in the population are boys, but daily percentages vary. Each hospital records days with **more than sixty percent boys**. || The question concerns how often an **extreme daily proportion** occurs. It does not ask which hospital has more boys in total. Both hospitals share the same underlying population proportion but calculate their daily percentages from different numbers of births. || Most respondents choose **about the same**. That answer treats the common population percentage as if it guarantees equal variability. It ignores how strongly a few outcomes can change a proportion based on a small sample. || **Sample size** affects stability. An unusual run has more influence when it accounts for a large share of the day’s births. Comparing the frequency of extreme days therefore requires considering the number of observations behind each percentage, not only the overall proportion of boys.
13|The **smaller hospital** records more days above sixty percent boys. A daily proportion based on **fifteen births** varies more than one based on **forty-five**. A short run of similar outcomes has greater influence when fewer observations determine the percentage. || Both hospitals sample from a population with approximately **half boys**. Sharing that underlying proportion does not make their daily results equally close to half. A smaller group more readily departs from the population pattern. || The larger hospital’s proportion is more stable because more outcomes contribute to each day. The comparison concerns variability of percentages, not whether the larger hospital can produce a greater absolute count of boys. || Choosing **about the same** neglects sample size, while choosing the larger hospital because it has more births confuses counts with proportions. The threshold asks about an unusually high fraction. The correct response depends on the greater variability of the smaller sample.
14|The **law of large numbers** relates larger random samples to more representative aggregate results. As more individuals are drawn, the sample is more likely to reflect the population pattern. A larger set is less dominated by a short unusual run of outcomes. || The relationship concerns the number of observations supporting an estimate. A small group can differ substantially from the population even when every observation is genuinely sampled from it. Random selection does not force each small group to look like a miniature population. || People often assume that **small samples should already be representative**. That expectation understates their variability and can produce mistaken judgments about extreme outcomes. The error concerns expecting the stability associated with a large collection from very limited evidence. || Small and large samples can therefore share a source without supplying equally stable estimates. Increasing size generally improves representativeness. It does not make every individual observation more typical or require a small sample to display the exact population proportions.
15|An **illusory correlation** is a relationship perceived as existing when it is absent or weaker than assumed. Expectations can influence which observations are noticed and remembered. The resulting impression can therefore overstate the association actually supported by the evidence. || A **stereotype** is an oversimplified generalization about a group, often involving negative characteristics. It can direct attention toward behaviors matching that expectation. Other behaviors contribute less to the remembered impression because they attract less attention. || Those selected instances then seem to confirm a link between the group and the stereotyped behavior. The initial expectation influences the apparent evidence used to reinforce it. The association can feel stronger without becoming more accurate. || This relates to **availability** because selectively attended behaviors are easier to recall. Their accessibility can be mistaken for evidence that they are especially common within the group. The process connects expectations, attention, memory, and judgment rather than establishing the stereotype’s validity.
16|**Lord and colleagues (1979)** gave the same article to people supporting and opposing capital punishment. Their starting attitudes differed, but the material they read was shared. Different responses therefore reflected evaluation of common information rather than entirely different evidence. || Supporters interpreted the article as favorable to capital punishment. Opponents interpreted it as favorable to their opposing position. Each group emphasized information consistent with its existing belief. || **Myside bias** is generating and evaluating evidence, or testing hypotheses, in a way favoring one’s own attitudes. Agreement with the prior position affects which information receives weight. Disagreeing material can be disregarded or discounted. || The shared article therefore did not produce one common interpretation. Participants’ beliefs influenced what they accepted from it. The bias concerns how evidence is assessed, not merely whether people begin with different opinions.
17|**Confirmation bias** favors information consistent with a hypothesis while overlooking possible contradictions. **Myside bias** is one form. A person can repeatedly encounter agreeable evidence because the search itself is organized to find it. || In the **2, 4, 6 task**, participants generate their own triples and receive feedback about whether each fits a hidden rule. They also give reasons for their tests and propose the rule once confident. The feedback does not directly reveal which narrower hypotheses remain wrong. || A common guess is **increasing by intervals of two**. Testing only triples with that pattern can produce repeated positive feedback. Those tests also fit the broader actual rule, **three numbers in increasing order of magnitude**. || Confirmation alone therefore fails to distinguish the guessed restriction from the genuine rule. Testing a case outside the two-step pattern could reveal that equal intervals are unnecessary. A successful test supports every rule it fits, not only the particular rule the participant intended to confirm.
18|**Decision making** chooses among courses of action whose outcomes differ in desirability and probability. **Utility** concerns what makes an outcome beneficial to the person. An attractive possible result is not necessarily the result most likely to occur. || **Expected utility theory** assumes rational choice using all relevant information. The preferred alternative has the **maximum expected utility**. Its evaluation combines how valuable possible outcomes are with their chances. || In a monetary version, the comparison concerns **maximum expected payoff**. A larger potential prize and a higher probability of winning are separate properties. Expected benefit depends on their combination rather than whichever one attracts attention first. || The theory supplies a consistent standard for selecting among uncertain alternatives. Applying that standard requires the relevant values and probabilities. The descriptive question is whether actual decisions follow that comparison when people evaluate the choices.
19|The **utility approach** provides procedures for determining a best choice under specified values and probabilities. This creates a consistent comparison among alternatives. It is an advantage of the approach as a decision standard. || People value **more than money**, so maximum expected monetary payoff does not encompass every desirable aspect of an outcome. The person’s evaluation of an activity can include considerations that are not captured by a cash amount. || Actual decisions also do not always follow the calculated best prospect. People continue **gambling in casinos** despite knowing the odds are unfavorable. Knowledge of those odds does not automatically prevent choosing the activity. || These behaviors limit an account treating people as automatic utility maximizers. A calculation can specify the best alternative under its assumptions while failing to predict every choice. Distinguishing the decision rule from observed behavior preserves the role of both.
20|Participants earn **one dollar for drawing a red jellybean**. They choose between a bowl with **one red bean among ten** and a bowl with **seven among one hundred**. The chance on a random draw depends on the complete proportion, not only the red-bean count. || The probabilities are \[\frac{1}{10}=0.10,\qquad\frac{7}{100}=0.07\] The first bowl gives a higher chance despite containing fewer winning beans. Its smaller denominator changes what the numerator means. || Most participants favor the **seven-out-of-one-hundred bowl**. Seeing more favorable items attracts their choice even though those items make up a smaller fraction of the possibilities. The larger winning count and better winning probability point toward different bowls. || This preference reflects considerations other than the actual success probability. Focusing on the numerator neglects how many nonwinning beans accompany it. The experiment separates the appeal of more visible winning opportunities from the likelihood that one randomly selected bean will win.
21|After **September 11**, some Americans reduced flying and increased driving. This substituted one travel risk for another rather than eliminating the risks of travel. Avoiding a frightening event can therefore have consequences in the alternative chosen. || The probability of dying in a **car accident is substantially greater than while flying**. The emotional impact of the attacks did not change that comparison into its opposite. Perceived danger and relative probability can provide different bases for action. || The reported road deaths associated with avoiding flying exceeded the passengers killed on the four hijacked flights. The shift intended to avoid harm was accompanied by greater harm through another mode of transport. || Evaluating the decision requires considering **both alternatives**. Concentrating only on the memorable air disaster can make driving seem safer without comparing its actual risk. A compelling remembered event can therefore guide choice away from the lower-risk option.
22|A **food convoy** is traveling to one refugee camp during a famine. Rerouting it to a larger camp would save **one thousand people** but cause **one hundred** in the original camp to die. Airplanes are unavailable, so the choice cannot be avoided by supplying both in that way. || Comparing lives saved and lost gives \[U(\mathrm{not\ reroute})=100-1000=-900\] and \[U(\mathrm{reroute})=1000-100=900\] Rerouting has the larger net benefit under this numerical evaluation. || **Ritov and Baron (1999)** found that most participants refused to reroute. Some refused even when changing the route would cost only **one life** at the first camp. Their choices did not follow only the larger net number saved. || The decision also concerns causing a loss through an active change to the existing route. Reluctance to produce that harm can outweigh a larger benefit elsewhere. The dilemma separates a net-outcome calculation from willingness to perform the action producing it.
23|The **Sacred Value Protection Model**, proposed by **Tetlock, Kristel, Elson, Green, and Lerner (2000)**, concerns values people treat as protected from ordinary exchange. Such a value is not evaluated simply by asking how much money or material benefit would compensate for surrendering it. || **Sacred and secular values** are kept in separate domains. Secular concerns can enter practical exchanges, while sacred commitments receive a different status. The proposed comparison itself can therefore threaten how a person believes the value should be treated. || A **taboo tradeoff** exchanges a sacred concern against a secular one and can provoke **moral outrage**. A **tragic tradeoff** places two sacred concerns in conflict, such as two lives when only one can be saved. || The tragic choice can remain painful while producing less outrage about the kind of tradeoff. Both alternatives protect a sacred concern and sacrifice another. That differs from treating a sacred value as something purchasable through material advantage.
24|In the **taboo scenario**, Robert can save **Johnny**, a young child requiring a liver transplant. Obtaining the transplant is expensive because local organs are scarce. The hospital must spend **one million dollars** that could otherwise support equipment and recruitment of talented doctors. || The choice compares Johnny’s life with other uses of money. The alternative hospital spending may have practical value, but the scenario treats a specific life as competing with a monetary allocation. That is the sacred–secular conflict. || In the **tragic scenario**, **two children require a transplant but only one liver exists**. Saving either leaves the other without the organ. The scarcity forces a choice between two lives rather than between a life and funds. || The distinction is the kind of value on each side, not simply whether a serious loss occurs. Both choices can have painful consequences. Their different structure changes whether the decision is experienced as a prohibited exchange or an unavoidable conflict between sacred aims.
25|**Moral outrage** depends on both **tradeoff type** and **whether Johnny is saved**. In the taboo condition, refusing to save him produces much greater outrage than saving him. Money is chosen over the child’s life in the most negatively evaluated outcome. || In the **tragic condition**, outrage is similar whether Johnny receives the liver or not. Only one of two children can be saved. Either outcome leaves a sacred concern unmet rather than selecting money over a life. || The difference between saving and not saving Johnny is therefore much larger under taboo than tragic conditions. This is an **interaction**, where the effect of the outcome depends on the kind of value conflict. || The highest outrage belongs to the **taboo, not-saved** outcome. It would be inaccurate to describe all failures to save Johnny as equally condemned. The reasons and competing values accompanying that outcome are part of what participants evaluate.
26|**Ginges, Atran, Medin, and Shikaki (2007)** studied sacred-value tradeoffs among people involved in an ongoing political conflict. Participants included **Israeli settlers, Palestinian refugees, and politically affiliated Palestinian students**. Their judgments concerned issues with direct significance to their groups. || The research offered **three kinds of agreement**. Varying the terms tested whether changing a proposed exchange changed opposition and emotional reactions. A benefit added by a negotiator was not assumed to feel beneficial to every recipient. || Participants could regard a contested issue as a **sacred commitment** rather than an ordinary negotiable preference. This status affects whether material compensation seems appropriate. The psychological question is therefore partly about the meaning of the offered exchange. || The setting connects sacred-value protection with actual group concerns. Evaluating an offer involves what it asks people to compromise and how it treats the underlying commitment. More material advantage does not necessarily remove the objection to making that kind of trade.
27|The **taboo proposal** asks Palestinians to recognize Jewish sacred and historical rights to Israel. It includes an Israeli state and a Palestinian state in **ninety-nine percent of the West Bank and Gaza**. Recognition of contested claims is part of the agreement itself. || **Taboo+** adds material compensation, with Israel paying Palestine **one billion dollars annually for ten years**. The addition changes the economic terms without itself acknowledging the other side’s sacred claim. || The **symbolic proposal** instead adds Israeli recognition of Palestinians’ historic and legitimate right to a state, together with an **apology for past wrongs**. These terms address acknowledgment and respect rather than increasing payment. || The offers therefore differ in what counts as an improvement. Money changes practical benefits, while symbolic reciprocity changes how a group’s commitment is recognized. People can evaluate those additions differently even though both are intended to facilitate agreement.
28|**Moral absolutists** treat an issue as a **sacred value** that should not be compromised regardless of the benefit. This differs from disliking compromise strongly but accepting it if consequences are sufficiently favorable. The criterion concerns whether a tradeoff is permitted at all. || Among settlers, the sacred issue involved giving up part of the **Land of Israel**. Among Palestinian students, it involved the **right of return and Jerusalem**. The particular commitment differed, while rejection of benefit-based exchange supplied the shared classification. || **Non-absolutists** also opposed compromise strongly. They were not simply unconcerned about the dispute. They differed in allowing the issue to be considered through consequences rather than placing it beyond such comparison. || The distinction is therefore between a protected commitment and a negotiable preference, not merely high and low emotional intensity. Recognizing it is necessary for interpreting why the same added benefit can produce opposite responses across groups.
29|For **moral absolutists**, adding money in **Taboo+** increased **anger, disgust, and support for violence** relative to the baseline offer. The proposed payment made compromise more objectionable. Treating a sacred concern as purchasable can intensify opposition rather than reduce it. || **Non-absolutists** responded in the opposite direction. The material benefit reduced those negative reactions and support for violence. An identical addition therefore had different effects depending on the value’s status for the participant. || **Symbolic recognition** was associated with lower anger or disgust and lower support for violence than the taboo offer. Recognition addressed the standing of the group’s claim rather than simply its material price. || The result distinguishes economic incentives from **symbolic reciprocity**. A larger financial offer is not automatically a more acceptable negotiation. The offer’s treatment of commitments influences how people evaluate the proposed compromise and the responses they report supporting.
30|Negotiations can use **material incentives**, benefits offered for agreement, or **disincentives**, costs attached to refusal. Both attempt to change the practical consequences of accepting a deal. Their psychological effect depends on how the targeted population interprets the proposed exchange. || **Dehghani, Atran, and colleagues (2010)** and **Dehghani, Iliev, and colleagues (2009)** examined these issues for **Iran’s nuclear program**. They asked whether people treating it as sacred would be willing to compromise in response to material benefits or penalties. || **Economic funds** increase the proposed benefit of acceptance, while **sanctions** increase the cost of refusal. These are different changes to the decision’s consequences. Neither directly changes whether the program is considered a sacred value. || A protected commitment can therefore produce responses unlike an ordinary negotiable preference. The research tests how the material terms affect approval and anger when consequence-based exchange itself may be rejected. The question concerns reactions to the offer, not an assumption that incentives must work as intended.
31|An online study conducted in **Farsi** involved **1,997 Iranian participants**. Outcomes included **personal approval**, **anger**, and whether a deal seemed **misleading**. These judgments measure distinct responses to the proposal rather than interchangeable labels for acceptance. || A **sacred-value measure**, based on **Baron and Spranca (1997)**, asked about giving up nuclear energy or weapons. Some answers endorsed relinquishment, while another allowed it if benefits were large enough. Those responses permit consequences to influence the decision. || The sacred-value response rejected giving up the program **no matter how great the consequences**. That answer identified participants treating the issue as outside ordinary benefit-based exchange. A strong preference that could change for enough benefit did not meet the same criterion. || Only a subgroup met it, approximately **fourteen percent for energy and thirteen percent for weapons**. The classification concerns how the value is held. Approval, anger, and misleadingness then concern reactions to the particular deals offered.
32|The experiment crossed **two program types**, nuclear energy or weapons, with **three deal types**, Taboo, Taboo+, or Taboo−. This produces **six conditions**. Program content and material terms can therefore be distinguished rather than combined into one undifferentiated comparison. || In **Taboo**, Iran gives up the relevant program and facilities to the United Nations, while Israel gives up its corresponding program and facilities. The baseline is reciprocal relinquishment. It contains no added payment or threat. || **Taboo+** adds **forty billion dollars in economic funds** to Iran. **Taboo−** adds further sanctions if Iran refuses. One changes the benefit of agreement and the other the cost of rejecting it. || Participants rated personal approval, expected public anger **if the Iranian government accepted**, and misleadingness. The anger question concerns anticipated response to government acceptance, not simply the participant’s own anger at reading the offer. Keeping the wording distinct preserves what each measure tests.
33|For participants treating **nuclear energy as sacred**, approval was lower with **Taboo+ payment** than with the baseline offer. The **sanctions condition** also produced lower approval. Neither addition made compromise more attractive within this group. || Their **anticipated anger if the government accepted** was highest with the financial incentive. It was also elevated with sanctions compared with the baseline. Payment therefore accompanied both lower approval and a stronger expected negative public response. || Participants without the sacred commitment had much higher approval and lower anger across the energy conditions. Their ratings changed relatively little among the offers. The large separation concerned the status of nuclear energy as a value. || Material benefits and costs therefore interacted with that commitment. The results do not support treating money as an automatic way to purchase acceptance of a sacred compromise. The unmodified agreement received the highest approval and lowest anger within the sacred-value group, despite offering neither the added funds nor the sanctions threat.
