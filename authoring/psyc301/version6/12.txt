2|**Representation and restructuring** concern how a problem’s elements are organized in the mind. A useful relationship can be difficult to recognize under the initial organization. Changing that representation can make a solution available, sometimes through sudden **insight**. || **Search** concerns finding a path between the initial state and the goal. The solver considers possible intermediate states and the operations connecting them. Stating the endpoints does not establish how to get from one to the other. || **Problem wording** influences which relationships become accessible. A checkerboard can be treated as a collection of squares or as two colors that every domino must pair. The same physical task can therefore encourage different representations and different solving approaches.
3|The **Russian marriage problem** begins with **thirty-two bachelors and thirty-two unmarried women**. Each required heterosexual marriage pairs one member of each group. Equal group sizes initially permit thirty-two such pairs. || After two bachelors die, the **sixty-two survivors** consist of **thirty men and thirty-two women**. Their total number would allow thirty-one pairs if group membership were irrelevant. The stated marriage requirement, however, makes the smaller group a limit on the number of valid pairs. || At most **thirty marriages** can be arranged, leaving two women unmatched. Rearranging every partnership cannot create another man. The impossibility follows from unequal category sizes rather than a failure to search through enough possible matches.
4|A domino covers **two adjacent squares of different colors**. Removing opposite checkerboard corners removes two squares of the same color. The remaining board has sufficient total area for thirty-one dominoes but unequal numbers of the two square types. || Every complete covering would have to use the colors in equal numbers. The extra squares of one color cannot be paired with a missing member of the other. Rearranging the dominoes does not change that count. || The constraint concerns **the kinds of member required for each pair**. Total area specifies how many pairs are needed, but color balance determines whether those pairs can exist. Recognizing the imbalance rules out all placements through the same relationship.
5|**Analogical problem solving** uses a solution from one situation to guide another. The **source problem** supplies the earlier relationship. The **target problem** is the one currently being solved, and **analogical transfer** is the use of the source to solve it. || The Russian marriage problem can serve as a source for the checkerboard. The two groups of people correspond to the two square colors, and marriages correspond to domino placements. Each valid pair requires one member from each category. || Removing two bachelors corresponds to removing two same-colored corners. Both changes create the same kind of imbalance. Learning the marriage solution improves checkerboard solving because the relationship transfers even though people, marriages, squares, and dominoes have very different appearances.
6|**Duncker’s radiation problem** requires destroying an inoperable stomach tumor without destroying healthy tissue. A high-intensity ray can destroy the tumor but also damages tissue along its path. A low-intensity ray spares the tissue but does not destroy the tumor. || Several **low-intensity rays can approach from different directions and converge simultaneously**. Each path receives a safe intensity, while their combined effect at the tumor is strong enough. Dividing the paths separates the effect on surrounding tissue from the total effect at the target. || Only about **ten percent** solve the problem without the useful analogy. Merely choosing one of the original intensities leaves a requirement unmet. The solution changes the delivery arrangement so that weak contributions combine where the strong effect is required.
7|In the **fortress problem**, an army is strong enough to capture a dictator’s central fortress. Roads through surrounding farms and villages provide access. Mines permit small groups to pass but detonate under a large force, making one full-scale approach dangerous. || A small group reaches the target safely but cannot capture it alone. A large group could capture it but cannot travel safely along one road. The difficulty therefore concerns satisfying different constraints on the route and at the destination. || The general can divide the army into **small groups using different roads**, coordinated to arrive together. Each remains safe on its route, while the combined force is sufficient at the fortress. The arrangement concentrates strength at the target without imposing the same strength on any individual approach.
8|The **fortress solution** combines small forces arriving by separate roads. Each route avoids the danger caused by a large group, while the groups together provide sufficient force at the center. Their timing allows the contributions to combine where needed. || The **radiation solution** uses low-intensity rays along separate paths. Healthy tissue along each path receives a low intensity. Their convergence at the tumor produces the larger combined effect required to destroy it. || The **fortress maps to the tumor**, the **small groups to weak rays**, and the **roads to tissue paths**. Both solutions distribute a safe approach and combine its effects at a central target. Simply weakening each contribution without bringing them together would fail the requirement at that target.
9|**Gick and Holyoak** distinguish **noticing, mapping, and applying** an analogy. Noticing means recognizing that another problem is relevant. Mapping identifies which elements and relationships correspond, and application uses them to construct the target solution. || After reading the fortress story, about **thirty percent** solved the radiation problem. The source was available, but many did not spontaneously connect military movement with medical treatment. Remembering a useful story therefore did not guarantee transfer. || When told to consider the earlier story, success rose to about **seventy-five percent**. The reminder helped participants identify and use the relevant source. The contrast separates having encountered the analogy from noticing its relevance when solving the target.
10|**Surface features** are the specific objects and details describing a situation. Fortresses and tumors differ strongly at this level. Their different contexts can prevent a solver from noticing a shared relationship despite an analogous solution. || Both tasks require a strong combined effect at a center while limiting what travels along each approach. That relationship is useful independently of whether the approaching elements are soldiers or rays. Focusing only on their names and appearance hides the correspondence. || Making the surface features more similar can facilitate **noticing and mapping**. A recognizable resemblance makes a source easier to retrieve, after which the solver can examine how its roles fit the target. Identifying a possible analogy and establishing its structural usefulness remain separate demands.
11|In the **lightbulb problem**, Ruth must repair a broken filament inside sealed glass. The bulb cannot be opened. A high-intensity laser could fuse the filament but would break the fragile glass, while a weak beam would protect the glass without repairing the break. || Several **low-intensity beams** can approach from different directions at the same time. They combine at the filament to produce the required local intensity. Each part of the surrounding glass receives only a low-intensity approach. || The solution distinguishes the intensity required **at the target** from the intensity safe **along a path**. Changing the arrangement satisfies both requirements. Using one powerful beam or merely weakening a single beam would still violate one of them.
12|The **radiation problem** provides a source for the lightbulb task. The tumor corresponds to the broken filament, while healthy tissue corresponds to fragile glass. Both require a powerful local effect without damaging the material surrounding the target. || The tasks also have relatively similar **surface features**. Each involves directing beams inward. This resemblance can make the analogy easier to recognize than the correspondence between radiation and an army approaching a fortress. || About **eighty-one percent** of participants who knew the radiation problem solved the lightbulb problem, compared with about **ten percent** of controls. The source supplied a transferable convergence procedure. Surface similarity helped make that structurally relevant solution accessible in the new setting.
13|**Surface features** identify the objects in a problem, while **structural features** determine the relationships governing its solution. Lasers, glass, and a filament describe the lightbulb’s setting. The reason a beam cannot simply be intensified is part of the structure. || In the fragile-glass version, a strong beam damages the surrounding material before reaching the target. This corresponds to radiation damaging healthy tissue. Both require a sufficient target effect with restricted intensity along the approach. || Two lightbulb versions can preserve the same objects while changing that reason. Keeping the surface similar does not guarantee an equally useful analogy. The underlying constraints determine whether the source explains why the target needs its particular solution.
14|In the **fragile-glass version**, a powerful laser would repair the filament but also break the bulb. A weak beam would preserve the glass but fail to fuse the filament. Both the target’s requirement and the surrounding-damage constraint must be satisfied. || Ruth directs **several weak beams from around the bulb**, converging simultaneously at the filament. The combined intensity repairs it while each glass location receives only a weak beam. The solution distributes intensity along the approaches and concentrates it at the destination. || About **sixty-nine percent** who read this source solved the radiation problem. The source shares the target’s reason for using convergence, not just its final procedure. Vulnerable surrounding tissue plays the same structural role as fragile surrounding glass.
15|In the **insufficient-intensity version**, the available laser produces only weak beams. Their intensity is inadequate to fuse the filament. The stated obstacle is limited power, rather than a powerful beam damaging surrounding glass. || Several lasers again converge on the filament, providing a sufficient combined effect. The physical procedure resembles the fragile-glass solution, but its explanation omits the surrounding-damage constraint central to the radiation problem. The same objects and action therefore preserve only part of the useful structure. || About **thirty-three percent** transferred this solution to radiation, compared with **sixty-nine percent** after the fragile-glass source. Similar surface details and a similar procedure did not produce equal transfer. Matching the constraints governing that procedure made the source more useful.
16|**Analogical encoding** involves comparing problems and identifying their shared relationships. It can make **structural features** more apparent by directing attention beyond the objects unique to each story. The common principle becomes available across different surface descriptions. || Comparing convergence problems can reveal a strong target requirement together with restrictions on individual approaches. Weak contributions combine at the center because sending one strong contribution along a route is unsuitable. The transferable information concerns those roles and constraints. || Asking people to compare source stories can therefore improve later analogical solving. The learner extracts a relationship rather than retaining only one familiar setting. A new target can be mapped onto that relationship even when its objects differ from the original ones.
17|The **analogical paradox** is that people often struggle to transfer analogies in laboratory tasks while using them routinely in real-world work. Failure to notice a supplied source does not mean analogical reasoning is absent from ordinary activity. Context changes how relevant knowledge is encountered and used. || Scientists use multiple analogies during laboratory meetings, relating current questions to other situations and known solutions. Such reasoning occurs within ongoing discussion rather than only when a researcher assigns an explicit source and target. || **In vivo research** observes problem solving in its natural setting. It preserves the ordinary information and interactions surrounding the activity. Observation is time-consuming, and variables cannot be isolated and controlled as readily as in the laboratory. The method gains natural context while limiting experimental control.
18|An **expert** becomes highly knowledgeable or skilled in a field through extensive learning, practice, and application. Expertise is therefore more than confidence or familiarity with a label. It reflects accumulated experience made usable within a domain. || A chess expert may spend **tens of thousands of hours** studying and playing. Such experience supplies knowledge about recurring positions and how to act within them. The learning is organized around the field’s actual demands. || Experts solve problems **in their field faster and more successfully** than beginners. The comparison concerns the domain in which that learning developed. Explaining the advantage requires considering how stored knowledge and practiced organization support the current problem, rather than treating expertise as a universal problem-solving ability.
19|**Chase and Simon (1973)** compared a chess master and a beginner who had little chess experience. They briefly viewed positions for **five seconds** and then reproduced the piece locations. The task measured what could be recovered after a limited opportunity to inspect the board. || The master had a large advantage for **actual game positions**. Long-term knowledge made familiar configurations available as **chunks**, organized units linking several pieces. Meaningful relationships could therefore support recall beyond isolated locations. || In **random arrangements**, those game-based chunks were unavailable and the advantage was greatly reduced. The pieces themselves remained familiar, but their organization no longer matched ordinary play. Expert recall depended strongly on how the material connected with domain knowledge.
20|**Chi and colleagues (1982)** studied how knowledge is organized for use in problem solving. Physics professors and students with one semester of physics sorted **twenty-four problems** by similarity. Sorting revealed which relationships made two problems seem alike. || **Novices grouped by surface features**, including the objects and their appearance. Problems involving inclined planes could therefore belong together because their physical setups looked similar. The grouping did not necessarily identify the principle required to solve them. || **Experts grouped by structural features**, such as an underlying physics principle. Different-looking objects could belong together when the same relationship governed their solutions. This organization supports access to knowledge relevant to solving rather than only retrieval of a matching scene.
21|A novice paired **problems 23 and 24** because both involved **inclined planes**. The visible slope and blocks supplied a shared surface feature. The objects looked similar even though appearance alone did not establish a common solution principle. || An expert instead paired **problems 21 and 24** through **conservation of energy**. One involved a block and spring on a horizontal surface, while the other involved an incline. Their different-looking arrangements could be treated as related through the principle needed for analysis. || **Problem 24** therefore received a different partner under each grouping. The contrast identifies the basis of similarity rather than requiring calculation of the incidental numerical values in the examples. Expertise changes which relationship organizes the problems and makes relevant knowledge accessible.
22|**Experts often spend longer analyzing a problem initially**. They identify relevant relationships before proceeding. This slower start can support more efficient and successful solving overall, rather than indicating poorer understanding. || Their advantage is **domain-specific**. Expertise does not make them better than novices on unrelated problems outside the field. The organized knowledge useful in one area does not automatically supply the principles needed elsewhere. || Familiar procedures can also reduce openness to a new representation. Some problems require rejecting the usual method in favor of a less common one. Under those conditions, strong reliance on the familiar approach can hinder the **flexible thinking** required by the task.
23|**Creativity** includes innovative ideas and new connections among existing ideas. **Divergent thinking** is open-ended generation of many possibilities. It broadens the set of potential solutions rather than immediately committing to one response. || Creative solving also involves **trial and error**. **Problem generation** includes finding problems and facts. **Problem formulation** defines what requires solving and develops possible ideas. These activities establish the task as well as candidate approaches. || **Problem solving** then includes evaluating possibilities and planning. **Implementation** puts a solution into action. Generating an idea does not automatically evaluate it or carry it out, so creativity involves a longer process than the moment at which a novel possibility occurs.
24|**Ideas are necessary for creative problem solving**, because without a proposed approach the process stops. Generating one is not sufficient to complete the task. Evaluation, planning, and implementation are additional demands beyond producing a possibility. || Extensive knowledge can sometimes hinder creativity when it makes accepted procedures dominate consideration. A familiar interpretation may prevent a less common relationship from receiving attention. The difficulty is rigid use of knowledge rather than knowledge simply being useless. || **Flexible thinking** includes reconsidering an accepted procedure when it does not fit the current problem. The solver remains able to draw on experience while questioning its usual application. Some creative solutions require rejecting the standard route and developing another use for the available information.
