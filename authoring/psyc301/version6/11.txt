2|A **problem** exists when an obstacle separates a **present state** from a **goal**, and the solution is not immediately obvious. The present state describes how things are arranged now. The goal specifies what would count as success, while the obstacle prevents reaching it directly. || In the numbered-tile puzzle, the goal is a particular arrangement of the tiles and empty space. The starting board contains the same pieces in different positions. Recognizing the numbers or describing the finished board does not supply the moves needed to get there. || Knowing the goal is therefore different from knowing a solution. The difficulty concerns finding an allowable route between the two arrangements. A clearly defined endpoint can still leave a genuine problem because the operations connecting it to the current situation remain unclear.
3|**Gestalt psychologists** explained problem solving in terms of how a problem is represented. **Koffka (1935)** connected this perceptual approach with learning, attitudes, and beliefs as well. A person’s mental organization of the available elements can make some relationships accessible and others difficult to recognize. || **Restructuring** means changing that organization. In **Köhler’s circle problem**, the task is to find the length of line **x** when the radius is **r**. Treating the line as an isolated unknown can obscure its relationship with the other elements. || The line is one diagonal of a rectangle. The other diagonal runs from the circle’s boundary to its center, making it a radius. A rectangle’s diagonals have equal length, so \[x=r\] Recognizing the rectangle reorganizes the problem around a relationship that directly determines the answer.
4|**Insight** is the sudden realization of a solution. A person can initially represent a problem one way and then recognize a crucial relationship through a different organization. The answer becomes available after that change rather than through a consciously experienced series of small improvements. || Gestalt psychologists associated restructuring with the **“Aha” experience**. This describes how the solution feels to the solver. The important change is from lacking a workable representation to recognizing one that makes the answer apparent. || Not all problem solving has this form. Some solutions are reached methodically through successive operations, with progress becoming increasingly clear. Sudden insight is one process, rather than a requirement that every correct answer arrive all at once.
5|**Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987)** compared **insight problems**, such as the triangle and chain tasks, with **noninsight problems**, such as algebra. They measured how close participants felt to a solution while attempting it. The question concerned the experience of progress rather than only the final success rate. || For insight problems, they predicted poor anticipation of the solution. A crucial reorganization could make the answer available while the person had still felt far from it. For a methodical algebra problem, completing successive operations should make approaching success more apparent. || Participants gave **warmth judgments every fifteen seconds**. Hotter ratings meant feeling nearer the answer, while colder ratings meant feeling farther away. Relating those reports to actual solution time tested whether the two kinds of problem produced different patterns of perceived progress.
6|The **triangle problem** requires moving **three of ten dots** so the upward-pointing triangle points downward. Seven dots must remain where they are. The solution therefore requires changing the relation among a few positions rather than moving the entire figure. || Move the original top dot beneath the center of the bottom row. Move the two outer bottom dots to the outer ends of the original two-dot row. The resulting rows contain four, three, two, and one dots from top to bottom, producing a downward triangle with only three moves. || The **chain problem** starts with four three-link sections. Opening a link costs **two cents** and closing it **three cents**, with a **fifteen-cent budget**. Open all three links of one section and use them to connect the remaining sections into a closed loop. Each connector is opened and closed once, so \[3(2+3)=15\] Treating one section as connector material makes the constrained solution possible.
7|In **Metcalfe and Wiebe’s (1987)** results, algebra solving produced a **gradual increase in warmth**. Participants increasingly felt close to the answer as they worked through the problem. Their subjective progress changed before the final solution was reached. || Insight problems produced a different pattern. Ratings stayed relatively low and then rose sharply near the solution. A participant could be about to solve the task without having reported a similarly gradual approach. || **Warmth measures perceived closeness**, not a direct count of completed mental operations. The contrast supports a distinction between the experience of sudden insight and methodical progress. Both can end in a correct answer, while the solver’s ability to anticipate that answer differs.
8|**Fixation** occurs when attention remains on a characteristic that obstructs a solution. **Functional fixedness** is a more specific restriction of an object to its familiar use. A useful property can remain unnoticed because the solver continues treating the object in its usual role. || The **candle problem** provides a candle, matches with their box, and tacks. The candle must be mounted on a vertical corkboard without dripping wax onto the floor. The box can be attached to the board and used as a platform, but thinking of it only as a container makes that function less accessible. || Participants given **empty matchboxes** were about twice as likely to solve the task. Removing the familiar contents made the box easier to treat as a usable object in its own right. The materials already allowed a solution, but their presentation affected whether the support function was recognized.
9|The **two-string problem** asks a person to tie together strings hanging too far apart to reach simultaneously. Holding one prevents simply grasping the other. The obstacle is bringing the ends together, not knowing what the final knot should look like. || **Pliers** can be attached to one string as a weight. Setting it in motion makes a **pendulum**, a weighted object swinging back and forth. The person can hold the other string and catch the weighted end when it swings within reach. || **Functional fixedness** makes the pliers’ familiar gripping function dominate their interpretation. The solution instead uses their weight. Recognizing that property changes how the string can be brought toward the solver without changing the original requirement to tie the ends together.
10|In the two-string task, an experimenter could apparently accidentally set a string swinging. After the hint, most participants could solve the problem. Motion made the possibility of a pendulum more accessible, changing the string from an unreachable stationary object into something that could move within reach. || Gestalt theory describes this as **restructuring**. The solver uses a new relationship among the same elements rather than receiving a different task. The hint affects how the available materials are represented. || A **mental set** is a preconceived approach based on earlier experience. In a **water-jug problem**, previous success with one measuring method can make that method the default for new capacities. Experience can therefore support solving while also making a different, simpler approach less likely to be considered.
11|A **water-jug problem** specifies the capacities of A, B, and C and a desired amount. With capacities **21, 127, and 3**, the target is **100**. One method is \[B-A-2C=127-21-2(3)=100\] It removes one A-sized amount and two C-sized amounts from B. || Repeated success establishes a **mental set** for that procedure. With **15, 39, and 3**, it again gives the target **18**, but the shorter method is \[A+C=15+3=18\] Both succeed, while only one avoids the unnecessary operations. || With **28, 59, and 3**, the target **25** is obtained directly by \[A-C=28-3=25\] The familiar procedure also works, but its availability can inhibit recognition of the simpler solution. The obstacle concerns which method prior experience makes the solver apply first.
12|**Newell and Simon (1956)** developed the **Logic Theorist** and described solving as **search**. The process includes the activity between receiving the problem and reaching its solution. It concerns finding a path around obstacles rather than only comparing the initial arrangement with the final one. || A **problem space** includes the **initial state**, possible **intermediate states**, and the **goal state**. The initial state gives the starting conditions. Intermediate states are arrangements reached during an attempt, and the goal state satisfies the solution requirements. || Knowing the endpoints does not specify the route. Search involves finding a sequence connecting them through available possibilities. This account therefore describes how the solver proceeds, including choices among intermediate situations that do not themselves complete the problem.
13|**Operators** specify the moves permitted within a problem space. Applying one changes the current state into another. They determine which transitions can belong to a valid solution, even when a forbidden move would be physically possible. || In the **Tower of Hanoi**, a stack must be moved to a goal peg. Only **one disk moves at a time**, and a disk cannot move while another rests on top of it. A **larger disk cannot be placed on a smaller one**, including during temporary arrangements. || The solver must therefore use intermediate pegs without violating the size restriction. Removing an upper disk may enable a later move that was initially unavailable. A solution is a connected sequence of legal states, not merely the final appearance of a correctly ordered stack.
14|Moving a Tower of Hanoi stack requires clearing smaller disks before a larger disk can move. The smaller disks must occupy temporary legal positions on the other pegs. Their rearrangement is part of solving the larger transfer, even when they are temporarily away from the goal. || Moving the largest disk is not the end if the smaller ones remain elsewhere. They must then be transferred onto it while maintaining the size rule. The whole solution therefore contains repeated transfers of smaller stacks. || For **sixty-four disks**, the required sequence would take on the order of **a trillion years** at one correct move per second. Fast individual moves do not eliminate the huge number of intermediate operations. Increasing problem size can greatly expand the search and action demands imposed by unchanged rules.
15|The tile puzzle contains **eight numbered tiles and an empty location**. The initial board places them in one arrangement, and the goal board specifies another. The same set of objects can therefore constitute different problem states because their positions differ. || The goal places the empty location first and the numbered tiles in the required order. Recognizing that order does not provide the sequence of moves needed from the starting board. Each intervening arrangement must still be reachable through permitted operations. || **State representation** must preserve the layout, not just the presence of the numbers. A tile already appearing somewhere is not equivalent to that tile occupying its goal position. The task is to transform relationships among positions through a connected series of intermediate states.
16|A **search tree** represents alternative states and the moves connecting them. In the starting tile arrangement, the empty location is in the lower middle. A neighboring tile can move into it, producing a new board and relocating the empty space. || Different available moves create different **branches**. Moving a tile from the left, above, or right produces distinct successors. Each contains the same pieces but a different layout, so each is a different intermediate state rather than a new set of objects. || Further moves produce more branches. The **goal** is a particular arrangement among these possibilities. Search requires deciding which connected states to pursue until a route reaches it. A state and an operator are distinct because one current arrangement can permit several different next moves.
17|**Means–end analysis** directs search toward reducing differences between the current state and the goal. The solver identifies a change that would remove an obstacle to progress. The desired action may require another condition to be achieved first. || A **subgoal** is that useful intermediate condition. It is not the complete solution, but accomplishing it makes part of the larger task possible. Search can therefore be organized around a series of enabling changes rather than only direct moves to the final arrangement. || In the **Tower of Hanoi**, freeing a large disk is a subgoal because smaller disks initially block it. Moving them elsewhere may not finish the puzzle, but it permits the required large-disk move. Their temporary relocation contributes through its relationship to the overall goal.
18|To move the largest disk, the solver must first **free it from the disks above**. In the three-disk sequence, the smallest moves to the third peg and the next to the middle peg. The largest is now uncovered, satisfying one subgoal. || The **third peg must also be free**. The smallest disk there would prevent placing the largest on it. Moving the smallest onto the other small disk on the middle peg clears the destination while preserving the size rule. || The largest can now move legally to the third peg. The smaller disks still need to be transferred onto it, so this is an intermediate achievement. **Means–end analysis** coordinates distinct obstacles at the source and destination rather than assuming that uncovering a disk automatically makes every desired move possible.
19|The **information-processing approach** specifies pathways from an initial state to a goal. Explicit states and operators describe which routes are possible. They do not, however, guarantee that a person will recognize the most useful property of the problem. || **How a problem is stated affects its difficulty**. Wording and presentation change which relationships the solver notices. Two versions can preserve the same underlying constraints while making different aspects easier to represent. || The **mutilated-checkerboard problem** asks whether a board can be covered by dominoes after squares are removed. A person might repeatedly try placements or instead identify a constraint applying to every placement. The representation influences which kind of search seems necessary and which reasoning becomes available.
20|A **sixty-four-square checkerboard** can be covered by **thirty-two dominoes**, each covering two adjacent squares. Removing two opposite corners leaves sixty-two squares, matching the area of thirty-one dominoes. Matching area alone does not establish that those dominoes can be arranged legally. || Every domino covers **one square of each color**. The opposite corners have the **same color**, so removing them leaves unequal numbers of the two colors. Any complete domino covering would require equal numbers, contradicting the remaining board. || The mutilated board therefore **cannot be completely covered**. The obstacle is color imbalance rather than too little total domino area. Representing that relationship supplies a reason applying to all possible arrangements without testing every unsuccessful placement sequence.
21|Solving the checkerboard problem requires representing each domino as covering **two squares of different types**. The constraints remain the same across presentations. What changes is how clearly the information directs attention toward the required pairing. || Conditions used a **blank board**, a **colored board**, words **black and pink**, or words **bread and butter**. Bread and butter made the contrast and pairing especially accessible. The labels encouraged treating each covered pair as requiring one of each kind. || Participants solved that version about **twice as fast as the blank version**. A useful representation reduced the time spent searching. The wording did not change which placements were legal, but it made the relationship explaining impossibility easier to recognize.
22|A **think-aloud protocol** records thoughts expressed during solving. Participants verbalize **new thoughts** rather than narrating their physical actions. The method targets information receiving attention while the solution is being sought. || In the bread-and-butter checkerboard problem, a solver can recognize that those labels matter to the required pairing. The relevant shift is from viewing squares merely as locations to treating them as two kinds that each domino must join. || This is a **representational shift**, related to Gestalt restructuring. The board and covering rule remain unchanged, but the solver now uses a different relationship. The verbal record can identify when that relationship becomes part of the person’s reasoning.
