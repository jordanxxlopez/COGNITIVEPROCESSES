2|**Decisions** are the process of choosing between alternatives, and **reasoning** is the process of drawing conclusions. Decisions rest on judgments we make, and applying those judgments can involve several kinds of reasoning. >> Choosing what to do almost always depends on first judging what is true or likely. || One of the main mechanisms behind our judgments is **inductive reasoning**, which is reasoning based on observations. It means reaching conclusions from evidence. >> We look at what we have seen and generalize to what is probably true.
3|**Inductive reasoning is the basis of scientific investigation**, since in science observations are made, data are collected and conclusions are drawn. The conclusions of inductive reasoning are **probably true, but not definitely true**, and they come with different degrees of certainty. >> No number of observations can prove a general conclusion beyond doubt. || The **strength of an inductive argument** depends on the **representativeness**, **number** and **quality** of the observations. A conclusion drawn from many varied, high-quality observations is stronger than one drawn from a few odd ones. >> Sometimes a single, very compelling observation carries a lot of weight, just as one black swan overturns the belief that all swans are white.
4|Inductive reasoning is used to make **scientific discoveries**, producing hypotheses and general conclusions. It is also used constantly in **everyday life**. >> We predict what will happen based on what has happened in the past. || We make predictions and choices from past experience, especially in familiar situations, so induction is a way of using past experience to guide present behavior. Some kinds of inductive reasoning even happen automatically, without our noticing. >> Expecting a familiar route to take the usual amount of time is induction at work.
5|When people use past experience to guide present behavior, they often use **shortcuts** to reach conclusions quickly. These shortcuts are called **heuristics**, which are rules of thumb that are likely to give the right answer but are not foolproof. >> Heuristics trade a little accuracy for a lot of speed. || Two commonly used heuristics are the **availability heuristic** and the **representativeness heuristic**. Both usually work well, but each one leads to predictable errors in certain situations.
6|The **availability heuristic** is that events that are **more easily remembered are judged to be more probable** than events that are harder to remember. We judge how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind, so the more examples we can think of, the more probable the event seems. >> Ease of recall stands in for actual frequency. || The problem is that other factors also make events stand out in memory, such as how **distinctive** they are, how **emotional** they are and how **often** we have encountered or recalled them. Those factors can make rare events feel common and common events feel rare.
7|People are asked whether English has more words that **begin with r** or more words that have **r as the third letter**. **Most people say more words begin with r**, even though in reality about three times as many words have r in the third position. >> The answer feels obvious, but it is wrong. || Words that start with r are much easier to call to mind, because we organize words by their first letter. That ease of recall is mistaken for how common those words really are. >> This is the availability heuristic leading people astray.
8|People are shown pairs of causes of death and asked which one is more likely in the United States. For several pairs, a large share of people picked the **less likely** cause, such as choosing **botulism** over **asthma**, even though asthma is far more common. >> The errors run in a consistent direction toward dramatic causes of death. || This happens because of the **availability** of the causes. A death from botulism makes the front page of the news, while deaths from asthma go almost unnoticed. >> News coverage makes rare causes easy to recall, so they feel more likely than they are.
9|The **representativeness heuristic** is judging how likely something is by **how much it resembles a typical case**. The probability that A belongs to class B is judged by how well A's properties match the properties normally associated with class B. >> We ask whether something looks like a typical member of a category. || America has **far more farms than libraries**. A randomly picked man named Robert wears glasses, speaks quietly and reads a lot, and the question is whether he is more likely a librarian or a farmer. >> Most people say librarian, because the description fits the stereotype. >> They **ignore the base rate**, the fact that there are so many more farmers than librarians.
10|People follow a simple rule. They use **base rate information** if it is all they have, but they use **descriptive information** whenever it is available and then disregard the base rate. >> A vivid description pushes the statistics out of mind. || Told only that a group is made up mostly of lawyers and partly of engineers, people correctly judge the chance that a random member is an engineer by the share of engineers. With base rates alone, people reason well. || But when a description is added of a man who is careful, ambitious, uninterested in politics and fond of carpentry and mathematical puzzles, people say he is an engineer. The description fits the engineer stereotype, so people ignore how few engineers the group actually contains.
11|The **conjunction rule** says that **the probability of two events together can never be higher than the probability of either one alone**. Linda is described as single, outspoken and very bright, a philosophy major who cared deeply about discrimination and social justice. >> People are asked whether it is more probable that Linda is a bank teller, or that she is a bank teller who is active in the feminist movement. || **Most people pick the second option**, which breaks the conjunction rule. Feminist bank tellers are a smaller group inside all bank tellers, so the combination can never be more likely than being a bank teller alone. >> The description sounds so much like a feminist that representativeness overrides logic.
12|People also make errors by **ignoring the size of the sample** their observations are based on. In the hospital problem, a town has a larger hospital with many births a day and a smaller hospital with fewer. >> About half of all babies are boys, but the share varies from day to day. || Each hospital records the days on which clearly more than half the babies born were boys, and people are asked which hospital recorded more such days. Only about a fifth of people chose the larger hospital, only about a fifth chose the smaller one, and **more than half said about the same**. >> Most people did not think sample size mattered at all.
13|The **correct answer to the hospital problem is the smaller hospital**. With fewer births each day, chance swings in the share of boys are much larger, so the smaller hospital strays far from one half much more often. >> The larger hospital's share stays close to one half, because chance differences even out over more births. || People got this wrong because they assumed both hospitals were equally representative of the population. They overlooked that a small sample is a much less reliable picture of the whole.
14|The **law of large numbers** says that **the larger the number of individuals drawn at random from a population, the more representative the resulting group will be** of the entire population. Small samples can easily come out unusual by chance. >> Large samples tend to match the population closely. || People often assume that representativeness holds for small samples too, and this leads to errors in reasoning. The hospital problem is a direct example of this mistake.
15|An **illusory correlation** is when a **correlation seems to exist but either does not exist or is much weaker than assumed**. It can happen when we expect two things to be related, so we fool ourselves into thinking they are. >> Expectation creates the pattern we think we see. || **Stereotypes** are oversimplified generalizations about a group of people, and they often focus on the negative. A stereotype can lead people to pay special attention to behaviors that fit it, and that selective attention creates an illusory correlation that strengthens the stereotype. >> This is related to the availability heuristic, because noticing those behaviors more makes them more available in memory.
16|**Lord and coworkers** had people who supported capital punishment and people who opposed it read the same article. Those in favor found that the article supported their view, and those against found that it supported theirs. >> The same evidence pushed each group further toward what it already believed. || This is the **myside bias**, the tendency to generate and evaluate evidence and test hypotheses in a way that is biased toward our own opinions and attitudes. People focused on information that agreed with their beliefs and disregarded information that did not. >> Each side read the article through the lens of its own position.
17|The myside bias is a type of **confirmation bias**, which is the tendency to look selectively for information that fits our hypothesis and to overlook information that argues against it. Confirmation bias acts like a pair of blinders, keeping us inside a view we think is correct. >> We never find out we are wrong, because we only look for evidence that we are right. || In the **number sequence task**, people were given the numbers two, four and six and asked to discover the rule behind them by trying their own sets of three numbers. After each set, they only learned whether it fit the rule. >> The most common guess was numbers increasing by two, and people kept testing sets that fit that guess. >> The actual rule was just any three numbers in increasing order, which people would only have found by testing sets they expected to fail.
18|**Decision making** means making judgments that involve choosing between different courses of action. **Utility** refers to outcomes that are desirable because they are in a person's best interest. >> A good decision is one that leads to the outcome with the most value for the person. || **Expected utility theory** assumes that people are **rational**. If they have all the relevant information, they will make the decision that results in the **maximum expected utility**, such as the biggest monetary payoff. >> The theory describes how a perfectly logical decision maker would choose.
19|The utility approach has a real **advantage**, because it gives **specific procedures for working out the best choice**. If the values and probabilities are known, the best option can be calculated. >> That makes decisions clear and checkable. || The approach also has real **problems**. People value things other than money, and many decisions do not maximize the chance of the best outcome, so people are not utility-maximizing machines. >> People gamble in casinos all the time, even though they know the odds are strongly against them.
20|In the **jellybean study**, people could earn a small amount of money each time they drew a red jellybean, and each time they chose which of two bowls to draw from. One bowl held **one red jellybean out of ten**, and the other held **seven red jellybeans out of a hundred**. >> The first bowl gives the better odds, since one in ten is a bigger share than seven in a hundred. || Even so, **most people chose the bowl with seven red jellybeans**. Seeing more red jellybeans felt like a better chance, even though the proportion was worse. >> Their choice was driven by something other than the actual probabilities.
21|After the attacks of September eleventh, **air travel went down and driving went up**, even though the chance of dying in a car accident is far greater than the chance of dying while flying. Fear made flying feel more dangerous than it was. >> People traded a smaller risk for a larger one. || In fact, more Americans died on the roads by driving instead of flying than the total number of passengers killed on the hijacked flights. Choices based on fear instead of probabilities cost lives.
22|In this dilemma, a convoy of food trucks is headed to a refugee camp during a famine, and a second camp turns out to have many more refugees. Sending the convoy to the second camp would save the many people there, but the smaller group in the first camp would die as a result. >> The question is whether to send the convoy to the second camp. || By expected utility, rerouting is clearly better, because it saves far more lives than it costs. Yet **Ritov and Baron** found that **most people did not choose to reroute the convoy**. >> Some people said they would not reroute it even if only one person in the first camp would die, so the act of causing deaths outweighed the lives saved.
23|**Tetlock and colleagues** proposed the **sacred value protection model**, which says that **people want to protect what they hold sacred**. Sacred values are kept in a separate compartment from ordinary, everyday values. >> Things like a human life are treated as different in kind from money. || When the two kinds of values are mixed together, people feel threatened and respond with **moral outrage**, which is called a **taboo** tradeoff. When two sacred values are weighed against each other, people are much less outraged, which is called a **tragic** tradeoff. >> Comparing a life with money feels wrong, while comparing one life with another feels sad but acceptable.
24|In the **taboo tradeoff scenario**, a hospital administrator can save a young boy who needs a liver transplant, but the transplant would cost the hospital a million dollars that could be spent on other needs. The choice sets a child's life against money. >> That mixes a sacred value with an ordinary one. || In the **tragic tradeoff scenario**, two children need a liver transplant, but there is only one liver. Here a sacred value is weighed against another sacred value, and there is no way to avoid losing one.
25|In the **taboo** case, people were extremely outraged when the administrator **did not save the boy**, and much less outraged when he did. Choosing money over a child's life felt like a moral violation. >> The gap between the two choices was very large. || In the **tragic** case, outrage was moderate and about the same whichever child was saved. Choosing between two lives was seen as painful but not as a betrayal of sacred values.
26|**Ginges and colleagues** extended these findings to a much more realistic setting. They interviewed **hundreds of Israeli settlers**, **hundreds of Palestinian refugees** and **hundreds of Palestinian students** affiliated with various political groups. >> These were people with a real stake in the conflict. || They offered three types of tradeoff deals as possible solutions to the conflict. Each deal asked people to give up something they might treat as sacred.
27|In the **taboo** deal, Palestinians would recognize the Jewish people's sacred and historic right to Israel, and there would be two states, a Jewish state of Israel and a Palestinian state in nearly all of the West Bank and Gaza. This deal asks people to trade away a sacred claim. || The **taboo plus** deal adds a large yearly payment of money from Israel to Palestine on top of the taboo deal. The **symbolic** deal instead has Israel recognize the Palestinians' historic right to their own state and apologize for the wrongs done to them. >> One adds money, and the other adds a symbolic concession that touches values directly.
28|**Moral absolutists** turned the disputed issues into **sacred values**. Among settlers, **nearly half** were moral absolutists who believed it was never acceptable to give up part of the Land of Israel no matter how great the benefit. >> Among Palestinian students, **just over half** treated the right of return and Jerusalem as sacred values. || **Non-absolutists** strongly preferred not to compromise on these issues, but they did not treat them as sacred. The difference between the two groups is what predicted how people reacted to the deals.
29|For moral absolutists, **adding money to a taboo deal made things worse**. Their anger and disgust went up and their support for violence went up when the taboo deal came with a payment, while non-absolutists became more accepting. >> Offering money for something sacred felt like an insult. || A **symbolic** concession worked better than money. Compared with the taboo deal, the symbolic deal reduced anger and lowered support for violence. >> When a value is sacred, respect and recognition matter more than material rewards.
30|Researchers also investigated how **material incentives and disincentives**, such as **economic sanctions**, affect negotiations that involve sacred values. Two separate studies asked how such incentives psychologically affect the people they target and whether they could change behavior. >> The focus was the Iranian nuclear program. || The question was whether Iranians who treat the nuclear program as a sacred value would be open to material incentives or penalties to compromise. This work drew interest from defense agencies and major news outlets.
31|The **experiments** included **about two thousand Iranian participants** in an online study conducted in Farsi, the main language of Iran. The outcomes measured were people's approval, how angry they thought people would be, and whether they found the deal misleading. >> These measures captured both personal views and expected public reaction. || The **sacred value measure** asked what participants thought about Iran giving up its nuclear energy or weapons program. The answers ranged from thinking it definitely needs to happen, to not objecting, to accepting it only if the benefits were great enough, to saying it should never happen no matter how great the consequences. >> People who chose that last answer were counted as holding a sacred value, and they were **a small minority** for both nuclear energy and nuclear weapons.
32|The study crossed two kinds of nuclear program, **energy** and **weapons**, with three kinds of deal. In the **taboo** deal, Iran would give up its program and surrender its facilities to the United Nations, and in return Israel would do the same. >> The **taboo plus** deal added a large package of economic aid from the United Nations. >> The **taboo minus** deal added a threat of further sanctions if Iran refused. || Participants were asked whether they personally approved of the deal, whether they thought people would be angry if the government accepted it and whether they found the deal misleading. These questions measured both private approval and expected public outrage.
33|For people **without** a sacred value, approval of the energy deals was high whether the deal came with incentives, sanctions or neither, and their expected anger was low. For people **with** a sacred value, approval was low, and it got even lower when money or sanctions were added. >> Adding incentives or penalties **lowered** approval for sacred value holders. || Anger followed the opposite pattern. For sacred value holders, expected anger **rose** when the taboo deal came with incentives or sanctions, while anger stayed low for everyone else. >> Material incentives and penalties can backfire when a value is held as sacred.
