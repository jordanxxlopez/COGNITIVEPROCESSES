2|The modal model's picture of short-term memory **turned out to be too simple**. Short-term memory is not just passive storage, because it also needs **control processes**, so a better model has to include them. >> Holding something in mind is only half the job, and the other half is working on it. || **Baddeley and Hitch** proposed **working memory**, a concept similar to short-term memory but broader. Working memory is a **limited-capacity system for the temporary storage and manipulation of information** for complex tasks such as **comprehension, learning and reasoning**. >> Storage means keeping information on hand, and manipulation means changing, combining or calculating with it.
3|Multiplying forty-three by six in your head makes the idea concrete. \[43\times6=258\] First you picture the problem, then you multiply the ones digits. \[3\times6=18\] >> You hold the eight in memory while carrying the one over to the tens. || Next you multiply the tens digit and add the carried one. \[6\times4=24,\qquad24+1=25\] Then you combine that result with the eight you have been holding all along to get the final answer. >> Every step depends on something an earlier step left in memory. || This one small task needs both **storage**, such as holding the eight, and **active processing**, such as carrying the one. That combination of holding and working is exactly what working memory is meant to capture.
4|The two models describe the same short-lived memory in very different ways. **Atkinson and Shiffrin's short-term memory** is a **single component for all types of information**, mainly used to **hold information for a short time**. >> In that view, words, pictures and locations all share one store. || **Baddeley and Hitch's working memory** has **three components**, which are the **central executive**, the **visuospatial sketchpad** and the **phonological loop**, and Baddeley later added a fourth, the **episodic buffer**. It is used for **manipulating information during complex thinking**. >> So working memory splits the store into specialized parts and adds a controller that directs them.
5|The **phonological loop** handles sound and language. It has a **phonological store** that holds verbal and auditory information, and an **articulatory rehearsal process** that keeps items in that store from decaying. >> The rehearsal process is like a quiet inner voice that keeps repeating what needs to be kept. || The **visuospatial sketchpad** holds **visual and spatial information**, such as shapes, colors and locations. It is where we picture a scene or keep track of where things are. || The **central executive** pulls information from long-term memory, **coordinates the other components** and directs and maintains attention. It does not store much itself, because its job is to control how the stores are used.
6|The full **Baddeley and Hitch** diagram places working memory between the senses and long-term knowledge. Input enters sensory memory and then reaches the **central executive**, which trades information back and forth with **long-term knowledge**. >> The central executive sits at the top, sending work down to the two helper systems. || The executive is linked to both the **phonological loop** and the **visuospatial sketchpad**, and each of those systems is also linked to long-term knowledge. So working memory is a busy network of connected parts, not a single box.
7|The phonological loop explains three well-known effects. The **phonological similarity effect** is that letters or words that sound alike get confused. The **word-length effect** is that memory for lists is better for short words than long words, because long words take longer to rehearse and to say during recall. >> Both effects come from memory working with sounds. || **Articulatory suppression** is when speaking during a task interferes with rehearsal, so memory gets worse. Saying something irrelevant out loud ties up the inner voice that the loop needs.
8|The basic **phonological similarity effect** is that **words that sound similar get confused**. In **Conrad's** study, participants saw target letters flashed quickly, and half the time the letters sounded similar while half the time they did not. They then wrote the letters down. >> When people made mistakes, they rarely swapped in a letter that looked like the target. >> Instead they usually swapped in a letter that sounded like it, such as writing a letter that rhymes with the target. || The letters were seen, yet the errors followed sound. That means the letters were being held in a sound-based form.
9|The phonological similarity experiment revealed that people confuse letters that **sound** alike, **even though the letters were presented visually**. Nothing about the display was spoken aloud, yet sound still governed the errors. >> So seeing a letter does not mean it is stored as a picture. || It also revealed that the phonological loop is needed for **converting** visual information into sound, not just for holding sound. Reading a letter silently already turns it into its spoken form, and that spoken form is what gets held.
10|The basic **word-length effect** is that people **remember fewer words when the words are long**. Studies found people recall more short words than long ones from the same kind of list. >> Long words take longer to rehearse, so fewer of them fit in the loop before they start to fade. || Bilingual children who speak Welsh and English have a **shorter digit span in Welsh than in English**, because Welsh number words take longer to pronounce. The famously long Welsh place name on the sign makes the point about long words in Welsh. >> A good rule of thumb is that a person's digit span is about the number of words they can say in roughly two seconds.
11|The basic finding of **articulatory suppression** is that **speaking while memorizing makes memory worse**, because speaking keeps the phonological loop busy. People who repeat something irrelevant out loud remember less. >> The talking takes over the inner voice that would otherwise rehearse the list. || Articulatory suppression also **reduces the word-length and phonological similarity effects**. If the loop is busy, there is no rehearsal for word length to slow down and no sound code for similar sounds to confuse. >> Losing both effects at once is strong evidence that they come from the same sound-based loop.
12|The **visuospatial sketchpad** holds **visual and spatial information**, and it is involved in **visual imagery**, such as picturing a room or a route. It does for pictures and locations what the phonological loop does for sounds and words. >> Picturing the layout of a familiar room uses the sketchpad. || In the working memory diagram, the sketchpad sits beside the phonological loop, and both are under the control of the central executive and both are linked to long-term knowledge. Having two separate helpers is what lets us hold a picture and a word at the same time. >> The next studies test whether the two helpers really work separately.
13|**Brooks** used a **sentence task** to test how the two systems share work. People memorized a sentence and then went through it word by word, indicating whether each word was a noun. In one condition they answered by **speaking**, and in another they answered by **pointing**. >> Pointing was easier than speaking. || The explanation is that the phonological loop was already busy holding the sentence. Speaking the answers competed with that same loop, while pointing used the sketchpad, which was free. >> When two jobs need the same system, they interfere with each other.
14|In Brooks' **F demonstration**, people memorized the outline of a capital letter F and then traced around it in their mind, saying for each corner whether it was an inside corner or an outside corner. Again they answered either by **speaking** or by **pointing**. >> This time speaking was easier than pointing, which is the opposite of the sentence task. || The sketchpad was busy holding the image of the F. Pointing competed with that visual work, while speaking used the phonological loop, which was free. >> So the same two response methods trade places depending on which system the memory task is already using.
15|**Tasks are easier when the information being held and the operation being done on it use different types of short-term memory**, one verbal and one visual. When both use the same type, they compete and performance suffers. >> Brooks' two tasks show this pattern in both directions. || That means the **verbal and visual stores are somewhat independent**, or at least have **separate capacities**. Filling one does not use up the room in the other.

17|In **mental rotation**, people see two drawings of block shapes and decide whether they are the same shape turned to a different angle. **Shepard and Metzler** found that the time to answer **grows steadily with the angle of rotation**. >> A small turn is judged quickly, and a large turn takes much longer. || The straight-line rise means people seem to rotate an image in their mind at a steady rate, just like turning a real object. The bigger the turn, the longer the mental rotation takes, which is strong evidence that visual imagery works like seeing.
18|The **central executive** does the real **work** of working memory. It **directs and maintains attention**, including focusing, dividing and switching attention, **suppresses irrelevant information**, **coordinates the sketchpad and the phonological loop**, and **performs calculations**. >> It is the part that decides what to do with the information being held. || Today its work is often split into three abilities, which are **updating**, **shifting** and **inhibition**. Updating replaces old information with new, shifting moves between tasks, and inhibition holds back what does not belong. || The idea of a central executive is often criticized as imagining a **little person in the head**, called a homunculus, that does all the work. Splitting the executive into specific abilities is one way to answer that criticism.
19|The **episodic buffer** is a **fourth component** that Baddeley added to working memory later. It has a **limited capacity of about four chunks**, and it **binds information from the loop, the sketchpad and long-term memory into integrated episodes**. >> It is where sound, image and knowledge come together into one experience. || It was needed because the original model could not explain some findings. People can recall a meaningful sentence of many words, far beyond their span for unrelated words, and some amnesic patients can immediately recall a story they just heard. >> Both cases need a place where long-term knowledge and current input are combined.
20|**Vogel and colleagues** studied **individual differences** in working memory and the brain. They first measured each person's working memory capacity and split people into a **high-capacity** group and a **low-capacity** group. >> Then they showed both groups simple or complex displays and recorded brain activity with electrodes on the scalp. || The task was a change detection task with an arrow telling people which side to remember. Some displays contained only items to remember, and others mixed in irrelevant items that should be ignored. >> Comparing the groups reveals whether capacity is about holding more or about ignoring better.
21|Panel b tracks brain activity that **continues well into the retention interval**, the blank wait after the display. For **high-capacity** people, activity is greater when there are four relevant items than when there are two. >> Their brain activity rises with how much relevant information they are holding. || Adding irrelevant items has **no effect for high-capacity people**, but it **increases activity for low-capacity people**. So low-capacity people seem to be storing the distractors along with the items they were told to remember.
22|Panel c relates **capacity** to a brain measure of **filtering efficiency**. People with higher capacity are also **less distracted by irrelevant items**, so they filter better. >> The two measures rise together across people. || This changes how differences in working memory capacity can be understood. High capacity may come less from having a bigger store and more from being better at keeping irrelevant information out. >> Good filtering leaves the limited space free for what actually matters.
