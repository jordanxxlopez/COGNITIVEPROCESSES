2|**Psycholinguistics** investigates the psychological mechanisms by which humans acquire and process language. It includes several connected questions rather than one general ability.||**Comprehension** concerns understanding spoken and written input, while **production** concerns creating spoken or written messages. **Representation** asks how language is organized in mind and brain; **acquisition** asks how that system is learned.||The distinctions matter for evidence. Recognizing a word, producing it, storing its form, and learning it are related behaviors, but a successful account must explain the operations involved in each rather than assume one measure answers every question.
3|A **phoneme** is a smallest contrastive speech-sound unit: replacing it can change a word's identity or meaning. **“Bit”** contains three phonemes, conventionally transcribed **/b/, /ɪ/, /t/**.||Phonemes concern **sound contrasts**, not written letters. A phoneme does not have to mean anything on its own; its role is to distinguish possible spoken forms.||This separates phonemes from **morphemes**, which contribute meaning or grammatical function. The definitions ask different questions—what sound contrast distinguishes words, versus what smallest unit carries a meaningful or grammatical contribution.
4|A **morpheme** is the smallest unit with **meaning or grammatical function**. It need not be a complete standalone word.||**“Table” has one morpheme. “Bedroom” has two**, bed and room, whose meanings contribute to the compound. **“Bedrooms” has three**, because plural **-s** adds a grammatical distinction.||Compare this with phonemes, which are contrastive sound units. Counting letters or syllables does not identify morphemes: the relevant issue is which pieces contribute meaning or grammatical information.
5|The **mental lexicon** is reusable knowledge of word forms that gives access to meaning. The vocabulary scale is substantial: well-read adults can understand **more than 50,000 words**, rather than reconstructing each form from scratch whenever it appears.||Stored-form accounts reach back to the **nineteenth century**. **Lichtheim** observed dissociations between written and spoken recognition, suggesting distinguishable access routes rather than one undifferentiated language operation.||**Lichtheim's 1885 spoken-word model**, reproduced through **Shallice (1988)**, links **auditory word representations, a conceptual center, and motor word representations**. Spoken input enters the auditory side and motor representations support spoken output. The conceptual route connects forms with meaning; a separately drawn auditory-to-motor connection illustrates another possible relationship between input and output.
6|Follow the full trial: **fixation cross → brief letter string, about 50 ms → mask → choice between D and K**. The target letter K appears as part of **WORK**, alone as **K**, or in **ORWK**, a nonword.||The task is **letter identification**, not deciding whether the whole string is a word. Better K identification in WORK is the **word superiority effect**, showing that whole-word context can help identify a constituent letter despite the extra surrounding letters.
7|**Bottom-up word recognition** starts with sensory features, identifies letters or phonemes, and uses those constituents to activate candidate words. The information initially comes from the visible or acoustic pattern.||**Top-down processing** allows activated word candidates to feed support back to their constituent letters or phonemes. A familiar word can therefore help identify a weak component, as in the word superiority effect.||The two directions interact. A candidate word needs support from the input, and once activated it can help resolve uncertain parts of that input. Recognition is not restricted to a one-way assembly of independent letters.
8|In **phonemic restoration**, a phoneme covered by a sound such as a **cough** can still be experienced as present. Listeners use the surviving word and sentence context to supply a plausible completion.||Participants may not locate the cough accurately relative to the restored sound. Importantly, **words occurring later** can also affect which missing phoneme is experienced. The phenomenon therefore reflects interpretation of linguistic context, not merely detection of an acoustic segment that was physically intact.
9|The **word superiority task** presents a **word, isolated letter, or nonword briefly**, follows it with a mask, and then asks which of **two letters** occupied a target position. The required response is letter identification in each condition.||Letters are identified more accurately within a real word than alone or in a nonword. Because exposure and masking limit inspection, the result shows that lexical context can support perception of a constituent.||The choice alternatives can be arranged so that **either letter would make a real word**. That prevents word status alone from revealing the answer. This is distinct from **lexical decision**, where the person explicitly judges whether an entire string is a word.
10|**McClelland and Rumelhart's interactive activation model (1981)** connects **feature, letter, and word levels**. Consistent input activates candidates upward, and activated word candidates can feed support back toward consistent letters.||The diagram includes competing candidates such as **WEAR, WORK, WORD, and WEAK**, supported to different degrees by the surviving visual features. Competition helps prevent every candidate from remaining equally active. The word-superiority result follows when a coherent word-level candidate supports a letter that would be harder to identify alone or in a string without the same lexical support.
11|A **corpus** is a large sample of utterances or written texts. It can estimate frequencies of **words, different meanings, and grammatical constructions**, providing evidence about the experience that could shape predictions during comprehension.||The slide's **Speer (2022), wordfreq** estimates list occurrences per million: **the 53,703; to 26,915; and 25,704; of 25,119; a 22,909; in 18,621; I 12,303; is 11,749; for 10,233; that 10,233**. These are estimates pooled across corpora, not a census of every speaker's experience.
12|A **word-frequency estimate depends on what is counted**: the source material, time period, and counting unit all affect the measurement. Frequency is tied to a sample rather than being a context-free property of a word.||The historical superlative display comes from the **Google Books Ngram Viewer**, which samples printed books across years. A changing rate can reflect both changing usage and changes in the material included.||Evaluate whether the corpus represents the experience relevant to the question. A normalized rate can be more informative than a raw count, but a book-based estimate should still not automatically be treated as a complete measure of conversation or an individual's personal exposure.
13|The **word frequency effect** is faster responses to more frequently encountered words. The example contrasts **HOME, about 646 occurrences per million**, with **HIKE, about 9**, using **Speer's (2022) wordfreq estimates**.||In **lexical decision**, answer yes to real words and no to nonwords such as **HIKM**; the higher-frequency word is typically accepted faster. During normal reading, **lower-frequency words also receive longer fixations**, offering a second behavioral measure of processing difficulty.
14|Using the **Speer (2022) wordfreq estimates**, **age of acquisition (AoA)** concerns when a word was learned. The comparison matches current frequency while contrasting early and later learning: **doll/thesis (Zipf 4.06/4.05), puppy/clause (4.00/3.99), cookie/audit (4.07/4.04)**.||The **Zipf scale** is a base-ten logarithmic frequency measure expressed relative to a billion words. Earlier-learned words are recognized and named faster even with approximately matched frequencies, so AoA contributes information beyond how often a word occurs now.
15|**Lexical ambiguity** occurs when a word form permits more than one meaning. **“I saw her duck”** can describe seeing her bird or seeing her lower her head or body. The same form supports different interpretations.||As **Traxler (2012)** emphasizes, these meanings are not equally frequent. Context and relative meaning frequency both affect which interpretation is readily available and eventually selected.||Recognizing the word form and identifying its intended meaning are therefore different operations. Ambiguous input can activate competing candidates, and later examples show how their competition changes with context and dominance.
16|Context can change how an **ambiguous word** is interpreted without changing its spelling or sound. A bird-related context supports the animal meaning of **duck**, while a context about avoiding an object supports the action of ducking.||More than one interpretation can be briefly available. Context helps favor the one that fits the sentence, but **meaning frequency** also affects how strongly candidates are activated.||Following **Traxler's (2012)** distinction among meanings, the time course should not be treated as every possible meaning always receiving equal activation. The next comparisons examine the interaction between contextual support and relative dominance.
17|**Meaning dominance** is the relative frequency of a word's different meanings. A meaning is dominant because it is used more often, not because the alternatives are incorrect.||**Biased dominance** describes unequal frequencies: **TIN** favors metal over a container. **Balanced dominance** describes more similar frequencies: **CAST** can refer to theatrical membership or a plaster cast.||The difference affects accessibility and competition during reading. The displayed bars are **illustrative proportions**, not exact measured frequencies; their purpose is to show the contrast between a strong leading meaning and more balanced alternatives.
18|The **dependent measure is fixation duration**, used here to compare processing of ambiguous and control words. Longer viewing can indicate added difficulty resolving competing meanings.||In **“the cast worked…”**, **CAST** has relatively balanced meanings and activates alternatives. Readers look longer at cast than the unambiguous control **COOK**.||In the neutral **“the tin was…”** context, the **dominant metal meaning** is favored, and biased **TIN** can be read about as quickly as a control. The contrast shows that ambiguity's cost depends on relative dominance, rather than every multiple-meaning word always producing equal competition.
19|In **“The miners went to the store and saw that they had beans in a tin,”** context supports the **less frequent container meaning**. That support can coexist with activation of the dominant metal meaning, producing competition and longer reading than **“cup.”**||When context favors the dominant meaning, less competition can occur. The comparison shows that **meaning frequency and sentence context work together**, rather than either being a complete explanation on its own.
20|**Semantics** concerns the meanings of words and sentences; **syntax** concerns rules for combining words. The distinction helps explain why a sentence can be grammatical yet odd in meaning, or meaningful in intent yet grammatically malformed.||**“The cats won't eat”** is the baseline. **“The cats won't bake”** keeps the structure but is semantically anomalous in the ordinary intended situation. **“The cats won't eating”** creates a verb-form problem after “won't.”||The comparison separates meaning from structure. An imaginative context might make baking cats interpretable, but it would not by itself repair the grammatical form of the eating sentence.
21|**Broca's area is in the frontal lobe**, whereas **Wernicke's area is in the temporal lobe**. Broca's aphasia includes **slow, labored, ungrammatical speech** and difficulty with some sentence structures; Wernicke's aphasia includes seriously impaired meaningful speech and comprehension.||Compare **“The apple was eaten by the girl”** with **“The boy was pushed by the girl.”** World knowledge strongly constrains the first, while the second needs syntax to determine who did what. The classical distinction links syntax and semantics to different patterns of impairment, while actual language functions depend on broader networks.
22|An **event-related potential (ERP)** is a stimulus-linked electrical response measured over fractions of a second. It contains components at different delays, allowing researchers to compare **when language-related effects occur**.||The diagram is **schematic, not real data**. A gray line represents an acceptable sentence, a blue dashed line a **semantic violation associated with N400**, and an orange dotted line a **syntactic violation associated with P600**.||The horizontal axis shows **milliseconds after the critical word**; the vertical axis shows amplitude in **microvolts (µV), with negative plotted upward**. N400 occurs around **400 ms** and P600 around **600 ms**, after earlier common waves.||The differing responses provide time-sensitive evidence about language processing. Their schematic shapes illustrate the argument and should not be used to calculate exact experimental effect sizes.
23|The ERP comparisons distinguish responses associated with **semantic anomaly** and **syntactic difficulty**. Read both the example sentences and the timing of the plotted waves.||In **panel A**, **“Whipped cream tastes sweet”** is compared with **“Whipped cream tastes anxious.”** The anomalous meaning is associated with a larger **N400 around 400 ms**. The course links this component with semantic processing and temporal structures, noting reduction after temporal damage.||In **panel B**, **“The spoiled child throws the toy on the floor”** is compared with **“The spoiled child throw the toy on the floor.”** The agreement violation is associated with a later **P600**. The course links this response with syntactic processing or reanalysis and frontal structures, noting reduction after frontal damage.||The axes show **time in milliseconds** and **amplitude in microvolts**. Negative N400 and positive P600 can appear in opposite plotted directions, so inspect the polarity labels. These are simplified functional associations; an ERP component is not the activity of one isolated brain region.
24|**Parsing** groups words into phrases to establish sentence relationships and meaning. A **garden-path sentence** supports a plausible early grouping that later input forces the reader to revise.||After **“After the musician played the piano…”**, endings such as **“she left the stage”** or **“she bowed to the audience”** fit an interpretation in which the piano is the object of played.||With **“…was wheeled off the stage,”** that grouping fails. The repaired reading is **“After the musician played, the piano was wheeled off the stage,”** with piano beginning the main clause.||This is **temporary ambiguity**: later words clarify the correct structure, but the earlier commitment creates difficulty before the sentence is successfully reinterpreted.
25|In **“The horse raced past the barn fell,”** the initial tendency is to take **raced** as the main verb. The later **fell** is then difficult to fit into the structure already built.||The successful reading is **“The horse [that was raced past the barn] fell.”** The bracketed part is a **reduced relative clause** describing which horse; fell is the main-clause verb.||The sentence therefore has a grammatical interpretation. Its garden-path difficulty comes from incremental commitment to the earlier structure and the need to **reparse** when the ending arrives.
26|In **“The burglar discovered behind the bushes fled,”** the reader can initially interpret **discovered** as the burglar's main action. **Fled** then creates a problem for that grouping.||The intended reading is **“The burglar [who was discovered behind the bushes] fled.”** The reduced relative clause describes the burglar, while fled supplies the main action.||Like the horse example, this separates an initially plausible parse from the structure required by the complete sentence. Later input resolves the ambiguity but can require effortful revision.
27|The **syntax-first approach** proposes an initial grouping guided by grammatical rules, with other information used during revision if that grouping fails. **Late closure** favors attaching new material to the current phrase for as long as possible.||Follow the flowchart: read a word, attach it according to the initial syntax rule, check whether the parse still works, and either continue or **reanalyze using additional information**. This is a proposal about processing order, not a claim that meaning is never used.
28|In the initial interpretation, **“the piano”** is attached as the object of **played**: **“[After the musician played the piano]…”** This is plausible while the sentence remains incomplete.||The continuation **“was wheeled”** exposes the problem. Piano must be available as the subject of that predicate, so keeping it inside the earlier object phrase no longer works.||The repaired structure is **“[After the musician played] [the piano was wheeled off the stage].”** Changing the phrase boundary assigns the same words different grammatical roles and makes the sentence coherent.
