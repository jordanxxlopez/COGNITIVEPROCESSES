2|The **syntax-first approach** proposes an initial parse guided by grammatical rules. **Late closure** favors adding a new word to the current phrase for as long as that remains possible.||A temporarily ambiguous sentence can therefore receive an early structure before all later information is available. When the ending no longer fits that structure, the reader must **reparse**.||The account places other information, including meaning and context, in the repair process after an initial syntactic commitment. The experimental question is whether that order describes what readers actually do or whether multiple sources constrain parsing earlier.
3|The syntax-first **parser** is proposed to operate automatically using rules that favor a relatively simple grammatical structure. A **syntactic tree** represents how words form phrases and how those phrases relate.||**Late closure** is one such initial rule: continue attaching incoming material to the current phrase when possible. The initial organization can be efficient while still producing a garden path under ambiguity.||When later words make the structure fail, an **attention-demanding repair** reorganizes it using additional information. The account therefore separates relatively automatic first attachment from more effortful reinterpretation.
4|**“The spy saw the man with the binoculars”** permits binoculars as the spy's instrument or as something the man possesses. The two bracketings assign **“with the binoculars”** to different parts of the sentence.||In **“The bird saw the man with the binoculars,”** world knowledge favors the man possessing the binoculars. The **interactionist approach** allows syntax and semantics to influence the developing interpretation together, rather than requiring meaning to wait until a failed initial parse.
5|In the **visual-world paradigm**, participants hear language while viewing a scene, and researchers measure their eye movements. **Tanenhaus and colleagues (1995)** used this to study interpretation as it unfolds.||Gaze indicates which visible objects or locations are being considered. When the sentence and scene support a revised interpretation, the time course of eye movements can reveal when that change occurs.||The findings support using **visual context, semantics, and syntax together during comprehension**, rather than requiring the scene's meaning to wait until a purely syntactic interpretation has failed.
6|With **two apples**, **“Place the apple on the towel in the box”** creates a need to identify which apple the speaker means. **“On the towel”** can specify the apple already resting there, leaving **the box** as the destination.||The interactionist interpretation uses the scene early: because the listener needs a distinguishing description, looking toward the relevant apple can reflect referent identification rather than first treating the towel as where the apple should be moved.
7|With **only one apple** in the scene, a phrase distinguishing it from another apple is less necessary. **“On the towel”** can therefore be interpreted initially as where the apple should be moved.||Gaze can shift toward the **other towel**, reflecting that destination interpretation. On hearing **“in the box,”** the listener must revise the instruction and can look back toward the apple.||The contrast with a **two-apple scene** changes visual context while keeping the instruction comparable. 10|The birdhouse passage says John was **pounding a nail** but does not explicitly name a **hammer**. 11|A **situation model** represents the events, objects, and actions a text describes, rather than only its words, phrase boundaries, or paragraphs. It can represent the situation from the protagonist's point of view.||**Simulation** refers to activating relevant perceptual or motor characteristics. After **“He hammered the nail into the wall,”** a **horizontal nail** fits the implied arrangement.||Participants identify a matching nail picture faster than an orientation inconsistent with the event. This connects comprehension with perceptual detail inferred from the situation, even though the sentence did not explicitly list the nail's orientation.
12|**“The ranger saw the eagle in the sky”** supports a situation representation of an airborne eagle with **extended wings**. 14|**Ross Metusalem et al. (2012)** measured **N400 responses** while participants read short passages. 15|Conversation is a **rapid, dynamic exchange** involving at least two people. Each speaker must track both what the partner says and what the partner already knows.||The **given–new contract** connects **given information**, already established for the listener, with a **new contribution**. This lets the listener relate the current sentence to shared knowledge rather than interpret it in isolation.||Once introduced and accepted, new information can become given for later utterances. **Common ground changes during conversation**, so successful production and comprehension require updating the shared context.
16|**Syntactic coordination** occurs when speakers use similar grammatical constructions. **Syntactic priming** is a mechanism that can produce it: encountering a construction increases the likelihood of using that form subsequently.||The alignment concerns **structure**, not necessarily repeated vocabulary or identical messages. Two sentences can use the same grammatical arrangement while describing different people and events.||Reusing an available construction can reduce the computational effort required to plan an utterance in rapid conversation. This helps explain how speakers coordinate understanding and production without designing every sentence form independently from scratch.
17|A conversational partner can be a **confederate** whose construction is controlled by the experimenter. The percentage concerns structural alignment, not general language accuracy or whether the same words were repeated.||The finding supports sensitivity to the partner's linguistic behavior: hearing a construction changes which form the participant is likely to produce next.||Conversation combines this **syntactic alignment** with the establishment of **common ground**. Coordinating forms and shared information can reduce planning and interpretation demands during a fast exchange; the reported rate belongs to the specific studied conditions.
19|The **Sapir–Whorf hypothesis**, associated here with **Whorf (1956)**, proposes that language can influence thought. **Winawer et al. 21|An **egocentric reference frame** expresses location relative to the person: left, right, front, and behind. The lecture also notes geographically dispersed examples in **Bali and Queensland**. “Raise your east hand” illustrates the difference: deciding which hand is east depends on orientation in the environment, not a permanently named body side.
22|Tzeltal's historically landmark-based **“uphill” and “downhill”** directions function as stable bearings rather than shifting with the speaker's left and right. Other speakers may use geographic terms such as **uptown or west in New York**, but the comparison concerns how pervasively a stable reference frame structures ordinary descriptions.

24|Natural languages had long been described through **formal internal structures**, while much mid-century experimental psychology resisted explanations using unobservable internal states. **Skinner's Verbal Behavior** applied operant conditioning to language learning.||**Chomsky's 1959 review** became important to the cognitive revolution by arguing that this account could not adequately explain language's productive structure. The disagreement connects linguistic theory to the broader question of what counts as an acceptable cognitive explanation.
25|**Substrate dependence** is the possibility that language's physical implementation matters to its explanation. A theory may need to account for the brain and mechanisms that actually perform the task, rather than describe only an abstract function.||Relevant constraints can include **memory limitations, errors, and associative learning**. These may affect which representations can be learned or which processing operations can be carried out.||This contrasts with an approach that treats implementation as safely separable from the formal problem. The issue is whether an adequate language theory can ignore those constraints, not whether an abstract description is ever useful.
26|**David Marr's three levels** distinguish explanatory questions. The **computational level** asks what problem is solved and why; the **algorithmic level** asks which representations and operations solve it; the **implementational level** asks how those operations are physically realized.||The lecture places Chomsky's formal language approach mainly at the computational level. The distinction helps separate a specification of a function from the particular method and hardware used to carry it out.||These are **levels of analysis, not successive processing stages**. Naming the levels does not establish that implementation is irrelevant to every problem; that independence must be assessed for the system being explained.
27|For a **cash register**, the computational goal is to obtain a purchase total from prices and calculate change from payment. 28|A **Type I problem** is described here as allowing a clean separation of these levels. Treating language that way permits formal work on **Universal Grammar** and language-specific processes without building errors, memory limits, or associative learning into the same account.||The lecture contrasts this with substrate-sensitive approaches. Its chronology note matters: **Chomsky's critique preceded Marr's framework by about a decade**. Both influenced **GOFAI**, the tradition of symbolic or “good old-fashioned” artificial intelligence.
