2|**Cognitive psychology** investigates the mind by asking how information is represented and transformed. To explain perception, attention, memory, or reasoning, begin with what information enters the system, how it is represented, and which operations lead to the observed response.||An **information-processing explanation** connects those intermediate steps rather than treating the mind as an unexplained box. A **computational perspective** also asks what problem the system solves and why the proposed operations would solve it.||The goal is to make an account explicit enough to compare with evidence. A response is something to explain; simply naming it does not identify the mechanism that produced it.
3|**Literature, philosophy, astrology, and cognitive psychology** can all discuss the mind, but they differ in how they justify an explanation. Compare them using three questions: **What are we trying to explain? How do we test whether an answer is true? How do practitioners reach agreement?**||These questions separate the topic of a claim from the method supporting it. Two accounts may both concern experience while accepting different kinds of evidence.||For cognitive psychology, the distinctive commitment is to explanations that can be examined using systematic measurement and testing. Agreement should depend on evidence that other researchers can evaluate, rather than the word “mind” making every approach scientifically equivalent.
4|**Cognitive psychology** studies how people process information about their environment and the mechanisms behind **perceiving, acting, and thinking**. An explanation connects a proposed mental operation with something observable, such as a response time or an identification error.||**Controlled experiments** vary relevant conditions so that differences in behavior can test competing explanations. **Simulations** implement proposed operations, while **statistical models** express relationships and predictions that can be compared with data.||**Publication and replication** let others examine and retest a claim. They support scientific evaluation without guaranteeing consensus: different models can remain plausible until a comparison distinguishes their predictions.

8|**Cognition** includes perception, attention, remembering, categorization, visualization, language comprehension and production, problem solving, reasoning, and decision-making. These activities can depend on several operations even when the final response feels immediate.||**Categorization** means treating some items as belonging together while distinguishing them from others. **Visualization** involves mental imagery; language includes both understanding messages and producing them. These are different functions, not interchangeable labels for one ability.||Many supporting operations are **hidden from conscious awareness**. Effortless experience therefore does not show that the mechanism is simple, or that a person can accurately describe every processing step they used.
9|**Cognitive psychology is the scientific study of the mind.** Everyday expressions show how broad that word is: “call to mind” refers to remembering, “put your mind to it” to directed thinking, and “make up my mind” to deciding.||“Sound mind and body” concerns mental functioning, while “a brilliant mind” describes intellectual ability. Each expression points toward a different aspect of cognition rather than identifying one directly visible object.||Scientific study turns these broad references into specific questions about functions and mechanisms. The task is to identify evidence that would support an explanation of remembering or deciding, rather than relying only on the familiarity of the expression.
10|The mind can be described through **processes** and **representations**. Processes include perception, attention, memory, emotion, language, deciding, thinking, and reasoning: they are operations that transform or use information.||A **representation** is information about the world that can guide action toward a goal. For example, representing where an object is supplies information that a decision or movement can use. The representation and the operation using it play different explanatory roles.||This distinction is useful without being beyond debate. Some theories question whether processes and representations can always be separated cleanly; the point is to recognize what each term contributes to an explanation.
11|Philosophers also investigate minds and knowledge. **Socrates** is associated here with epistemology, or questions about knowledge, while **Laozi** raised concerns about perception's reliability. A claim must connect with observations that other researchers can obtain and evaluate again.||The difference is therefore not simply which questions are interesting. It is how questions are asked, how answers are tested, and what procedures let researchers compare explanations and establish agreement.
12|**Franciscus Donders**, a Dutch physiologist, investigated decision time in **1868**. The recorded duration includes detecting the light, any required response selection, and making the movement.||Donders could not observe a decision directly. Detecting the signal is enough to specify the response.||In **choice RT**, a right-side light requires one button and a left-side light requires another. The participant must identify which light appeared and select the corresponding response. Comparing these conditions is the basis of Donders' subtraction logic.||The illustrated response keys are **J after the light in the simple task**, and **J for the left light versus K for the right light in the choice task**. 14|A **reaction-time apparatus** measures the interval from stimulus presentation to the recorded response. The late-nineteenth-century equipment illustrates the practical need to present an event and register an action with consistent timing.||That measured interval includes **detecting the stimulus, any decision or response selection, and executing the movement**. The instrument records the combined duration, not a separate visible decision occurring inside the mind.||To estimate one operation, compare tasks that share other relevant stages. A raw reaction time alone cannot tell you how many milliseconds belong to deciding; the mental interpretation depends on the experimental comparison.
15|In the **simple task**, the light appears, the participant detects it, and a prepared button press follows. In the **choice task**, the participant must also determine which light appeared and select the corresponding button.||The recorded **behavioral response** is observable. The intervening detection and selection stages are **inferred mental operations** proposed to explain the timing difference.||The subtraction argument treats the tasks as sharing relevant stages apart from the added decision. If the tasks also change other operations substantially, their difference cannot automatically be attributed entirely to decision time.
16|Donders estimated **decision time = choice RT − simple RT**. If those shared operations take comparable time, subtraction leaves an estimate of the extra response-selection requirement.||The result is an **inference from a controlled difference**, not a direct recording of a thought. If the tasks also differ in other time-consuming operations, their difference cannot be assigned automatically to deciding alone.
17|**Donders (1868)** directly measured reaction times and used their difference to infer the duration of a mental operation. The behavioral measurement and the cognitive interpretation are separate parts of the argument.||This is a general feature of cognitive psychology: observable timing, accuracy, or errors provide evidence about processes that cannot be watched directly. The task comparison determines how strongly a result supports the proposed operation.||The **subtraction approach** also appears in neuroimaging, where conditions are compared to estimate activity associated with added processing. Again, interpretation depends on what the conditions differ in and whether the comparison isolates the proposed contribution.
18|**Wilhelm Wundt founded an experimental psychology laboratory in Leipzig in 1879** and used controlled self-observation to investigate conscious experience. Specifying the conditions was intended to make experience a subject of systematic study.||**Titchener's structuralism** emphasized basic elements of experience. Trained observers used **analytic introspection** to describe those elements, with the aim of explaining the structure of conscious experience.||The problem was verification: reports could vary and proved difficult to quantify and replicate. Without reliable agreement across observers and repetitions, introspective descriptions had limited power to settle scientific disagreements.
19|**Ebbinghaus (1885/1913)** investigated forgetting at the University of Berlin using lists of **nonsense syllables**. These reduced support from familiar meanings, making the learning task more controlled.||He repeatedly read a list aloud until he could reproduce it without errors. After a retention interval, he relearned it and measured the effort required. He repeated the procedure across many lists and delays.||Shorter intervals generally required fewer repetitions to relearn. Relearning provided a quantitative sign of retained learning even when the list could not initially be recalled perfectly, allowing forgetting to be studied as a function of time.

21|**William James** taught the first psychology course at Harvard and wrote **Principles of Psychology**, addressing thinking, consciousness, attention, memory, perception, imagination, and reasoning. 22|**John Watson (1878–1958)** objected to two features of introspective research: reports varied greatly between people, and explanations involving invisible mental processes were difficult for others to verify.||His proposed alternative was **behaviorism**: make directly observable behavior the object of study and explain relationships between stimulus conditions and responses. This removed reasoning, thinking, emotions, and other unobservable mental processes from the explanatory focus.||The proposal aimed to make evidence publicly measurable. It also restricted the kinds of explanations psychology would accept, creating the later disagreement over whether behavior could be adequately explained without internal operations or representations.
23|In the **1913 behaviorist manifesto**, Watson describes psychology as an **objective experimental branch of natural science**. Its goal is to **predict and control behavior** without appealing to unobservable consciousness.||The evidence is the relationship between observable stimulus conditions and responses. An account is expected to specify how behavior changes with those conditions rather than use an invisible mental state as the explanation.||The proposal gains a publicly measurable target but restricts the questions its explanations address. Asking what behavior reveals about internal processing becomes difficult if the theory excludes internal processing from the outset.
24|Compare two questions about the same response. **“What does behavior tell us about the mind?”** treats behavior as evidence from which internal operations can be inferred. **“What is the relationship between environmental stimuli and behavior?”** makes the observable relationship itself the target.||The second expresses Watson's behaviorist shift. It emphasizes how conditions and responses relate, rather than explaining the response with an internal representation.||Both approaches can use experiments and measure behavior. Their disagreement concerns what counts as an adequate explanation of the measurements, which becomes central to the later cognitive revolution.
25|In **Watson and Rayner's 1920 Little Albert experiment**, the lecture describes a **nine-month-old** becoming frightened by a rat after rat presentations were repeatedly paired with a **loud noise**. The noise already elicited fear; the rat became able to elicit it after pairing.||This is **classical conditioning** because changing a relationship between two stimuli changes responding to a formerly neutral stimulus. The critical relationship is rat–noise, rather than a consequence delivered because the child performed an action.||Watson used the result to illustrate an analysis of behavior through observable stimulus relationships. The design differs from **operant conditioning**, where what follows an action changes its future probability.
26|Pavlov's original research concerned **digestion**. Anticipatory salivation showed that responding could come to occur before the food itself, in relation to predictive events.||In **classical conditioning**, pair a previously neutral stimulus with a stimulus that naturally elicits a response. After repeated pairings, test whether the formerly neutral stimulus now elicits responding. The important change is the learned relationship between stimuli, not a consequence delivered after a voluntary action.
27|For **B. F. Skinner**, especially during the **1940s–1960s**, behavior is studied through relationships among **situations, actions, and consequences**. **Operant conditioning** occurs when consequences change the future probability of an action.||**Reinforcement increases** that probability; **punishment decreases** it. To apply either term, identify the behavior, what followed it, and whether its later occurrence increased or decreased.||The key relationship differs from classical conditioning. Classical conditioning concerns associations between stimuli, while operant conditioning concerns an action and its consequences. Calling any pleasant event reinforcement without checking its effect on behavior misses the definition.
28|The timeline moves from **Donders' reaction-time work in 1868**, through **Wundt's laboratory in 1879**, **Ebbinghaus' forgetting curve in 1885**, and **James' Principles of Psychology in 1890**. 29|In **Tolman's 1938 experiment**, rats explored a **four-armed maze** and were then trained to reach food at **B** from a starting point at **A**. 30|In Tolman's maze, a rat starting at **A** reaches food at **B** by turning **right**. 31|A **cognitive map** represents spatial relationships in an environment. 32|In **Verbal Behavior (1957)**, Skinner explains language learning through **operant conditioning**. Children hear speech, imitate it, and receive reinforcement for correct productions.||The proposed mechanism connects a produced response with its consequences: rewarding particular speech should increase its later likelihood. The explanation therefore appeals to learning history rather than an independently specified internal grammatical structure.||The next question is whether imitation and reinforcement are sufficient for the range of sentences children produce. The account must explain not only repeating familiar expressions but also creating new forms, including systematic errors that were never rewarded.
33|**Chomsky's 1959 critique** emphasizes two observations: children produce **sentences they have never heard**, and they make **novel errors that were not rewarded**. These are problems for an account relying only on imitation and reinforcement.||Imitating a heard form can explain repetition more readily than the productive creation of new combinations. Reward history alone also does not straightforwardly explain why children generate systematic new errors.||Chomsky argues that an explanation needs **internal cognitive structure**. The objection concerns the sufficiency of a stimulus–response account, not a claim that experience has no role in acquiring language.
34|The **cognitive revolution of the 1950s** shifted explanation from stimulus–response relationships alone toward the **operations of the mind**. Internal processing again became a legitimate scientific target.||Behavior remained the observable evidence. Researchers used timing, accuracy, and other measurements to ask what happens between receiving information and producing a response.||The change therefore concerns explanation rather than abandoning experiments. A cognitive account makes proposed operations and representations explicit enough to generate predictions, then compares those predictions with behavior.

41|A **model** selectively represents a system to explain it, visualize it, or predict its behavior. 45|Cognitive psychologists begin with **observable behavior**, such as reaction time, accuracy, or a systematic error.
