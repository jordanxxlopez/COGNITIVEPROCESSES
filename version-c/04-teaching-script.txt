2|**Attention** is the ability to focus on particular stimuli or locations. Different terms distinguish how that focus is allocated and how competing information affects it.||**Selective attention** prioritizes one thing while ignoring others. **Divided attention** distributes processing across more than one task or input. **Distraction** occurs when one stimulus interferes with another's processing.||**Attentional capture** is a rapid shift prompted by a salient event such as a loud sound, bright light, or sudden movement. It can interrupt an intended focus, illustrating that selection reflects both current goals and properties of competing input.
3|**Selective attention** promotes relevant information for further processing while limiting competing information. We do not attend to a large fraction of everything physically present in the environment.||Following one message can therefore reduce what becomes available from another, even though both signals reach the senses. This distinguishes **receiving sensory input** from selecting it for detailed processing or later report.||The demonstration makes that limit visible by engaging attention in one task while other events occur. Missing an event does not mean no sensory information reached the eyes; it raises the question of which processing attention supported.
4|**Colin Cherry (1953)** used **dichotic listening** to separate two simultaneous messages, presenting one to the left ear and another to the right. Participants **shadowed** the designated stream by repeating it aloud as it arrived.||Shadowing checks that attention is engaged with that message. Researchers can then ask what the participant detects or later reports from the **unattended ear**.||The method distinguishes a physically delivered signal from a selected signal. Comparing available information across the ears tests how selective attention limits processing without assuming that the unattended sound never reached the sensory system.
5|Participants generally could not report the unattended **message's content**, yet knew a message was present and could notice **speaker-gender changes or replacement by a tone**. Physical or acoustic information therefore survived even when meaningful content was poorly reported.||This motivates the cocktail-party selection problem: following one conversation does not imply that every other signal disappears at the sensory level. The evidence concerns different depths of processing, not a simple all-or-none claim that an unattended ear receives nothing.
6|The three model families differ in **where selection limits processing**. **Broadbent (1958)** proposes an early filter before meaning; **Treisman (1964)** proposes attenuation with both early separation and later effects; **MacKay (1973)** provides evidence relevant to late selection after meaning.||To compare them, ask what happens to the unattended message at each stage. Naming a model is less informative than predicting whether unattended physical features, words, or meanings can influence behavior.
7|In **Broadbent's early-selection model**, both incoming messages initially enter a brief **sensory store** for a **fraction of a second**. This holds information before the filter selects the stream that will continue.||The store makes initial input available without requiring that every message already be understood. **Sensory registration and semantic analysis are separate stages** in the proposed sequence.||Following the arrows is essential: the filter comes after this initial store but before the detector's higher-level analysis. That order explains how both streams can be registered while only one receives later meaning analysis in the strict account.
8|Broadbent's **filter** selects a message using **physical characteristics**, such as its ear of presentation or voice properties. Only the attended stream is passed to the detector.||Because the criterion is physical, the model does not need to understand every message before choosing one. Selection is **early**, preceding the later analysis of meaning.||The resulting prediction concerns unattended content: a stream rejected at this point should not receive the detector's semantic analysis. A meaningful influence from that stream therefore poses a challenge to a strict early block.
9|Broadbent's **detector** analyzes the message that has passed the filter, extracting higher-level characteristics including **meaning**. The preceding filter determines which input it receives.||“Processes all information” at the detector means **all selected information reaching that stage**, not both original messages. In the strict model, unattended information has already been excluded from this route.||This separates **selection from interpretation**. To test the model, researchers ask whether an unattended message can influence meaning despite its predicted exclusion before the detector.
10|After Broadbent's detector analyzes a selected message, its output enters a **short-term memory stage**, described here as retaining information for approximately **10–15 seconds**. Some information may then transfer to **long-term memory**.||The diagram therefore distinguishes initial sensory storage from later retention of selected, analyzed information. The two stores occupy different positions in the processing sequence.||For the attention argument, the crucial point is the ordering: **incoming messages → early filter → detector → later memory**. Information cannot be assumed to reach the later store merely because it initially arrived at the senses.
11|Broadbent's filter is a **bottleneck** because it restricts which information continues to later processing. It selects on **physical characteristics** rather than passing every message more slowly.||The attended channel proceeds, while the unattended channel is excluded from the later route in the strict account. Calling this a bottleneck identifies a selective restriction, not a general reduction in processing speed for all input.||It is **early selection** because the restriction comes before meaning analysis. A meaningful influence from rejected input would therefore require revising the claim that the channel was completely blocked at that stage.
12|Two findings challenge complete early exclusion. In **Moray (1959)**, listeners' own names were presented to the **unattended ear**, and approximately **one third** noticed them. Meaningful breakthrough occurred for some listeners despite the competing task.||In **Gray and Wedderburn (1960)**, the ears received **“Dear–4–Jane”** and **“8–Aunt–2.”** Participants could report **“Dear Aunt Jane,”** following a meaningful sequence across channels.||A rigid physical-ear filter cannot readily explain those patterns. The evidence motivates accounts allowing meaningful information to influence report even when it does not remain entirely within the designated stream.
13|**Treisman's attenuation theory** replaces a complete early block with a reduction in the strength of unattended input. The **attenuator** analyzes physical characteristics, language, and meaning while separating attended from unattended information.||The attended stream remains strong; the unattended stream is weakened but can continue to later processing. This makes the model an **intermediate-selection account**, with early separation and possible later effects.||The modification helps explain how most competing content remains difficult to report while an important word can sometimes break through. Reduced strength and different activation thresholds together determine what becomes available.
14|In **Treisman's attenuation theory**, the **attended message passes at full strength**. Its stronger representation is more likely to activate relevant words in the following **dictionary unit**.||Selection therefore changes how strongly information is represented. The attended stream has an advantage, but it need not be the only stream capable of producing any later activation.||This distinction helps explain selective listening together with occasional unattended breakthroughs. The comparison is between strong and weakened input, rather than a fully open attended route and a completely erased unattended one.
15|In **Treisman's theory**, the **unattended message passes at reduced strength**. Weakening the signal limits its effectiveness without removing every trace of it from the processing route.||The following dictionary unit contains words with different **activation thresholds**. Weak input can still activate an especially low-threshold word, such as an important or familiar item.||This links selective listening with occasional breakthroughs. Most unattended content may be poorly reported while one meaningful item becomes available, without assuming that every competing message received equally strong processing.
16|Treisman's **dictionary unit** contains word representations with different **activation thresholds**. A threshold is the activation level needed for that word to become available.||**Common or personally important words** have relatively low thresholds; **uncommon words** have higher ones. A listener's own name can therefore be activated by weaker input than many unfamiliar words.||The effect depends on both sides of the comparison: attenuation reduces unattended signal strength, while the word's threshold determines how much strength is required. This explains selective breakthrough without treating the entire unattended message as fully attended.
17|Treisman's account permits an ordered hierarchy of analysis: **physical cues → syllabic patterns → specific words → grammatical structure → meaning**. Each step describes the signal at a more organized linguistic level.||The unattended stream is **attenuated rather than shut off**, so its reduced strength limits how reliably it supports later analyses. The dictionary unit's activation thresholds also matter: a common or personally important word can respond to relatively weak input.||Together, strength and threshold explain why some unattended material becomes available while much of the competing message remains difficult to report. The hierarchy does not mean every weak message is processed equally well at every level.
18|**MacKay (1973)** tested whether an unattended word could influence the meaning of an attended sentence. Participants heard **“They were throwing stones at the bank”** in the attended ear and **“river” or “money”** in the unattended ear.||**Bank** permits a riverbank or a financial-bank interpretation. The biasing words favor different meanings, so a directionally appropriate effect would go beyond detecting a change in pitch or sound.||The experiment is relevant to **late selection**, which allows analysis for meaning before selection for awareness or response. The important question is whether unattended semantic information affects interpretation, even without conscious report of hearing it.
19|In **MacKay's 1973 test**,  participants chose between a **river-side** paraphrase and a **savings-and-loan** paraphrase. Their choice shifted toward the interpretation related to the unattended biasing word, even though they did not report awareness of that word.||MacKay's inference is that the word's **meaning** influenced processing. This challenges a strict filter that prevents any semantic analysis of unattended input; the behavioral effect and conscious awareness need not coincide.
20|A **late-selection model** proposes that inputs receive meaningful analysis before the main restriction on access to awareness or response. The route can be summarized as **sensory registration → perceptual analysis → selection → later retention and response**.||Selection determines which analyzed information is task-relevant enough to guide behavior. Failure to report an unattended word therefore does not by itself show that its meaning was never processed.||This is the model's claim, rather than proof that all real-world input always receives unlimited analysis. MacKay's semantic-bias result supports meaningful unattended processing under the tested conditions.
21|The three models differ in what happens to **unattended information** and where selection restricts access. **Broadbent (1958)** selects a physically defined channel **before meaning**, passing the attended stream and blocking the competing one from later analysis.||**Treisman (1964)** weakens the unattended stream instead of eliminating it. Low-threshold words can still activate, helping account for **Moray's own-name findings** and meaningful cross-ear sequences such as **“Dear Aunt Jane.”**||**Deutsch and Deutsch (1963)**, and late-selection interpretations of **MacKay (1973)**, place meaningful analysis before selection for awareness or response. An unattended biasing word can therefore influence interpretation without being consciously reported.||Use the evidence to compare predictions: ordinary shadowing shows restricted access, meaningful breakthroughs challenge complete early exclusion, and semantic bias shows that unattended meaning can matter under some conditions.
22|The early–late debate has **no single selection point that describes every condition**. Some tasks show relatively early exclusion, while others reveal meaningful effects of unattended information.||Researchers therefore ask **which conditions control attention**, rather than assuming that one fixed filtering location must explain every result. Task demands and available processing resources become part of the mechanism.||This changes how models are evaluated. An unattended semantic effect and a failure to report unattended content need not contradict each other if the experimental demands differ; a useful theory explains when each pattern should occur.
23|**Automatic processes** require little deliberate attentional control under the relevant conditions. They differ from effortful operations that need active coordination or item-by-item inspection.||In **visual search**, a distinctive feature can stand out and be detected readily despite surrounding items. This provides a concrete example of relatively low control demands.||Automaticity concerns the way processing uses control and resources. A quick response alone does not establish it: a familiar task can still require attention, and the demands must be evaluated in the actual task conditions.
24|An automatic process can interfere with the current goal. In the **Stroop task**, the required response is the **ink color**, but reading the word can activate a competing color response even when the word is irrelevant.||When word meaning and ink color conflict, reporting the ink becomes harder. The difficulty reflects competition between an automatically engaged process and the more controlled task instruction.||This explains why automaticity is not always beneficial. The same easy-to-trigger operation that helps ordinary reading can disrupt a task that requires responding to a different stimulus dimension.
25|**Processing capacity** is the amount of information the person can handle at a moment. **Perceptual load** concerns the demands of the current perceptual task: high-load tasks use more of that capacity, while low-load tasks leave more available.||The key comparison asks how much capacity remains for **irrelevant input**, not whether a harder task is generally easier to perform. Less distractor interference can occur precisely because the main task consumes more processing.
26|In **Forster and Lavie (2008)**, a **task-irrelevant stimulus** increased response time. That increase was greater when the main task was easier than when it was harder.||Interpret the difference relative to the corresponding no-distractor condition. Under low load, spare capacity can process the distractor; under high load, less capacity remains. A smaller distraction cost does not imply that the high-load task has shorter overall response times.
27|**Overt attention** involves moving the eyes toward information; **covert attention** shifts processing priority while gaze stays fixed. An **eye tracker** records **saccades**, rapid gaze shifts, and **fixations**, pauses at selected locations.||**Central vision** falls around the current point of gaze and includes the high-resolution **fovea**. **Peripheral vision** lies away from it and has lower spatial resolution. Where the eyes point and where attention is allocated are related but not identical measures.
28|**Stimulus salience** describes how much a region stands out because of physical properties such as **light–dark differences, brightness, contrast, color, and motion**. Salient information can guide selection from the image itself.||An involuntary shift toward a salient event is **attentional capture**. A sudden movement can interrupt a current task even when the observer did not intend to inspect it.||This is **bottom-up guidance** because the relevant influence originates in stimulus properties. It differs from looking somewhere because a scene schema or current goal predicts useful information there, although both influences can contribute to the same scan pattern.
29|**Top-down guidance** uses meaning, knowledge, and goals to influence eye movements. The observer seeks information relevant to what they expect or intend to do, rather than following only the most conspicuous feature.||A **scene schema** supplies expectations about which objects belong in a setting and where they usually occur. Those expectations can guide successive fixations toward plausible locations.||Different viewers or tasks can therefore produce different gaze patterns on the **same image**. The sensory display is shared, but the information being sought and the knowledge used to organize the search differ.
30|**Võ and Henderson (2009)** compared viewing behavior for objects that are **semantically consistent or inconsistent with a scene**. Scene knowledge makes some object–setting combinations expected and others unusual.||**Fixation duration** provides an observable measure of how viewing changes with that consistency. An object that violates the scene's expected contents can attract different processing from one that fits.||The explanation concerns meaning and expectation, rather than simply color, brightness, or physical contrast. Distinguishing semantic context from visual salience is what makes the result relevant to **top-down guidance**.
31|Eye movements can be guided by the **current task**, not only by what stands out visually. In meal preparation, gaze shifts toward objects needed for the next action.||Sampling information about the relevant object helps coordinate the sequence of actions. As the task changes, a different object or location may become useful even though the surrounding scene remains available.||This is **top-down scanning** because the observer's goal determines what information is sought. The same image or workspace can therefore produce different fixation patterns when the task changes.
32|**Precueing** directs attention to a location before a target appears. In **Posner et al. (1978)**, participants maintained fixation while cues created expectations about where the target light would occur.||Responses were faster at an expected location than an unexpected one. Because the eyes stayed fixed, the advantage cannot be explained simply by moving the target onto the fovea through a gaze shift.||The reaction-time difference provides evidence for **covert attention**, a change in processing priority without an eye movement. Controlling fixation is essential for separating covert selection from overt looking.
33|In **Posner et al. (1978)**, a **valid cue** directs attention to the location where the target actually appears. An **invalid cue** directs attention elsewhere, so processing must be redirected when the target arrives.||Valid cues are associated with faster responses, while invalid-cue responses include a cost of shifting from the misleadingly prioritized location. The relevant comparison is **reaction time under maintained fixation**.||This supports spatial selection without an eye movement. The benefit concerns where attention was allocated, rather than merely how long it took the eyes to move toward the target.
34|**Egly et al. (1994)** tested whether attention is organized by **objects as well as locations**. The cue marks **A**; a target there is valid, while targets elsewhere can be invalid but on the same object or invalid on a different object.||Compare two invalid locations **equally distant from the cue**. Faster detection at the same-object location is the **same-object advantage**: attention directed to part of an object also benefits other parts of that object.||Equal distance helps exclude a purely spatial-distance explanation. The result connects attentional selection with the visual system's organization of features into objects.
35|The labels **A, B, and C** mark positions, not fixed levels of attention. Whether **C has an advantage over B** depends on how the display groups those positions into objects.||When the object arrangement changes, the advantage can disappear or return. **A and C belonging to the same object again** restores the relevant grouping relation. Attention follows perceived boundaries and organization rather than a privileged coordinate called C.
36|**Schneider and Shiffrin (1977)** combined retaining a **memory set of one to four target characters** with monitoring rapidly presented **test frames**. Frames could contain a target, distractors, or random-dot patterns.||To respond correctly, the participant keeps the set available and checks incoming frames for a match. The task therefore coordinates retention with visual monitoring rather than testing one isolated character judgment.||Repeated practice allows researchers to examine whether initially effortful components become more efficient. Improved coordination can make divided attention more successful under the trained conditions, without implying unlimited capacity for simultaneous tasks.
37|The participant first retains a **target set**, then inspects **20 rapidly presented test frames**. A response indicates whether a target from the set occurred in any frame.||Each frame must be compared with the retained information, while targets must be distinguished from distractors. The task therefore coordinates **maintaining a target representation and monitoring incoming visual input**.||Repeating the procedure allows researchers to track how practice changes performance. Improvement can reflect more efficient coordination rather than a change in which characters or frames were physically presented.
38|In the reported practiced condition, the task became relatively **automatic after about 600 trials**. Performance improved toward a stable high level as the required combination of operations was repeated.||The change concerns control demands: operations that initially required effortful coordination could be performed more efficiently after training. Automaticity was acquired through practice rather than present from the first trial.||The number applies to these conditions, not to every skill. A result after 600 trials does not mean any task will become automatic after the same amount of repetition.
39|Schneider and Shiffrin interpreted practice as enabling more effective **division of attention**. Automatic processing can occur without a fresh intention to carry out each small step and uses fewer control resources than effortful performance.||The performer example raises a related possibility: consciously monitoring highly practiced components can sometimes disrupt fluent execution. This is a context-dependent claim, not a recommendation to stop attending to every practiced activity.
40|**Automaticity is task dependent.** Practice can reduce control demands, but a sufficiently difficult task may continue to require attention even after substantial repetition.||Distinguish improvement from complete automaticity. A person can become faster or more accurate while still using resources that another simultaneous task would compete for.||The task's structure and training conditions constrain whether attention can be divided successfully. Familiarity alone therefore does not establish that an activity consumes no resources or that an additional task can be performed without interference.
