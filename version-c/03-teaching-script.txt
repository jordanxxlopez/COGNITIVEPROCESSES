2|The **Gestalt laws of perceptual organization** describe systematic grouping. **Good continuation** favors straight or smoothly connected contours; **Prägnanz**, or good figure, favors a simple coherent organization; and **similarity** groups elements with shared visual properties.||These principles concern how features are organized into perceptual units, rather than merely naming each feature. A contour or color can affect which elements are experienced as belonging together.||Classical Gestalt theory treats these tendencies as **intrinsic or innate**, with a comparatively minor role for learning. The next question is whether environmental experience can also explain why such grouping tendencies are effective.
3|Perception can use **regularities in the environment** to interpret limited sensory evidence. **Physical regularities** concern recurring properties such as orientation and illumination; **semantic regularities** concern objects and activities typical of different scene types. This connects to Helmholtz's unconscious inference.||Encoding regularities is **adaptive** in the sense that it can improve identification of what is present when the image is incomplete or ambiguous. A useful expectation reduces the number of interpretations that need to be considered.||That usefulness does not by itself identify the bias's origin. **Ontogeny** concerns change within an individual's development; **phylogeny** concerns evolutionary change across generations. Learning, inherited capacities, and their interaction can all contribute to an adaptive perceptual system.
4|The **oblique effect** is an advantage for perceiving **vertical and horizontal orientations** over tilted, or oblique, orientations. The effect concerns detection and discrimination performance, not simply choosing to look at upright objects.||Verticals and horizontals occur frequently in the environment, so the effect fits an account in which perception is sensitive to environmental regularities. Useful orientations can receive stronger representation or more effective processing.||Keep the behavioral result separate from its explanation: the measured orientation advantage is evidence to explain, while experience and neural tuning are proposed contributors to why it occurs.
5|The **light-from-above assumption** uses a common illumination pattern to interpret shading as depth. Light and shadow on a surface are evidence about its orientation relative to the assumed source.||A region light above and dark below tends to look raised; the opposite arrangement can look indented. The assumption is useful because overhead illumination is common, but the shading does not uniquely determine depth on its own.||If the picture is rotated or a different illumination direction is assumed, a different depth reading may become favored. The example illustrates sensory evidence being interpreted through a prior about the environment.
6|In the **sand pattern**, the shading can support either raised relief or carved depressions. Under the light-from-above assumption, the arrangement of bright and dark regions makes one depth interpretation particularly compelling.||Rotating the photograph changes the shading's orientation relative to the assumed light source. Areas that appeared raised can then look recessed, even though the sand was not physically rebuilt.||The reversal reveals how an illumination assumption contributes to the percept. The surface pattern supplies evidence, but the experienced depth depends on how that evidence is interpreted.
7|The **deer** is perceived as a foreground figure partly because shadows make its contours and surface relief interpretable. Illumination cues help distinguish raised and recessed regions from the surrounding background.||This connects **depth interpretation** with **figure–ground organization**. The shadows help the system group a coherent figure rather than experience only a flat collection of patches.||Explaining the demonstration therefore requires more than recognizing the word “deer.” Identify how the lighting pattern supports depth and how that depth arrangement separates the animal from its background.
8|A **scene schema** is knowledge of what a type of setting ordinarily contains and how those contents are arranged. **Semantic regularities** concern the activities and functions associated with the setting.||For a **cognitive neuroscience classroom**, you might expect students, an instructor, seating, and presentation equipment. Those expectations come from knowledge of classrooms rather than from the current image alone.||The schema narrows likely objects and locations, helping guide recognition and search. Its usefulness depends on matching the actual setting; a familiar expectation can also make an unusual object more difficult or surprising to interpret.
9|In **Palmer's 1975 context experiment**, a scene appeared for **two seconds** before an object was presented for identification. The initial scene established expectations about what was likely to be seen.||Identification was most accurate after **appropriate context** and least accurate after **inappropriate context**. A **loaf of bread in a kitchen** fits the setting better than a **mailbox in that kitchen**.||The measured outcome is object-identification accuracy, while context is the explanatory comparison. The result shows that prior scene information contributes to recognition beyond the target's isolated local features.
10|The **inferential approach** describes perception as selecting a probable interpretation from the current image and knowledge of the world. The image can fit more than one possible source, so sensory compatibility alone does not settle the percept.||**Helmholtz's likelihood principle** explains selection in terms of what most probably caused the input. **Environmental regularities** supply the experience-based constraints that make some causes more plausible than others.||The preferred percept must therefore fit both available evidence and prior knowledge. This is constrained inference about a source, not a claim that expectations can produce any arbitrary experience regardless of the stimulus.
11|**Thomas Bayes (1701–1761)** supplies the probability framework. A **prior** represents the starting plausibility of a hypothesis, and the **likelihood** asks how compatible the current evidence is with that hypothesis.||These answer different questions. A rare event can strongly predict a sign when it occurs, while still being an unlikely explanation of that sign because the event is rare. Combining the two avoids confusing a good symptom match with a high overall probability.
12|Bayes' rule is **P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H) / P(E)**. In perception, **H** is a candidate interpretation and **E** is the observed image. The **posterior, P(H|E)**, asks how probable that interpretation is after seeing the image.||The **likelihood, P(E|H)**, asks how expected the image would be if H were true; the **prior, P(H)**, expresses H's plausibility beforehand. **P(E)** normalizes the result across possible explanations.||Likelihood and posterior reverse the conditioning and are not interchangeable. Combining prior plausibility with evidence, as discussed by **Kording and Wolpert (2006)**, allows an interpretation to be updated rather than selected from either source alone.
13|For **Mary's cough**, compare three possible causes: **cold, lung disease, and heartburn**. The Bayesian question is which explanation is most plausible after combining prior frequency with how well each cause predicts coughing.||In the illustrative **prior panel**, cold and heartburn are relatively common while lung disease is relatively rare. In the **likelihood panel**, coughing fits cold and lung disease more strongly than heartburn. These panels answer different questions.||Combining them favors **cold**, which has both a strong prior and a strong match to the evidence. Lung disease has a good symptom match but a weaker prior; heartburn has a stronger prior but a poor symptom match in this example.||This shows why **P(cough|cold)** is not the same as **P(cold|cough)**. A cause is not selected merely because it can produce the symptom or merely because it is common. The qualitative bars illustrate probability relations rather than clinical estimates.
14|Group the four accounts carefully: **Helmholtz, environmental regularities, and Bayesian inference** explicitly emphasize prior experience in deciding what is present. Classical **Gestalt theory** emphasizes built-in organization that can operate regardless of a person's deliberate interpretation.||The distinction is not absolute. Modern accounts allow experience to help shape organizing principles too. These theories can address the same percept while differing in the source and description of the constraints selecting it.
15|The **Müller-Lyer illusion** occurs when central line segments appear unequal because of the fins surrounding them, even when the shafts are physically equal. The judgment concerns **shaft length**, not the total extent of the fin-to-fin figures.||The surrounding geometry systematically changes experienced length. Recognizing that the shafts are equal does not necessarily remove the perceptual difference.||The cross-cultural comparison asks a further question: does the size of this effect remain the same across environments and populations? Variation would challenge an assumption that one frequently studied sample represents all human perception.
16|The lecture's **Segall et al. (1966)** example reports that line **a** needed to be about **one fifth longer** than line **b** for an equality judgment in the illustrated affected group. This is an approximately **20% physical adjustment**, not a claim that the two drawn shafts differ by that amount.||The **San forager comparison from the Kalahari** is presented as showing little or no susceptibility in that sample. The contrast motivates examining environmental experience rather than assuming one sample's illusion magnitude is universal.
17|The argument contrasts **Fodor's (1983)** expectation of relatively invariant low-level vision with the population differences discussed by **Henrich et al. (2010)**. Even basic perceptual judgments can vary across human groups.||The **carpentered-world** account connects the illusion to experience with culturally produced right-angled environments, discussed here with **Henrich (2008)**. In Bayesian terms, experience can shape the priors used to interpret geometry. The proposed mechanism concerns exposure and learning, not an inherent ranking of populations.
18|**WEIRD** stands for **Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic**. **Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan (2010)** question the practice of generalizing from these heavily studied populations to all humans.||The concern is **sampling and generalizability**: an effect measured reliably in one cultural setting may differ elsewhere, including on tasks often treated as basic or low-level. A diverse comparison is needed before assuming universality.||The slide also gives the critical mnemonic **MYOPICS—Materialist, Young, Obsessed with self, Pleasure-seeking, Isolated, Consumerist, Sedentary**—and references Neuroanthropology. This is a critique of social context and sampling, not a measured personality profile of every person in a WEIRD country.
19|The developmental question is how different environments could produce different **perceptual expectations**. The contrast between **Fodor (1983)** and **Henrich et al. (2010)** concerns whether even low-level vision should be assumed to vary little across humanity.||The Müller-Lyer example motivates a role for exposure to **carpentered environments**, discussed with **Henrich (2008)**. Repeated experience with particular geometry could influence priors used in interpreting a later display.||Distinguish the population difference from a full causal explanation of it. A developmental account must identify what information people encounter, how that experience could tune perception, and which resulting differences are actually measured.
20|Evidence for orientation tuning includes **single-neuron recordings in monkeys** and **brain-scanning studies in humans**. Some neurons respond especially well to frequently encountered stimulus properties such as horizontals and verticals.||**Natural selection** concerns inherited characteristics that improve survival and reproduction across generations. **Learning** changes response properties through experience within a lifetime. Both can be relevant; showing useful neural selectivity does not by itself tell us how much each contributed.
21|**Experience-dependent plasticity** is a change in brain structure or response properties through experience. **Blakemore and Cooper (1970)** tested its role by restricting kittens' early visual exposure to **vertical black-and-white stripes**.||The kittens later responded to a **moving vertical stick** but largely ignored horizontal sticks. Their visual cortical neurons responded predominantly to the experienced vertical orientation.||The study links altered experience with both **neural tuning and behavior**. It supports a developmental contribution to selectivity rather than only an adult consciously expecting one kind of stimulus, illustrating how the environment can shape what the visual system processes effectively.
22|The **fusiform face area (FFA)** is a region within the ventral visual system that responds strongly to faces in the illustrated category comparisons. Its name connects an anatomical location with a characteristic response preference.||Relative activation helps researchers investigate specialization: comparing responses across categories reveals which kinds of input engage a region especially strongly. It does not require the region to respond only to faces under every possible condition.||The next expertise experiments ask how this response pattern develops. Strong face selectivity is an observation; whether it reflects inherited organization, learning, or both requires additional evidence.
23|The **parahippocampal place area (PPA)** responds strongly to places or scenes in the comparison introduced here. Contrast this preference with the **FFA's stronger response to faces**.||The contrast suggests differentiated processing within vision. A broad pathway can contain regions with distinct response patterns rather than treating every category in exactly the same way.||The relevant claim is **relative selectivity**. Greater scene-related activation identifies a useful specialization without implying that one isolated region contains all knowledge of places or never responds to other input.
24|**Faces and places activate differentiated regions within the ventral stream.** The FFA is especially responsive to faces, while the PPA is especially responsive to scenes or places in these comparisons.||This gives two levels of description: the **ventral stream** is a broad pathway important for object perception, whereas **FFA and PPA** describe more specific regional response preferences within that pathway.||Specialization does not imply total independence. The result supports organized differences in processing while leaving room for interaction among regions and contributions from other parts of the visual system.
25|In **Gauthier et al. (1999)**, participants underwent **four days of Greeble-recognition training**. Greebles are novel objects, so training allows researchers to change familiarity and discrimination expertise rather than compare only lifelong categories.||After training, the lecture reports an FFA response to Greebles comparable to its response to faces. Because the measure is regional brain activity, describe increased **FFA activation**, rather than assuming that a whole area literally behaves as one firing neuron.
26|**Gauthier et al. (1999)** ask whether expertise contributes to the FFA's strong response to faces. Before training, unfamiliar **Greebles** produce weaker responses than faces; after **four days of recognition training**, their FFA response increases to the level reported for faces.||The before–after comparison shows that experience with a nonface category can change activity in a face-associated region. It therefore supports **experience-dependent plasticity** as a contributor to selectivity.||The result does not establish that all face selectivity is learned or that brief Greeble training reproduces lifelong face expertise. Its specific contribution is showing that the response pattern is modifiable through experience.
27|In **Gauthier et al. (1999)**, the **Greeble novice-versus-expert comparison** tests how training changes responses to a novel object class. The key comparison is the response to Greebles before versus after expertise develops.||A stronger response after training links recognition experience with functional change in the **FFA**, a region associated with face processing. This provides evidence for **experience-dependent plasticity** rather than merely showing that experts can name the objects.||The interpretation is a learned contribution to selectivity. It does not mean the objects have become faces or that a short training period reproduces every feature of lifelong face experience.
28|Movement can make a distant animal easier to identify. A **stationary horse** may be difficult to distinguish, while its walking supplies a changing pattern that supports recognition; the lecture extends the point to **kangaroos**.||There are two sources of useful change: **the object moves**, revealing its dynamic organization, or **the observer moves**, obtaining views unavailable from one location.||Both add constraints beyond a single snapshot. Walking around an animal or object can clarify shape and hidden surfaces, illustrating how action changes the evidence available for perception.
29|**Perception and action interact continuously.** As you move through a scene or manipulate an object, visual information is updated and even small changes can affect the next movement.||The cup example separates **perceiving the cup, reaching toward it, and grasping it**. Identifying the object supplies useful information, while its changing position and shape relative to the hand help guide the unfolding action.||This is a feedback relationship: perception guides movement, and movement changes what can be perceived next. A static image alone omits the ongoing adjustments involved in effective action.
30|The **sticky-mittens** example connects manual exploration with visual development. Mittens give infants opportunities to contact and act on objects before ordinary grasping is fully effective, changing the experience available to them.||The hypothesis is that **enhanced exploration** can accelerate some aspects of visual development while encouraging independent motor skills. Action changes which views, contacts, and object relationships the infant experiences.||The relevant link is between opportunities to act and opportunities to learn perceptually. The example does not require assuming that the mittens improve every visual ability or that vision develops independently of movement.
31|For **Garner interference**, classify one dimension while an irrelevant dimension is either **constant or variable**. The key outcome is the response-time cost of irrelevant variation.||For **integral dimensions**, such as brightness and saturation, irrelevant variation slows classification. For **separable dimensions**, such as shape and color in the example, the cost is small or absent. What attention can select independently depends partly on how perception has bundled the features.
32|The concluding questions connect **individual differences, environmental knowledge, neural tuning, and representation**. Two people may interpret the same stimulus differently because they bring different experiences and expectations to ambiguous evidence.||Regularities in the world can make those expectations useful, while **experience-dependent plasticity** provides a mechanism by which learning changes response properties. Behavioral effects and brain responses offer complementary evidence about the relationship.||A complete account therefore specifies both what information is available now and what the perceiver has learned or developed. It can then ask how perception interacts with stored knowledge and how these functions are represented in the brain.
