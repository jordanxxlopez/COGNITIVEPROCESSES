2|**Perception** is the brain's interpretation of sensory input. We experience the result of that processing, rather than a complete, unprocessed description of the world. The opening puzzle is why this reconstruction is usually useful and accurate even though the input can be ambiguous.||The answer connects **sensory evidence** with **knowledge of environmental regularities**. The world contains recurring patterns, so some interpretations of an image are more plausible than others. Perception can exploit those patterns to identify a likely source.||This approach explains both ordinary accuracy and systematic illusions: an expectation that usually helps can favor the wrong interpretation in a specially arranged display.
3|The **R. C. James Dalmatian image** contains the information needed to see a dog, but its patches may initially appear unrelated. Once the dog is recognized, the same patches become parts of a coherent object and scene.||The physical pattern has not been replaced. What changes is the organization and interpretation available to the perceiver. This prepares the distinction between incoming evidence and knowledge that helps interpret it.
4|**Perception supplies information for attention, memory, language, and reasoning.** Before an object can be selectively attended, remembered, named, or reasoned about, sensory input must be organized into something those processes can use.||That makes perception an active part of cognition. The experience of a coherent object depends on interpreting sensory information rather than simply receiving a ready-made description.||Other cognitive processes can also influence perception. Knowledge and goals can change which information is sampled and how an ambiguous pattern is understood, so the relationship is interactive rather than a one-way handoff.
5|**Perception is experience resulting from stimulation of the senses.** Its content depends jointly on incoming sensory information and what the perceiver brings to that information.||Four properties develop this definition. **Added information can change a percept**; interpretation can resemble **reasoning or problem solving**; perception occurs in conjunction with **action**; and **prior knowledge** helps resolve ambiguity.||The Dalmatian pattern makes the knowledge contribution concrete. Knowing what to look for can turn disconnected-looking patches into an animal without changing the retinal pattern. Such knowledge usually helps identify the source accurately, but it can also produce a misleading interpretation when the usual assumptions fail.
6|Perception's speed makes its processing **invisible to awareness**, not simple. The lecture notes that roughly half the primate cortex is devoted to vision and contrasts rapid human recognition with persistent difficulties in machine perception.||Formal tasks such as **chess, theorem proving, and algebra** became tractable for computers before reliable object recognition. The **2007 DARPA Urban Challenge** illustrates how demanding real-world visual interpretation and action can be. Success on one kind of task does not establish that a system has solved perception generally.
7|The **2007 DARPA Urban Challenge** used hand-written rules and sensor fusion to complete a **60-mile urban course**, slowly and under controlled course conditions. Contemporary deep networks instead learn from millions of labeled images and can match or exceed human accuracy on particular recognition benchmarks.||High benchmark accuracy does not resolve every recognition problem. **Novel viewpoints, occlusion, unusual lighting, and odd scene context** can still change how a trained system interprets an object.||The difficulty is more than sensing: cameras can record considerable detail while the interpretation remains wrong. A stop sign altered by stickers illustrates why recognizing many familiar examples does not guarantee reliable recognition when the input changes.
8|Visual recognition must solve three linked problems: **infer the source of a retinal image, recognize objects from incomplete or degraded views, and recognize the same object across viewpoints**. These arise even when the sensory system is working normally.||A retinal projection does not uniquely specify the world that produced it. Missing contours and changes in viewing angle add further uncertainty about which object is present.||The common task is to resolve ambiguity using reliable structure and knowledge. Recognition therefore involves more than copying an image or comparing it with one memorized snapshot.
9|The **inverse projection problem** is working backward from a retinal image to the object that produced it. At a fixed viewing arrangement, an object projects an image, but the reverse mapping is not unique.||A **small nearby object and a large distant object** can produce the same retinal projection. More generally, different three-dimensional shapes and positions can fit the same two-dimensional evidence. The projection-line diagram represents this family of compatible possible sources.||Perception must favor a plausible cause using additional constraints. The image supplies evidence, but it cannot by itself uniquely determine the source's size, distance, and shape.
10|**Occlusion** means another object hides part of a target. Blur, low light, and peripheral viewing also reduce available detail, but we often recognize the target from the surviving fragments.||In **perceptual completion**, contours are experienced as continuing behind the occluder. The wheelbarrow behind a wall is interpreted as one partly hidden object, not several unrelated fragments. Determining which edges belong together is part of perception's work, not a label already supplied by the image.
11|**Viewpoint invariance** is recognizing an object as the same object across changes in viewing angle. Its identity can remain stable even though each rotation changes the actual retinal projection.||Observers have not memorized every possible view of every object, yet often recognize unfamiliar views. Recognition must therefore tolerate a changing sensory pattern rather than require one exact two-dimensional match.||This joins inverse projection and occlusion as an ambiguity problem. Different images can belong to one object, while one image can fit several possible sources; the system needs constraints that support stable identification across those variations.
12|**Bottom-up processing** brings information from sensory receptors into the perceptual system. **Top-down processing** reflects knowledge, expectations, and attention that influence how that evidence is sampled and interpreted.||These directions can contribute to the same percept. The sensory pattern constrains possible interpretations, while experience and current goals make some candidates more plausible or more relevant.||The useful question is what each contribution supplies. Calling an experience top-down does not mean that sensory evidence is absent, and identifying a bottom-up signal does not mean knowledge has no influence on its interpretation.
13|**Bottom-up, or data-driven, processing** begins when environmental energy stimulates receptors. In Mary's example, reflected **light reaches the retina**, retinal activity produces signals carried through the **optic nerve**, and further brain processing contributes to a representation.||The sequence is **stimulus → receptors → neural signals → representation**. It identifies the origin of incoming evidence without requiring that the observer already know Mary or expect her to be present.||Knowledge can influence the later interpretation of that evidence. Externally stimulated perception starts with input from the environment, whereas **mental imagery** need not begin with a currently present external stimulus.
14|**Top-down, or knowledge-driven, processing** originates in the perceiver's cognitive state and experience. It influences interpretation alongside the current sensory signal.||Three contributions are distinguished: **knowledge** supplies what you know about Mary; **expectation** concerns who or what you predict you will encounter; and **attention** affects which features you sample. Each can change how incoming information is used.||Bottom-up and top-down are sources of influence, not two mutually exclusive kinds of experience. To explain a percept, identify the sensory evidence and then specify what knowledge, prediction, or selection contributed.
15|The **direct-perception** side emphasizes information available in the environment; in Gibson's account, a moving observer receives an **optic array** that specifies environmental layout. Parts-based descriptions emphasize extracting and assembling stimulus information.||The **constructive** side emphasizes expectations, experience, and context: the percept is built through interpretation, and illusions demonstrate departures from the physical stimulus. The lecture's synthesis is that construction is **constrained by reliable environmental information**, rather than unconstrained guessing or a purely passive readout.
16|**Irving Biederman's recognition-by-components theory (1987)** proposes elementary volumetric features called **geons**. Recognition depends on both identifying these components and representing how they are arranged.||The lecture gives approximately **36 geons**, with around **three appropriately arranged geons** sufficient to identify many common objects. A partly hidden or unfamiliar view can still work if enough diagnostic components and their arrangement remain visible; simply detecting unrelated parts would not identify the object.
17|An **isolated blurry patch** contains limited information about identity. Several objects can fit the same local arrangement of shape and shading, so the patch alone may be hard to name.||**Context** supplies additional constraints: the surrounding scene makes some objects, positions, and scales more plausible than others. Restoring that setting can change recognition without replacing the local pattern.||The demonstration therefore separates information in the patch from information about the scene. It sets up a test of whether object identity depends on local features alone or also on the interpretation supported by the setting.
18|In **Torralba and Oliva's blob demonstration**, pixel-for-pixel identical local patches are interpreted as a **shoe, a car, or a pedestrian**. The patch's orientation and surrounding scene change the plausible identity.||A local shape-only account cannot explain why identical regions receive these different interpretations. The scene contributes expectations about which objects belong there and their likely scale and position. This is a concrete example of context supporting top-down recognition.
19|In **“I hope it's in my desk,”** the acoustic stream does not contain printed-style spaces at every word boundary. **Speech segmentation** is the listener's ability to locate those boundaries anyway.||An unfamiliar language can sound like a continuous stream because the listener cannot apply the same linguistic knowledge. The incoming sound is bottom-up evidence; recognizing likely words and boundaries from language and context supplies top-down support.
20|**Spectral rotation** alters the frequency structure of speech. In the comparison, rotated speech mainly exposes long pauses, while intact speech is experienced as separate words.||The **temporal envelope** is preserved, but the entire acoustic signal is not identical: its frequencies were transformed. The important control is that obvious differences in perceived word segmentation are not explained by newly inserted temporal gaps. Linguistic knowledge can organize the intelligible signal into words.
21|The **direct-pathway model** proposes nociceptors sending signals from the skin to the brain, with pain intensity tracking tissue damage. It is a bottom-up account of the relationship between stimulation and experience.||**Expectation, attention, and distraction** show why that relationship is incomplete. In **Atlas et al. (2012, Journal of Neuroscience)**, visible delivery of pain medication was associated with increased frontal activity and reduced activity in areas linked to nociception. The same stimulation can be experienced differently depending on what the person believes is happening.
22|**Perception as inference** means interpreting sensory evidence in terms of what probably caused it. An ambiguous image can fit several objects or arrangements, so the image alone does not select one.||**Helmholtz's likelihood principle** emphasizes the interpretation favored by past experience. **Bayesian inference** makes the relationship between prior plausibility and current evidence more explicit.||Both address selection among compatible causes. The claim is that knowledge helps resolve uncertainty in the input, not that perception ignores the input or requires a conscious verbal argument every time an object is seen.
23|Around **1860**, **Helmholtz** proposed **unconscious inference**: experience supplies assumptions about the environment that help resolve ambiguous sensory evidence. The **likelihood principle** favors the interpretation most likely to have produced the image given past experience.||This addresses the inverse projection problem, where many sources can fit the same retinal pattern. Familiar environmental relationships make some sources more probable than alternatives.||The inference is **inaccessible and involuntary**. “Unconscious” does not refer to a Freudian motive, and recognizing the ambiguity does not necessarily let you switch off the percept or inspect the reasoning that produced it.
24|The retinal configuration **(a)** can be produced by **(b), two overlapping rectangles**, or **(c), a rectangle aligned with an unusually notched shape**. Both alternatives are geometrically compatible with the visible pattern.||Most observers experience overlapping rectangles. The notched-shape account requires a precise coincidence of shape and position, while ordinary overlap fits familiar relationships among objects.||The example separates **possible sources from probable sources**. Sensory compatibility is necessary but does not uniquely determine the percept; experience favors the less coincidental explanation, illustrating Helmholtz's unconscious inference.
25|Keep the three quantities separate: a **prior** expresses how plausible an interpretation was before this input; a **likelihood** expresses how well the input fits that interpretation; a **posterior** updates the interpretation using both.||The slide's multiplication is a proportional relationship: **posterior ∝ prior × likelihood**, with normalization needed for a probability. A strong light-from-above prior can favor one reading of ambiguous shading. Different environmental experience can change priors and therefore change which interpretation is favored.
26|The disc is **light at the top and dark at the bottom**. Either a **bump lit from above** or a **dent lit from below** can produce that arrangement, so shading alone is ambiguous.||The **light-from-above prior** favors the bump interpretation. Rotate the picture and the shading orientation reverses relative to the assumed light source, so the favored reading can become a dent. The object depicted has not physically changed; the image's orientation relative to the prior has.
27|**Perceptual organization** groups regions, points, and contours into candidate objects. Before a scene can be understood as a set of identifiable things, the visual system must determine which visible features belong together.||An image does not explicitly label every boundary as belonging to one object or another. Grouping helps resolve that problem, including when contours cross or partly disappear behind another surface.||**Gestalt principles** describe systematic ways the field becomes organized. Their contribution is not merely recognizing a name after an object is formed; they help determine the coherent units that recognition can operate on.
28|The structuralist account associated here with **Wundt** treats overall experience as a combination of elementary **sensations**. A face would be understood through its elements; motion through successive views at different locations.||The **Muybridge sequence of stills** makes the snapshots idea concrete. The Gestalt challenge is whether simply adding elementary experiences can explain the organized percept, especially when the experienced property is absent from each individual display.
29|The **Gestalt psychologists, around 1912**, rejected the claim that organized perception is just added sensations. Their claim is that grouping changes the experience: **the whole is different from the sum of its parts**.||In **apparent movement**, discrete changes in a display produce experienced movement even though no object physically travels along the perceived path. Fixating the ring demonstration helps separate the changing physical display from the moving pattern the observer experiences.
30|**Good continuation** favors straight or smoothly curved paths through a display. Points along such a path are treated as belonging together, including when an object temporarily hides part of the contour.||In the rope display, the traced route shows the continuous path perception favors over arbitrary switches between segments. Following a cable in a tangle or a line on a subway map uses the same grouping tendency.
31|**Prägnanz**, also called **simplicity** or **good figure**, favors a simple organization consistent with a pattern. The Olympic symbol is perceived as **five overlapping circles**, rather than **nine irregular closed shapes** fitted together.||Both descriptions can account for the lines, but the circles provide a simpler coherent structure. The principle concerns which organization is experienced, not merely whether individual line segments are detected.||Compare this with Helmholtz: Gestalt emphasizes the **simplest** organization, while Helmholtz emphasizes the **most likely** source. The accounts can predict the same percept when a simple organization also corresponds to a common real-world arrangement.
32|The **law of similarity** groups elements that resemble one another in **color, shape, size, orientation, or lightness**. Organization depends on relationships among features, not only their separate detection.||In the dot display, changing the coloring can make columns emerge even though the positions remain fixed. The comparison isolates how similarity changes grouping without moving the elements.||This matters before recognition: grouping partitions the field into candidate units that later identification can use. The same geometry can therefore be experienced differently when feature relationships supply a different organization.
33|Apply each principle to the beach scene. **Good continuation** connects the horizon and waterline behind the person; **similarity** groups sand, water, and sky; **Prägnanz** supports three simple horizontal bands rather than a patchwork.||**Figure–ground segregation** separates the person from the background. **Common region and proximity** provide additional grouping cues. Real scenes can engage several principles at once, so one example need not have a single exclusive explanation.
34|The classical Gestalt emphasis is on **intrinsic organizing principles**, with a relatively small role for experience. This suggests early appearance and broad cross-cultural similarity.||The alternative emphasis is that smooth contours and similar surfaces often belong to the same real object, so grouping tendencies may reflect **learned environmental statistics**. Experience can matter without overriding all grouping principles. The common bridge is that environmental regularity makes those tendencies useful.
35|**Environmental regularities** are recurring patterns in the world, rather than random arrangements on each encounter. Some orientations, lighting directions, surfaces, and object combinations occur more often than alternatives.||Perception can use knowledge of those patterns to reduce uncertainty when current evidence is incomplete. The system need not consciously calculate a probability each time for an expectation to influence interpretation.||The next distinction is between **physical regularities**, concerning properties such as shape and illumination, and **semantic regularities**, concerning the objects and activities typical of a particular kind of scene.
36|Physical examples include frequent **verticals and horizontals**, light from above, relatively uniform surface color, and objects that tend to be **opaque and rigid**. These constrain how shape, shading, and overlap are interpreted.||Semantic examples connect function and content: **kitchens** contain stoves, kettles, and sinks; **offices** contain screens and chairs; **streets** contain cars, signs, and pedestrians. Physical expectations concern how a world is arranged; semantic expectations concern what is probable in a particular kind of place.
37|The **oblique effect** means better perception of vertical and horizontal orientations than of tilted ones: **finer acuity, faster detection, and more accurate orientation judgments**. The effect concerns performance, not simply a preference for looking at upright objects.||The lecture connects frequent orientations in both **natural environments and buildings** with a greater representation of those orientations in cortical tuning. Environmental statistics, behavioral advantage, and neural selectivity are three distinct levels of the same proposed relationship.
38|With a **light-from-above assumption**, light on top and shadow below favor a protruding bump; shadow on top and light below favor an indentation. The shading is interpreted in relation to the assumed illumination.||The two panels are the **same picture rotated 180 degrees**. Rotation changes its orientation, not the depicted surface's actual shape. The perceptual reversal illustrates a prior affecting depth interpretation rather than a change in the physical object.
39|The **sand sculpture** can be read as shapes raised above the surface or as depressions carved into it. Begin with where the light and shadow lie, then consider which depth arrangement would produce them under overhead illumination.||The **light-from-above assumption** makes one arrangement especially plausible. A different assumed illumination direction could support the opposite reading of the same shading.||Committing to a raised-or-carved interpretation makes the subsequent rotation informative. If the percept reverses when the photograph turns, the change reveals how shading and an illumination prior jointly determine experienced depth.
40|The sand demonstration uses **the same photograph rotated**, rather than a different sculpture. Turning the image changes the orientation of bright and dark regions relative to the **light-from-above assumption**.||A region that looked raised can appear carved, and a carved-looking region can appear raised. The visual system favors whichever depth reading fits overhead illumination in the new orientation.||The object has not physically changed. What changes is the relationship between the image and the assumed light direction, showing how an ordinarily useful, involuntary prior can determine the experienced depth.
41|A **scene schema** includes both **what belongs in a scene and where it usually belongs**. Imagining a kitchen brings up a stove, sink, and cabinets even when those objects were never explicitly requested.||The unfamiliar door handle makes the spatial component concrete: you know roughly what to seek and do not normally search the ceiling. Physical regularities constrain possible surfaces and shapes; semantic schemas narrow probable objects and locations in the current setting.
42|**Palmer (1975)** tested whether scene context changes object recognition. Participants saw a scene before a briefly flashed object. A congruent object, such as **a loaf of bread in a kitchen**, was identified more accurately than an incongruent object, such as **a mailbox in that kitchen**. The scene helped constrain which identity was plausible.||**Potter (1976)** tested how rapidly scene meaning becomes available. Pictures appeared in rapid succession at approximately **one eighth of a second each**, yet observers could detect whether a previously described target scene had appeared. This indicates that useful **scene gist** can be available before detailed inspection of every object.||**Torralba and Oliva's blob demonstration** shows how scene information changes interpretation of a local region. **Pixel-identical patches** can be perceived as a **shoe, a car, or a pedestrian** in different settings because the surrounding scene supplies different candidate identities.||Together, the examples show context narrowing recognition possibilities. Rapidly available scene meaning helps determine which objects to expect, so local input is interpreted in relation to a plausible setting rather than independently of it.
43|**Perception and action form an ongoing interaction.** Perception guides where we look, move, and reach, while those actions change the evidence available for the next percept.||Moving around an object can reveal a hidden part; turning it can expose a new surface; changing position can clarify its distance. The observer is therefore able to seek information rather than wait passively for a single image.||A stationary snapshot leaves out this feedback. We perceive in order to act effectively, and act in ways that make subsequent perception more informative.
44|Movement addresses all three earlier problems. Moving sideways reveals different relative motion for near and far objects; moving around an occluder reveals hidden parts; changing viewpoint supplies additional information about three-dimensional shape.||In **motion parallax**, nearby objects sweep quickly across the visual field while distant ones drift more slowly. This is an especially clear example of useful information in a changing **optic array**, consistent with the direct-perception emphasis on an actively moving observer.
45|Trace the pathways anatomically: **ventral: occipital → temporal**, emphasizing object identification; **dorsal: occipital → parietal**, emphasizing spatial location and visually guided action. Different damage can impair recognition or reaching while sparing the other ability.||**Ungerleider and Mishkin (1982)** used monkey lesion evidence for “what” and “where.” **Milner and Goodale** emphasized “perception” and “action,” favoring **“how”** for dorsal processing. The streams interact; these names indicate functional emphasis, not completely isolated systems.
46|After **carbon monoxide poisoning and ventral damage**, **D.F.** struggled to report a slot's orientation or match it with a held card, yet rotated her hand appropriately when **posting the card through the slot**. The same orientation information supported action more effectively than conscious judgment.||**Mirror neurons** are a separate observation: some premotor neurons respond during both performing an action and observing that action. D.F. supports a distinction between visual functions; mirror-neuron responses support overlap between some action-observation and action-production mechanisms. Neither observation implies that all perception and action are identical.
47|The chapter's argument begins with **ambiguity**: inverse projection, occlusion, and viewpoint changes mean that several possible sources can fit retinal input. A literal image readout therefore cannot explain every object percept.||**Bottom-up evidence and top-down influences** jointly constrain the solution. Knowledge, expectation, attention, and scene context help favor one interpretation; **Helmholtz and Bayesian accounts** describe that selection in terms of inference from experience and current evidence.||**Gestalt grouping** organizes the field into candidate objects, while **physical and semantic regularities** explain why many perceptual assumptions work well. The assumptions are usually useful because they reflect real patterns in the environments in which perception develops and operates.
48|The claim that **Helmholtz and Gestalt are fundamentally incompatible is false** in the comparison developed here. Both address how one interpretation is selected from several that fit the image.||Helmholtz emphasizes the **most likely source based on experience**. Classical Gestalt theory emphasizes the **simplest coherent organization** and treats organizing principles as intrinsic.||They differ in explanatory emphasis, but their predictions can agree. In ordinary environments, the simplest organization is often also a likely source, so accepting one useful insight does not require assuming that every prediction of the other must be opposite.
49|Perception is usually accurate because **the world is regular and perceptual assumptions reflect those regularities**. The brain combines ambiguous sensory evidence with expectations that generally match how objects, lighting, and scenes are arranged.||This makes reconstruction constrained rather than arbitrary. An expectation narrows possible interpretations, while current evidence limits which of them remain plausible.||The same mechanism can produce an illusion when a particular display violates its usual assumptions. Ordinary success and occasional systematic error can therefore follow from the same information-processing strategy.
